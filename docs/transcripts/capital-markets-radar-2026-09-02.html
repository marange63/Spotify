Capital Markets Cross-Asset Radar

Transcript · September 2, 2026

Good morning, it's September 2nd, and here's your capital markets radar.

Start with the calendar, because it frames everything. On September 9th, the Treasury begins buying at least four billion dollars per operation in the ten- to thirty-year sector. Six days later, the Fed meets, and the market thinks it may hike. Two arms of the same government, pushing the same curve in opposite directions, inside a single week. That's the story.

Here's how we got here. Treasury Secretary Bessent announced on August 19th that he would double the size of long-dated bond buybacks. They go from two billion to at least four billion dollars per operation, running September 9th through November 4th. It is an explicit attempt to cap long yields. Then Fed Chair Kevin Warsh used his first Jackson Hole address, on August 28th, to say that soft recent inflation prints do not tell him that underlying trends have meaningfully improved. Markets flipped. September hike odds went from near zero to a high probability, with the CME FedWatch reading quoted this week at roughly sixty-six percent. Treat that as a number that reprices daily, not a settled fact. Then on August 31st Bessent pushed back in public. He argued the economy faces a supply shock, that you don't raise into one, and that core inflation remains, in his words, very, very restrained.

Four days ago this program flagged exactly this as live headline risk: a hawkish chair leaning against an administration that wants lower rates. It is no longer a tone dispute. A sitting Treasury Secretary is now arguing rate policy in public and backing the argument with market operations.

Be precise about what a buyback actually does. It is a duration swap, not debt retirement. Treasury pulls long bonds out of private hands and typically funds that with bills elsewhere on the curve. Whether it caps the thirty-year depends entirely on its size relative to gross coupon issuance in that sector. We could not get that number from the Treasury's own release, so nobody should imply they know whether the program works.

There is a sharper problem underneath it. The rift itself is a term-premium input. A Treasury Secretary publicly instructing the Fed on rates is the exact fact pattern that raises the independence premium the buyback is designed to compress. The intervention may be fighting itself. If it does work, though, the payoff reaches past the curve. A capped thirty-year pulls down mortgage rates and long-dated corporate borrowing costs into year-end. That is the one channel through which this institutional fight touches real borrowers rather than just traders.

Bessent's whole case rests on one claim, that this is a supply shock. Here's what can actually be checked. US forces struck Iranian targets again after tanker attacks near the Strait of Hormuz. Brent crude climbed three-point-eight percent to ninety-four dollars and thirty-six cents, putting WTI on pace for its highest settlement in over a month. The IEA has cut its 2026 global oil supply forecast to 102 million barrels a day. That's a decline of four-point-three million barrels a day on the year, and the agency cites the collapsed ceasefire and the renewed strait closure. That is real evidence for Bessent's framing, and it is the strongest evidence either man has.

But there's a horizon problem neither official is addressing. A supply loss that size, sustained for months, stops looking like an inflation shock. It starts looking like a demand-destruction shock. At that point a hike is tightening into a growth contraction rather than into an overheating economy. Both men may be arguing about the wrong horizon.

Note also what sustained crude above ninety does inside the equity index. Energy producers take the benefit. Consumer-facing and transport-heavy names absorb the cost in margin. So this is a rotation trade before it is an index-level trade. The index itself has been orderly about it. The S&P 500 fell zero-point-seven percent on Monday to seven thousand six hundred thirty-one, with the Dow down four hundred nineteen points. That is a pullback, not a repricing of earnings.

That argument is being had over a curve that has already moved. The thirty-year hit five-point-three-three percent intraweek in late August, its highest since 2007. That print came before the buyback announcement, which then helped stabilise it. The ten-year kept climbing anyway, rising for a fifth straight session to four-point-seven-nine percent, a new high since January 2025.

Follow-up data adds the useful detail, with a caveat on precision. One data feed puts the two-year near four-point-four percent against a thirty-year near five and a quarter. Those are directional reads, so hold the decimals loosely. The shape is the point. The front end is largely contained. The long end is doing almost all the work. That is a fiscal and term-premium signature, not a Fed-led parallel shift. It argues the Treasury lever, not the Fed lever, is what's actually moving this market right now.

Volatility tells the same story in a different register. Cboe's weekly cross-asset note has the twenty-year bond vol index jumping from the thirteenth to the thirty-second percentile week over week. The same note has the VIX up nine-tenths of a point to fifteen-point-one over the week of August 24th, a week in which the S&P fell one-point-four percent. Those percentiles are one vendor's framework, so carry them as a relative signal. But the MOVE index, the broad rates-vol gauge, sits at seventy-three. That is the fifty-fourth percentile of its own history, squarely mid-range. A nineteen-year high in the long bond without a new high in rate volatility is a repricing, not a dislocation.

That distinction has teeth in one place. Yields parked near the five-point-three area keep pressure on available-for-sale bond books at regional banks. That channel has been dormant since 2023. It is the most plausible route from a rates event to an actual credit event, and it works off the level of yields, not their volatility.

Now the pattern break. Yesterday this program said gold, the dollar and bitcoin were refusing to confirm the hawkish bond move, and leaned toward the bond market over-reading. That call did not survive the session. Gold is at a two-week low, about four thousand three hundred seventy dollars an ounce, roughly twenty-two percent below its January record. Copper fell toward six dollars forty a pound, a one-month low. Bitcoin sits near seventy-eight thousand one hundred fifty dollars, about thirty-one thousand dollars below where it traded a year ago. And the dollar index, rather than resisting, has firmed to its recent high above ninety-nine and a half.

The dissent has collapsed. Gold falling into a geopolitical oil shock is the genuinely non-consensus read here. The market is pricing this as a real-yield story, not a currency-debasement or stagflation story. Just don't over-read one week of capitulation. The drawdown from January is the durable fact. The daily print is not.

And there's an asymmetry buried in that new unanimity. If the Fed holds on the 16th, the dovish surprise is now measured against two-thirds odds of a hike rather than near-zero. Gold, the dollar and duration would all have to reverse at once. The more completely the cross-asset complex confirms, the more violent the unwind if it's wrong.

One asset hasn't been asked the question yet. High-yield spreads sit around two hundred seventy to two hundred eighty basis points. That is the richest decile of the index's history, against a long-run median near four hundred fifty. Four days ago this program said that if bond volatility woke up, credit would widen first. It hasn't. But the freshest print available is dated August 24th, before Warsh even spoke. That test is unscored, not passed. A stale print cannot disconfirm anything. And an index in the richest decile of its own history has far more room to move wide than tight, right into a live rate decision. Nobody is being paid for that asymmetry.

There is one more duration bill nobody has totalled. If the Fed hikes into a cost-push oil shock, the front end and the long end tighten at the same time. The most duration-sensitive block in the index is the AI capex complex, whose valuation rests on cash flows years out. A simultaneous move at both ends of the curve is a rarer headwind than either one alone.

So here's what resolves this. Payrolls land September 4th, and nothing before that tests a hike probability built on tone and oil alone. The first buyback operation is September 9th. The Fed decides on the 16th. Watch one thing above all. If the thirty-year richens through that first buyback operation while hike odds hold steady, the Treasury lever is beating the Fed lever, and the curve-flattening trade is the one to own.
