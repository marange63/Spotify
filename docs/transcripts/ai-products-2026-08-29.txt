Good morning. It's Saturday, August 29th. Three companies shipped agents that act on your behalf inside a two-day window this week, and they disagreed about almost everything except what not to measure.

Start with the lead. On August 26th, Anthropic took Claude in Chrome to general availability on every paid Claude plan. That's a real threshold: not an enterprise pilot, every paid subscriber. The bigger change is what the extension can now do without asking first. Claude can act autonomously in the browser, clicking links, filling forms, navigating pages using your existing logins, instead of stopping to ask approval on every step. A safety classifier checks each action before it executes. The scope is still narrow in one respect worth noting: Anthropic says the extension doesn't run on other Chromium browsers, and not on mobile yet.

Here's the callback worth naming. Earlier this week this show noted that Anthropic's browser-use tool went generally available with no published performance figure attached. Anthropic has now published one. Before any additional safeguards, prompt-injection attacks succeeded against Opus 4.5 roughly eighteen percent of the time, and against the newer Opus 5 only about four percent of the time. With the new safety classifier layered on top, Anthropic reports no successful attacks at all against Sonnet 5 or Opus 5. Those are Anthropic's own numbers, from Anthropic's own launch post.

So they answered the question this show has been asking. But look at which question they answered. That's a security number, not a task-completion number. On a direct read of the primary post, there is no figure anywhere for how often Claude actually finishes the browsing task you gave it, correctly. The post measures whether the agent can be hijacked, not whether it works. Give the security result its due first, because the honest version is better than the cynical one. Anthropic also discloses what the strongest defenses available back in November 2025 could do against that same Opus 4.5. They took the attack success rate from roughly eighteen percent down to about seventeen. Barely a point. So this isn't a flattering baseline trick. Nine months of defensive work moved that number almost not at all, and this generation moved it a lot.

The problem is what sits underneath the percentages. Anthropic doesn't disclose how many attack attempts or how large a probe set produced any of them. A zero percent success rate against ten probes and against ten thousand are very different claims, and an outside party can't tell which one this is. And the scope limit that matters most, restricting autonomous action to approved domains, is disclosed only for Enterprise plans. If you're an individual paid subscriber letting this thing loose on your own logged-in email or banking session, there's no stated vendor-side guardrail beyond your own judgment. That's the enterprise IT question flipping over. It's no longer whether to allow agents. It's whether session cookies and single sign-on, which were built to authenticate a human, mean anything when the principal holding them is software. So what does a buyer take from this launch? An agent that resists hijacking is necessary, not sufficient. Whether it can reliably complete a checkout or a form remains unmeasured and unpublished.

That unresolved question is exactly where the next product picks up, because Anthropic's agent and OpenAI's newest browser feature disagree on method entirely. Claude in Chrome operates a page it doesn't control. It clicks and types the way a human would, against an interface that was never built with an agent in mind. A day earlier, on August 25th, OpenAI shipped the opposite premise inside ChatGPT's built-in desktop browser: Site tools, its implementation of an experimental open standard called WebMCP. Instead of an agent guessing at a visible interface, a website offers structured, callable actions directly to the agent, and the agent works with the same live, signed-in page a human is using.

In principle that's the better architecture: cooperative and durable, rather than fragile scraping that breaks the moment a page redesigns itself. In practice, the gating tells you how early this is. Site tools only works on two specific model variants, GPT-5.6 Sol and Terra. The third variant, Luna, currently has WebMCP disabled outright. And it's unavailable in Enterprise and Edu workspaces, which happen to be exactly the tenancies where agentic commerce would need governance most. A standard its own author has switched off for its enterprise customers isn't yet a standard. It's a preview with a name. OpenAI also paired the launch with a ten-day developer challenge backed by prizes from commerce and hosting partners. That's a supply-side recruitment drive to get the tools built, not evidence that demand exists. Whether WebMCP becomes the cross-vendor convention that MCP itself became for tool-calling, or stays an OpenAI lane, is the open question, and nothing in this week's launch settles it.

Still, watch the shape this would take if it works, because three things would follow. First, being legible to agents becomes a commercial discipline, the way search optimization did. Second, platforms like Shopify would sell that legibility as a feature, so individual merchants wouldn't rebuild it once per AI vendor. And third, the platform, not the merchant, would inherit the decision about which agents get served.

Whichever architecture eventually wins, there's a cost problem sitting underneath both of them. An agent that runs all day against a live page is a metered expense. And right now both OpenAI and Google are selling the tokens that agents burn at promotional rates below their standard pricing, which this show flagged on Wednesday. Those rates expire on named dates: November 21st for OpenAI, January 1st for Google. That's the constraint Perplexity's newest launch is built to answer. On August 25th, Perplexity and Nvidia launched Portable Computer, an on-device version of Perplexity's agent that runs entirely on a user's own RTX graphics card with at least twenty-four gigabytes of video memory, roughly a GeForce RTX 3090 or newer. It's available now to Pro, Max, Enterprise Pro and Enterprise Max subscribers on Linux, with Windows support following in September.

Running the local model costs essentially nothing per task, because there's no API meter running. Perplexity's own internal benchmark, reported by VentureBeat, put the local Qwen model at fifty-nine point six percent on its task evaluation. Escalating the harder cases to a frontier cloud model, Claude Opus 5, raised that to seventy-three percent, at roughly forty-one and a half cents per task, which VentureBeat describes as about two-thirds of the cost of running everything in the cloud. That's the honest way to read the pitch. But read the other side of the same number. Even with cloud escalation available, more than a quarter of tasks still fail. And zero marginal cost is a relocation of cost, not an elimination of it. The user already bought a high-end GPU and pays the electricity, and Nvidia is the partner with the clearest interest in that reframing. Still, if hybrid local-and-cloud economics work as advertised, it's a template for competing on cost per task rather than on subscription price, and that pressures every agent vendor whose margin currently depends on rates that reset later this year.

One closing beat, briefly. Through August, GitHub pushed Copilot toward model choice over model loyalty, adding Moonshot AI's Kimi K3 across Copilot Pro through Enterprise plans, and, per trade reporting, Microsoft's own coding model and xAI's Grok as selectable options in the same agent. If the browser is where the agent surface is being contested, the model underneath it is being commoditized in parallel. Note what that is and isn't: it's a supply-side option, with no published figure on how many developers actually switch models per task rather than staying on the default.

Here's the thread across all of it. Every load-bearing figure today, Anthropic's injection rates and Perplexity's benchmark, comes from the party the number benefits, measured on its own terms and on its own tasks. That's run for more than a week on this beat, so treat it as a standing feature of this market, not a fresh discovery. What would break the streak is a third party publishing a task-completion rate for a browser agent, measured independently, on tasks the vendor didn't choose. Nobody has done that yet.
