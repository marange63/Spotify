Digital Money, Crypto, and Market Structure

Good morning, it's Sunday, October 25th, and this is your digital money briefing.

The new number this week isn't a price. It's who did the buying. Per Farside's tracking, BlackRock's IBIT took in $565.7 million over the five sessions through Friday, October 23rd. Every other spot bitcoin ETF, combined, lost about $264.5 million over the same stretch. Net those two and you land on the complex's provisional weekly total of $301.2 million. In other words, the entire week's net demand came through one fund.

That revises what we told you Saturday. Our read then was breadth in, breadth out. Wednesday's buying had come through IBIT, Fidelity's FBTC and Grayscale's GBTC, and Thursday's selling hit the same big issuers. Over the full week, though, the selling was broad and the buying that held was not. Concentration is back as the live risk.

One caveat on all of these numbers. They are tracker figures relayed by the press, not issuer disclosures, and weekly totals differ by provider. Friday's figure is still provisional, because Farside's table was missing VanEck's fund data as of the latest reporting we have.

There are three honest readings of the split, and the flow data can't tell them apart. One: IBIT is simply the real allocator channel. Institutions concentrate in the deepest, cheapest wrapper, and their demand survived a drawdown. That's the bull case. Two: this is rotation. Holders are moving out of pricier funds into IBIT. That's a change of wrapper, not new money, so the net figure overstates fresh buying. Three: the smaller funds carry more of the fast, basis-trade money, and what we're watching is that money leaving first. Treat any choice among the three as inference.

Now the strongest bull argument, stated plainly. On Thursday the 22nd, $635.49 million left the complex in a single day. That was the largest outflow since June 25th, and we led with it yesterday, so take it as a callback. Friday added roughly another $138 million out.

Together, those two days pulled $773.5 million and trimmed what had been a stronger early-week gain. And yet the complex still finished the week positive. That is real evidence of a sticky core holder. It just arrived through one door.

Here's the second-order problem with one door. If a single fund is the whole marginal bid, a turn in IBIT's flows has no offset anywhere else in the complex. Issuer economics also concentrate along with the flows, which squeezes the smaller sponsors on fees and scale. That's our inference, not something the data shows yet. But it's the risk the positive weekly number hides.

That one-door bid got tested again on Saturday, so here's the price side. The line this desk has tracked since mid-October is Glassnode's estimate of about $81,500 as the average spot ETF holder's cost basis. Yesterday we called Thursday's dip to that line a pierce, not a breach.

On Saturday, bitcoin went further. The Block reports it fell to about $79,280 around 11:20 in the morning, Eastern time. By 1 p.m. it was back at $80,138. The Block itself describes that as a brief drop below $80,000. We found no source reporting a daily close below $80,000. We also found no updated Glassnode statement that the cost-basis line broke on a closing basis. So the test stays open.

Be precise about what that leaves. Even at the recovery price, the average ETF holder sits a little under the line on marks. On our arithmetic, it's less than two percent under. Underwater on marks is not the same as forced to sell. It makes de-risking by advisers and risk committees possible. Nothing in the data shows any mandate acting on that line.

The leverage side explains the speed of the move. The Block puts liquidations across crypto at $436.44 million in the four hours to 1 p.m. Eastern on Saturday. Of that, $404.15 million were longs, about ninety-three percent.

The same article gives a wider twenty-four-hour total of about $815 million. That window comes with no long-short split, so it can't carry the long-heavy story. The four-hour figure is the scoped one. Either way, this was a long squeeze. And a same-day recovery after that kind of flush is a small point for resilience. The market absorbed a leveraged unwind without a confirmed breakdown.

The same question, who stays in under stress, is what the stablecoin rulebook just answered for issuers. The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency's final rule implementing the GENIUS Act was published in the Federal Register on October 23rd. It takes effect January 18th, 2027. On Thursday we covered its looser side: the OCC dropped a proposed presumption against affiliate-paid rewards. The run side is new.

The standard redemption deadline stays at two business days after a qualifying request. But the proposal's automatic extension to seven calendar days is gone from the final text. So the rulebook is looser on what issuers can pay around their tokens, and tighter on what they have to survive in a run.

Removing an automatic extension isn't the same as banning extensions. It means a delay no longer kicks in by default. The mechanism still pushes one way. An issuer facing a redemption surge has to plan on meeting a two-day deadline out of its own reserves. By our inference, that favors overnight and very short bills over term paper. That's a mechanism, not a measured outcome yet.

There's a genuine counter-reading. A hard two-day deadline with no automatic delay could make bank-issued tokens more credible to corporate treasury desks. It could equally make them less flexible for the issuers themselves. Which effect wins is unknown.

Two limits on how far to run with this. First, the OCC is one regulator. The Fed's companion rules and Treasury's own rule aren't final, so the stack is still incomplete. Second, nothing launches under these rules before January. Any competitive threat to Tether and Circle is a 2027 story. And whatever banks do launch will likely carry tighter run obligations than the incumbents face today. That last part is our inference too.

Put the week together and the pattern repeats on both sides. The institutional layer of digital money isn't broadening under stress. It's narrowing to its sturdiest channel. One ETF carried the week's entire net demand while the rest of the complex sold. And one regulator just made bank-issued stablecoins less flighty by making them less flexible. Concentration, not breadth, is the live story.

Three things to watch, all still pending. First, the Fed's decision on Wednesday, October 28th. Second, Coinbase's third-quarter results on Thursday the 29th. That quarter ended in September, before this week's sell-off, so the guidance and commentary matter more than the reported numbers. Third, whether IBIT keeps holding the line while the rest of the complex bleeds, or whether that single channel starts to crack.

That's the digital money briefing for October 25th. Talk to you next time.
