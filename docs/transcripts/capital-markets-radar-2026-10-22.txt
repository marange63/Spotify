Good morning, and welcome to Capital Markets Radar for Thursday, October 22nd, 2026.

Last week the tape was about AI credit. This week the pressure is coming from somewhere else. It is coming from the long end of the Treasury curve, and from outside the United States.

That is a change from how this desk has been talking. For five days we described the rise in the ten-year as a referendum on the Fed path, a story about higher expected short rates. We said it was not a rising term premium. The latest read says we were at least partly wrong about the source of the pressure.

Start with the levels. The ten-year Treasury yield is sitting at about 5.1 percent. On Tuesday the broker Mitrade had it at 5.14 percent, a touch above the Treasury's own par curve, so treat 5.1 as the honest number. The dollar index closed Tuesday at 101.99, after briefly topping 102.

The notable part is the pairing. Yields and the dollar rose together. Mitrade's description is blunt: the push came from the long end, and from outside the US. One caveat on source quality. Mitrade is broker content, not a primary source, so I would hold this attribution loosely. But it fits the other data we have today.

Now the Fed, because it explains what is not driving this. Futures put only about a 17 percent chance on a hike at the October 27th and 28th meeting. The hold is priced. December is the opposite. FXStreet, citing fed funds futures, says the chance of a hike in December stands near 88 percent. Outlets range from the low 80s to about 90, so call it roughly 88.

That matters for how you read next week. A hold in October is a non-event. A December hike is nearly in the price. So the decision itself carries almost no information. The risk sits in the dots and the language, and it skews hawkish, because there is little dovish surprise left to deliver.

So the so-what is this. If the long end is selling off for global reasons, this is a broader tightening than a Fed-on-hold narrative suggests. Higher term premium weighs on equity multiples, on long-duration tech, and on corporate and mortgage funding costs. And none of it is waiting on Jerome Powell.

Which brings us to the first driver, oil. Brent touched 111.99 dollars on Tuesday, its highest in five months. It then settled at 108.79, down 76 cents. The reporting attributes the upside to a widening threat to Middle East shipping.

The transmission chain runs straight through the lead. Oil pushes up inflation expectations and headline CPI. That supports the December hike pricing. That pushes the long end higher. And that feeds the dollar. Gold has been weak in this setup, consistent with higher real yields, though I do not have a verified gold level to give you today.

The risk here is two-way, and the market is asymmetric around it. Trump says the US is not negotiating. Iran says indirect talks continue. Those accounts conflict. A credible deal headline would drop oil sharply, pull the long end lower, and lift gold and risk assets at once. So oil is both the cause of the squeeze and the cleanest exit from it.

The second driver sits in Europe. French bonds are selling off, and the euro is paying for it. The euro weakened to 1.1183 against the dollar on Tuesday as French paper sold off. Because the euro is the largest weight in the dollar index, euro weakness mechanically helps lift the dollar.

On the numbers, the ten-year French-German yield spread reached about 150 basis points on Tuesday's session, widening roughly eight and a half in a day. Other sources run from the high 140s to the high 150s, so say about 150. I would not lean on any claim that this is the widest in fifteen years, because that is not well sourced.

The dated catalyst is tomorrow. France's Treasury confirms that Moody's next scheduled review is Friday, October 23rd. Moody's currently rates France Aa3 with a negative outlook. A one-notch downgrade would take France out of the AA category. The outcome is unknown as I speak, so this is setup, not a forecast.

If it does come, the second-order effects are fairly direct. A downgrade into a global term-premium selloff could force benchmark-driven selling of French bonds. It would hit French banks, which are already weak. It would weigh on the euro, and that means a firmer dollar again.

And the dollar has its own pressure point. The yen held near 158.5 to the dollar on Tuesday, below the 159 level that has acted as an intervention watch zone. A strong dollar from external bond stress pushes the yen toward that line. Japanese intervention would be a sharp dollar-funding and carry-unwind event. I would call that a tail risk the market may be underpricing, not a base case.

So the so-what for FX is simple. The dollar bid is not a vote of confidence in the US. It is the world's duration problem showing up in the reserve currency.

Now the crypto read-through, because it is the cleanest test of what a five percent ten-year does to speculative assets.

Bitcoin cost about 82,700 dollars on Wednesday morning, per Cryptoticker. That is holding up better than you would expect against this rates backdrop.

The flows were constructive too. On Monday, 527 million dollars flowed into US spot Bitcoin ETFs, the strongest day since September. BlackRock's IBIT alone took in more than the entire net inflow for the day, which means other funds saw outflows.

But look at leverage. On the OKX exchange, the Bitcoin perpetual funding rate stood near zero on Wednesday morning, about three and three-quarter percent annualized. That is very low. So this is a spot-led bid, not a leveraged one.

I read that as fragile, not sturdy. A leverage-light bid is not stretched, which is good. But it also has no forced short covering underneath it. If the ten-year breaks decisively higher, there is nothing structural holding the price up. I would also not read one Monday as a regime change. Trackers disagree on the Wednesday flow figure, and it may be the same number dated differently.

The rotation inside crypto is clearer. US spot Ether ETFs have shed 1.08 billion dollars so far in October. That is the biggest monthly outflow of 2026. It leaves year-to-date net inflows at roughly 485 million. So money is moving into Bitcoin and out of Ether, the same pattern of owners stepping back that we have flagged for a week.

The structural news is regulatory. The OCC finalized its GENIUS Act rule for payment stablecoins. The framework takes effect on January 18th, 2027. It keeps the ban on interest or yield paid solely because a customer holds, uses, or retains its stablecoin.

What matters is the boundary. Issuer-paid yield stays banned. That favors bank and fintech issuers with clear compliance paths. It leans against exchange reward programs that look like yield. It also shapes the competition between USDC and USDT for share. I will not give you market caps, because I do not have verified figures. The point is that stablecoin balances are a marginal buyer of front-end Treasury bills, so the rule touches demand at the short end too.

One more slow-moving item, and I want to frame it as a standing watch, not news. Fitch said on October 13th that average redemption requests at business development companies fell to 9.8 percent of assets in the third quarter, from 10.8 percent. That is still well above the 5 percent quarterly cap. The queue of unmet requests rolls forward.

This is the same private-marks-follow-public-credit inference we have been running. Higher-for-longer rates and an oil shock raise the odds that stress shows up there before it shows up in headline spreads. It is the slow channel, but it is the one the market may be underreading.

So here is what to watch. Moody's on Friday, October 23rd. Hormuz shipping headlines, and whether any deal talk turns real. Whether the long end keeps leading the dollar. And on the 28th, the Fed. The hold is priced. December is nearly priced. The only live question is whether the dots and the language confirm it.

That is Capital Markets Radar for October 22nd. Stay cautious, stay optimistic, and we will see you tomorrow.
