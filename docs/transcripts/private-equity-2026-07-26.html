Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Equity & the Deal Economy

Transcript · July 26, 2026

Good morning. It's Sunday, July 26th, and this is your private equity briefing.

Yesterday we covered Blackstone's front door — the IPO reopening, three exits since May, the fee-related-earnings machine outrunning realized carry. Today we're looking underneath the front door, at the side doors the industry has built to move cash to LPs while the real exit market stays half-shut. And we start with a rare instance of the front door actually working: a genuine, contested, cash take-private.

EasyJet's board has agreed in principle to a takeover by Castlelake, the Minneapolis-based private capital firm, at 690 pence a share in cash — valuing the airline at roughly five-point-two billion pounds, five-point-five billion fully diluted, or about seven-point-three billion dollars. That price is the fifth bid in an escalating sequence that started at 560 pence in late May and climbed after four rejections. It's a 73% premium to where the stock closed the day Castlelake's interest first became public. Under the UK Takeover Code, Castlelake now has until August 3rd to convert this into a firm offer or walk away; the board says it will recommend a firm offer once made.

Two things make this worth an allocator's attention. First, it's genuine price discovery: this is what a sponsor now has to pay to move a contested public board in a capital-intensive, cyclical sector, outside the usual US mega-cap tech and software lane. A 73% premium after five rounds tells you boards are extracting real value before they'll cede control — take-private math isn't getting easier just because financing markets have loosened. Second, watch how Castlelake is described. Some reporting calls it a private credit firm, others a private equity buyer, and that ambiguity matters: this may be a control buyout funded substantially through a credit and asset-based platform rather than a conventional equity-led LBO. The line between the equity and credit sides of private capital is blurring exactly where you'd expect it to — in the financing of the deal, not just the credit markets covering it.

So that's the clean version of an exit: real cash, a real price, a real seller. It's also rare. Underneath it, the bigger story this year is how much of the industry's liquidity is being manufactured rather than earned, and the secondaries market is where that manufacturing happens at scale.

Preqin is forecasting a record roughly 250 billion dollars of secondary market volume in 2026, building on more than 220 billion last year. GP-led secondaries — where a sponsor moves assets into a vehicle it continues to manage rather than selling to a third party — hit 115 billion dollars in 2025, up more than 50% year over year, and continuation funds now make up 75% of that GP-led volume. By the third quarter of last year, GP-led secondaries already accounted for 16% of all sponsor exit volume industry-wide. There's plenty of capital chasing this: secondaries dry powder reached an estimated 315 billion dollars by the same point, and fundraising keeps confirming the trend — Coller Capital closed its ninth secondaries fund at 17 billion dollars in January, its largest ever, and a spinout from Ardian called Clipway closed a 6.4 billion dollar debut fund just four days ago.

Here's why that 16%-and-rising number should give you pause rather than comfort. In a continuation vehicle, the general partner is on both sides of the trade — it's the seller of the asset from the old fund, and it's also the manager of the new vehicle buying it. The same party that benefits from a high mark gets real influence over the price that mark converts into. And critically, distributions-to-paid-in, the metric LPs now use to decide on re-ups, doesn't distinguish clean cash from continuation-vehicle cash. An LP who rolls into the new vehicle gets no cash at all; only the LP who elects to cash out does. So as GP-led deals climb toward a fifth of all exits, headline DPI increasingly overstates what's actually landing in limited partners' accounts.

On pricing, Jefferies' full-year 2025 secondary market review — the closest verified read we have — put average LP-led portfolio pricing at 87% of net asset value, a 200 basis point discount widening from 2024, with buyout-strategy portfolios specifically at 92% of NAV. The real signal is the vintage split: funds under five years old priced at 95% of NAV, while tail-end funds over ten years old priced at just 73%. That 22-point gap tells you exactly where the stress concentrates — older, harder-to-price assets that GPs have held past their intended term, which is precisely the cohort continuation vehicles exist to recycle. Jefferies doesn't break out GP-led pricing specifically, so we can't yet say whether continuation vehicles themselves are pricing at a discount to the GP's own mark or closer to par — that's a real gap in what's currently disclosed, and worth watching for when H1 2026 data lands.

The same engineered-liquidity logic is now reaching retail. Annual flows into semi-liquid private equity vehicles have grown from 10 billion dollars in 2020 to a projected 74 billion dollars in 2025, and Preqin counted 123 new evergreen fund launches last year, a record, with another 30 in just the first two months of 2026. That capital is flowing into periodically-redeemable wrappers holding genuinely illiquid assets — a structural mismatch this show has flagged building elsewhere in the evergreen category this month. It's worth remembering that as inflows accelerate. A third channel does similar work at the institutional level: the NAV lending market, roughly 100 billion dollars today, is projected to more than triple to 350 billion by 2030, letting GPs distribute cash to LPs against portfolio value without selling a single asset — another lever that can inflate reported DPI without the disclosure LPs would need to tell it apart from a true realization.

All of this engineering exists to answer a real shortfall. Distributions ran at roughly 6% of buyout assets under management in the year to June 2025, against a ten-year historical average closer to 14%. Exit activity did rebound to 905 billion dollars globally in 2025, but 78% of that value concentrated in mega-exits, leaving mid-market inventory largely stuck and building a backlog of companies held past four years — exactly the pool continuation vehicles are built to absorb. Baseline forecasts see distribution yields improving roughly five points in 2026, into a 17 to 19% range, but that's one research house's estimate, not a settled consensus.

So where does the cycle stand. The real exit market is opening at the margin — Blackstone's IPOs, easyJet's take-private — but slowly and unevenly, concentrated in trophy assets and mega-deals. Underneath that, the industry has built three parallel channels, continuation vehicles, NAV loans, and evergreen retail wrappers, that manufacture distributions without always delivering true cash realizations, and the metric LPs rely on to judge managers is drifting from what it's supposed to measure. Watch three things next: KKR reports Thursday, with Apollo and Carlyle to follow, for whether peer managers confirm Blackstone's realization trend; easyJet's firm-offer deadline on August 3rd, for whether this deal actually closes at 690 pence; and EA's 55 billion dollar take-private, still stuck at CFIUS into a September 28th outside date, a reminder that even when financing is available, the binding constraint on the biggest deals is now regulatory, not financial.

That's the briefing. Have a good day.
