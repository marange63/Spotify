Good morning. It's Monday, August 24th, and this is your capital markets radar.

For six straight runs this program has pointed you at the same instrument. Watch the credit spread, not the yield, for where this market breaks first. New positioning data from Bank of America says that was the wrong thing to watch, and it changes what matters this week. One caveat before we start. U.S. markets have not printed a session since Friday's close, so every level in this briefing is a Friday reading or older.

Start with the mechanics. Bank of America's systematic-flows note, published Saturday, describes the books as they stood at the end of last week. Its language on rates is blunt. Commodity trading advisor positioning in U.S. Treasury futures remains heavily short. And last week's sharp rise in yields gave those shorts additional room, pushing their covering triggers further away rather than closer. That means the systematic bid that would normally arrive on a further yield backup is not sitting there waiting. Read it the other way too, because it cuts both ways. Trend followers that far from their triggers are also not about to be forced into buying Treasuries, which removes a support the long end has leaned on before.

The same note flags a squeeze building in currencies. Stretched short-euro positions held by slower-moving trend followers came under real pressure when the dollar dropped sharply midweek. If that squeeze extends, the dollar rebounds, and dollar funding conditions tighten from a direction almost nobody is currently watching. That would be a second tightening impulse arriving through foreign exchange rather than through rates.

Now the equity side, and this is the number that matters. Bank of America puts potential systematic selling above one hundred billion dollars if prices decline significantly from here. Its own thresholds have S&P 500 selling accelerating past roughly a three percent drawdown, and Nasdaq-100 selling accelerating past roughly five percent. Be precise about what that is. Those are not observed flows. They are Bank of America's conditional estimate of what would sell, not a position that has to unwind. But they tell you what kind of seller shows up first, if one shows up at all. It is mechanical, not fundamental. A shock does not need a fundamental justification to become self-reinforcing once it crosses those levels.

Here is the part that should unsettle you rather than comfort you. The calm itself is the setup. The VIX closed Friday at 15.13, down five and a half percent on the day. High-yield spreads sat at two hundred seventy-five basis points as of Thursday. Read separately, those look like two independent all-clears. They are not independent. Low realized volatility and tight spreads are the same crowding condition measured twice, because low volatility is precisely the condition that lets systematic and volatility-sensitive books carry maximum size. That inverts the heuristic this show has been applying all week. If the first mover is a machine rather than a fundamental deterioration, credit does not lead equities into the move. Credit reprices afterward, once the marks arrive.

And there is one place stress is already visible, because it never has to pass through a spread at all. In private credit, the share of business development company loan books using payment-in-kind — deferring cash interest rather than collecting it — has risen from roughly six percent to roughly ten percent since early 2022. Payment-in-kind is a negotiated deferral, not a traded price. It never shows up in an index, and it reaches investors in steps tied to the reporting calendar rather than continuously on a tape. So public-market investors get no early warning from private credit at all. The honest read on credit right now: it is fine where it is marked to a market, and quietly deteriorating where it is not.

So what would actually trip a setup like this? Something the market has to reprice on fundamentals rather than on positioning. There is a candidate from last week that this program has not covered. Walmart's fiscal second-quarter report, last Thursday.

Take the good news first, because the reaction only makes sense against it. Walmart beat on both lines. Adjusted earnings came in at eighty-one cents a share against a seventy-four cent consensus, a beat of roughly ten percent, on revenue that also came in above expectations at one hundred eighty-seven point nine billion dollars. Management then raised full-year guidance across all three lines, taking sales growth to four to five percent and full-year earnings to a range of two dollars eighty to two dollars eighty-seven. And the stock fell eight point nine percent in premarket trading.

The mechanism is the guidance, not the quarter that just closed. Walmart guided third-quarter earnings to sixty-two to sixty-four cents a share, against a FactSet-compiled consensus of sixty-eight. Management confirmed that same range on the call. And buried in that call is the tell on earnings quality. Operating income growth in the quarter included a net benefit of about seven hundred fifty basis points from tariff refunds received in the period. That is an operating income figure, not a gross margin figure, but it frames the question analysts are actually asking. How much of this quarter's profit strength was a one-time refund rather than something repeatable? Underneath all of it, U.S. comparable sales rose just two point six percent. That is the smallest gain Walmart has posted since a one point nine percent print more than six years ago.

There are three ways to read that, and they are not the same story. One: the consumer is genuinely rolling over, and Walmart is the cleanest real-time read on it. Two: Walmart is deliberately spending margin on price cuts to take share from weaker retailers, a competitive move dressed up as bad news. Three: investors are repricing the multiple on margin durability rather than on growth, now that a chunk of the quarter's profit is revealed to be a refund. The evidence supports the third most directly, because the company raised full-year guidance the same morning the stock fell nine percent. It does not rule out the first. And the second reading has the widest read-through, because if Walmart is buying traffic with price, peers have to match without Walmart's scale of tariff-refund offset. That compresses retail margins sector-wide into the holiday quarter.

Now set that against yesterday's lead on this program. The flash composite reading on U.S. business activity ran at its fastest pace since April of 2022, the strongest top-down growth signal in four years. Walmart's transaction data is the bottom-up counterweight, and it is a deliberate margin sacrifice at the largest retailer in the country. Do not resolve that divergence. Name it. The growth question now has evidence on both sides, and neither side settles it.

Last, the calendar, because that is the actual payload. Three dated catalysts land inside three weeks, on a market with no spread cushion above it and conditional systematic supply beneath it. The Jackson Hole symposium runs August 27th through the 29th, Kevin Warsh's first as Fed chair. The Senate's procedural vote on the CLARITY Act digital-asset bill is set for September 15th, pushed there after senators delayed the floor vote over ethics-rule disputes going into recess. And the Fed decides the following day, September 16th.

Two notes on how those land. On crypto, do not assume the legislation is what is priced. Crypto-linked equities ripped on Friday, with Robinhood up thirteen percent, in a week when that vote was delayed rather than passed and bitcoin rallied on a Treasury operation aimed at Treasuries. That is liquidity beta wearing a regulatory costume, which means a September 15th disappointment would hit the equities harder than it hits spot. On equities, dealer positioning showed S&P 500 hedger gamma of three point two billion dollars as of Wednesday, with roughly two point eight billion of it tied to expiries around Jackson Hole week itself. That gamma can mute the market's first reaction to whatever Warsh says. It does not remove the repricing. It defers it to the week after, once the hedge rolls off and those systematic triggers are still sitting underneath.

Nothing has to break this week for any of this to matter. The point is that this market has arranged itself so that whatever breaks first gets amplified rather than absorbed.
