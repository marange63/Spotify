Good morning. It's November 20th, 2026, and this is your briefing on artificial intelligence in biopharma R&D.

Two days after the largest private funding round in biotech history, Isomorphic Labs' next announcement wasn't a patient dosed. It was another partnership. Meanwhile, an AI-native company's lead drug just had its make-or-break trial defined. Underneath both stories sits one question. Who is actually holding the clinical risk in this industry right now? Today we follow that question from the design vendors to the one AI-native company carrying its own risk.

Start with the lead. On November 19th, Merck and Isomorphic Labs entered a strategic oncology collaboration. It covers a single undisclosed target, which Merck's release calls "an oncology target of strategic importance." The structure is the real story. Per Merck's own language, Isomorphic "will perform in silico predictions and deliver molecular designs." Merck, by contrast, "will be responsible for preclinical and clinical development." Translate that. Isomorphic designs on a screen. Merck does everything that happens in a living system, an animal, or a human being. That's pharma buying design as a service, while keeping the wet lab and all the clinical risk in-house.

Now put the clock next to it. Isomorphic's stated target, set by Demis Hassabis in January, is first human dosing by the end of 2026. The five-billion-dollar Series C release named no date. The Merck announcement names none either, and neither one restates or revises the target. About six weeks remain. That's two announcements in three days, one check and one partnership, and neither is a dosed patient.

Back to the deal terms. Isomorphic gets an upfront payment, plus research, development, regulatory and commercial milestones, and tiered royalties on net sales. One more term deserves attention. Isomorphic also gets an option to co-fund development in exchange for elevated royalties. That option is how a vendor turns itself into a part-owner of drug risk. Our read, not anything either company has said, is that this option is where the new five billion could convert into real clinical exposure. If Isomorphic starts exercising options like this, its valuation basis changes. It would look less like a licensing platform, and more like a royalty-bearing drug developer.

What we can't do is price Merck's conviction. Fierce Biotech and STAT both report the financial terms as undisclosed. The best comparison is Isomorphic's 2024 deal with Eli Lilly. That release disclosed a forty-five million dollar upfront payment. It put the potential total deal value at up to one point seven billion dollars. That's the familiar shape of pharma's AI-platform deals. A small premium is paid up front, and most of the value waits on milestones. For Merck, we don't even know the premium.

We do know one new thing from Merck's release. The collaboration incorporates, in Merck's words, "select high-quality proprietary data sets supplied by Merck." So Merck's data is feeding Isomorphic's engine. But neither Merck's release nor Isomorphic's announcement says who owns the resulting designs, the IP, or any model improvements trained on that data. For an industry worried about who captures the value of a model trained on someone else's data, that silence is itself a data point.

Merck's own research executive frames the value this way. Quote, "The key to successfully leveraging AI slash ML for molecular invention lies in close coordination of wet-lab and dry-lab." That's the buyer saying the model alone isn't the product. And in this deal, Merck owns the wet lab. So the takeaway on Merck is simple. Pharma is buying designs and keeping the risk.

The same template shows up at a second big pharma, with a second vendor. Earlier this month, per secondary coverage, Roche agreed to apply Chai Discovery's models across several discovery programs. No financial terms were disclosed. That's a second top-ten pharma spreading design work to a second AI vendor, rather than anointing one. It suggests the model itself is becoming a thinner moat. When pharma can rent design from several engines, differentiation moves to whoever controls proprietary experimental data, and whoever runs the clinic.

That has a budget consequence, as we read it. Design-only deals keep the downstream preclinical, clinical and manufacturing spend with the pharma sponsor and its outsourcing partners. The AI platform collects fees and royalties. It does not capture the development budget. That's a different business from the one the venture money appears to be pricing.

Now the opposite model, an AI-native company that keeps its own clinical risk. On November 18th, Recursion announced FDA alignment on a registrational Phase 3 design for REC-4881, its drug for familial adenomatous polyposis. As this show covered last week, REC-4881 is a MEK inhibitor that Recursion in-licensed from Takeda. Recursion's AI picked the target and the indication, not the chemistry. The new trial's primary endpoint is a composite measure of event-free survival. Per Recursion, the FDA agreed that removing adenomas of ten millimeters or larger, in the upper or lower GI tract, counts as an event in that composite. The randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial is expected to enroll about two hundred adults with FAP after colectomy or proctocolectomy. Recursion plans to begin it in the second half of 2027.

That makes this trial a specific kind of test. There are two kinds of AI drug claim. One is AI as a selector, choosing which target or disease to aim a molecule at. The other is AI as a designer, generating a new molecule. REC-4881 is the selector case, now headed for a controlled exam. But be precise about what this week delivered. It's a trial-design and regulatory-strategy win, not a clinical result. Recursion's own release says the alignment, quote, "does not ensure trial success or regulatory approval." Recursion calls the endpoint novel. Nothing in the record says AI shaped it, or shaped the FDA's acceptance of it. So nothing here tells you whether AI improved the odds. There is one possible spillover. If this composite endpoint holds up through Phase 3, other rare GI programs could cite it in their own FDA negotiations, whether or not AI was involved.

The designer case has a date too. Insilico Medicine's rentosertib is a pulmonary fibrosis drug with an AI-identified target and an AI-generated molecule. Its placebo-controlled Phase 3 has an estimated completion date of October 2029, per its ClinicalTrials.gov record. So there is now one controlled test for each kind of AI claim. Both are dated, and both are years away. The takeaway on Recursion: the exam is scheduled, but the grade is years off.

Zoom out for the capital-markets read. The money and the headlines sit almost entirely in design platforms. That means Isomorphic's five billion dollars, and the Merck and Roche partnerships. Clinical exposure sits mostly with the pharma sponsors, and with the few AI-native names like Recursion that run their own trials. The ordering of capital still runs opposite to the ordering of clinical exposure. For large pharma, these deals work as options to sample several AI engines without taking on platform risk. With terms undisclosed, we can't say how cheap those options are. For the AI-design names, a new partner is validation, but validation priced well ahead of any clinical proof. For contract research and manufacturing organizations, a crowded design layer shouldn't threaten revenue. Development spend still flows through them, whoever drew the molecule.

So pull the threads together. Nothing this week moved a measured clinical success rate, because nothing this week touched a patient. The two controlled tests are dated. REC-4881's Phase 3 starts in the second half of 2027. Rentosertib's Phase 3 is estimated to complete around October 2029.

Here's our judgment on the bigger question. If AI lifts clinical success rates, the gain most likely comes first from selection. That means picking the right target and the right disease. Much of Phase 2 attrition comes from the wrong mechanism or the wrong disease, not the wrong chemistry. A design-only deal like Merck's sharpens the chemistry against a target already on the table. So even if it works perfectly, it attacks what we think is the smaller part of the failure problem. That's why the 2027 selector trial matters more for success rates than the design partnerships grabbing the headlines.

On R&D spend, expect a reallocation, not a cut. Pharma is turning AI budgets into multi-vendor design options, while keeping its development budgets, and its clinical risk, in-house. Real-world data produced no new evidence this cycle. Eligibility and enrollment modeling on that data remains, in our view, a more defensible near-term cost lever than discovery-stage claims. But nothing this week tested it.

Three things to watch. First, whether Isomorphic doses a patient or files an IND before its year-end target lapses. Second, whether Merck's terms, its data contribution, or the IP ownership ever get disclosed. Third, whether REC-4881 starts its Phase 3 on schedule in the second half of 2027.

That's the briefing for November 20th. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
