Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Credit & the Corporate Credit Cycle

Transcript · August 7, 2026

Good morning. It's Friday, August 7th, and this is your private credit briefing. This earnings season, the story in private credit isn't defaults. It's marks and gates. The cycle is turning through valuation and liquidity mechanics before it shows up in the numbers everyone watches for stress.

Start with Blackstone Secured Lending, ticker BXSL, the largest publicly traded business development company by net asset value. It reported second-quarter net income of just nine million dollars, four cents a share, against net investment income of a hundred and seventy-four million dollars, seventy-five cents a share. That's a near-total gap between what the fund actually earned from its loans and what it reported to shareholders. The difference is fair-value markdowns. Blackstone cut BXSL's net asset value to twenty-five dollars and fifty-three cents a share, the lowest since the end of 2020, and the second straight quarter of a sharp NAV cut. Here's the part that should make you sit up. Non-accruals, the loans actually not paying, fell to one-point-eight percent of the portfolio's fair value in the second quarter, down from three-point-one percent in the first. So the fund's credit performance, by the metric that's supposed to signal distress, improved. And the valuation, the mark the manager puts on the book, got worse anyway. That inversion is the whole story. Losses may be showing up as valuation marks before they show up as formal default. Two readings compete here, and BXSL's own disclosures don't settle which one is right. Either the manager is being conservative, marking a book that's ninety-plus percent first-lien ahead of any real credit event, which would be bullish. Or non-accrual accounting is lagging what's actually happening in the underlying companies, which would be bearish. We don't have the sector or name-level composition of the markdown to know which. So watch whether this pattern repeats across the rest of the BDC sector this earnings season, because that's what would tell us whether marks are catching up to reality or BXSL just got conservative.

And where defaults do print elsewhere in private credit, they're increasingly the soft kind. Fitch's tracked universe of roughly thirteen hundred private credit borrowers shows a trailing twelve-month default rate of six percent in the second quarter, a fresh record, up from five-point-seven percent in the first. But more than half of this quarter's default events were maturity extensions, not missed payments or interest deferrals. That's the private-credit echo of the syndicated-loan liability-management story we've been tracking: a soft amend-and-extend counted as a default, inflating the headline rate without necessarily reflecting fresh cash-flow stress. Worth noting the sector split cuts against a clean read too. Industrials and manufacturing posted the weakest numbers, while technology software actually improved, to a one-point-two percent default rate from two-point-three. So the six percent headline probably understates cash-flow stress in the weak sectors and overstates it in software, and neither the BXSL markdown story nor the Fitch default print is telling you the full picture on its own.

Now separate the manager from what it lends. The same week BXSL reported, Ares Capital, the largest listed BDC, disclosed its largest new non-accrual: an eighty-four-million-dollar subordinated loan to AmeriVet Veterinary Partners, marked down to roughly sixty-two cents on the dollar. AmeriVet had already burned FS KKR, which put the same borrower on non-accrual back in the fourth quarter of last year. Ares Capital's net asset value fell to nineteen dollars and thirty-five cents a share from nineteen ninety-four at year-end, even as net investment income still covered about a hundred and four percent of the dividend. Now look at the parent. Ares Management, the manager that runs Ares Capital, raised a record thirty-six-point-four billion dollars in the quarter, grew assets under management seventeen percent year over year to six hundred seventy-one billion, and is sitting on a record hundred and seventy billion dollars of dry powder. Same house, same week, two completely different stories. The manager's fee and fundraising machine is booming. The vehicle that actually deploys and holds the loans is eroding. That gap between fundraising momentum and deployed, performing capital is exactly the distinction to hold onto whenever a manager's headline AUM number gets cited as evidence the asset class is healthy. So record dry powder that isn't being deployed profitably doesn't help fee growth, and NAV erosion with dividends held flat is how you get to payout cuts down the road.

The strain shows up in the plumbing too. Blue Owl's two flagship retail credit funds, Credit Income Corp and the smaller Technology Income Corp, both invoked their five percent quarterly redemption cap for a second straight quarter. Credit Income Corp saw redemption requests equal to eighteen-point-eight percent of shares, about three-point-six billion dollars. Technology Income Corp saw requests for thirty-eight-point-one percent of shares, about one-point-one billion dollars. That's a materially larger stress signal than anything we've seen from this corner of the market, and it escalates a thread that's been building for weeks across sponsors. Both funds had ample combined liquidity to meet the cap, so this isn't an insolvency story. It's a structural one: interval funds offering periodic liquidity against loans that don't trade. That mismatch is showing up right as the other side of the ledger cools too. New private credit loan issuance fell to forty-four-point-eight billion dollars in the three months through May, down roughly forty percent from seventy-four-point-six billion in the first quarter, while retail flows into alternative products fell seventeen percent month over month in May, the second straight monthly decline, with private-credit-specific flows down thirty-five percent. So sponsors can't simply out-raise redemptions when both new deployment and retail inflows are falling at the same time. That's a two-sided squeeze, not a one-off gating event.

Zoom out to the systemic linkage. A Federal Reserve Bank of Boston analysis, resurfacing in the news cycle this week, shows bank lending commitments to business development companies have grown from about ten billion dollars in 2013 to more than fifty billion by last year. The Boston Fed's own framing is reassuring: those commitments are under two percent of large banks' Tier 1 capital, and banks generally hold senior secured, first-priority claims. But Fed Vice Chair for Supervision Michelle Bowman told Congress in June that regulators still lack a complete view of how that bank-funded capital is actually being used once it reaches the private credit system. That's the tension to end on. The industry's own data says bank exposure is modest and well-collateralized. The regulator overseeing it admits a visibility gap.

Pull it together. The credit cycle isn't turning through a wave of defaults. It's turning through marks that are moving opposite to reported non-accruals, default rates flattered by maturity extensions, a widening gap between manager fee economics and vehicle-level performance, and a liquidity mismatch in retail vehicles that's escalating quarter over quarter. The single biggest unresolved question is whether BXSL's markdown is the first of a broad, overdue asset-class reset or an idiosyncratic, conservative call at one fund. Watch the rest of this earnings season's BDC prints for the same pattern, watch for further dividend-coverage cuts, and watch whether the next default print keeps leaning on amend-and-extend rather than genuine repayment. That's your private credit briefing. Have a good weekend.
