Good morning, this is The Throughline for Tuesday, October 13th, 2026.

Today's headline: four banks have spent more than six months unable to offload thirty-eight billion dollars of construction debt tied to a single Oracle-leased data-center project, because AI-linked exposure is bumping against their own internal limits.

Nine briefings shipped today. Robotics and Physical AI, Space and the Orbital Economy, and AI in Biopharma R&D did not publish. We'll start with the macro backdrop, then move into the AI and compute stack.

Strategic Power says FERC rejected the core of PJM's fast lane, which would have let data centers connect without matching new generation, because it intrudes on state authority over retail rates. The desk calls it the fourth stop in an arc it has tracked since last Tuesday: authority over who pays for data-center power is moving from grid operators to the states. Separately, uranium's long-term contract price hit a nominal record, even as uranium equities like Cameco keep selling off.

From power scarcity to capital flows. Capital Markets Radar says money leaned toward bonds and away from equities and crypto last week, with spot bitcoin ETFs logging their largest single-day outflow since June, though the desk cautions that the record bond-fund inflow comes from one newsletter's tally. Broad high-yield spreads stay calm, so the desk reads this as a rates repricing, not a credit-cycle one. Today's bank earnings and Wednesday's September CPI, neither of which has happened yet, are the first live tests of whether that move holds.

Digital Money picks up the crypto thread. Bitcoin took its second long squeeze in three days and fell last week while gold rose, which the desk reads, on a single week of evidence, as bitcoin trading like levered risk beta rather than a hedge. Underneath, Coinbase is building real institutional plumbing, from a USDC default in Samsung's wallet to a Citi settlement tie-up, but none of it shows up yet in a stock down more than fifty percent over the past year.

Private Credit and the Corporate Credit Cycle carries today's headline, per Financial Times reporting: some lenders will now sell pieces of that Oracle-linked package to non-bank buyers at a discount, while Oracle's credit-default swaps sit at levels last seen in 2009. The desk's skeptical read is that a package that size can breach every buyer's single-name limit without anyone doubting Oracle's credit, so this is a concentration problem at least as much as a credit verdict. And the private credit books that would absorb that risk already show a record Fitch default rate of six point three percent, though nearly nine in ten of those defaults are deferrals and extensions, not actual failures.

Private Equity and the Deal Economy finds the week's first price signal that didn't come from the seller. In first-half secondaries data, single-asset continuation deals cleared near or above the sponsor's own carrying value, while seventy-one percent of multi-asset deals traded below net asset value. The desk's read is that liquidity is real at the top of the quality stack and discounted everywhere else, and sellers take those discounts because median distributions still trail paid-in capital for every vintage back to 2018.

Now the AI and compute stack, where last week's OpenAI revenue headline is still working through the system. One disclosure: Anthropic makes the model behind this show, and it features in two of these briefings, so weigh them accordingly.

Semiconductors and Compute Infrastructure frames this week as the test of a split verdict. Chip equities sold off on the Financial Times report putting OpenAI's annualized revenue near fifty billion dollars, well under the roughly seventy billion that had circulated, while Micron's 2027 HBM contract book, locked in before that headline, showed no such doubt. ASML reports Wednesday and TSMC Thursday, and their forward commentary, not this quarter's results, will show which market is reading the room correctly.

Frontier AI Lab Competition leads with Meta's Muse Wonder Full, which the Artificial Analysis index places behind only Anthropic's Claude Opus 5.5, yet Meta has kept it off its public API and inside its own Muse Code subscription. The desk reads that as a reversal of Meta's open-weight posture, competing for the developer's seat with a product only Meta controls. On that OpenAI revenue gap, the reporting ties it to how partner-channel revenue gets counted, and since Anthropic also sells through Google's and Microsoft's clouds, the size of OpenAI's lead over Anthropic is far less certain than the market had priced.

AI Products and Applications tracks who controls the agent layer. GitHub shipped local sandboxing for its Copilot agents, but it's off by default unless an enterprise mandates it, so the containment burden still sits with the buyer. Atlassian, meanwhile, brought OpenAI's GPT-6 models into its platform while keeping its organizational context, the more defensible layer, for itself.

Enterprise Technology has the first channel-level sign that security budgets are turning: in a Jefferies survey of twenty-five resellers, none reported a pipeline decline, a first in eleven quarters, though pipeline is not booked revenue. The gains are concentrating in three platforms, Cloudflare, Palo Alto Networks, and CrowdStrike. And two of those three, CrowdStrike and Palo Alto, are founding partners in Anthropic's new Critical Infrastructure Defense Program, as Anthropic pays its way into the channels enterprises already trust.

Here's the thread worth carrying out. Since Thursday, this show has tracked the AI buildout's bill migrating onto balance sheets that didn't originate it: private credit behind Broadcom's chip for OpenAI, SpaceX's credit behind xAI's compute, and the Pentagon taking warrants in Wolfspeed. Today shows the next step, as banks that already underwrote that risk try to hand it to non-bank buyers whose marks aren't set by the credit-default-swap market.
