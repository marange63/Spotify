Good morning, it's Thursday, November 5th. Standing disclosure before we start: the model generating this show is one of Anthropic's, and today's lead story is about Anthropic's own financing and filings.

This week's frontier-lab competition turned less on model quality than on who everyone is financially tied to. Google went deeper into Anthropic's balance sheet, on top of being its chip supplier and its rival. OpenAI moved the opposite way, buying developer plumbing to route around Microsoft's GitHub. And a frontier-scale open-weight model landed underneath the coding-agent market both of them are fighting over. Let's take them in that order.

Start with Google and Anthropic. Anthropic holds a twenty-year lease with Raylan Data, a TeraWulf subsidiary, for about 401 megawatts of critical IT load at a campus in Hawesville, Kentucky. Yesterday, TeraWulf's own 8-K filing with the SEC confirmed something that had previously circulated only in secondary reports. In the filing's words, Google agreed to backstop certain obligations of Anthropic under what it calls the Justified Leases. Under certain circumstances, Google can instead pay a termination fee. And here's the detail that wasn't in the earlier coverage we had. Paying that fee entitles Google to acquire an equity interest in Raylan itself. So this isn't just Google co-signing Anthropic's rent. In effect, Google holds an option on the data-center subsidiary that houses Anthropic's compute.

Now the two things the filing does not confirm. The widely cited 19 billion dollars comes from TeraWulf's original lease announcement. It's the contracted lease revenue over the initial term, not the size of Google's backstop. Google's actual exposure isn't disclosed in the filing or anywhere we found. The second is a clause reported by secondary outlets like Unite.ai. Under it, Google generally can't walk away in the first six years unless installed TPU pods would draw less than half the tenant's critical power. That clause isn't in the 8-K. If it's accurate, though, it tells you whose silicon the backstop is built around. Our inference is that Google is protecting an anchor tenant for its own TPUs at least as much as it's extending credit to Anthropic. There's a plainer reading too: ordinary credit enhancement a neocloud needed to finance the build, priced by Google as a cheap way to lock in TPU demand. What the filing does pin down is timing. Capacity arrives in phases beginning in late 2027 and concluding in the second quarter of 2028. That's a touch later than the "full delivery in early 2028" framing that had been circulating.

So what does this mean? Yesterday this desk said Google sits on both sides of Anthropic's chip hedge, as TPU supplier and frontier rival. Now it has taken a third seat, standing behind Anthropic's data-center credit, with a path to owning part of the facility if things go wrong. It's also the next rung on the financing ladder we traced on November 1st. That ladder ran from CoreWeave's bonds, to Broadcom arranging financing for Anthropic's chips, to OpenAI's own equity round. Now a hyperscaler's balance sheet is standing in for a lab's credit.

The filing story gets a second chapter. Anthropic filed an amended S-1 on November 2nd. We couldn't read the amendment directly, but Dealroom's summary puts third-quarter revenue at 16.96 billion dollars. That's up from about 11.6 billion in the second quarter, a jump of nearly half in three months. The same summary reports a 567 million dollar operating loss for the quarter, which Dealroom characterizes as a slide back into the red. That complicates the adjusted-profitability story from the first half. According to that summary, the amendment doesn't mention the TeraWulf lease, the Google backstop, or the Kentucky campus at all. The financing relationship and the public filing haven't yet met on paper.

The filing also shows a dependence that pairs directly with the backstop. As Fortune reported it, Anthropic's top five customers made up about a quarter of revenue over the first nine months of the year. And nearly half of revenue flows through cloud marketplaces run by Amazon, Google, and Microsoft. So count the roles Google now holds: chip supplier, sales channel, credit backstop, and model rival. Amazon and Microsoft each hold at least two of those roles as well. One caution on the channel number. Marketplace billing isn't the same as hyperscaler-originated demand. Many enterprises buy through those marketplaces to burn down committed cloud spend, so "flows through" can overstate how much the hyperscalers control the customer. Now the two readings. The bull case is that hyperscalers this invested in Anthropic's compute won't let it fail, which should lower its cost of capital. The bear case is that whoever controls the channel, the chips, and the credit has leverage over Anthropic's take-rate and its independence. That is precisely the question the IPO has to price.

The entanglement runs the other way at the chip layer. According to wire reporting, AMD recorded a 1.07 billion dollar non-cash charge in its fiscal third quarter. The charge covers warrants tied to its chip-supply deals with OpenAI and Meta. Each of those warrants could eventually let the holder buy up to roughly 10 percent of AMD if milestones are met. The milestones haven't been disclosed, so this is an accounting recognition, not confirmed ownership. Still, it's the mirror image of the Google story. Google stands behind a lab's obligations, while AMD pays two marquee AI customers partly in its own equity. So at both ends of the supply chain, lab compute is increasingly financed through someone else's balance sheet.

Where Anthropic rents its distribution through rivals, OpenAI is buying its way out of that dependence. Yesterday OpenAI announced it's acquiring Pierre Computer, a developer-tools startup, and folding its Git-based code-storage product into Codex, OpenAI's coding agent. Pierre's Trees and Diffs libraries already run inside the Codex app. Its open-source projects stay open, and existing Code Storage customers keep access after the deal closes. Axios frames the deal as OpenAI taking on Microsoft-owned GitHub. On November 2nd, our AI products briefing covered GitHub's Copilot Fusion, which routes each coding task to models GitHub picks. GitHub is taking model choice away from the labs, and OpenAI's answer is to own the repository underneath. Treat the deal itself cautiously, though. No terms, headcount, or close date were disclosed. It could be a modest acqui-hire dressed up as a platform move. And moving enterprise repositories off GitHub is a far higher bar than shipping a Git backend inside an app. Still, the direction is clear. The coding-agent fight is moving down the stack, from who has the best model to who owns the repository.

And underneath that fight, the price floor moved. Moonshot AI has published open weights for Kimi K3.1 Pro, a release this desk has flagged repeatedly. A correction to our own record first. Yesterday we called it pending. Some coverage dates the posting to November 2nd, which would mean our status was already stale. Sources disagree, so treat the date as approximate. The model is a mixture-of-experts design with 2.8 trillion total parameters, 104 billion of them active, and the weights ship in MXFP4. Do the arithmetic. At roughly four bits per parameter, the weights alone run to something like 1.4 terabytes. That's a multi-node serving job. So the near-term beneficiaries are hosted inference providers, not companies self-hosting. The reported license terms conflict across sources, and we couldn't confirm them. And claims that it beats Claude on coding agents are vendor and aggregator framing, with no independently verified benchmark yet. Open weights also aren't adoption. China provenance and data-sovereignty concerns cut against enterprises trusting it with their code. So any pressure on Claude Code and Codex pricing arrives through hosted providers, if it arrives at all.

Tallying the dimensions: on compute and capital, Anthropic gained security but ceded leverage to Google. On distribution, Anthropic depends on rivals' marketplaces while OpenAI integrates vertically. On ecosystem control, it's OpenAI versus GitHub now, not just OpenAI versus Anthropic. On the open-weight front, Moonshot adds pressure that's real in theory and unproven in practice. On raw capability, there's no new independent read since our last briefing.

Three things to watch. First, whether any primary filing discloses the size of Google's backstop or the TPU clause. Second, whether a direct read of Anthropic's amended S-1 confirms that third-quarter operating loss, and what drove it. Third, whether anyone outside Moonshot publishes an independent agentic-coding benchmark for Kimi K3.1 Pro.
