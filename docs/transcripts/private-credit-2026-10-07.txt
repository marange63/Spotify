Good morning. It's Wednesday, October 7th, and this is your private credit briefing.

The headline this week is that bank examiners have gone inside the private-credit plumbing itself. Semafor reported Monday that Fed examiners have been running on-site reviews at four major banks since the spring: JPMorgan, Wells Fargo, Barclays, and Morgan Stanley. According to the people Semafor spoke to, the Fed has concluded its review of some of those banks, including JPMorgan. The same sources say the review was prompted in part by JPMorgan's own move in March to mark down large swaths of its private-credit loans. Those markdowns were concentrated in loans to software companies threatened by AI. No dollar figure for that writedown has been reported.

Hold that story at the right weight. It rests on unnamed sources, and the Fed has issued no statement. Nobody knows whether these exams end in public guidance, capital charges, or only private feedback to the banks. Supervisors moving before any blowup can be read two ways. Either they see a real linkage risk, or this is a routine horizontal review that the press has framed as alarm.

Here's the scale marker that matters either way. Loans to nonbank institutions have risen from 300 billion dollars in 2016 to more than 1.5 trillion today, according to FDIC data cited in the same report. That is now 11 percent of all bank loans outstanding. But that figure covers lending to all nonbank financial institutions, not private credit funds specifically. Nobody has broken out how much of it is subscription lines, NAV facilities, or back leverage to direct lenders. So treat it as the size of the channel, not a private-credit exposure number.

The SEC is working the other end of the same pipe. On September 28th, SEC staff warned registered funds that boilerplate or overly aggregated disclosure on private-asset fair values may not give investors enough context. So the SEC is checking what the funds say they own, and the Fed is checking what banks lent against it. The retail layer sitting on top of both has grown fast. The SEC's own statement puts private credit inside registered funds at 270 billion dollars as of last December. That's up nearly 60 percent from 170 billion five years earlier. So semi-liquid retail capital and bank leverage now sit in the same structures, and regulators are examining them from two directions at once.

The pressure isn't only American. The Bank of England's Financial Policy Committee, in its record dated September 30th, warned that higher interest rates could increase debt-servicing pressure on leveraged borrowers, particularly those using broadly syndicated loans or private credit. Taken together, supervisory attention has now moved down the stack, from the marks inside the funds to the leverage underneath them.

Now, the skeptical read. Most of what banks lend to funds is senior and secured. Subscription lines sit against uncalled investor commitments. NAV facilities are collateralized by diversified loan portfolios. Fund equity sits beneath the bank and takes the first loss. So the real question for banks is correlation and concentration, not first-loss exposure. The more plausible failure mode isn't bank losses from private credit going bad. It's banks pulling back on how much leverage they extend. That would raise the funding cost inside BDCs and direct-lending vehicles. And it would compress the levered-equity returns that underpin the yields these funds market to investors. Nobody has reported that happening yet, but it's the point to bank. The migration of lending from banks to nonbanks is partly bank-financed. Risk hasn't left the banking system so much as changed seats, and that financing can get more expensive without a single loan defaulting.

Banks are one source of leverage into direct lending. Securitization is the other. Private-credit CLOs, which package direct-lending loans, now account for roughly 20 to 25 percent of total CLO issuance, up from about 10 percent a few years ago. That's a market estimate rather than a clean LSTA series, but the direction fits everything else here. And banks typically sit on both sides, as lenders to the funds and as senior buyers and warehouse lenders in the CLO stack. So the two channels may overlap rather than add up, and that overlap is the transmission path from private books to bank balance sheets.

So why would examiners look through to collateral instead of trusting the disclosures? Because the disclosures may understate what's there. A Wall Street Journal analysis of four flagship direct-lending funds, including Blue Owl, found they reported software exposure of roughly 19 percent. The Journal's own count put it closer to 25 percent. That reaches us secondhand, via a summary of the Journal piece. A six-point gap could also reflect classification choices, like where software-enabled services get bucketed, rather than concealment. Still, it's exactly the kind of gap that makes an examiner want the loan tape rather than the fund's own sector-limit math. The so-what: stated concentration limits may protect less than they appear to.

Blue Owl itself shows where that software stress is actually landing. Investors sought to withdraw 4.2 billion dollars from Blue Owl's two non-traded credit funds this quarter. That's down from 4.7 billion in the second quarter and a record 5.4 billion in the first. So the aggregate dollar queue has shrunk for two straight quarters. The exception is Blue Owl's tech-focused fund, OTIC, a roughly 5 billion dollar vehicle built mainly on technology lending. Holders of OTIC asked to redeem 39 percent of its shares in the third quarter. Its sister fund, OCIC, saw requests of 16.8 percent, and Blackstone's flagship BCRED held at about 10. Blue Owl says much of the OTIC figure is investors resubmitting requests that went unfilled. That's the manager's explanation, not a verified one. Either way, redemption stress is sorting by sector, not spreading across the complex, and it's sorting toward software.

The realized-loss data points the same way, slowly. According to PitchBook, non-accruals across the ten largest public BDCs rose to 3.95 percent of debt at cost in the second quarter. That's up 20 basis points from the first quarter. Nothing newer has printed, because third-quarter BDC results haven't been reported yet. So the credit deterioration under all this regulatory attention is real, but it's a creep, not a cliff.

If the numbers themselves are now the regulatory battleground, the business of producing them becomes worth owning. KKR agreed this week to buy Gen II Fund Services for an enterprise value of 5.1 billion dollars. Gen II is a fund administrator serving more than 275 investment managers with over 2 trillion dollars in assets. Much of that client base is other managers' funds, not KKR's own. Read it as a fee-related-earnings play. It's a bet that compliance and reporting burdens rise with regulation, and a step away from reliance on carried interest as fundraising growth normalizes. One limit worth naming. Fund administrators calculate NAV and shadow the books. Managers and third-party valuation firms set the actual Level 3 marks. So KKR is buying the paperwork around private credit, not a lever over whose marks get challenged. Whether rival managers stay comfortable with KKR handling their back office is an open question.

KKR is betting on the reporting layer. The question of who ends up holding the risk itself shows up most sharply in AI financing. Lambda closed a delayed-draw term loan above a billion dollars on October 1st to buy more than 30,000 Nvidia GPUs. It's Lambda's first U.S. fixed-rate financing and its first institutional debt deal above a billion dollars. The loan carries investment-grade ratings from Moody's and Morningstar DBRS. It priced at a fixed 6.78 percent with semi-annual coupons, and it was oversubscribed. There's no benchmark spread in hand to judge whether that coupon pays for GPU obsolescence risk. And we don't know who the lenders are. But fixed-rate, investment-grade GPU paper is shaped for insurers and other IG-mandate buyers. That widens who ultimately carries AI capex risk beyond the high-yield and unrated investors who've carried it until now.

Pulling it together. Realized credit stress remains a slow grind rather than a break. BDC non-accruals were creeping up through the second quarter, this week brought no new default or non-accrual signal, and one sector-concentrated fund is absorbing most of the redemption pressure. The biggest unresolved risk has moved up a level. It's whether both the leverage behind these funds and the exposures inside them are larger than anyone's disclosures show. Watch for any public Fed guidance after these exams, and for bank third-quarter disclosures on nonbank lending. Then alternative-manager earnings from late October, and third-quarter BDC results, the first non-accrual update since the second quarter.
