Good morning. It's Tuesday, November 10th, and this is Cautious Optimism Briefings.

On Saturday we said KKR's print would tell us whether Carlyle's guided pullback was one firm's wobble or the sector's direction. KKR reported yesterday, and it answered loudly. The question now is how much of that answer is cash. And how much of it is private equity at all.

Start with the headline. In its earnings release filed with the SEC, KKR says it expects to return more than forty billion dollars to clients in 2026. It says that beats its previous annual record by more than fifty percent. For scale, the same release puts KKR's average for 2021 through 2025 at twenty-one billion dollars a year. So the guide is roughly double the firm's recent run-rate. That is the strongest realization claim of this earnings season.

Now read the definition, because KKR states it precisely. The figure covers distributions of realized proceeds by carry-paying funds and related vehicles. And it spans Private Equity, Real Assets, and Credit together. KKR doesn't break out how much comes from each strategy. So some unknown share of that forty billion comes from real-asset and credit funds, not from sponsors selling portfolio companies. Treat it as a firm-wide liquidity claim, not proof that the buyout exit window has reopened.

It's also guidance for a year that hasn't finished. And the release never names the old record in dollars. By simple arithmetic, at forty billion, the fifty-percent claim implies the old record was below about twenty-seven billion dollars. But that's our math, not a disclosed figure. So bank the direction, and hold the precision loosely until the full-year print.

The marquee exit behind the story is USI, the insurance brokerage. Aon agreed in August to buy it from KKR for seventeen billion dollars. On the original 2017 investment, KKR says the sale should deliver about six times its cost. On KKR's balance-sheet capital invested, the reported return is three-point-four times. Which multiple you hear depends on which capital you count, and six times is the flattering cut. Two things remain open. The deal hasn't closed, so none of this is cash yet. And KKR hasn't said whether USI's proceeds sit inside the forty-billion-dollar guide. So USI is real evidence, but it is still a promise on an unclosed deal.

KKR also says embedded gains across its platform stood at eighteen billion dollars at the end of September. Think of that as the reservoir. A sale like USI converts part of it from paper into cash. So a record realization year spends down gains already on the books. That's our inference, not a management claim. But it means next year's carry pipeline depends on fresh marks rising, not on harvesting what's already there.

KKR's guide completes the scoreboard we've tracked since the start of the month. Blackstone's third-quarter net realizations fell twenty percent from the second quarter. But management said fourth-quarter carry realizations already under contract are expected, on their own, to exceed all of the third quarter's net realizations. That's a measurable bar for January. It's also a lower bar than it sounds, because the third-quarter base had just dropped. Carlyle went the other way. It guided that private-equity segment distributable earnings will be lower in the fourth quarter on realization timing. That metric isn't like-for-like with KKR's client distributions. Still, with KKR pointing sharply up, Carlyle's guide-down now looks like one firm's fund timing rather than the sector's direction. Every upturn at the top is a fourth-quarter promise, not banked cash. The cash test lands with the January and February prints.

There's an earnings-mix consequence too. If Blackstone and KKR deliver, their fourth quarters tilt toward carry. Carry is the volatile line that public investors pay the least for. Hold that thought, because the fee side of the story is about to get harder.

Before we get there, scale KKR's record against the industry it sits inside. McKinsey's latest private-markets report measured distributions at roughly six percent of assets under management over the six months to June 2025. McKinsey sets that directly against its ten-year average of about fourteen percent. That's data through mid-2025, so it shows the depth of the hole, not the current level. McKinsey's survey of limited partners adds the other half. Fifty-four percent now rate distributions-to-paid-in as critical or most critical, level with multiple on invested capital. So LPs now grade managers on cash returned as heavily as on paper multiples.

Against that backdrop, look at the other side of KKR's ledger. The firm holds a record one hundred forty-six billion dollars of dry powder. It raised one hundred sixteen billion over the trailing twelve months. Both figures are firm-wide, across strategies, like the forty-billion guide. Cash is flowing back to clients and new commitments are flowing in, at the same firm, at the same time. That reads as vintage rotation at the top of the industry, not a market that's broadly clearing. Our inference is that LPs who get cash back from KKR are most likely to recommit it to KKR. That deepens the fundraising gap between the largest platforms and everyone below them. The thaw is real, but it's concentrated.

Dry powder only counts once it's spent, and the deal side has a live test. More than a week ago, reports attributed to the Financial Times said CD&R and GTCR are in talks to take Global Payments private. The reported enterprise value is about forty billion dollars. GTCR, already the company's largest outside shareholder, would roll its roughly ten-billion-dollar stake into the deal rather than cash out. Nothing is signed, and the talks are described as preliminary. A signed deal would show dry powder converting into deployment at megadeal scale.

Back at the top, the same concentration shows up in how managers engineer liquidity while exits stay selective. KKR says it's building a new GP-led secondaries strategy, which it expects to launch in 2027. Nothing has been raised yet. It would enter a market that just posted its strongest first half on record. Single-asset continuation vehicles made up about fifty-three percent of GP-led volume in that half, according to Evercore. Here's the problem. The largest sponsors already sell assets from their own funds into continuation vehicles. Now they're also raising capital to be the buyers in those deals. That eases liquidity in the near term. But it means fewer arm's-length prices testing what those assets are actually worth. More sponsor-affiliated buyer capital can postpone the real test of today's marks, rather than pass it.

The other liquidity leg is moving the opposite way. Apollo reported a week ago. It said its Global Wealth channel raised four billion dollars, and called that "a strong result against industry-wide softness." That's management's own characterization, not measured flow data. And Apollo's franchise is mostly credit, so read it as a channel-level signal, not evidence of private-equity redemptions. Still, a top-tier manager just described the whole retail channel as soft. Add the gates we covered on Saturday. Partners Group's flagship evergreen private-equity fund hit its redemption cap for a second straight quarter. Its proposed fix splits the fund into one sub-fund built to sell holdings and one built to compound, with temporary fee cuts, and holders vote on it this week. And Blackstone's retail credit vehicle, BCRED, drew redemption requests at double its limit. The picture is two liquidity taps turning in opposite directions. Institutional realizations are opening at the very top. Retail inflows are cooling across the channel.

That's where the earnings-mix point bites. Public investors pay a premium for the managers' fee line, and the fee-growth story leans on that retail channel. If wealth inflows soften while the fourth quarter tilts toward carry, the mix shifts the wrong way. So a record realization quarter could be a weaker valuation story than it looks.

So where does the cycle stand? It's a real but concentrated realization thaw. It's guided rather than banked. It runs mostly through the largest diversified platforms, on a headline number that includes credit and real assets. Meanwhile, the retail engine meant to fund the next leg of growth is showing strain. For everyone below the top tier, the binding risk is still the exit-and-distribution logjam. Where's the market complacent? In reading top-tier guidance as an industry recovery in distributions, and in treating wealth-channel inflows as steady. Where might it be over-discounting? In waving off KKR's number as marketing. It's a specific, falsifiable guide that management will have to own at its next print.

Watch four things. First, whether Blackstone's and KKR's fourth-quarter results, due in January and February, show the cash. Second, whether the USI sale closes, and where KKR counts its proceeds. Third, whether Partners Group's holders approve the split in this week's vote. And fourth, whether the Global Payments talks become a signed deal.

That's the briefing. Talk tomorrow.
