Good morning, it's Thursday, November 5th. Here's the Capital Markets Radar.

Tuesday's midterm vote repriced almost every asset on this board in a single session, but the more useful story is what it didn't reprice. It drained the war premium out of oil, pushed the dollar to a five-week low, and took some yield out of the long bond. It left two premia untouched: the December Fed hike the market still prices, and the discount on AI infrastructure. So this was a repricing of political and geopolitical risk, not of the cost of money.

Start with the scoreboard. Democrats won key races, and the Dow Jones Industrial Average surged more than 3 percent on Wednesday, its best day since April of last year. CNBC's headline credited a "clear-cut Democratic win, falling oil." That framing bundles two catalysts the market can't cleanly separate. One reading is gridlock, lowering the fiscal and term premium. A second is that the vote was read as a check on renewed military action against Iran, which drains oil and is disinflationary. A third is mechanical short-covering, which we'll come back to. None of the three is proven. The Dow is also a narrow, price-weighted index. The broader-index figures circulating appear to describe election day, not Wednesday, so I won't lean on them for breadth. So what: the headline gain is real, but its cause is contested, and the oil leg is the piece we can actually test.

So take the second half of CNBC's headline first. Brent had peaked near $118 a barrel the previous Friday. On Wednesday it broke below $100 for the first time since early October. By Thursday morning in Asia it stood at $93.63, its lowest in about two months, as traders bet on diplomacy over a return to war. That's roughly a fifth of Brent's value gone in under a week. Last week this desk argued that Brent's price was mostly risk premium rather than scarcity. On Tuesday we named the vote as the next scheduled headline. The headline came, and the premium kept draining.

But read that quote carefully. It describes a bet on diplomacy, not a diplomatic outcome. Nothing in the reporting shows the Hormuz risk resolved, and a premium that drains on a headline can refill on the next one. Data vendors also disagree widely on the absolute price, so trust the size of the move more than any single tick. So what: oil is doing a lot of the rally's lifting, and it's the least durable piece of it.

If that were purely a war premium draining, you'd expect gold, the classic conflict hedge, to fall with it. Gold went the other way. It jumped about 3 percent on Wednesday, to $4,040 an ounce, as the dollar index slid to a five-week low. In CNBC's words, a weaker dollar and lower Treasury yields helped gold recover above the key $4,000 level. The ten-year yield kept falling overnight, to about 4.87 percent in early Tokyo trading.

That closes a question we left open on Sunday. Gold had just hit a one-year low, and we asked whether it would keep falling. It has now reclaimed $4,000. It also confirms the read we gave on Tuesday, that gold is trading as an inflation and rates instrument, not a conflict hedge. Rallying while the war premium in oil collapses is about the cleanest evidence that read could get. A weaker dollar also eases dollar-funding conditions and supports dollar-priced assets across this board. There was no funding squeeze through this repricing. So what: gold is trading on the dollar and on yields right now, and the dollar is easing, not tightening.

And yet the price of short money didn't follow. Traders still see an 82 percent chance of a Fed rate hike in December, according to CME's FedWatch tool. That's a hike, not a cut. It's also roughly where futures sat after last week's FOMC, as we reported Sunday. That was a different snapshot, so read it as a level, not a precise change. Since then, the dollar is weaker, Brent is down about a fifth from its peak, and long yields are lower. Every one of those argues against tightening, and the hike pricing is still standing.

Here's my read, and it's an inference, not something in the data. Easier financial conditions may actually reinforce the hawks. A stock rally and a softer dollar don't look like an economy that needs relief. And if hike odds hold with energy this much cheaper, the market is saying the hike is about core inflation and financial conditions, not oil. That makes the core CPI print, not the headline, the decisive number. October payrolls land Friday, and October CPI follows on November 10th. Neither has been released, so treat today's hike pricing as positioning ahead of those prints, not an outcome. Meanwhile, floating-rate borrowers, including the AI issuers this desk has tracked all week, get no relief on the short leg of their funding costs. So what: the relief reached the long end and the currency. It has not reached the policy rate.

That brings us to the other premium the election didn't relieve, and it sits inside the equity market itself. Even as the major indexes surged, AI-infrastructure names fell. Per Investing.com, CoreWeave dropped as much as 3.2 percent in premarket trading, and neocloud names took the sharpest losses. Oracle slipped too, giving back part of Tuesday's gain. That landed on a group already hurting. Goldman Sachs' Broad AI basket fell 15 percent in October, and it was more than 24 percent below its peak heading into Wednesday. Evercore ISI's take was that this "could create incrementally more negative sentiment for AI infrastructure exposed names."

There's a physical layer under that sentiment. Data Center Watch counted at least 45 data-center projects, worth $68 billion, disrupted by local opposition in the second quarter. That's a months-old baseline, not a post-election number, but it's the friction a political shift could intensify. Our Strategic Power desk spent Tuesday on the same fault line, from grid-curtailment deals to local ballot fights over data centers.

Which read dominates? We don't know. The first is genuine political and regulatory risk. The second is that the most crowded, highest-beta names simply funded the rally elsewhere. The third is that the vote was an excuse to extend a derating already well underway. One structural point argues the regulatory read is overdone. Even if Democrats hold both chambers once every race is called, any federal AI bill would still need a Republican president's signature. So the near-term exposure is sentiment and state-level friction, not imminent federal law. So what: a political and permitting premium is now stacking on top of the financing premium this complex already carried.

Last stop is positioning, where the relief showed up most cleanly. Bitcoin jumped back above $80,000 on Wednesday. It moved with gold and the weaker dollar, not with the AI-infrastructure names that fell the same day. That breaks the framing this desk used on Tuesday, when we called bitcoin the high-beta tail of the AI tape. For one session, it traded as a dollar-and-liquidity proxy instead. The break was from the AI-infrastructure subset, not from tech broadly, and one session is not a regime.

The flow data is partial confirmation at best. Spot bitcoin ETF outflows narrowed by roughly 71 percent on Tuesday, to $115.6 million. Small inflows at Bitwise and Fidelity partly offset money leaving BlackRock's fund. That's the session before the rally, and narrowing outflows are not inflows. Inflows would be the real signal.

Underneath sits a stretched positioning backdrop. Citadel Securities estimated systematic, trend-following equity positioning at about negative 3.3 standard deviations, with that cohort net short for 26 straight sessions. That reading likely predates the vote, and its exact as-of date isn't confirmed. On Monday this desk argued that crowded hedges loaded the upside with squeeze risk. We had the November data prints in mind, but a benign election works the same way. Wednesday's surge is consistent with that squeeze, but nothing here shows the trend followers actually bought. So what: mechanical re-leveraging is a live extension path if volatility settles, but it's a path, not a forecast, until the flows turn.

Pulling it together: the vote repriced political and geopolitical risk, not the cost of money. Three things to watch from here. First, Friday's payrolls and the November 10th CPI, especially the core print, against that 82 percent hike pricing. Second, whether Brent holds below $100, or the premium refills on the next headline. Third, whether bitcoin ETF flows flip to actual inflows, and whether AI infrastructure keeps diverging from the broader tape.

That's the Radar for today. Trade carefully out there, and I'll see you next time.
