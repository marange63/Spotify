Enterprise Technology & Software

Good morning. It's Tuesday, September 1st, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

For the past week this beat has been building one argument: enterprise vendors are fleeing the per-seat meter. ServiceNow's chief executive said half its net-new business is already non-seat-based. CrowdStrike sold a consumption envelope called Falcon Flex instead of licenses. Salesforce reported a self-defined agent run-rate instead of user counts. Today GitHub does the opposite with Copilot. It is hardening the seat, not abandoning it.

Here is what actually changes, and when, because GitHub's own changelog is more precise than the initial reporting suggested. Starting today, September 1st, GitHub reinstates credit-card and PayPal sign-ups for new Copilot Business and Enterprise customers. Any newly assigned seat then requires payment before the user gets access. That is the whole of what happens today. The harder move is charging upfront for seats that are already assigned on existing accounts. That does not start today. GitHub's changelog states plainly that the upfront charge on already-assigned seats applies to existing Copilot Business and Enterprise customers paying by credit card or PayPal, starting October 1st, a full month later. So this is a staged rollout, not a single switch flip. Friction on new seats first, then, after a month's notice, a forced charge on the installed base. That staging strengthens the underlying story rather than weakening it. It reads like a vendor managing a shelfware problem carefully, giving IT admins thirty days to audit and de-provision seats nobody is using before the bill arrives. Watch October's numbers, not September's, for any sign of a purge.

Why does forcing prepayment matter more than it sounds? Because requiring payment before access converts a seat that was merely assigned into a seat someone's budget owner actually chose to fund in advance. That is a different kind of evidence than anything self-reported. You will hear claims that Copilot Enterprise usage runs into the tens of thousands of organizations and touches most of the largest companies in America. Treat those figures with real caution. They trace only to secondary aggregator write-ups. A deep dive on this story went looking for a dated statement from GitHub or Microsoft behind either number and came up empty. GitHub's own changelog and its own August enterprise roundup, checked directly, contain neither one. Take them as reported, not verified. And note what else is missing. Neither GitHub nor Microsoft publishes a standalone Copilot revenue line, annual recurring revenue, or paid-seat count anywhere in this material. So any attempt to size what prepayment does to the Copilot profit and loss is inference, including mine.

There is a trap in that inference worth naming. Collecting cash earlier improves working capital. It pulls billings and deferred revenue forward without proving that one additional developer opened the product. So the cash-flow line inside Microsoft's Copilot business can look better in a quarter when actual engagement got worse. Do not read the one as the other. For a chief information officer holding a pile of assigned-but-unused seats, the instruction is narrower and more urgent: the seat audit you have been deferring now has a deadline of September 30th.

There is a second story inside the same announcement, and it is about model lifecycle, not billing. Also on September 1st, GitHub is deprecating six models across Copilot at once, spanning several different vendors' offerings. That single-day churn is itself an argument for one side of a bet this show framed yesterday. Salesforce picked a single default model. GitLab shipped a model-selection menu inside Duo. When a vendor can retire six models in one afternoon, hard-coding your engineering workflow to a specific model version becomes a liability. The rational buyer response is an abstraction layer that treats the model as swappable infrastructure. That is exactly the menu architecture GitLab bet on.

Now turn to the other side of that same developer-platform budget line, because Anthropic just moved from the opposite direction. Anthropic has taken self-hosted environments for Claude Code into public beta for Team and Enterprise customers. That lets engineering teams run agentic coding sessions on their own infrastructure, with internal network access, custom tooling, and compliance controls. Pair it with a browser now built directly into Claude Cowork, on by default for paid plans, and shared memory that carries context across chat and Cowork so users stop re-briefing the same information. Together these read as an attempt to make Cowork a self-contained agent runtime, rather than a tool bolted onto a Chrome extension.

Here is the handoff. GitHub is raising payment friction on Copilot in the same week Anthropic removes the objection that keeps agentic coding tools out of regulated industries. That objection is network isolation and compliance, and it is the first thing finance, healthcare, and government IT teams raise. But be precise about what that is and is not. Removing an objection is not winning a customer. There is no usage figure, no customer count, and no revenue disclosure behind any of this from Anthropic. These are feature announcements, one of them still in public beta, not adoption metrics. And there is a real trade-off buried in the phrase "run it on your own infrastructure." Self-hosting means Anthropic gives up the telemetry from those sessions that would otherwise improve its next model. That is a concession as much as a selling point, and which side of the trade wins is unresolved.

Let us close with the one number from last week's earnings tape that has not aired on this show yet, because it answers a complaint we have made since August 27th. Salesforce's Agentforce run-rate, above one and a half billion dollars, has been reported repeatedly without a denominator. A big growth rate, and no sense of how many actual customers sit behind it. We now have one. According to a secondary report of remarks from Salesforce's president and chief operating officer, the company added two thousand paying Agentforce production customers in the quarter, up seventy percent quarter over quarter, bringing the total past six thousand. Divide the one and a half billion by that customer count and you land near a quarter of a million dollars per paying customer per year. That is our own arithmetic across two separate disclosures, not a Salesforce metric, and it needs two caveats. A third of those customers were added only this quarter, so their contribution is a partial-period ramp. The true steady-state figure for a mature account is lower than the average implies. And "paying customer in production" is a Salesforce-defined phrase with no published threshold. One workflow in one department could count the same as a company-wide rollout, the identical defect this show flagged in Workday's own agent-activation count on Sunday. Still, a quarter-million-dollar order of magnitude describes a platform commitment, not a feature attach. If that holds up, the binding constraint on Agentforce is implementation capacity rather than demand, which makes systems-integrator capacity the leading indicator to watch next quarter.

So step back. This category spent the last week arguing about how to price agents. Consumption credits, contract value, self-defined run-rates. What nobody in that argument has supplied is a reliable count of how many people are using any of it. That is why GitHub is defending its seat count with prepayment at the very moment everyone else is walking away from seats. And it is why the industry's favorite adoption statistics, the tens of thousands of organizations, the percentage of the Fortune 100, keep showing up unverified. Every one of them counts an assignment, not a use. Until someone reports active engagement instead, expect that gap to keep shaping how these stories get told.

That's the briefing for September 1st. Talk to you tomorrow.
