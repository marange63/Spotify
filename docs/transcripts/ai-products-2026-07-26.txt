Good morning. It's Sunday, July 26th, and this is your AI products briefing.

Today the fight isn't about which model scores highest. It's about who gives agents an identity, and where all that agent activity actually runs. Two stories this week make that case, and neither of them is a new flagship model.

Start with identity. Jack Dorsey's Block launched a product called Buzz on July 21, announced by Dorsey himself on X. Buzz is an open-source team workspace, licensed under Apache 2.0, that bundles chat, code hosting, and workflow automation into one tool. Block is positioning it as a direct replacement for both Slack and GitHub inside its own company. The interesting design choice is underneath: Buzz is built on Nostr, the decentralized messaging protocol, and that gives every AI agent in the workspace its own cryptographic keypair, independent of the platform itself. Practically, that means an agent can post messages, review code, run approved automations, and submit patches, and every one of those actions traces back to the human who authorized it.

Here's why that architecture matters more than the feature list. Every other agent platform on the market today is AI bolted onto software that was designed for humans first — Slack with a copilot added, GitHub with Copilot suggestions layered in. Buzz inverts that: agents are first-class participants from the ground up, with portable, auditable identity, not a guest account. That's the same thread running through this week's other agent news — 1Password wiring credential injection into Claude, which we covered on the 21st, and OpenAI's Presence platform bundling guardrails and approval workflows for production agents. Buzz pushes that thread to its logical endpoint: don't just supervise the agent, give it real, verifiable identity.

There's a second-order angle here worth naming even at this early stage. If agent identity ever becomes something enterprise security teams demand — a verifiable record of which agent did what, on whose authority — then the incumbents don't get to ignore it. Slack, Microsoft Teams, and GitHub would face pressure to retrofit cryptographic agent identity and audit trails into tools that never contemplated a non-human participant. And that seeds a standards fight. Buzz's answer is Nostr, an open decentralized protocol. The platform vendors would almost certainly prefer their own proprietary identity scheme. And there's a third camp forming in the model-context-protocol world that's already wrestling with how agents authenticate to the tools they call. Whoever's approach wins becomes plumbing every enterprise inherits. That's the real stake in a release almost nobody is running yet — not the product itself, but the standard it's trying to seed.

Now, the caveats, and they're substantial. This is version 0.4.21 — pre-1.0. Desktop clients exist for Mac, Windows, and Linux; there's no mobile app yet. There are no external enterprise customers confirmed anywhere in the reporting. The only production deployment anyone can point to is Block using it on itself, which is the vendor's own use, not independent adoption. And Nostr-based identity has zero track record at enterprise scale — nobody has shown how it plugs into the single sign-on and identity systems that big companies already run. So treat this as an early, crypto-adjacent strategic bet from Dorsey, not proven workplace software. The idea is sound. Whether it survives contact with a real enterprise IT department is unknown.

That question — who controls the agent — leads naturally into a second question: where does all this agent activity actually run? For an answer, look at Fireworks AI, the inference and model-customization platform whose customers include Uber, Shopify, GitLab, and Cursor. Fireworks announced a $1.505 billion Series D on July 16, led by Atreides Management, Index Ventures, and TCV, at a $17.5 billion valuation. More telling than the raise is the usage data behind it. Fireworks says it has crossed a billion dollars in annualized revenue run rate, up fivefold from a year earlier, and that token volume on its platform has climbed from 15 trillion to more than 40 trillion tokens a day over that same period. The company also says more than 95% of that traffic comes from models customized on the customer's own proprietary data, not off-the-shelf frontier models called through an API.

That 95% figure is the real headline. It says that where enterprise AI spend actually concentrates isn't in raw calls to GPT or Gemini or Claude — it's in fine-tuned, customized models running on specialized inference infrastructure built for cost and latency at scale. The model has become an input; the value is migrating to the layer that customizes and deploys it cheaply. A $17.5 billion valuation on roughly a billion dollars of revenue — about 17 times revenue — tells you investors believe that layer, not the frontier labs alone, is where durable enterprise AI economics live. One caveat worth keeping in view: every one of these numbers, the revenue, the token count, the growth rate, is self-reported by Fireworks in its own funding announcement. There's no independent audit of any of it.

Two more details in the round sharpen the point. Nvidia itself joined the raise, alongside Lightspeed, Bessemer, and Menlo. A chip vendor putting money into a customized-inference platform tells you Nvidia sees this layer as a demand channel for its hardware, not just the frontier training runs everyone fixates on. And that seventeen-times-revenue valuation now becomes the number every rival inference platform gets measured against. When Together AI or Baseten raise their next rounds, this is the multiple in the room. So the capital market is pricing the customization-and-deployment layer as a durable business in its own right, not a thin reselling margin on top of someone else's model.

One more item, smaller but worth a mention for anyone building on Claude. Anthropic's Claude Code has quietly shipped a change with real governance implications: background subagents now run by default, and when one finishes work in its own worktree, it will auto-commit, push, and open a draft pull request without a human in the loop at that step. Combine that with Claude in Chrome, the browser agent, now available on every paid plan rather than just the top tier, and you get a lot more code entering repositories that originated from an autonomous agent rather than a reviewed human pull request. That's not a reason to avoid the tooling — it's a reason engineering teams need to think now about code-review and CI policy for agent-originated commits, before volume makes it someone else's emergency.

So the pattern across all three stories is the same. The model itself is becoming a commodity input. The real contest has moved to who gives an agent identity and accountability, and who owns the infrastructure that runs it cheaply at scale. Worth watching from here: whether Buzz gets any adoption outside Block itself, whether an independent source ever checks Fireworks' numbers, and Moonshot's Kimi K3, an open-weight model release expected July 27th, as the next test of how open-weight models keep pressuring that infrastructure layer.
