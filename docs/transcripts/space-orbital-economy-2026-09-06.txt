Good morning, it's Sunday, September 6th, and this is your Cautious Optimism Briefing on the space and orbital economy.

Start with the constraint, not a company. Money is not scarce in this sector right now, and ambition is not scarce either. Flights are. Every piece of evidence today points the same direction, so let's take it in order.

On Friday, this show said it wouldn't put a Chinese launch-cadence number next to the allied launch gap, because nothing fresh and dateable had surfaced. Here is the number. China's two rival low-Earth-orbit broadband megaconstellations are Guowang and Qianfan, the latter also known as Thousand Sails or SpaceSail. Both have kept launching through the summer on Long March vehicles. As of mid-2026, roughly 190 Guowang satellites are in orbit, against a stated year-end target of 310. Do that arithmetic out loud. Hitting 310 by December means the current run-rate has to more than double inside four months.

Qianfan is at a similar scale, with roughly 218 satellites delivered as of its thirteenth deployment group in July. Together, the two programs are projected to consume 70 or more Chinese launches this year alone. Be honest about what this is: a status roll-up compiled from launch tracking through the summer, not a development that happened in the last few days. What makes it worth leading on is that it is the first time this beat has had arithmetic to put against the adversary side of the launch-capacity question.

Then there is the plan beyond this year: 900 satellites in 2027, and 3,600 a year starting in 2028. Treat those exactly as you would treat a Western company's out-year guidance, because that is what they are. And notice what is missing from all of it. There is no subscriber number, no revenue figure, and no terminal count in any of the reporting available on either constellation. That absence is the most informative fact here. The consensus read is that China is catching up to Starlink. The more defensible read is narrower: China is paying full price, in launches, to reserve orbital shells and spectrum-filing priority, while the commercial case sits deferred indefinitely.

That is a rational thing to buy, which is the part Western operators should sit with. Orbital slots and spectrum priority accrue to whoever files and then actually flies, and they accrue regardless of whether a paying subscriber ever appears. So a constellation that never closes a business case can still be a valuable strategic asset, and a Western operator can be locked out of a frequency-and-orbit combination by satellites that never earn a dollar. So this is a launch-capacity problem wearing a constellation's clothes.

That number only means something set against what a single commercial operator does in the same year. On August 15th, SpaceX launched two Falcon 9 missions 38 minutes apart, from pads on opposite U.S. coasts. That is a record for the shortest gap between orbital missions by one provider, and it is weeks old, not news from this week. It is also not primarily a rocket achievement. It is a ground-infrastructure and range-operations achievement, and that is the part nobody else has matched.

Resist reading that as an unassailable moat, though. Cadence at this level is a function of pad count, booster fleet size, and range availability. Those are capital projects, and a determined state can replicate them, which is arguably what Beijing is doing at a slower pace. The sharper observation runs the other way. Falcon 9 cadence this high is precisely what has let SpaceX defer Starship's economics without any commercial consequence. Here is the structural point worth banking: in both cases, Beijing and SpaceX, the launcher and the constellation owner are the same entity. Cadence at this level is not a service being sold into a market. It is a capability being consumed internally. That leaves every third-party operator exposed to two levers it does not control: the price of a ride, and the order of the queue.

So who opens that queue for everyone else? The Western hope was Rocket Lab's Neutron, a medium-lift reusable vehicle meant to fill the gap below Falcon 9. As of this week's reporting, Neutron has cleared real hardware milestones. Its fairing has completed testing, and its Archimedes engine has logged hundreds of hot-fires. But the company has no firm launch date, and analysts increasingly expect a slip from 2026 into 2027. That 2027 date is press and analyst inference from a narrowing window. Rocket Lab itself has given no new target. What is not inference is the elapsed time: Neutron was announced in 2020, and six years later it still hasn't flown.

Now the number worth doing the arithmetic on. Rocket Lab plans one Neutron launch in its debut year, ramping to three in year two and five in year three. At roughly 50 million dollars a launch, which is the analysis's working price rather than a published Rocket Lab price list, a full year's slip subtracts only about 50 million dollars from forecast revenue. That is close to a rounding error. So the revenue damage is trivial, and that inverts the obvious take. The real cost is time. National-security launch certification requires flight heritage, and a one-flight debut year pushes that heritage years further out. Note also what a one-flight debut year says about the vehicle itself. A reusable medium-lift rocket that flies once a year is not yet a reusable-economics story.

Worth one line on what carries Rocket Lab in the meantime. Its largest recent win was not a launch contract at all. It was an 805 million dollar satellite award under the Space Development Agency's missile-tracking architecture, delivering in fiscal 2029. So the honest way to underwrite this equity is as a defense spacecraft manufacturer with launch optionality, rather than as a launch pure-play.

Now the customer paying for that bottleneck. We said on Friday that the volatility in AST SpaceMobile's stock is exactly the window into which the company tends to raise capital again. Here is the balance sheet behind that call. AST holds 2.7 billion dollars of cash against roughly 3 billion dollars of long-term debt. Last quarter it posted a 230.9 million dollar net loss against 31.5 million dollars of revenue.

Be careful with that loss figure. A net loss is not cash burn. Satellite companies at this stage carry large non-cash charges, including depreciation and stock compensation, and no cash-flow statement is available here. So any runway estimate is an inference, not a fact, and I am not going to make one. The point that needs no estimate is this: cash and long-term debt are now roughly the same size. That means AST's next constellation tranche gets financed by new capital rather than by what is already on the balance sheet. The mechanism to watch is therefore dilution or refinancing, into an equity whose marginal holder is thin and narrative-driven, and the timing of that raise is set by launch availability rather than by AST's own factory.

Last, the one development that could ease this constraint, and a correction. On September 2nd, this show described SpaceX's next Starship test, Flight 14, as the first genuine orbital insertion in Starship's test history, on an enthusiast outlet's reading of an FCC filing. That characterization looks like tracker confusion, and it is inconsistent with SpaceX's own numbering of prior flights. So retract it. What is actually confirmed, as of secondary reporting on September 4th, is narrower. SpaceX has dropped the attempt to catch the ship's upper stage back at the launch site, and the flight has slipped further into September. No date is confirmed, so treat anything more specific as unsettled. This is a scope and schedule change, not a failure.

Pull the thread together. Three consecutive briefings have now produced three different answers to one binary question about a single Starship flight. All three came from the same small pool of trackers reading the same filings. The honest conclusion is that the reporting layer on this beat cannot currently resolve a yes-or-no question, so treat pre-flight detail as provisional until SpaceX itself says otherwise. The bigger picture holds regardless. Every additional month without a demonstrated ship catch is another month the queue behind SpaceX keeps paying today's price for a ride. That is not SpaceX's near-term problem, because Falcon 9 cadence covers it. It is everyone else's.

So watch three things. Whether China's launch cadence can step up toward the pace its 2028 plan would require. Whether Rocket Lab gives Neutron a firm date. And whether Starship actually attempts, and lands, that ship catch.
