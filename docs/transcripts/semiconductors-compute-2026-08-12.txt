Good morning. It's Wednesday, August 12th, and this is your semiconductor and compute briefing.

All week this show has described SK hynix as memory's scarcity king, the company writing full-passthrough pricing into its contracts on the strength of a sold-out HBM roadmap. Today that lead gets its first real challenge, and it comes from two directions at once: a rival's yield curve, and SK hynix's own labor floor.

Start with the yield curve. TrendForce reported this week that Samsung's HBM4 yield has reached 80%, up from below 60% when mass production began in February. That's ahead of Samsung's own year-end target, reached early. Samsung is now guiding to more than triple its HBM4 revenue in the third quarter, expects HBM4 to make up over 60% of its total HBM sales in the second half, and is targeting roughly 38% of the overall HBM market by year-end. Six months ago Samsung was the laggard in this race. It no longer is.

Here's the caveat that matters, and it's more precise than it looked a few days ago. The open question was never whether Samsung is even allowed to sell into Nvidia's next-generation Vera Rubin platform. Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang confirmed back in early June that Samsung, alongside SK hynix and Micron, had already passed certification as an HBM4 supplier for Vera Rubin. So qualification is settled. What isn't settled is volume. Analyst estimates put SK hynix at somewhere between 60 and 70% of Vera Rubin's HBM4 volume today, with Samsung at 25 to 30% and Micron taking the remainder. Nvidia hasn't published those numbers itself, so treat them as directional, not guidance. But the picture is clear enough. Samsung has the yield and the certification, and still doesn't have the share. The real swing factor now is whether Nvidia actually reallocates volume toward Samsung in coming quarters, not whether Samsung can technically supply it.

Now the other half of SK hynix's week. In its own second-quarter earnings call in late July, SK hynix's management described HBM4 yield and quality only in qualitative terms, saying they're "showing levels close to HBM3E, which has already entered a mature stage." That's notably softer than the roughly 80% figure that's been circulating as an industry-insider estimate for SK hynix. The company itself has not put a number on it. When your challenger publishes a hard percentage and you respond with a comparison to your prior product generation, that's worth noting on its own. It could be commercial caution, or it could be a hint the real number is less flattering than the estimate. Either way, the concrete figure on the table right now belongs to Samsung.

Layer onto that a domestic labor dispute. SK hynix's management and its union failed to reach agreement in the fifth round of 2026 wage and profit-sharing talks. The sticking point: management wants to pay more than half of this year's profit share in treasury shares with lock-up periods attached, rather than cash. This is not yet a strike. But it's a supply-continuity risk sitting on top of a company that the entire AI accelerator supply chain is currently depending on for the majority of qualified HBM4 volume. If those talks escalate into any kind of work stoppage, it tightens output at exactly the moment demand is accelerating.

So what does this actually change? Not, yet, who ships the most HBM4 into Vera Rubin. SK hynix still holds that today. What it changes is the one-way-bet framing. This show has spent the week treating SK hynix's scarcity pricing as structural, something buyers just have to accept. A credible second qualified source, with a harder yield number and no labor overhang, is the first real counterweight to that pricing power. Remember why that pricing power mattered in the first place: SK hynix posted a 76% operating margin last quarter on the back of it. Margins that extreme are only defensible while buyers have nowhere else to go. The moment Nvidia has a genuinely interchangeable second source at volume, the premium becomes negotiable rather than dictated. If Samsung's yield keeps compounding while SK hynix is distracted by a domestic dispute, Nvidia has every incentive to shift allocation at the next contract cycle, and SK hynix's premium gets tested for the first time since this cycle began.

There's a second-order effect worth naming too. None of this frees up capacity for ordinary DRAM. Samsung, SK hynix, and Micron between them control the overwhelming majority of global DRAM production, and all three are still pulling wafers toward HBM, not away from it. A more competitive HBM4 market doesn't loosen the memory squeeze that's been pushing up consumer and enterprise DRAM and NAND pricing all year. It just changes who benefits from HBM's premium economics. The tax on PCs, consoles, and non-AI servers keeps running regardless of who wins the HBM4 fight. If anything, a Samsung yield surge that pulls even more wafers onto HBM lines makes that consumer-memory squeeze marginally worse, not better.

Now to the demand side of the same bottleneck. AMD's most recent forward guidance, not a fresh print this week but the operative number the market is working from, calls for second-quarter revenue of $11.2 billion, plus or minus $300 million, about 46% growth year over year, with non-GAAP gross margin near 56%. More telling is what AMD said about 2027. Management expects the data-center segment to more than double year over year, with the data-center AI business specifically growing, in their words, "well over 100%," driven by the ramp of Helios and the MI450 family. Server CPU revenue is guided to grow more than 80% year over year in the second half of this year, and more than 70% for all of 2027.

Why does that belong in the same story as Samsung and SK hynix? Because AMD's flagship MI455X accelerator is specified to carry 432 gigabytes of HBM4 memory and roughly 40 petaflops of FP4 compute. Those are roadmap specifications, not yet independently benchmarked shipped-volume figures, so hold them loosely. But that 432 gigabytes is about 50% more HBM4 per accelerator than Nvidia's comparable Rubin part carries. So on a per-chip basis, AMD's design actually leans harder on the same scarce memory Nvidia is queuing for. Two accelerator platforms, from two competing chipmakers, both drawing on the same handful of qualified memory suppliers, with AMD's part making the heavier claim per socket.

And it isn't only memory they're both bidding for. Every one of these accelerators still has to pass through TSMC's advanced packaging, the CoWoS step that fuses the logic die to its memory stacks, and that line has been running at full capacity all year. So Nvidia and AMD are effectively queuing twice for the same two scarce inputs: the HBM4 stacks, and the packaging that mounts them. That's the mechanism connecting a Korean labor dispute to AMD's 2027 growth guidance. Every additional gigabyte of HBM4 AMD needs for Helios is a gigabyte Nvidia and Samsung are also bidding for, and every accelerator both firms want to ship competes for the same packaging slots.

Put it together. After a week of describing SK hynix's memory lead as close to unassailable, the first credible challenge has arrived. Not from a headline-grabbing new entrant, but from a qualified competitor's yield curve and the incumbent's own labor negotiations. Qualification for Samsung is no longer in question. Allocation share is. That's the number to watch heading into the next contract cycle, not the yield percentage itself.
