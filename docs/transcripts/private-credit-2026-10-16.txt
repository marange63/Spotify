Good morning, it's Friday, October 16th, and this is your private credit briefing.

Today's thesis: private credit now has more money than it has good loans to put it in, and the marginal dollar is flowing toward the risk banks won't hold.

Start with the clearest data point in months. Reuters, citing Preqin, reports that direct-lending funds raised a record $129.15 billion in the first nine months of this year. That's up 35 percent from a year earlier, and the highest nine-month total in Preqin's data going back to 2016.

Now set that against the other side of the ledger. Estimated third-quarter direct-lending volume fell 28 percent year over year, according to PitchBook LCD. So capital is piling up faster than lenders can put it to work on acceptable terms.

And it's not just a deployment lag. The flow of loans is actually reversing. In the third quarter, $16.39 billion of direct-lending loans refinanced out into the broadly syndicated market, against just $2.60 billion moving the other way. Reuters notes this flips the 2022 and 2023 pattern, when more loans migrated from syndicated markets into direct lending than back out. The migration this asset class is built on, lending moving from banks to non-banks, is running backwards at the margin.

What's driving that reversal is price competition. Reuters reports that borrowers able to access both markets naturally gravitate toward whichever is cheaper, once fees and refinancing costs are counted. So the syndicated market is reclaiming the borrowers with optionality. That reads more like a cyclical repricing, with a reopened and cheaper syndicated market, than a structural retreat. But it leaves direct lenders competing hardest over everyone else.

Raymond James's private capital advisory team frames what that does to underwriting in two ways. First, plentiful capital chasing scarce deals could pressure lenders to concede on spreads, leverage, or documentation. Second, the real test of discipline is whether these managers are willing to leave money undeployed. Park the capital, or chase deals you'd otherwise pass on.

Royce Stone Capital's chief investment officer flags the mechanism to watch. Borrowers with fewer alternatives become a bigger share of the loans left in private credit. Hold a caveat on that, though. Reuters itself notes it doesn't necessarily mean the borrowers staying in private credit are weaker credits. Some are simply smaller companies for whom syndicated financing was never a fit. Either way, record fundraising is committed capital, not deployed or performing capital. It says nothing yet about returns, and undeployed money is a drag on limited partners' net returns until it's put to work. So the money either sits, or it goes somewhere riskier.

So where does the money that does get deployed actually go? One deal this week answers that directly. Before I get into it, a disclosure. Anthropic builds the model behind this show, and Anthropic is the compute counterparty in the deal I'm about to describe.

Volta is a startup AI-infrastructure developer building data-center capacity under a compute agreement with Anthropic. It's raising roughly $5 billion in leveraged loans to fund it. An initial pass at SOFR plus 500 failed to clear. The deal was relaunched more than a full point wider, at SOFR plus 625 to 650, with a deeper original issue discount. These are marketed terms, reported with no one on the record, and the deal may still price differently.

The structure matters more than the coupon. JPMorgan and Goldman Sachs are underwriting only a $1.3 billion senior slice. The remaining $3.7 billion, earmarked for about 36,000 Nvidia GPUs, is being marketed on a best-efforts basis. A $1.5 billion order from Apollo is reported to anchor the deal. The loan also includes a rate step-down if Anthropic reaches investment grade. That tells you lenders are pricing the tenant's credit, not just the hardware behind it.

That's a familiar shape. Earlier this week, Oracle's $38 billion construction-debt package ran the same way: banks couldn't distribute the risk themselves and went looking for non-bank buyers. Banks keep the senior paper, and private credit takes the junior piece banks won't hold.

Volta is also a useful check on a view this show leaned toward yesterday: that AI credit risk wasn't being priced, based on a modest, secondhand spread premium on AI-related loans generally. Volta cuts against that, at least at the deal level. A single-tenant, GPU-backed structure failed to clear at the first price, and had to reprice materially wider. The market can still force discipline when the structure is concentrated enough.

Don't over-read one deal, though. A young sponsor, the deal's size, and best-efforts distribution may explain as much of the repricing as AI risk itself. What's unresolved is whether an all-in yield above 11 percent actually compensates for depreciating GPU collateral and single-tenant concentration. The other possibility is that Apollo's anchor order reflects the same deployment pressure described above. That would be a manager with capital to place, reaching for the deal that will take it.

The backdrop is cooling, too. Semafor tallies more than $450 billion of AI-related debt deals so far this year. But Morgan Stanley reports September was the second-slowest month of the year for AI financing, and it expects the fourth quarter to slow further. So there's discipline at the deal level. But the underlying junior risk still ends up marked on private balance sheets, untested by a real downturn.

That's the asset side. Now the funding underneath the lenders themselves. Bloomberg, as relayed by Private Equity Wire, reports that Goldman Sachs and UBS are becoming more selective in financing Australian private credit managers. They're prioritising larger, established players, and pulling back from smaller firms and those with heavy property exposure.

The trigger is a property-linked insolvency. Sydney developer Bathla Group entered insolvency in August owing about 3.4 billion Australian dollars, largely to private debt managers. And around half of Australia's roughly 200 billion Australian dollar private-debt market is reportedly tied to real estate.

The IMF warned this week that links between banks and non-bank lenders are deepening, and that those links are where stress travels. Australia is that channel in practice, arriving through bank risk appetite rather than regulation. Keep the scope tight. The reporting says existing commitments are being maintained, so this is rationing at the margin, not a run. And there's no evidence yet that it's spreading beyond Australia. But banks favoring the biggest managers is the same dynamic as the deal market. Funding scarcity, like deal scarcity, rewards scale.

Last, the liquidity question in retail vehicles, because that's where a mismatch would show first. Max's Digest, a newsletter summarizing other reporting, says redemption requests at HPS Corporate Lending Fund were 11.5 percent of shares in the third quarter. That's down from 13.3 percent the quarter before.

Easing, yes. But it's still more than double the 5 percent quarterly cap that KKR's comparable fund runs under. And the reporting doesn't say what HPS's own cap is, or whether those requests were paid in full or prorated. For contrast, KKR's retail BDC, K-FITS, saw requests just over its 5 percent cap earlier this month, and paid them in full.

So record capital is coming in the front door, while exit demand at one large semi-liquid fund runs far above a 5 percent gate. Whether these vehicles keep honoring requests, rather than prorating them, is the open question. And it gets sharper if the bank funding beneath lenders keeps tightening.

So where does that leave the cycle? Defaults aren't the live story right now. Deployment pressure and a thinning, lower-optionality borrower pool are. Record capital is chasing fewer borrowers with options. That pushes the marginal dollar into concentrated, model-marked AI risk, just as bank funding beneath smaller private lenders starts to tighten. If the better credits keep leaving, BDC portfolio statistics will increasingly reflect the borrowers who had nowhere else to go. That would show up in non-accruals and payment-in-kind with a lag, and that's our inference, not yet data.

Here's what would confirm or refute the read. Watch Volta's final pricing and allocation. Watch the alternative managers' late-October earnings calls for actual deployment pace and dry powder. Watch Apollo's asset-level marks, due October 30th. And watch the first third-quarter non-accrual and payment-in-kind reads from Goldman Sachs BDC and Kayne Anderson BDC in early November.

That's the lending machine for today. I'm your host, and I'll see you next time.
