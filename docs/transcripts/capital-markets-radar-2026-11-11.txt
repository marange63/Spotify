Good morning, it's Wednesday, November 11th, and here's your Capital Markets Radar.

October's CPI moved the December Fed debate from whether the Fed hikes to how much. The front end repriced hard on the news. But the equity index and broad credit barely flinched. So far, the cost of a hawkish Fed is landing narrowly, on a few names and channels, not on the market as a whole. That gap is the thing to price into December.

Start with the print itself. Headline CPI rose 0.4% in October, against a Dow Jones consensus of 0.3%. Core CPI rose 0.3%, against a consensus of 0.2%. Yesterday we said watch core, not headline, because that's where the hike case lives. Core is exactly where the surprise showed up.

There's a competing read, though, and it deserves airtime. Both prints were slower than September's. Headline decelerated from 0.6%, and core only matched September's pace. So this is hot against Wall Street's forecast, not hot against the recent trend. There's also a hole in the data. The Bureau of Labor Statistics couldn't publish a twelve-month rate for October. It didn't collect October 2025 prices during last year's funding lapse. With no year-over-year anchor, the hike case now rests on a short run of monthly prints. So what: the surprise is real, but it's a surprise against consensus, and that's a thinner foundation than a genuine reacceleration would be.

Here's how the rates market read it. Last week this desk watched December hike odds fall after last Thursday's AI-led equity selloff, then rebuild after Friday's payrolls. That round trip is over. CME's FedWatch tool, via CNBC, now fully prices a quarter-point hike in December. The odds of a half-point move went from zero before the data to about 10.5% after it. Fed Governor Christopher Waller put the bigger move on the record. As reported by Kitco, he said a half-point hike "should be considered" in December. He added that "hiking only 25 basis points would put our credibility at risk, a risk I am not eager to take." That's the sharpest policy language of the week. But it's one governor's view, and the December meeting hasn't happened. What's priced is a near-certain quarter point with a live tail at a half point, not a decision.

The front end did the repricing, the way this desk said it would. The 2-year Treasury yield surged nearly 19 basis points to 4.736%, per GV Wire. That's up from the 4.51% we noted after payrolls. The 10-year moved too, up 13 basis points on the day per CNBC, after briefly trading above 5% intraday.

Set those two moves side by side. The 2-year rose about half again as much as the 10-year. That's a bear flattener. On our read, it says the market is pricing front-loaded tightening, not a jump in long-run inflation expectations. That matters for duration. A flattener driven by policy expectations is a bet that the Fed gets ahead of inflation. If Waller's camp wins the argument, the long end may hold up better than the front. So what: the hike lives in the 2-year, and the curve is reading this as a policy story, not an inflation-regime story.

So who actually paid for that move? Not the index. The S&P 500 fell just 0.38%, and the Nasdaq fell 0.74%. Against a near-19 basis-point jump in the 2-year, that's a muted equity reaction, not a duration rout. There are two ways to read it. Either the market doubts the Fed follows through at size. Or the index simply hasn't caught up to what the front end now prices. Both are live, and the next Fed voice or the next print should separate them.

Where the damage did land was narrow. Nvidia fell about 5.4% on the day, roughly seven times the Nasdaq's decline. It's now down 9.6% over two sessions. But don't pin that on CPI. Some context first. As we covered yesterday, Nvidia's board has opened an independent investigation into CEO Jensen Huang's personal relationship with a woman reported to be an alleged Chinese intelligence asset. No findings have been published, and that intelligence link is reported, not confirmed. The Motley Fool ties Tuesday's drop to a fresh press report that deepened that scandal. Another account frames the same session around Waller's comments instead. No source weighs the two causes against each other. So Nvidia is carrying a market-wide rates story and its own governance story at the same time. Blaming the Fed for all of it overstates how much of this is about rates.

That points to a second-order risk, though Nvidia isn't clean proof of it, because its drop has its own cause. The logic runs like this. If the index keeps under-reacting while the front end reprices, the adjustment shows up first in the most crowded and most rate-sensitive corners. It also shows up in crypto's institutional channel, which we'll get to. That raises single-name and dispersion risk more than index risk. So what: a hedge on the index may miss where the next leg of this repricing actually hits.

Gold is the cleaner rates-driven case. Spot gold fell 2.8% to about $4,021 an ounce, per CNBC. Last Thursday it held up as yields fell. On Tuesday it dropped as the front end jumped. That confirms a pattern we flagged last week. Gold is trading as a rate-sensitive asset, not as a debasement or haven hedge.

The same rates logic runs through crypto's dollar plumbing, with a structural twist. On Sunday we described falling stablecoin supply as a leak. New data reported by CoinDesk reframes that. Over the first nine months of the year, so through September, total stablecoin supply rose just 0.4%, to $305 billion. Over the same stretch, payments using stablecoins climbed 45%. So the float is flat, but the money inside it is moving much faster. CoinDesk ties that to the GENIUS Act's ban on stablecoin issuers paying interest to holders. In CoinDesk's words, "GENIUS did not kill yield. It moved it one layer up," into yield-bearing tokenized Treasury products.

Here's our inference on top of that. With the 2-year near 4.7%, parking idle dollars in a zero-yield stablecoin just got more expensive. That should sharpen the migration into tokenized T-bill wrappers. It also implies a crypto-native bid for short-dated Treasuries. One caution: the CoinDesk data predate this week's repricing, so this is a structural read, not a same-week signal.

Crypto's institutional channel, meanwhile, keeps exiting. BlackRock's IBIT has now logged seven straight sessions of net outflows, totaling $1.66 billion. That extends the outflow run we've tracked since last week, which had already pushed spot bitcoin ETF flows negative for the year. IBIT leads the outflows partly because it's the biggest fund. And our only bitcoin price predates Tuesday's CPI release, so we can't yet say how bitcoin itself took the hike repricing. Still, the flow data points to institutional de-risking. So what: on-chain dollar usage is accelerating while the ETF wrapper keeps bleeding, and those are two different investor bases.

Credit hasn't caught up yet. On Sunday we noted that the St. Louis Fed's credit series stopped at Thursday. Friday's print is now in. The ICE BofA high-yield option-adjusted spread tightened to 3.42% on November 6, from 3.48% the day before. That closes our open question. Pricier credit protection on CoreWeave and Nvidia hadn't spread into the broad high-yield market, at least through Friday. But it's a pre-CPI number, so it says nothing about credit's reaction to the hike repricing. On our read, if this repricing holds, the borrowers who feel it first are the floating-rate, lowest-rated, sponsor-backed names, not the index. So what: liquid credit is lagging, not confirming, and lag isn't the same thing as resilience.

Three things to watch. First, October producer prices land Friday, and they haven't printed yet, so don't anticipate the number. Second, whether broad high-yield and investment-grade spreads finally move once they digest this week's repricing. Third, more Fed voices either backing or fading Waller's call for a half point. Right now it's a single governor's view against a market pricing it only as a tail. And keep Nvidia's November 17th earnings in view, with the board investigation still open.

That's the radar for November 11th. Stay cautious, stay optimistic, and I'll see you back here tomorrow.
