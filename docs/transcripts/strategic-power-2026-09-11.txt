Good morning. It's Friday, September 11th, and this is your strategic power briefing.

This beat spent the past week arguing over how many barrels OPEC-plus was holding back. It never asked the more basic question: could those barrels actually leave the Gulf. This week answered it, and the answer changes the frame.

On September 8th, Houthi forces fired dozens of ballistic missiles and drones into southern Saudi Arabia. Saudi authorities counted seventy-three people wounded. The strikes hit energy facilities in the Jazan region, along with sites near Abha and Najran. Saudi officials confirmed fires at several locations and what they called a temporary halt in some operations.

Here is the correction this beat owes you. Jazan's four-hundred-thousand-barrel-a-day refinery was not a healthy plant hit fresh on September 8th. Saudi Aramco shut that refinery on July 27th, after an earlier Houthi strike damaged its gasification complex and its tank farm. Those are structural process and storage assets, not surface damage. Repairs were only tentatively projected to finish by August 15th. So the September strikes landed on a plant that had already been down, intermittently, for six weeks. Treat this as a compounding outage, not a new one.

Now the honest limits. No source separates how much of today's disruption is July's unresolved damage from September's fresh hit, and no verified barrels-per-day figure exists for what is currently offline. Say that plainly rather than implying a number. And resist the blockade narrative. Analysts covering this describe the Houthis as testing the limits of Saudi restraint, not as having established an interdiction of Saudi exports. So a hundred-dollar Brent print may be pricing the risk of interdiction rather than barrels actually lost. Risk premia are far more reversible than physical shortages.

The reason this matters beyond one refinery is the chokepoint behind it. The Strait of Hormuz has been effectively closed since the Iran war began in late February, and six months on it remains at three percent of pre-war traffic. Riyadh has rerouted most of its crude through the Red Sea port of Yanbu instead. That means the kingdom no longer has two export routes. It has one, and the Houthis just demonstrated they can reach it. The Red Sea was never redundancy. It is now a single remaining point of failure.

The response so far punishes, it does not restore. The Saudi-led coalition struck Houthi positions roughly forty times by September 10th, backed by about a hundred American military personnel sharing intelligence and an AI targeting program, explicitly not participating in the strikes themselves. Separately, US forces destroyed five Iranian crude tankers this week after an attempted attack on an American warship. None of that reopens a strait.

The market read it the same way. Brent crossed a hundred dollars a barrel, roughly ten dollars above where it sat on August 26th, with WTI near ninety-six. US equities sold off on the inflation implications of the oil move, the S&P finishing down about six-tenths of a percent. So the real story isn't one fire. It is that the fallback route for the world's most important oil corridor is now itself the target, which makes OPEC-plus spare capacity far thinner in deliverable terms than the quota arithmetic implies. This beat's oil analysis earlier this week priced the quota and ignored the corridor. And to be clear on what has not happened yet: August CPI lands at eight-thirty this morning, after this briefing goes out, and the Fed does not decide until September 16th. Treat neither as settled.

Now, what does a state do when its energy lifeline is under direct fire? It asks for its own fuel cycle. The Trump administration submitted a civil nuclear cooperation agreement with Saudi Arabia to Congress on August 25th, starting a ninety-day continuous-session clock. Unless Congress passes a joint resolution to block it, the deal takes effect automatically. Inaction ratifies it, which inverts the usual burden on a proliferation decision.

The details here come from nuclear trade press reading the transmitted text, not from a State Department or Energy Department release, so hold them accordingly. That text caps any US-origin enrichment equipment below five percent U-235. But it explicitly leaves the door open to a follow-on study that could push that ceiling toward just under twenty percent. That is the threshold widely treated as the last meaningful step before weapons-grade material. Contrast the UAE's 2009 agreement, the so-called gold standard, under which Abu Dhabi renounced domestic enrichment entirely. Saudi Arabia makes no such renunciation and is not required to adopt the IAEA's Additional Protocol.

To be fair, a study path is not a granted permission, and the proliferation alarm may be running ahead of the text. But two side letters remain classified, and that cuts both ways. Nobody outside government can confidently read this agreement in either direction. The takeaway is the mechanism rather than the ceiling. Congress has to act to stop this, and silence is approval.

Shift now from the Gulf's constraint, which is a shipping lane, to America's, which is a factory. This answers a question this beat asked on Tuesday: why grid capacity wasn't clearing even at record prices. The mechanism is gas turbines. GE Vernova's turbine order backlog hit a hundred and sixteen gigawatts at the end of the second quarter, up from a hundred gigawatts in the first. The company builds roughly twenty gigawatts a year, worldwide, for every customer on the planet. On its July 22nd earnings call, GE Vernova confirmed that a heavy-duty turbine ordered today won't arrive until 2031. Meanwhile US data-center power demand is projected to climb from forty-one gigawatts this year to sixty-six gigawatts in 2027. The constraint was never capital or incentive. It is machinery, and machinery does not scale on a hyperscaler's timeline.

One caution on that backlog. It is a second-quarter figure, roughly seven weeks old, and a backlog is also a sales record. The seller has every incentive to present scarcity as durable. The sharper question is whether some of those hundred and sixteen gigawatts are slot-holding orders rather than plants anyone intends to build, which is the same speculative-queue behaviour this beat found in ERCOT's interconnection numbers. Nothing available lets us test that, so hold the number with the caveat attached.

That is the backdrop for PJM, the mid-Atlantic grid operator. Its board has set a one-time backstop capacity auction, running September 30th through October 21st, to cover a six-point-eight gigawatt shortfall in its most recent auction for the 2028 delivery year. The price cap for that backstop was raised seventy-one percent, to five hundred fifty-five dollars per megawatt-day. Alongside it, large new loads without dedicated generation, meaning most data centers, would have to curtail or switch to backup power when the system nears emergency conditions, starting June 1st. That last piece is a proposal still facing FERC review and hard opposition from data-center operators, not a settled rule.

This is the auction this beat asked for on Tuesday and came back to on Wednesday, the one where the reliability target finally clears. But given that turbines ordered now deliver in 2031, nothing bid into an October auction for 2028 delivery can be a new heavy-duty gas plant. So the honest read is that this raises the price paid to existing generators without adding a single new megawatt. It is rationing dressed as a market mechanism.

Put the two chokepoints side by side. Oil that cannot ship and power that cannot be built are the same problem in different clothing: scarcity being repriced, not relieved. And the two feed each other. A hundred-dollar Brent print raises the marginal cost of the gas and diesel generation actually serving data-center load through 2028, so an oil shock now lands directly on AI infrastructure economics, not just on consumer prices. Follow the beneficiaries: incumbent generators inside PJM's footprint, the turbine makers holding the order books, and non-Gulf crude and LNG suppliers whose barrels never transit a contested strait. The loser is the working assumption that Gulf spare capacity backstops any supply shock.

Three things worth watching. Whether anyone produces a verified figure for Saudi export volume actually lost. Whether Congress moves on the Saudi nuclear agreement before its window closes. And what clears in PJM's backstop auction on October 21st.

That's it for today. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
