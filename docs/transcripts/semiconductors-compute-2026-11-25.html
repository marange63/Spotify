Good morning. It's Wednesday, November 25th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

On Sunday, this desk said Nvidia's reported revenue now carried a credibility discount. Monday tested that read. Nvidia's board brought Jensen Huang back, and the whole chip complex rallied with him. Today's question is what the reinstatement settled, and what it left open. Then we'll look at a reported move that could turn Nvidia's grip on TSMC from a market fact into an industrial one.

Start with the release itself. On Monday, November 23rd, Nvidia's board voted unanimously to reinstate Huang as president and chief executive, effective immediately. Colette Kress, the chief financial officer who had served as interim CEO, steps back from the top job.

The board framed its decision in national-security terms. It said it acted "in light of recent reporting that Mr. Huang has been the target of a foreign intelligence effort." Note the wording. The board is citing press reporting, not a finding of its own. It also said it "received input from senior levels of the United States government." And it said it is confident the reinstatement "poses no national security risk." Alongside that, the board disclosed a recently formed National Security and Export Control Committee, made up solely of independent directors. Hold onto that committee. It's the most durable part of this story.

First, though, the market. CNBC's own headline said Nvidia stock "skyrockets 31%" on Monday. That move didn't hold. By Tuesday, a second CNBC headline had Nvidia shares falling more than 5 percent as the comeback rally cooled. On our read, a pop that partly reverses the very next session looks more like relief and positioning than new fundamental information.

The context points the same way. Nvidia fell over 4 percent on November 12th, when Huang's resignation was announced. Then last Friday, it closed down 14 percent at a one-year low, after Reuters reported SEC scrutiny of its accounting. Much of Monday's bounce is plausibly a rebound from that rout. So the size of the move overstates how much it tells you.

Now the supply side, where the move was even more revealing. TSMC's New York-listed ADR rose nearly 14 percent on Monday, per reporting that relays TradingView data. Taipei trades a session later. There, on Tuesday, TSMC's local shares surged 9.9 percent, the exchange's daily limit. So if you've seen two different TSMC numbers, that isn't a dispute about the tape. It's two listings, in two time zones, reacting to one event.

The dollar figure is the one to hold onto. Per Fortune, Tuesday's limit-up move added about 158 billion US dollars to TSMC's market value in a single day.

Here's why it matters. Nothing about CoWoS allocation, wafer starts, or leading-edge utilization changed over those two sessions. Yet a personnel decision at one customer moved the world's leading foundry by 158 billion dollars. On our inference, the chip-equity complex now trades as a leveraged Nvidia factor. And the coupling runs both ways. Last Friday, the day of the Reuters report, the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell 10.73 percent. Monday was the same move in reverse. The so-what: TSMC's valuation is currently being set by Nvidia headlines, not by its own fab loading.

That coupling has a cost for the suppliers. On Sunday, this desk noted that TSMC's packaging plans and the memory makers' HBM commitments are sized partly off Nvidia's reported demand. That's the channel. If investors doubt Nvidia's reported numbers, they also doubt the demand signal those supplier builds were sized against. So Nvidia's accounting headlines now land on TSMC's and the memory makers' valuations, even if end demand doesn't move at all.

So what did Monday's rally not price? The accounting question. The reinstatement release doesn't address it. CNBC's November 24th report, headlined that the investigations aren't over, still carries Reuters' account that the SEC is looking into Nvidia's accounting practices. The SEC declined to comment. That's a non-answer. It tells you the inquiry is unresolved, not that it's advancing or stalling.

The same CNBC report adds a second line of scrutiny. California's Department of Justice is probing whether Nvidia's advanced chips or confidential information may have gone to China. It is also probing whether Nvidia misled investors and the public. And Huang is expected to appear before Congress twice in December. So the credibility discount this desk named on Sunday still stands. If anything, it now has a state-level probe attached. Leadership continuity is restored. The integrity of the reported numbers is still open. Those are separate dimensions, and the rally invites you to conflate them.

The more durable change is in the boardroom. As this desk has been tracking, export controls keep moving closer to the product, from where chips are installed, to how TSMC vets customer designs, and now to Nvidia's own board. A committee of independent directors makes export compliance a governance function, not just a legal one.

The regime that committee governs is itself contested. Axios reported earlier this month that Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick resisted tighter China chip controls. Per that reporting, which rests on unnamed sources, Lutnick warned that tighter controls "will start a trade war with China." So Nvidia's new committee is policing a moving target. Treat the Lutnick account as evidence of disagreement inside the administration, not as a signal of where policy lands.

Here's the second-order effect, and it's our inference. A board-level committee implies tighter internal compliance on China-linked sales and remote-access cloud arrangements. That plausibly shrinks Nvidia's gray-market-adjacent demand, even with no new rule written. The so-what: part of export enforcement is moving out of government rulebooks and into corporate governance.

That brings us back to TSMC, whose coupling to Nvidia may be about to turn industrial, not just financial. Multiple reports say Nvidia has asked Samsung, SK hynix and Micron to make a key HBM layer at TSMC. The most prominent comes from the Korean outlet JoongAng. That layer is the base die for Nvidia's next-generation custom HBM. Treat this entirely as reported. Nothing is signed, and no company has confirmed it. TSMC has said publicly that it remains focused on contract chipmaking, its core business. This is also a separate event from Monday's rally, so don't let the two TSMC stories blur.

Here's why the base die is worth fighting over. It's the logic layer of the stack. For custom memory like this, it's the part Nvidia most wants to shape. If that logic sits on TSMC's leading-edge process, value capture in the stack shifts toward TSMC. JoongAng frames this as a risk that Samsung and SK hynix become subcontractors on the logic layer, even as they keep supplying the memory itself. Be careful with that framing. The reporting doesn't establish where each memory maker builds its base die today. So it can't tell you how much would actually change for each of them.

On our read, the clearest exposure is Samsung, because the shift would hit Samsung Foundry as well as Samsung's memory business. A base die built at TSMC is leading-edge logic work that Samsung Foundry doesn't get. That pressures a foundry still trying to win advanced-logic customers. If the plan firms up, expect the memory makers to build more logic capability in-house, or to align with alternative foundries. So score this on the right dimensions. It's about customer relationships and value capture for the memory makers, and about foundry scope for TSMC. It is not a verdict on memory technology leadership.

Now the competing read. A request that leaks to the press is also a negotiating lever. Nvidia may be leaning on the memory makers over HBM pricing, just as the price curve this desk covered last week points higher into 2027. On that view, this is about margin, not process. The so-what: whether it's a manufacturing plan or a bargaining chip, Nvidia is reaching for control of the memory next to its GPU. Last week, this desk called that memory the fastest-rising cost line in AI infrastructure.

Here's the scorecard, by dimension. Leadership continuity at Nvidia is restored. The integrity of Nvidia's reported numbers is still open, with a state-level probe now sitting beside the SEC inquiry. Export-compliance governance is institutionalized at board level, policing a regime that is still contested. Value capture in the HBM stack may be shifting toward TSMC, with Samsung Foundry the clearest loser on our read, but the report is unconfirmed. And on equity coupling, TSMC and the memory makers are trading on Nvidia's headlines as much as on their own fundamentals.

What to watch from here. First, any on-record statement from Nvidia or the SEC on the accounting inquiry. Second, the new committee's charter or membership showing up in a filing. Third, Huang's two December appearances before Congress. And fourth, any confirmation or denial from TSMC, Nvidia, or the memory makers on base-die production.

That's the briefing for November 25th. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
