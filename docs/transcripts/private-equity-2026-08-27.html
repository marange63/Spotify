Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Equity & the Deal Economy

Transcript · August 27, 2026

Good morning. It's Thursday, August 27th, and this is your private equity briefing.

We've spent this week tracking one story from different angles. Liquidity in this industry has stopped being something a general partner delivers to its limited partners. It has become something the general partner manufactures. Wednesday it was continuation vehicles inside the fund. Monday it was allocators like CalSTRS declining to roll into them. Today the same behavior takes one more step outward. Bloomberg reported on August 24th that sponsors are increasingly buying bespoke exit financing from other large managers. It names Apollo and Bain Capital specifically. The purpose is to generate cash for limited partners when a real sale isn't available. Bloomberg's explanation for why is the vintage overhang. Global buyout firms amassed close to four trillion dollars in assets bought when rates were low and valuations were high. Now, with rates higher, many are struggling to exit those investments and send money back to investors. So the mechanism has migrated again. All week this show has located manufactured liquidity inside the fund structure the general partner itself controls. This is the version where a different manager underwrites it, off its own balance sheet.

Now, what this reporting actually gives us matters as much as what it claims. It's aggregate and it's unquantified. There's no disclosed deal size, no named counterparty transaction, no portfolio company, and no pricing on any individual piece of this. Treat the behavior as reported and the scale as unknown. And don't let that four trillion dollar figure do double duty. It's the stock of low-rate-vintage assets sitting unsold. It is not a measure of how big this exit-financing channel actually is. Those are two different numbers answering two different questions.

Here's the harder open question, and I want to be precise about it rather than guess. Does a financing like this show up to limited partners as a real distribution, counted toward DPI, or does it sit on the fund's books as leverage? That distinction is the entire ballgame. It determines whether reported cash-back-to-investors numbers this cycle are measuring genuine realizations or borrowed liquidity that has to be serviced or unwound later. I went looking for a clean answer and didn't find one. The closest material is ILPA's guidance on net-asset-value facilities, which flags concern about facilities used to fund early distributions — what practitioners call DPI management. That guidance is about NAV lending generally. It does not resolve the treatment of this specific bespoke product, and no SEC rule or named general-partner or limited-partner statement does either. So I'm naming that as the finding itself. The accounting treatment is genuinely unresolved on the public record, and anyone telling you confidently which bucket it falls into is guessing ahead of the evidence.

That ambiguity is exactly why the standing supervisory context matters here. The Financial Stability Board flagged this category back in May — dated background, not news. It identified four vulnerability clusters tied to the growth of NAV lending and fund-level financing: bank interconnections, borrower credit quality and valuation opacity, concentration and liquidity mismatches, and data gaps. The FSB didn't size any of this or find a failure. It named where supervisors are watching. But it's the reason the leverage-versus-realization distinction isn't academic. A NAV-style facility can force the recall of distributions already paid out if valuations fall and covenants trip. So what looks like liquidity today can become a liability tomorrow if the marks don't hold. And there's a second-order trap in the supervisory response itself. If regulatory attention tightens the terms on fund-level facilities and preferred-equity structures, it removes the very tool sponsors are currently leaning on. In that case the logjam doesn't ease gradually. It reappears all at once. That's the risk sitting underneath a financing structure that, by design, is harder for an outside allocator to see than a straightforward sale.

Now turn to who's actually making money off all of this, because it clarifies what's happening. The large multi-strategy managers posted their second-quarter numbers a few weeks ago, and the pattern is stark. Blackstone's fee-related earnings rose 22 percent year over year to 1.8 billion dollars. Apollo posted record fee-related earnings of 785 million dollars. And KKR delivered the largest monetization quarter in its history, 848 million dollars of realized performance income. Set those against the industry benchmark. McKinsey puts distributions-to-paid-in at 6 percent of assets under management for the twelve months ended June 2025, against a 2015-to-2019 average of 16 percent. That's the most recent available read, and it's more than a year stale, so it is not a same-period comparison to these second-quarter prints. The gap is still the story. A record monetization quarter at KKR and a structurally depressed industry-wide DPI number are not a contradiction. Read them together and you get K-shaped concentration. Realizations are happening at the handful of franchises with the scale and the structuring capacity to make them happen. The industry benchmark stays weak because everyone else is stuck. Worth flagging one wrinkle on KKR specifically. It shifted how it classifies certain realized performance fees into fee-related revenue, at a much lower compensation ratio. So part of its reported fee-related-earnings growth this year is a reclassification, not pure organic growth. The headline number is real. The year-over-year comparison isn't entirely clean.

So the honest read is this. Fee-related earnings are an annuity on gathered assets. Carry is a claim on a realized sale. Right now only one of those two is reliably working. Arranging liquidity for other people's stuck portfolios — the exact behavior in our lead story — is a new fee line for the largest managers at the precise moment their own funds face the same exit drought. That's good for the equity holders of Apollo, Blackstone and KKR. It is neutral to bad for the limited partners sitting inside the underlying buyout funds, who are the ones actually waiting on cash.

If fee income is the annuity everyone wants a piece of, GP stakes is the market that prices it directly. And that market is telling you something too. Blue Owl's sixth GP-stakes flagship has raised 10.6 billion dollars against a 13 billion dollar target, after multiple timeline extensions. Management described it on the second-quarter call as being in the final stretch. That raise is still an enormous fund by any standard, and fundraising elongation right now is close to universal across private markets. So don't overread this as a GP-stakes-specific verdict, and note it's an interim number rather than a final close. But at the margin, institutions are pricing the fee-related-earnings annuity a little less enthusiastically than the manager earnings prints alone would suggest. There's a knock-on effect if that persists. A slower GP-stakes bid narrows the exit route for mid-sized managers trying to fund succession or growth capital by selling a minority stake. Their alternative is an outright sale, which concentrates the industry further into the same handful of platforms already winning the fee race.

So where does the cycle actually stand? The exit-and-distribution logjam is still the binding constraint, and today's development shows it migrating rather than easing. It has moved from inside the fund, to allocator pushback, to third-party balance-sheet financing. The scale of that financing channel and how it's reported to limited partners are both open questions, not settled ones, and I'd resist anyone who tells you otherwise this week. Watch three things to confirm or refute this read. First, whether any individual exit-financing deal gets disclosed with actual terms. Second, whether September-quarter marks hold up when the next round of earnings lands in late October. And third, whether Blue Owl's final close lands at or below its thirteen billion dollar target.
