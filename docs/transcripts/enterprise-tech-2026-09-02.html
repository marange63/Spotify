Good morning. It's September 2nd, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

Dell reported fiscal second-quarter results after the close last night. One number in that report is the most useful AI figure this beat has seen in a week. Revenue came in at 47 billion dollars, up 58 percent year over year. But the number that matters is the backlog. Dell exited the quarter with a record 95 billion dollars of AI-server orders booked but not yet shipped. That figure is different in kind from most of the AI numbers enterprise vendors have been putting on the table lately. It isn't a run-rate a company invented for itself. It isn't an internally defined contract-value metric either. It's signed customer orders under contract, sitting in Dell's backlog because Dell has not yet built and delivered the hardware.

Look at the mechanics and the picture sharpens. Dell booked 60.9 billion dollars of AI-server orders in the quarter alone. It recognized only 16.4 billion dollars of AI-server revenue in that same quarter. Divide the 95-billion backlog by that quarterly recognition rate and you get roughly six quarters of already-sold work sitting unshipped. That division is our arithmetic, not a Dell disclosure, so treat it as an estimate. Dell gave no timeline of its own for converting the backlog. But the conclusion it points to is plain. Dell's constraint right now is conversion, not demand. Customers are committing money faster than Dell can turn that money into delivered servers.

That is the same story Nvidia told the market on August 29th. Nvidia called itself supply-constrained rather than demand-constrained, and disclosed supply commitments that had more than doubled to 279 billion dollars. It's also the same story behind the memory pricing our semiconductor beat has tracked all week, where high-bandwidth memory has been the one input cost that would not come down. Dell's backlog is that identical chokepoint, just measured lower down the stack. In boxes rather than in wafers.

Now to what this quarter says about what kind of company Dell has become. Its infrastructure solutions group, the servers-and-storage business that houses the AI hardware, grew revenue 89 percent year over year. Its client solutions group, the PC business, grew 20 percent. That gap is not a good quarter. It's a change in identity. Dell's growth is now overwhelmingly an AI-infrastructure story, and management's full-year guide of roughly 74 billion dollars in AI-server revenue, about triple the prior year, says it expects the gap to keep widening. That guide is a forecast, not a result.

The natural worry with a mix shift this aggressive toward server hardware is margin dilution. Reselling GPU-heavy boxes has traditionally been a thinner-margin business than storage or software. That worry does not hold up against the numbers Dell actually reported. Company-level non-GAAP operating margin expanded from 7.7 percent a year ago to 12.6 percent this quarter. The infrastructure segment expanded even more, from 8.8 percent to 15 percent. So the AI-server ramp is not diluting Dell's profitability, at least not on this print. Margin moved up, not down, at both the company level and inside the segment carrying the AI hardware. Be careful about why. Dell did not explain the expansion, and no management commentary we could find quantifies whether AI servers specifically are accretive or dilutive to that blended number. So don't over-attribute it to any single driver. But the plain reading is that whatever bear case assumed GPU-server reselling was a low-margin, working-capital drag for hardware makers, this quarter argues against it. So what: Dell now trades less like a legacy hardware cyclical and more like a capex-cycle asset, tied to the same buildout as the chipmakers, backlog swings and all. And the margin data says that repricing is not coming at the expense of profitability.

Where does all that hardware actually go once it ships? Broadcom answered part of that question at VMware Explore this week. It announced that VMware Cloud Foundation now supports validated on-premises deployment of external AI models. The list includes models from Google, Nvidia, NEC, Alibaba Cloud, and the Chinese lab Z.ai, branded VMware Private AI Cloud. Alongside it Broadcom shipped a software layer called VMware AI Factory for deploying and governing those workloads. Note the ambiguity in that word "validated." The sources do not establish whether it means a commercial licensing relationship with each model provider or simply compatibility testing, and those are very different facts.

Broadcom cited its own commissioned survey claiming 56 percent of enterprises are already running or planning to run production AI inference on private infrastructure. Treat that as a vendor measuring its own addressable market rather than as independent data. No customer names, deployment counts, or revenue were disclosed alongside the launch. So this is a product announcement, not adoption evidence. Still, it's the connective tissue between the hardware backlog and where inference actually runs. If a meaningful share of enterprise AI workloads stays on infrastructure the enterprise owns, that's demand for the exact servers piling up in Dell's backlog. It's also a leak in the assumption that AI compute spending flows only to the hyperscalers.

Now up to the software layer, where the disclosure gets vaguer. This show has spent the last week ranking enterprise vendors by how honestly they measure AI. On August 30th it named MongoDB's September 1st print as the next test of whether anyone would put a real number on the table. MongoDB reported after the close last night. Total revenue was 771.8 million dollars, up 30 percent year over year, its fastest growth in years. Its Atlas cloud database grew about 29 percent, and the company raised full-year guidance, attributing the raise to Atlas. And MongoDB gave no AI figure at all, only the qualitative phrase "early momentum with AI use cases." The flagged test resolves in the negative, and that's the more interesting outcome.

Resist the temptation to credit MongoDB with honesty, though. The absence of an AI number is equally consistent with there being no material AI contribution worth isolating. A reacceleration after several quarters of cloud-spend rationalization may simply be core enterprise workload growth. The company attributes the raise to Atlas consumption, not to AI. The structural point stands anyway. MongoDB doesn't need an AI metric, because Atlas is priced on consumption. If AI agents are reading and writing more operational data, that shows up automatically as usage growth inside an existing product, not as a new line item a chief financial officer has to define. So what: the vendors making the loudest AI disclosures may simply be the ones whose pricing model requires inventing a metric to show what consumption pricing captures for free.

Zoom out, and McKinsey's State of AI 2026 survey supplies the counterweight this all needs. Forty percent of large organizations now report scaling AI agents, up from 27 percent a year ago. Nearly a third of respondents say they've decided against buying a software product or feature because it could be built in-house with agentic coding tools. Set against that acceleration, only 37 percent of respondents attribute any profit impact at all to AI use, and that figure hasn't moved in a year. This is self-reported perception data, not audited financials. So read the profit number as a measure of attribution difficulty as much as a verdict on returns. But the pairing lands the point of this whole briefing. Dell is shipping 16.4 billion dollars a quarter of AI hardware into an economy where the share of firms able to point to any bottom-line impact hasn't budged. And where a growing share of buyers are choosing to build rather than purchase. The best-disclosed AI number in enterprise technology this week came from a server maker's order book. The disclosure only gets vaguer the further up the stack you go. At the very top, economy-wide profit attribution still hasn't moved at all.
