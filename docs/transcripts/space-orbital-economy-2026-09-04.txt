Good morning. It's Friday, September 4th, and this is your space and orbital economy briefing.

For the past week this beat has made the same argument. In orbit, capital, factories and concrete keep arriving faster than anything converts into revenue or flights. Today that pattern breaks, and it breaks in the least glamorous corner of the sector. Planet Labs, the Earth-observation company most people had written off as a seller of commoditized pixels, posted its first-ever adjusted profitable quarter. Revenue was a record 116.1 million dollars, up 58 percent year over year. Adjusted EBITDA came in at 13.9 million dollars, against 6.4 million a year earlier.

Now qualify that number before you get excited about it. Adjusted EBITDA is not net profit. Planet's own full-year guidance puts adjusted EBITDA at 3 to 10 million dollars on projected revenue of 430 to 441 million. Call that a one-to-two-percent margin. So the honest read is not that Planet has become lucrative. It is that Planet has become self-funding. In a sector that has mostly run on other people's capital, that is a real milestone, but it is not an earnings story.

Now look at where the money came from, because that is the more interesting fact. Defense and intelligence revenue grew more than 90 percent year over year, by Planet's own account. The company disclosed an 8 million dollar contract from the National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency, and a German government tender worth up to 25 million euros over five years. That German number is an authorized ceiling, not booked revenue. Notice that both disclosed figures are small next to a 116 million dollar quarter. Most of that defense growth is therefore being carried by contracts the release simply does not itemize.

The takeaway is that sovereign defense demand closed Earth observation's business case. It was not the commercial subscription market this sector was originally funded to build. That is a genuine pivot, and it moves the concentration risk rather than removing it. Planet has swapped diffuse commercial churn for a small number of government budget lines. Those lines are stickier than enterprise subscriptions, because a five-year sovereign tender does not churn in a bad quarter. They are also lumpier, and they carry appropriation risk that no amount of product quality can hedge. The German tender does help, because it is a template other allied governments can copy. But copying it diversifies the customer base geographically without reducing the underlying dependence on state spending.

One more thing the release does not tell you. Everybody wants to underwrite the analytics layer sitting on top of the imagery, the AI models trained on a daily archive of the whole planet. Nothing in these numbers separates analytics revenue from imagery revenue. So the read-across is narrower than the headline suggests. It runs to whoever else can sell dedicated capacity to sovereigns rather than pixels to enterprises, which means BlackSky, Iceye and Maxar. Selling a government a guaranteed slice of a constellation is a different business from selling a company a subscription, and right now only one of the two is visibly working.

Planet converted because it sells a service. Now turn to the company that sells rides, where the conversion question is still wide open.

On Wednesday we told you the upper-stage catch looked unlikely on Starship's Flight 14. As of a report dated September 3rd, that read may have flipped. The account says the FAA approved reentry and a tower-catch landing for the ship, and that Elon Musk confirmed SpaceX will attempt it. The same report describes an authorization that expands the Pacific landing areas and clears temporary airspace closures for an additional reentry trajectory back to Starbase. That is at least consistent with a catch being live.

But hold all of it loosely, because this is an unresolved three-way dispute rather than a settled correction. A report dated September 2nd, the day before, still described FAA approval as outstanding. A separate account derived from SpaceNews said a splashdown remained the more likely outcome. And a third, undated account agrees, saying flatly that the ship splashes down in the Indian Ocean and the tower catch is deferred to a later flight. None of this has been verified against a primary FAA document, so the approval is something reporting describes rather than something on the record.

Be precise about what a regulatory clearance would even be. The FAA granting a catch attempt tells you SpaceX asked for the option. It does not tell you SpaceX will exercise it on the day, and SpaceX has a track record of scrubbing catch attempts late. So treat the catch as an option restored, not an event scheduled.

Here is why the distinction matters even inside the fog. Reaching orbit on Flight 14 would prove capability. Catching the upper stage is the only part of that flight that touches cost per kilogram, because it is the step that makes the ship reusable rather than expendable. Booster catches are now routine. The ship catch is the piece nobody has done. If it happens, it derisks the cost case behind both bulk Starlink V3 deployment and the Artemis lunar lander. If it does not, Starship stays priced like a test article, and every satellite operator and NASA program queued behind SpaceX keeps paying today's rate.

Even the good outcome has a ceiling worth naming. Starbase cadence is capped by a single qualified catch tower. One successful catch proves the technique. It does not immediately become a flight rate. And the flight is targeted no earlier than September 15th, which is a floor, not a booking.

The same organization chasing that milestone has a second deadline fifteen days later, and it has nothing to do with rockets. SpaceX has reorganized leadership across its AI data-center business, after reports that some facilities ran for months without backup cooling and power. Take those failures as reported, not established. The reporting names two executives moving in, Wesley Salandro and Logan McConnell, both out of rocket production and Starbase operations. That means launch-side leadership bandwidth is being redirected in the exact weeks Flight 14 needs it.

The stakes are real. SpaceX's AI compute-rental revenue hit 2.6 billion dollars in the second quarter, up from 737 million a year earlier. That growth now sits against a compute agreement with Google worth 920 million dollars a month. Run that monthly rate across a quarter and the single Google contract is roughly the size of that entire quarter's compute revenue. Both of those figures come from the same secondary reporting on the shakeup, not from a SpaceX disclosure. The contract requires SpaceX to deliver committed GPU capacity by September 30th. There is a one-month grace period, so this is not a hard cliff. But if SpaceX falls short, Google can terminate the agreement, or accept fewer GPUs and cut its payments proportionately. Note what this is and is not. It is terrestrial infrastructure sitting inside a space company, and a capital-allocation risk, not an orbital-compute development.

The pattern here is not that a data-center problem is stealing attention from the rocket. Moving experienced operators into a struggling unit is what a functioning company does, and Starbase has depth. The sharper point is cultural. Iterating fast and accepting failure is rational when the asset is a test article you expect to lose. It is a much more expensive habit when the asset is a contracted uptime commitment and the counterparty holds termination rights. SpaceX is running both playbooks at once, in the same month, with some of the same people.

So where does that leave the sector. All week this beat catalogued backlog, inventory and ground infrastructure accumulating while very little actually flew. The conversion we were waiting for finally showed up, and it showed up not in a rocket but in imagery sold to governments. Meanwhile the two developments that would genuinely reprice the sector, Starship's reusability milestone and SpaceX's own GPU delivery commitment, land inside the same three-week window. Watch September 15th and September 30th. One tells you whether the vehicle underpinning Starlink and Artemis gets cheaper. The other tells you whether SpaceX's fastest-growing revenue line survives contact with its own execution.

That's your briefing for September 4th. Have a good weekend.
