Space & the Orbital Economy

Transcript · November 21, 2026

Good morning, and welcome to the Cautious Optimism Briefing on the space and orbital economy. It's Saturday, November 21st, 2026.

After a week dominated by rocket failures, the more useful question is who is actually paying for the space economy right now. This week the answer sorted into three tiers, and the ordering matters. Start with the most durable tier, which is government demand.

The Pentagon's Golden Dome missile-defense program says a continuing resolution isn't slowing it down. Golden Dome deputy director Marcia Holmes said this week, quote, "We have 100% of our funding obligated." She added, "We are on contract and we are executing." That cuts against the usual pattern, where a CR leaves new-start programs waiting on Congress.

The mechanism isn't the annual budget line at all. Congress put a separate pool of multiyear money into Golden Dome through the reconciliation bill. Holmes put it simply: "Congress gave us a lot of money in '26." That money doesn't ride on the current fight over annual appropriations. Hence her line that the program office is, quote, "quite frankly not concerned about the CR."

One caveat belongs right next to that claim. Obligated means on contract. It does not mean delivered, and a fully obligated program can still slip on hardware.

The program's command-and-control layer is also now in a formal test campaign. Holmes says it "passed with flying colors." Treat that as the program grading its own homework. The Pentagon hasn't named the contractors, and there is no independent read on the results.

The satellite money is more concrete. L3Harris is building eighteen missile-tracking satellites for the Space Development Agency under a 955 million dollar order. That's missile-defense demand turning into backlog for a traditional prime. The open question is how the value ultimately splits. On one side are the primes building satellites. On the other is SpaceX, whose Starshield role in the architecture remains a swing factor.

Now the next tranche. Senate appropriators want to add another 9.4 billion dollars to Golden Dome. That sits on top of a base appropriation of roughly 400 million dollars.

By our arithmetic, the add alone is more than twenty times the base line. That tells you the program's real money doesn't run through the annual base budget. But the add is a committee proposal, not enacted law.

The same appropriators attached a condition that cuts against the triumphant framing. They want an independent Golden Dome cost estimate delivered within ninety days of enactment. Their report also points to the Congressional Budget Office. In an analysis done for Senator Jeff Merkley, CBO put Golden Dome's cost at more than 1.2 trillion dollars. The committee calls that number "a far cry from numbers the program office previously provided the committee."

So the people writing Golden Dome's checks are signaling, on the record, that they won't take the program's own cost math on faith. The funding is obligated. The price tag is not settled. In a sector short on bankable revenue, defense is the line that is actually on contract. But how big its hardware orders eventually get may be decided by that cost fight, not by the CR.

Obligated money still needs a ride to orbit, though, and that supply is thinner than it looks. ULA's Vulcan Centaur lost forty Amazon Leo satellites in a failure last Sunday, and it remains grounded. The FAA is overseeing the investigation, and there is no return-to-flight date. So customers with money in hand, government and commercial alike, are short on reliable rides.

That scarcity is showing up in the second tier, which is commercial launch pricing. Rocket Lab disclosed this month that it has signed more than 515 million dollars of new Neutron launch contracts with constellation operators. That lifts the Neutron manifest to sixteen missions.

Rocket Lab also raised Neutron's base launch price, from 55 million dollars to 70 million. Customers are paying more for a rocket that still hasn't flown. Its debut is now scheduled for next year, after slipping more than once. And booked missions are backlog, not recognized revenue.

Analysts expect Rocket Lab to post positive adjusted EBITDA of 41 million dollars in 2027. That's an analyst number, not company guidance. Our read is that it implicitly assumes Neutron flies and ramps next year. That is a schedule this vehicle has already missed before.

There are two honest ways to read the price increase. One is scarcity rent. With Vulcan grounded and Blue Origin's New Glenn also sidelined, Neutron is one of very few non-SpaceX options on the horizon. Customers are paying a premium just to hold a seat.

The other reading is that the original price was simply too low. On that view, this is a normal correction for a mid-size reusable vehicle with real demand behind it. This week's numbers can't separate those two stories.

Either way, the second-order effect points the same direction. By our arithmetic, Neutron's price is up about 27 percent before its first flight. If other new vehicles follow, the assumption that launch costs only fall gets weaker. That assumption is baked into a lot of constellation payback models. So an order book priced up on an unflown rocket is a bet that the launch bench stays thin. It is not proof that Neutron's unit economics work.

That brings us to the third tier: the one Western provider that is flying reliably, and where it is putting its capital. On its October earnings call, SpaceX reported third-quarter capital spending of 19.6 billion dollars.

For the fourth quarter alone, SpaceX guided to 35 to 40 billion dollars. That's roughly double, in a single quarter. It's a company-wide figure, not broken out by segment. So saying most of it is chasing AI compute rather than rockets is our inference, not a disclosed fact.

But look at where the growth is. SpaceX's Space segment, which is launch, grew just 6 percent year over year, to 1.8 billion dollars. That segment is lumpy, and it jumped sharply from the prior quarter. So treat the annual figure as one data point rather than a trend.

Connectivity, by contrast, grew 69 percent year over year. The picture is a company spending like a hyperscaler building data centers. It is not spending like a company racing to add launch capacity. Our read is that this is part of why the launch bench stays thin. It is also part of why Rocket Lab can charge more for a rocket it hasn't flown.

That dependence on SpaceX's manifest shows up again in direct-to-cell. AST SpaceMobile is targeting nationwide intermittent service activation in the US before year-end. Its next satellites, BlueBirds fourteen through sixteen, were shipped to Cape Canaveral in September. They are still awaiting confirmation of a SpaceX launch date.

"Intermittent" is the operative word there. It's a step toward a service, not continuous coverage. And AST is building a direct-to-cell rival to Starlink while waiting on SpaceX for the ride. Its year-end push runs straight through the incumbent's manifest.

So pull the threads together. What's genuinely inflecting this week is government demand. Golden Dome's money is obligated even under a continuing resolution, and appropriators are proposing more. Launch challengers are gaining pricing power too, but that power rests on scarcity rather than flight history.

What's still a demo dressed as momentum is almost everything else. A command-and-control test graded by the program running it. A Neutron rocket priced at a premium before its first flight. A direct-to-cell service that will start out intermittent.

Watch four things next. First, whether the Senate's 9.4 billion dollar add survives into an enacted bill, which would start the ninety-day cost-estimate clock. Second, whether ULA gives Vulcan a firm return-to-flight date. Third, whether Rocket Lab commits to a month for Neutron's debut, not just a year. And fourth, whether AST's BlueBirds get a SpaceX launch slot before year-end.

Until those land, the space economy's money is sorting itself by who is obligated to pay, not by who has actually delivered.

That's the briefing for November 21st. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you next time.
