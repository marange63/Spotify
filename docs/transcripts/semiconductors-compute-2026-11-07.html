Good morning, and welcome to your semiconductor briefing for Saturday, November 7th, 2026.

Yesterday on this desk, we argued that if power and permits become the binding constraint on AI deployment, packaging should be the first bottleneck to soften. The logic was simple. On Goldman's numbers, CoWoS capacity is on track to more than double next year. If fewer data centers can get power and permits, that new capacity outruns demand first. The same day, Reuters reported that Nvidia is putting billions of dollars into more packaging capacity. So who's right about where the chokepoint actually sits?

Start with what Reuters reported on November 6th. Nvidia is financing a multi-billion-dollar expansion of CoWoS packaging capacity at Siliconware Precision Industries, or SPIL, the ASE Technology unit. The claim rests on one unnamed industry executive. Reuters says Nvidia did not respond to a request for comment. ASE said it does not comment on, quote, "market rumours involving its customers." There's no dollar figure, just "billions." And there's no stated mechanism. That gap is not cosmetic. An equity stake gives Nvidia a claim on SPIL's balance sheet. A loan puts a receivable on Nvidia's books and leaves control with ASE. A prepayment just buys a place in line. Treat this as a single-sourced report, not a disclosed transaction.

One more limit matters for yesterday's question. The date of Reuters' report is not the date the deal was struck. The financing may well predate Thursday's chip selloff on the midterm results, which is what prompted our power-and-permits read. So the timing is weaker evidence against that read than it first looks.

What we do have is context, and it's useful. Reuters says the money will support, quote, "new factory space and equipment" at SPIL. There's no wafers-per-month figure and no completion date. Separately, ASE's CFO, Tung, said the company raised its 2026 capital spending budget to 11.5 billion dollars, up from 10.5 billion. He also said ASE could seek financing from customers to fund expansion. That doesn't confirm the Nvidia deal. But it does show the packaging layer is capital-hungry and open to buyer-funded capacity. Across the industry, Yole Group projects 2026 capital spending on advanced packaging will near 28 billion dollars. Yole also expects advanced-packaging revenue to overtake traditional packaging for the first time. And AMD has announced investments of more than 10 billion dollars across the industry, including ASE, SPIL and Powertech. That's not all packaging money, but packaging is the stated focus. So this isn't an Nvidia-only story. Both leading GPU vendors are now putting capital into the outsourced-assembly layer beneath the foundry.

So what about the binding-constraint question? We looked for anyone on record ranking packaging ahead of wafers, HBM, or power as the scarcest input. Nobody does. The closest language comes from Powertech's Jim Lin, who said, quote, "the most important thing right now is capacity." AMD's Lisa Su said the industry needs more capacity overall. Both describe packaging tightness in isolation. Neither ranks it against anything else. So here's the honest read. Nvidia financing packaging capacity shows it still sees packaging as a constraint worth paying to relieve. That isn't proof packaging is the binding one. And it isn't proof yesterday's frame was wrong.

Better to score it by competitive dimension than to pick a winner. For Nvidia, it's a hedge on supply resilience. For TSMC, the question is pricing leverage. As we noted yesterday, TrendForce reported in late October that TSMC is weighing CoWoS price hikes of up to 20 percent. A second packaging source is the textbook customer response to a supplier flexing on price. Our judgment is that if SPIL's new capacity comes online, TSMC's leverage in those talks weakens at the margin. But don't read this as Nvidia deliberately diluting TSMC. More SPIL capacity fits just as well with expanding total supply, or with absorbing TSMC's overflow. For ASE and SPIL, it means fresh capital, and deeper ties to the two leading GPU vendors as both customers and financiers.

One pattern is worth flagging, and it's judgment built on two unconfirmed reports. On November 5th, we covered trade-press reports that Nvidia asked the HBM makers to move its next-generation base die to TSMC. Now Nvidia is reportedly spreading packaging toward SPIL. That reads as concentrating where integration matters, and diversifying where raw capacity is scarce. Customer-financed supplier capex is vertical integration by contract. Our inference is that the same model could spread next to substrates and HBM. The takeaway: the scarcest layer in the stack is increasingly being financed by its customers, not just bought from its supplier.

Now follow the chips downstream, starting in China. The stranded-chip story we flagged yesterday now has a number. Reuters reporting, relayed by Semafor, says about 600,000 of Nvidia's advanced AI chips are stored in Hong Kong awaiting shipment to the mainland. Beijing has yet to approve large-volume imports. Recall the twist from yesterday. Washington had already loosened its own rule on H200 sales to China. So the hold-up sits in Beijing. That's the permission constraint we described yesterday, now quantified. It's a count of chips in storage, not lost revenue. No price is attached, so don't convert it into dollars.

That constraint belongs to Beijing. Nvidia is now adding permission rules of its own, reaching downstream as well as upstream. The Financial Times reported, via CNBC, that Nvidia told cloud providers reselling access to its chips that they must sell at least 51 percent of services to non-Chinese customers. Nvidia hasn't confirmed that rule. Bloomberg separately reports that Nvidia is boosting chip tracking in Southeast Asia to fight smuggling.

Why would Nvidia police its own customers this hard? There are two honest readings. One is defensive. Nvidia protects its US export licences and gets ahead of enforcement scrutiny. That's the reading Bloomberg's smuggling framing implies. The other is commercial. Nvidia closes off remote-access routes to compute that Chinese labs can't import directly. CNBC reports that Moonshot AI sought Nvidia chip access in Europe, though that's single-sourced and no deal is confirmed. One more inference from us. Tighter vetting in Southeast Asia could slow regional neocloud build-outs that had served as a release valve for China-adjacent demand. Whichever motive dominates, the cost is the same, and it's forgone sales. That's a cost only a seller confident in scarcity can comfortably absorb.

Both of those moves, financing capacity and rationing access, assume the demand underneath is solid. The weakest link in that assumption is the buyer that borrows. Figures compiled by the tech site Trending Topics, an aggregator rather than a filing, put CoreWeave's debt at about 51.6 billion dollars, including lease obligations. Its equity value has fallen to about 30 billion. That leaves debt at roughly 1.7 times market cap. The same source says neocloud stocks fell 26 to 40 percent from early October to early November, far more than chip stocks or the Nasdaq. Two caveats apply. The debt figure folds in leases, and the ratio moves with a depressed share price, so it overstates stress. And we've seen no evidence of actual order cuts.

Still, it draws a clean line. Compute-constrained demand means hyperscalers and frontier labs buying because they can't get enough chips. Economics-constrained demand means leveraged neoclouds that may lose access to credit before they lose the need for compute. Our inference is that if neocloud credit stays shut, it shows up first as reduced GPU and HBM orders from those buyers. If any of the new packaging capacity is sized for neocloud demand, that slice is exposed. So supply is being financed from strength, while part of the demand is financed from credit that's closing.

There's also a slower route to new capacity. Intel Foundry released version 0.9 of its 14A process design kit on November 2nd. It's the first version built on measured silicon rather than models. That's a real engineering milestone. But it's design enablement, not a customer commitment or a shipping product. Reports that Tesla has signed on as a 14A customer remain unconfirmed by any source we've seen quoted. So do rumors that Nvidia, AMD, Apple, Microsoft, or OpenAI are evaluating Intel's leading nodes. Wafer-level diversification away from TSMC is measured in PDK version numbers, and it takes years. Packaging diversification, if the SPIL story holds up, is measured in checks written this quarter.

Put it together. Nvidia is spending to loosen a supply constraint it doesn't own, while tightening its grip on the demand it does control. Two things to watch. First, whether anyone puts a size and a form on the SPIL financing. Second, whether Applied Materials' results on November 12th show capex caution reaching the tool-makers.
