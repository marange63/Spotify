Good morning. It's Saturday, September 5th, and this is your private equity briefing.

For a week now, this show has argued one thing. Private equity assets are increasingly priced by whoever controls the sale. On Friday we watched EQT pay roughly sixteen to eighteen times fee-related EBITDA to buy Coller Capital. That was a sponsor buying the toll booth on secondaries pricing itself.

Today the customers write back. ILPA, the trade body that represents the limited partners writing the checks, has drafted new continuation vehicle guidance. It is the first real institutional pushback to appear on this beat all week.

Say up front what this is and what it is not. ILPA published the draft in June. The public comment period closed on August 5th. ILPA's own site says final guidance is coming, in its words, later this year, with no fixed date attached. So this is not this week's news. It is new to this conversation, and it reframes where the leverage sits.

Here is what the draft actually asks for, as reported by Mercer Capital's read of it. It lengthens the minimum window limited partners get to decide whether to roll into a continuation vehicle or cash out. That window goes to thirty business days, up from twenty under the prior framework. It requires the general partner to run what ILPA calls a targeted competitive bidding process supported by independent third-party price validation. It asks the sponsor to share an anonymized summary of the final-round bids with investors. It pushes toward live meetings of the limited partner advisory committee, with at least ten business days to review conflicts materials, rather than a rushed written consent.

Then there is the sharpest clause, and it is the one on fees. Rolling investors must be shown no overall increase in management fees or carried interest. And ILPA wants that shown, in its language, in actual dollars rather than headline percentages. That distinction is the whole point. A reset carry basis at an unchanged headline rate can quietly raise the dollar take on the very same asset.

Strip away the mechanics and the honest read is this. Process has replaced price as the limited partner's protection. Think about what a mandatory bidding process with outside price validation concedes. It concedes that nobody can independently verify a fair mark when the general partner sits on both sides of the trade. The sponsor is selling an asset from one fund it manages to another fund it also manages. You cannot check the price directly. So you police the procedure instead.

Why now. Continuation vehicles went from about five percent of sponsor-backed exits globally in 2021 to roughly fourteen percent in 2025. Single-asset vehicles alone ran to thirty-four billion dollars of deal value in the first half of this year. Sponsor-led deals made up over half of a secondary market that topped one hundred twenty billion dollars in that same half. Those figures already ran on this show earlier in the week, and I use them here only to size the stakes, not as fresh news. That is the scale that forced ILPA's hand. A niche liquidity tool has become the dominant exit route, right as the listing and merger windows stayed selective.

Now the skeptical turn, because there are three reasons to doubt this bites.

The first is enforcement. ILPA is a trade association, not a regulator. The guidance is voluntary. It binds only as far as investors are willing to walk away from a deal that fails to meet it.

The second is timing. Limited partner bargaining power is at a cyclical low exactly when it is needed most. An investor starved of distributions does not walk away from the vehicle manufacturing their cash back over a ten-day paperwork window.

The third is the perverse case, and it is the one I would actually underwrite. This could legitimize continuation vehicles rather than restrain them. Once a documented, ILPA-blessed process exists, contesting the actual mark gets harder, not easier. That reading gets support from an unexpected corner. The law firm Mayer Brown, advising sponsor clients this summer, told them that a market-tested price paired with a fairness opinion is substantially more defensible than a fairness opinion standing alone. The same alert calls ILPA's standard, quote, benchmarks against which institutional investors evaluate GP conduct for the foreseeable future. That is a firm telling general partners to get in front of this, not to fight it.

Worth noting what we cannot see here. ILPA keeps every comment-period submission strictly confidential. So there is no public letter from any investor or sponsor objecting to any of this. The only visible reaction on the record is a law firm advising the sell side. Absence of public dissent is not evidence of consensus. The process itself suppresses that signal.

Two second-order effects follow. The first is who pays. Independent price validation is a new fee line for secondaries advisers and fairness-opinion providers. That cost sits inside the vehicle. So the investors this guidance protects are the ones funding the protection. The second is where the money goes. Raising the process cost of a continuation vehicle does not remove the need for liquidity. It relocates it. The likely destinations are net-asset-value loans and strip sales. Both disclose less to investors than the route ILPA is now policing. The industry would not have reduced its disclosure risk. It would have moved it somewhere darker.

Set all of that against the one dated transaction this week that needed none of this machinery.

KKR agreed to buy A1 Garage Door Service for around two billion dollars, in a deal reported on September 2nd and 3rd. A1 is a residential garage-door repair and replacement platform. The seller is Cortec Group, which put growth capital into the business back in 2022. So this is sponsor to sponsor. One fiduciary is selling to a genuinely different fiduciary. That is exactly the arm's-length pricing a continuation vehicle structurally lacks. KKR already owns Neighborly and Groundworks in adjacent home-services categories, so this reads as a bolt-on to an existing platform rather than a new sector bet.

Now the catch, and it is a real one. Nobody has confirmed the number. It is reporter-sourced from people familiar with the matter. KKR, Cortec and A1 all declined to comment. There is no entry multiple and no leverage structure disclosed. A price with no multiple attached tells you appetite exists and nothing about the return Cortec actually realized. And even if it closes exactly as reported, the asset never leaves the private equity universe. It just changes which sponsor's fund holds it. So a healthier distributions number can coexist with an unchanged inventory of companies waiting for a genuine exit.

That inventory problem shows up one loop earlier, on the fundraising side.

Funds over five billion dollars that closed in the first half of this year spent more than three times as many months on the road as similarly sized funds did five years ago. That figure comes from PERE. Flag clearly that the underlying funds are real-estate vehicles, including Blackstone Real Estate Partners Europe the Seventh and Brookfield Strategic Real Estate Partners the Fifth. So treat it as a read-across for alternatives fundraising broadly, not a buyout-specific number.

The mechanism is simple. An investor who has not been paid back cannot fund the next vintage. So the exit logjam becomes a calendar problem for the sponsors raising money right now. The concrete case is Blue Owl's GP Stakes Fund the Sixth, targeting thirteen billion dollars. The firm had expected to hit that target by the end of last year and is now guiding into this year instead. That is roughly a full year of slippage. The amount actually raised against the target was not disclosed, so a slipped close is not the same as a failed raise.

So where does the cycle actually stand. Limited partners have moved from disputing the mark on continuation vehicles to writing the procedure that produces it. That is what a market does when it has effectively given up on verifying the price directly.

Three catalysts to watch from here. Whether ILPA's final language, due later this year, survives contact with sponsor lobbying. Whether sponsor-led secondary volume in the second half softens on process friction, against that hundred-and-twenty-billion-dollar first-half run rate. And whether the first continuation vehicle Coller prices under EQT's ownership follows this new standard, or tests how much of it is optional in practice.

That's the briefing. Have a good weekend.
