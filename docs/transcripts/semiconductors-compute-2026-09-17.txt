Good morning. It's Thursday, September 17th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

For six straight sessions, this beat has had to discount nearly every big number in the industry, because the party quoting it was also the party who benefits from it. We've asked, more than once, for someone to put a real capability figure on the record instead of leaving capacity and process leadership to TrendForce estimates and vendor earnings calls. This month, two companies finally did. And fittingly, the number landed in the one part of the stack this beat had already concluded was not the binding constraint.

At the SPIE lithography conference earlier this month, Intel Foundry and ASML jointly disclosed that High-NA extreme ultraviolet lithography has now processed more than one million wafers, across tool certification, R&D, and volume production. Read the release directly and it names the product. High-NA is running on select layers for a subset of Intel's Core Ultra Series 3 processors, code-named Panther Lake, on the 18A process. Intel and ASML also say those layers meet or exceed the performance of comparable layers patterned on standard 0.33-numerical-aperture EUV tools.

Before we say what this means, be precise about what kind of number it is. One million wafers is cumulative, across three very different buckets. So it measures how far up the learning curve the tool has climbed, not how much shippable silicon Intel is producing today. Capability, not capacity. And the phrase "select layers" is doing a lot of work. Neither release discloses how many layers, or which ones. High-NA patterning a handful of layers on one product line is a materially different claim from High-NA as the patterning backbone of 18A. One more discount before the analysis. This is a primary source, and a primary source is not a disinterested one. Intel needs High-NA to look production-ready, and ASML needs it to look like a qualified production tool rather than a strategic bet.

There's a second wrinkle, and it's the one the headlines miss. Being first to volume-produce with High-NA is genuinely ambiguous evidence, not straightforwardly good news. The economic case for buying a first-generation tool at a large premium over a standard EUV system is strongest for the fab whose existing multi-patterning density economics are weakest. That is exactly what TSMC's own posture suggests. TrendForce reports that TSMC's chief executive, C.C. Wei, has said the company already owns High-NA equipment and is actively running R&D on it. The same reporting has TSMC senior vice president Kevin Zhang saying the upcoming A13 and A12 nodes, both targeted for 2029, aren't expected to need High-NA at all, because TSMC can hold similar complexity through multi-patterning. Samsung has pushed its own insertion point further out still, to its A10 node and below, targeting mass production around 2030.

Read those together and the competitive picture inverts. TSMC is not locked out of High-NA. It has the tools and is choosing not to insert them, because its density economics don't force the decision yet. So Intel isn't ahead because it solved a problem faster than everyone else. It's ahead partly because its own process economics gave it less choice. Score it honestly. That is a real but narrow lithography lead, and it tells you nothing about 18A or 14A yield, and nothing about whether Intel Foundry wins the external customers that actually decide whether this turnaround succeeds.

There's also a shelf life on the lead. Intel and ASML say customers can capture High-NA's benefits using today's standard six-inch mask format, either by floor-planning within it or by using Intel Foundry's stitching capability. That lowers the adoption barrier for whoever follows. So the more this capability proves out, the faster Intel's head start depreciates. Note what is still missing, too. Neither company disclosed how many High-NA systems have shipped or been installed. Without a tool count, the read-through to ASML's own order book can't be written as a number at all, only as a direction. The so-what for Intel is modest. It has banked a capability milestone and no scoreboard win.

Which raises the question this beat keeps circling. If lithography was never the binding constraint, where is it now? We flagged packaging slots on Tuesday and memory inventory the day after that. Today it's moved somewhere with even less published data attached to it.

China's Commerce Ministry has imposed provisional anti-dumping cash deposits, effective September 8th, on dichlorosilane imported from Japan, at rates ranging from 80.8 to 99.2 percent. Dichlorosilane is a specialty gas used in thin-film deposition on silicon wafers, with few substitutes. Shin-Etsu Chemical draws the maximum rate, and the minimum goes to a different Japanese producer. That spread is wide enough to look like targeting specific companies rather than a uniform industry finding. Two caveats before the interpretation. These are provisional deposits from a preliminary determination, not a final duty, and the final ruling can land somewhere else. And we have no figure for how much dichlorosilane volume actually flows from Japan into Chinese fabs, so treat the exposure as unquantified rather than implying a number.

Now resist the instinct to call this a global supply shock, because these are deposits China is levying on its own imports. The immediate cost lands on the Chinese fabs buying the gas, SMIC and its domestic peers, not on chipmakers elsewhere. Read it instead as China taxing its own fabs' input costs to force faster domestic substitution in specialty chemicals, with a real chance Japan responds in kind on semiconductor materials. That is the same escalation logic that ran through lithography tools, now running through gases, where the chokepoints are less visible and the substitution timelines are shorter. The pattern is the point. The scarcity premium in this industry keeps migrating to whatever input nobody models, because nobody pre-builds capacity for an input that never appears in a forecast.

All of that is a supply-side story. This week the demand assumption underneath it got tested too, and for once by a buyer rather than a seller.

Broadcom, the company that co-designs Google's TPUs and builds custom accelerators for the hyperscalers, had its chief executive Hock Tan publicly answer Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei's essay arguing the industry should moderate the pace of frontier-model capability gains. Speaking on Monday, Tan said demand for AI compute infrastructure remains strong and durable, and that nothing in Broadcom's outlook has changed. On its own, that's worth very little. Tan is the seller here, and sellers reassure. Apply this show's standing discount symmetrically, and his reassurance carries about as much weight as any other beneficiary's forecast.

The evidence that actually settles the question came from Anthropic itself. The lab's own announcement of an expanded Google and Broadcom compute deal commits to multiple gigawatts of next-generation TPU capacity starting in 2027. That's a purchase commitment from the buyer, not a talking point from the vendor. Bound it carefully, though. "Multiple gigawatts" is capacity language with no dollar value, no chip count, and no delivery schedule attached. And a commitment that starts in 2027 tells you nothing about what anyone spends in 2026.

The right read is that these two things aren't in contradiction at all. Amodei's essay is a public-policy argument about how fast the frontier should move. Anthropic's TPU commitment is a procurement decision. Advocacy and purchasing run on different clocks. And if a frontier lab can argue publicly for slowing capability gains while its own order book for compute keeps growing, that tells you something specific about what's driving the orders. Demand is increasingly a function of inference and deployment economics rather than training-run escalation alone. Which means slowdown rhetoric and rising silicon orders can coexist indefinitely. The market treating the essay as a demand signal looks like a mispricing.

So tie it together. The two most credible pieces of evidence this week both moved off the parts of this industry everyone already models. A lithography capability disclosure from the fab with the industry's worst roadmap credibility, and a specialty-gas tariff that appears in nobody's capacity forecast. Two things to watch next. Whether TSMC or Samsung ever put their own High-NA insertion timing on the record directly, rather than through analyst aggregation. And whether Japan answers the dichlorosilane deposits in kind.

That's the briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
