Good morning, it's Saturday, August 1st, and this is your capital markets radar.

Start with a number that looks like crypto weakness but isn't. Stablecoin market capitalization just shrank for the first time in four years. It's down roughly ten billion dollars from May's peak to about three hundred billion, and the June decline alone was seven point seven billion, the largest single-month drop since Terra collapsed in May of 2022. If you stopped there, you'd call it risk-off. You'd be wrong. June also set an all-time record for stablecoin settlement volume, one point seven nine trillion dollars in adjusted transaction volume. The rails have never been busier. So strip it down and one thing moved the opposite way from the balances: velocity. The same pool of on-chain dollars is turning over faster while fewer of them sit idle, which tells you stablecoins are behaving more like a payment rail and less like a place to park cash. The balances aren't disappearing. They're moving somewhere else.

That somewhere else is yield-bearing tokenized Treasury funds. They've grown from a record eleven billion dollars in March to nearly sixteen billion by late July. The mechanism is regulatory, not sentimental: the GENIUS Act bars stablecoin issuers from paying yield on payment stablecoins. So for years, institutional cash sat in zero-yield stablecoins because there was no compliant alternative. Now there is. Circle's yield-bearing USYC has overtaken BlackRock's BUIDL, and a JPMorgan entrant grew eighty-seven percent in a single month. This is a clean case study in how one piece of legislation can redirect billions of dollars of collateral within a year of taking effect, and it's worth remembering as MiCA and other jurisdictions write their own stablecoin-yield rules.

Now, why is this happening now rather than gradually over the last year. Rates are the amplifier. We covered the hawkish nine-to-three FOMC vote on Wednesday, the most divisive Fed decision since 2016, with three regional presidents dissenting in favor of a hike rather than a hold. Since then, the market has pushed its September pricing further toward that hike. A dated CME FedWatch reading from July 30th put the odds at eighty-one percent for a quarter-point hike, with zero percent priced for a cut. I'll flag that as one specific reading rather than a settled consensus, because other trackers in the same window showed odds in the low sixties — the exact number is moving day to day, but the direction is not in question. The market has swung from pricing a cut under a new Fed chair to pricing a hike. That's the story that matters here, not the third decimal point.

Here's why that direction is the amplifier for the stablecoin story. The higher real yields go, the more it costs you to hold a dollar balance that legally cannot pay you anything. The opportunity cost of a zero-yield stablecoin is now the entire front-end yield — north of three and a half percent, with the funds rate sitting at three and a half to three and three-quarters. That was tolerable when short rates were low or falling. It's much less tolerable when the Fed is signaling higher-for-longer and a compliant, yield-bearing tokenized Treasury product is one click away. So the same regime that's been repricing curves and equities all week is now reaching into on-chain dollar collateral and squeezing out the idle balances.

That squeeze has a clear winner and loser. The losers are Tether and Circle, in their role as stablecoin issuers — every dollar that migrates out of a zero-yield stablecoin is a dollar of float income they no longer earn. The winners are the sponsors of the compliant alternatives: Circle's own USYC product, BlackRock's BUIDL, and increasingly banks like JPMorgan building tokenized cash vehicles that can legally pass yield through. So don't read this as bad news for Circle across the board — it's bad news for one line of Circle's business and good news for another, which is a nuance the headline number misses. And step back for the bigger structural read. This is float income migrating out of two crypto-native issuers and toward regulated asset managers and banks. In other words, the yield ban is quietly re-intermediating digital dollars back toward the traditional financial system that the stablecoin model was built to route around.

The second effect is smaller today but worth tracking. Tokenized Treasury funds are becoming a new marginal buyer at the front end of the curve, right as net Treasury issuance stays elevated. I want to size that honestly: sixteen billion dollars is nothing against a multi-trillion-dollar T-bill market. This is not yet a macro-material source of demand. But the growth rate — from eleven billion to sixteen billion in four months — is the kind of curve that turns into a real allocator base if the yield differential persists, and higher-for-longer is exactly the environment that keeps that differential open. For a rates desk, that's a slow-building bid for bills that leans, at the margin, against the term-premium selloff happening further out the curve.

Which brings us to the forward catalyst. The Treasury's next quarterly refunding announcement lands this coming Wednesday, August 5th, covering coupon auction sizes, buyback programs, and borrowing estimates for the quarter ahead. It arrives at an interesting moment: a small but fast-growing new buyer base is emerging at the front end of the curve through tokenized Treasuries, right as the Fed's hawkish tilt pushes term premium higher across the curve. This isn't a case where I'd expect the refunding announcement itself to reference tokenized demand — it won't. But it's the next data point that tells us whether Treasury is leaning into the front end or extending duration into a market that's still digesting a more hawkish rate path. Watch the coupon mix and the buyback size Wednesday as confirmation, or contradiction, of how the market has been pricing this repricing all week.

So pull the thread together. This isn't a crypto story about weakening demand for digital dollars — settlement volume at a record high rules that out. It's a rates story wearing a crypto costume. The same higher-for-longer regime that's been steepening curves and splitting AI-capex winners from losers in equities this week has now found its way into on-chain collateral, and it's doing what higher-for-longer regimes always do: punishing anything priced at zero and rewarding anything that can legally compete for yield. The GENIUS Act built the plumbing for that competition to happen instantly rather than over years, and we're watching the first quarter of it play out in real time. The number to watch from here isn't the month-over-month stablecoin decline — it's whether that sixteen-billion-dollar tokenized Treasury base keeps compounding the way it has, from eleven billion in March to nearly sixteen by late July, because that's the line that tells you whether this stays a niche reallocation or becomes a real player at Treasury's auctions.
