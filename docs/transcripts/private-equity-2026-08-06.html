The Q2 2026 alt-manager earnings season is now complete, and Carlyle's print closes it the way we flagged yesterday it would. Carlyle was the last major to report, and we said its result would tell us whether the carry thaw KKR showed on its July call was broadening across the industry or staying confined to one franchise. Here's what it said. Carlyle posted record fee-related earnings of 358 million dollars, up 11 percent year over year, and its highest distributable earnings in nearly four years at 472 million dollars. On the headline, that reads like a manager firing on every cylinder. But dig into the line that actually measures cash carry realized for limited partners, and it's thin. Carlyle discloses realized net performance revenues of just 115 million dollars for the quarter, 135 million year to date. So the record distributable-earnings figure is not being driven by a flood of realized carried interest. It's being driven by fees.

You can see that in the composition. Carlyle's own private equity segment posted distributable earnings of 219 million dollars, up nearly 50 percent sequentially, which sounds like a monetization surge until you notice distributable earnings is a broad pre-tax cash measure, not a clean carry line. Alongside it, Carlyle put up a record fee-related performance revenue line of 89 million dollars, more than double last year's second quarter. That number is real and it's growing fast, but it sits inside fee-related earnings, not carry. It's a fee earned for hitting a performance hurdle on a fund, not a cash realization event where a company was sold and proceeds went back to investors. So the season's verdict is the one we've been building toward all week. KKR, which reported what it called the highest monetization quarter in its history, remains the outlier, not the vanguard of a broad thaw.

Widen out and the pattern is uniform. TPG closes the roll call with fee-related earnings up 43 percent to 315 million dollars, on a 50 percent margin. That follows Blackstone's record 1.35 trillion dollars of assets under management, Apollo's record 785 million dollars of fee-related earnings, up 25 percent, and Ares' record quarter of gross fundraising, over 36 billion dollars raised in three months. Every single reporting manager this quarter, KKR, Blackstone, Apollo, Carlyle, Ares, TPG, hit a record or near-record on the fee line. None of them broke out a comparably large realized-carry number alongside it. So what that tells you is that the durable, compounding part of this business right now is the fee annuity, not the carry cycle. And that has a direct read-through to how you should value the alt-manager cohort's own public equity. You are being asked to pay for a bond-like fee stream that keeps re-rating, not for a returns cycle that's turning.

Here's the sharpest way to see the gap between paper and cash. The stored-up carry these firms are sitting on is enormous, and it's mostly still on paper. KKR reports gross unrealized carried interest of 10.2 billion dollars as of the end of June. Blackstone's net accrued performance revenue, the money it has earned on paper but not yet crystallized, stands at a four-year high of 7.5 billion dollars, about 6 dollars a share. Those are the reservoirs. Now put them next to the trickle actually coming out: Carlyle's 115 million dollars of realized carry, Blackstone's net realizations up 27 percent year over year but still a modest line, KKR's single strong quarter. The reservoir is measured in billions and it keeps filling as marks rise. The realized flow is measured in hundreds of millions and it stays lumpy. Blackstone even told you why the reservoir keeps filling: its corporate private equity funds appreciated 3.7 percent in the quarter and 14 percent over the last twelve months. The marks are going up. The cash coming back is not keeping pace. That is the whole cycle in one contrast, and it is why a record distributable-earnings headline can coexist with a distribution problem.

Now, Carlyle does disclose one genuinely large cash number worth separating out carefully. It says it's distributing nearly 7 billion dollars during the quarter and 37 billion over the past year, and it's positioning that as being an industry outlier in returning capital to clients. That's real cash, and it's a lot of it. But don't conflate it with private equity carry realization. That figure spans Carlyle's whole platform, including its global credit segment, which is returning principal and interest, not private-equity carried interest. A diversified manager can move a lot of cash off a credit book while its actual PE carry realization stays stuck at 115 million dollars. That gap is exactly why a single "distributions are back" headline number is unreliable across this sector. You have to ask what's inside it, and how much of it is credit paying down versus a company actually being sold.

Step back and there's a second-order problem building underneath all these fee records. Every one of these managers is raising money faster than it can put it to work at attractive entry prices. Ares just posted its highest fundraising quarter ever, and dry powder across the industry is sitting near record levels. That capital carries a clock. If it gets deployed into deals at today's entry multiples while the exit environment stays this slow, it pressures the returns on the vintages being invested right now. In other words, the fee machine's own success stores up a future returns problem. The more committed capital these firms gather, the more management fees they earn today, but also the more pressure builds to deploy into a market where exits aren't clearing. Allocators are starting to notice, and it shows up as a K-shaped market: capital keeps concentrating in the largest, most trusted names while smaller managers struggle to close at all.

That two-speed read carries right down to the manager-level equity bet too. Blue Owl's flagship GP-stakes fund just reached a final close at 10.6 billion dollars, 71 percent larger than its predecessor. GP-stakes investing is precisely the strategy built to compound on committed, fee-paying capital regardless of whether portfolio exits happen, which is exactly why allocators like it while the exit side stays jammed. It's a bet on manager consolidation itself. But the detail that matters is that this close only landed after several timeline extensions. Even the hottest consolidation strategy in the asset class needed extra time to get limited partners to commit. So what that tells you is that the slow LP environment isn't just hitting traditional buyout funds. It's showing up even where the fee-annuity logic is cleanest and the story is easiest to sell.

Which brings us to the one thing that would actually change this picture. Everything I've described so far is the entry side wide open, the largest leveraged buyout in history closed just this week, and the fee side compounding at record pace. What's missing is the exit. Bankers are describing the sponsor-backed IPO pipeline as simmering, thawing but not yet flooding. Some market strategists estimate as much as a third of 2026's IPO activity could involve sponsors exiting portfolio companies they've held since the 2022 and 2023 slowdown. Treat that as a forecast, not a realized statistic. There's no named, priced sponsor-backed listing on the calendar in the next few days to point to. But it's the right thing to watch, because a genuine IPO reopening returns cash directly to limited partners rather than rolling exposure forward into another continuation vehicle. That would move the one measure that actually matters, cash back to investors, more than another quarter of record fee-related earnings ever could.

So where does the cycle stand. The entry side is wide open and the fee machine across every major manager is compounding at a record pace. But the reservoir of unrealized carry keeps filling faster than it drains, and the exit side, the thing that actually converts paper marks into realized returns, stayed franchise-specific to KKR this quarter, not systemic. The binding constraint isn't assets under management, which grows everywhere. It's cash back to limited partners. Watch the IPO calendar, not the next earnings print, for the signal that the thaw is finally broadening.
