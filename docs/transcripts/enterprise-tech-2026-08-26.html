Good morning, it's Wednesday, August 26th, 2026, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

Start with a single afternoon that tells you how nervous this market has gotten about who owns the enterprise software budget. On August 20th, Bloomberg reported that Anthropic is preparing to file publicly as soon as the end of this month, for a share sale that could rival or exceed SpaceX's record seventy-five billion dollar initial offering. That seventy-five billion is the size of the offering, not the company's valuation. Anthropic's preliminary second-quarter revenue topped eleven and a half billion dollars, and its annualized run rate hit sixty-five billion dollars by the end of July. For scale, Microsoft disclosed last month that Azure had passed one hundred billion dollars of annual revenue for the first time. Anthropic's run rate is now about two-thirds of that. This is not a niche lab anymore. It is a company approaching hyperscaler-sized revenue. And it has not filed anything yet. The filing date and the offering size are both unconfirmed, and Anthropic has said nothing publicly about terms.

The more interesting reaction was in public markets that same afternoon. MongoDB fell four and a half percent. Cloudflare fell four percent. Okta fell about five percent. Zscaler fell four and a half percent. Four liquid, widely held enterprise software names, down together, on a report about a different company's share sale. The trading-floor read, as relayed by the market press rather than by any of the companies, was blunt. If frontier AI labs go public and disclose granular usage data, that data might show them displacing the per-seat software spend these companies live on. The news touched none of these four businesses. It repriced them anyway.

Now hold that against where this briefing stood two days before that. On August 18th, a rotation lifted a different set of beaten-down enterprise software names, for the mirror-image reason. AI capital-expenditure plans looked shaky, and investors treated per-seat software as the safer, cash-generative hedge against an overbuilt AI trade. Two days later, an AI lab looking strong was enough to sell the same complex off. That is not a verdict on these businesses. It is a correlation regime. Enterprise software is now the mechanical short leg of the AI trade in both directions. Good news about AI capex discipline lifts it. Good news about an AI lab's growth sinks it. Good news for these companies and good news about AI can no longer both be true at once in the market's eyes.

Be precise about what actually got priced on August 20th. It was a forecast about what Anthropic's filing might disclose, not anything Anthropic has disclosed. Nobody has published seat-level usage data showing Claude displacing MongoDB, Okta, or Zscaler spend. We went looking for the other side of that ledger too, and came back nearly empty. MongoDB's most recent quarterly release discloses a total customer count north of sixty-seven thousand seven hundred as of the end of April. That is the only hard number we could pull. No net revenue retention figure, no seat trend, and nothing from Okta or Zscaler showing per-seat consumption decelerating. There is also no disclosure anywhere of Claude's enterprise seat count or per-seat pricing. That is a confirmed absence, not a gap we failed to fill. So nothing public would confirm or falsify the displacement story. The move was sentiment. One line in an eventual filing would settle it: usage broken out by category or by seat.

There is also a tension with this show's own read from August 22nd. Working off Ramp's corporate spending data, we argued that businesses run more than one lab and route work by cost, which makes any single frontier model a substitutable input rather than a platform. A vendor that cannot hold pricing on its premium tier is a strange candidate to be quietly eating the enterprise software budget. Both readings cannot be fully right. Today's evidence favors the substitutable-input version, because that one rests on observed spending behavior. The displacement version rests on an unfiled document.

If public software is the short leg of that trade, look at what capital is long. Databricks closed a five billion dollar funding round on August 13th at a one hundred ninety billion dollar valuation. Six months earlier the same company was valued at one hundred thirty-four billion. On our own arithmetic across those two marks, that is roughly a forty-two percent markup in half a year. The company says it crossed a seven billion dollar annualized revenue run rate in its second quarter, up more than eighty percent year over year. Take that as management-stated and unaudited, because it is. A private round is priced by the investors buying into it, not discovered by continuous trading. And no Databricks IPO timeline is confirmed, whatever the speculation says.

That is the real structural split this week. MongoDB, Cloudflare, Okta and Zscaler get re-marked in a single afternoon by anyone with a brokerage account. Databricks gets marked up twice a year by a small group of investors with every incentive to mark generously. That is not proof that private AI-native platforms are winning the argument. It is proof they are not being tested the same way. And there is an uncomfortable second-order effect. While the biggest AI-native platforms stay private, the only tradable expression of the AI-adoption thesis is shorting the public incumbents. That mechanically amplifies moves like August 20th.

The trade above assumes AI labs can actually reach into the enterprise stack, so here is this week's evidence on the mechanism. On August 24th, Anthropic announced general availability of enterprise-managed authorization for Claude's MCP connector framework, adding Datadog, Notion and Slack to a list that already included Asana, Atlassian, Canva, Figma, Linear and Supabase. This is an admin-control feature — governance, permissioning, audit — not a model upgrade. It sits on top of Claude Cowork, Anthropic's existing product for private plugin marketplaces. Those plugins are built from a customer's own GitHub repositories. That plugin layer is the established base here, not today's news.

Now the correction this show owes you. Yesterday I argued that once the agent-to-agent messaging protocol was handed to a neutral standards body, no vendor could charge for it — so the last layer anyone could bill for was identity, permission scoping and audit trails. Anthropic had shipped exactly that layer as a platform feature the day before. I made the argument anyway. If enterprise-managed authorization becomes table stakes across model vendors, the way single sign-on did for cloud apps, then governance stops being a differentiated SKU and becomes a checkbox. That pressures the pricing power of the identity and agent-governance vendors selling precisely that capability. The defensible layer keeps moving up the stack, and it keeps getting given away.

One caveat cuts against the bear case as well. There is zero adoption, seat or revenue data attached to Claude Cowork. Shipping the governance plumbing is necessary for org-wide deployment. It proves nothing about whether org-wide deployment happened. And a connector list is not displacement. Connecting Claude to Slack, Notion and Datadog makes Claude a client of those products, which is at least as consistent with complementarity as with the SaaS-eating thesis the market priced on August 20th.

None of this has shown up in a profit-and-loss statement yet. McKinsey's State of AI 2026 survey, covering 1,719 professionals, found that thirty-seven percent of respondents attribute at least some earnings impact to AI use. That figure comes via The Register's summary this week, and we could not verify the primary report directly. Read the bar carefully. "At least some" is the loosest possible threshold, and only about a third clear it. It is self-reported, so use it to size a gap, not to claim AI does not work. Markets repriced an entire software category on a displacement forecast. The same week, the loosest available test of AI's financial impact finds two-thirds of practitioners cannot attribute any of it. That gap is what makes the August 20th trade look like a narrative price rather than an earnings price.

There are no actuals, and I will not quote pre-earnings guidance as if it were a result. But think about what a strong Salesforce print would mean under the correlation regime just described. It could read as proof that the core subscription business is resilient. It could equally read as noise against the displacement fear that hit the sector on August 20th. Watch the stock reaction as much as the number.
