Good morning. It's Friday, October 30th, and this is the private credit briefing.

Blue Owl reported its third quarter yesterday. It's the first alternative-manager result this desk has had in hand this season. And it moves something we've tracked all week into a manager's own reported numbers. The money raised for private credit is now outrunning the money actually lent.

Start with the headline. Blue Owl's assets under management hit $330 billion, up 12 percent year over year. The firm raised $49 billion of new capital across its platforms. One clarification before we go further. These are figures for Blue Owl the asset manager, not its listed BDC, which reports next week. On its own, the headline reads like the industry's preferred story of scale and demand.

Now look inside the direct-lending book. Blue Owl's earnings deck, filed with the SEC, reports gross originations of $5.2 billion for the quarter. But reported net deployment was slightly negative, at minus $0.3 billion. The deck doesn't fully bridge the two, and sell-downs are a likely but unconfirmed reason for the difference. The point stands either way. The direct-lending book did not grow.

Co-CEO Marc Lipschultz offered the causal story on the call. In his words: "Until there's a more robust M&A environment, I don't think one would logically expect there to be rapid net deployment." His read is that soft deal activity is holding back net growth, not caution on risk. He added that "repayments happen at 100 cents on the dollar." In other words, the loans leaving the book are paid off in full, which is good for realized losses. CFO Alan Kirshenbaum pointed to a 45 percent increase in gross deployment in direct lending. That growth was gross, and it still left the net book flat. And Lipschultz said flatly that "the M&A environment will come," and that "when that happens, then probably both those numbers are going to accelerate." So management's frame is cyclical, not structural.

Still, there's a second reading worth holding alongside that one. This desk has argued in recent episodes that the best borrowers are refinancing out of private books. Par repayments fit that story. They're benign for losses today. But the strongest credits are the ones most able to exit. What stays behind skews toward borrowers who can't easily go elsewhere. So reported portfolio quality can drift down even while realized losses hold steady.

The fee data makes the decoupling concrete. Credit-segment fee-paying AUM was $97 billion. The deck itself calls that "relatively unchanged" from a year ago. Meanwhile headline AUM grew 12 percent. Asset gathering and lending growth have come apart. My inference is that if this persists, the fee growth that matters will rest on capital raised rather than capital lent.

On credit quality, Blue Owl cited a realized loss rate of 12 basis points a year. It also reported no meaningful change to its watchlist. Treat that cautiously. It's self-reported, and it excludes unrealized markdowns. Realized losses also lag by construction. They only arrive after amend-and-extend and payment-in-kind have run out. And it isn't a non-accrual rate. No non-accrual or PIK figure was disclosed in the deck or on the call.

Returns come in two flavors. Management cited a 9.3 percent gross return for direct lending over the last twelve months. The deck's net figure for the same period is 5.7 percent. That's a gap of more than three and a half points between the headline and what investors kept. Gross headlines, net reality. Keep both in view.

Now the other side of that ledger. Bloomberg reports that Ares is raising its next flagship direct-lending fund faster than its prior record vintage. It's raising across two vehicles. One is a closed-end fund, Fund IV. The other is called Core, the firm's first open-ended senior direct-lending fund for institutional investors. Separately, Ares Credit funds closed about $6.3 billion of direct-lending commitments across 55 transactions in the third quarter. Those are commitments, not funded or net deployment. But they show Ares is still writing middle-market deals.

Two inferences follow, and I'll label them as mine. First, more dry powder chasing a book that isn't growing on net means pressure on spreads. Or it means a push into larger, less diversified structures. Second, an open-ended institutional vehicle carries its own liquidity mismatch. It's the institutional version of the retail private-credit funds that have been limiting redemptions. The lesson sits right next to Blue Owl's print. Fundraising momentum is not deployed, performing capital.

So where does capital go when the core book won't absorb it? Part of the answer is the SpaceX deal this desk has been following. That's the roughly $40 billion GPU lease an Apollo-led syndicate committed to SpaceX. There's one new wrinkle, and it's the rent structure. Each quarterly payment includes one-twentieth of financed cost. On top of that come a fixed rate on the outstanding balance and expense pass-through. That means full principal payback over twenty quarterly payments, or five years. That's fast amortization. My read is that the syndicate underwrote SpaceX corporate credit with quick deleveraging. It is not mainly a bet on what Vera Rubin chips are worth in five years.

For scale, SpaceX spent $48.1 billion on capex over nine months. At that pace, the lease equals roughly seven to eight months of its spending. Two caveats remain. The commitment is binding but not closed, with closing expected before year-end. And the lease rate is still undisclosed, so nobody outside can judge the compensation. The residual-value risk looks contained by structure. The concentration risk does not. Apollo, BlackRock, Goldman's asset-management arm, and KKR-advised accounts all sit on one borrower.

Concentration is one place credit risk hides. Bank balance sheets are another. The Financial Times reported on October 19th that the Bank of England is examining Barclays. We hadn't covered it, and it belongs in this week's story. The question is "whether Barclays traders misrepresented illiquid private credit loans." The instruments are total-return swaps tied to those loans.

Barclays' own numbers frame the size. It reports about £15 billion of private-credit exposure, with the vast majority in the banking book. The swap book is far smaller. It carries a mark-to-market value of about £300 million on roughly £1.5 billion of underlying notional. That's a valuation, not a loss. This is an open inquiry prompted by an internal complaint, not a finding. Barclays says its treatment is consistent with accounting and capital rules. But if supervisors did find a problem, scrutiny of how banks support private credit would rise across the channel. The point for now is simpler. The marks question that has dogged BDCs all week now has a bank leg.

And what do actual losses show? Proskauer's private-credit default index eased to 2.41 percent in the third quarter. That's down from 2.51 percent in the second, a second straight quarterly decline. The index covers 746 loans. Underneath that improving headline, Proskauer says trailing-twelve-month defaults are rising. It also says defaults among software and technology borrowers are increasing. It doesn't publish those figures, so I can give you the direction only. That fits the redemption requests at Blue Owl's tech-focused fund earlier this week, another sign of software stress. And it's the independent check on Blue Owl's 12 basis points. A dipping quarterly print alongside a rising trailing rate means stress is still building on a twelve-month view. It is not proof the cycle has turned benign.

Pull it together. Where does the cycle stand? Realized losses are low and the headline default rate is easing. But the trend measures and the software book are moving the wrong way. That's late-cycle drift, not a turn. What's new is that the deployment gap has moved from industry aggregates into a top direct lender's own numbers. AUM and fundraising are at record pace, net deployment is negative, and credit fee-paying assets are flat. The biggest unresolved risk is what's left behind as the strongest credits repay and exit. And it's who is marking what remains. That's managers, as always, but now, as Barclays shows, sometimes banks too.

Watch three dates. Apollo reports on November 3rd. Blue Owl's own BDC reports on November 4th. KKR follows on November 9th. Look first at net deployment and non-accrual lines. Blue Owl gave us the first and withheld the second.

That's the briefing. Have a good weekend.
