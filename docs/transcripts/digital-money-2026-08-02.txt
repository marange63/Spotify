Good morning. It's Sunday, August 2nd, 2026, and this is your digital money briefing.

Start with what's not new. Bitcoin is sitting around $63,200, still roughly half its October 2025 all-time high of $126,198, and the spot ETFs flipped back to net selling on July 31st. We covered that flow reversal in detail yesterday, so we're not re-litigating it. The tape is unchanged. The real story today is underneath the price, in the plumbing.

For most of this week, the working thesis on this show has been that institutional dollars were migrating into the stablecoin float and into tokenized Treasuries — that the on-chain dollar system was quietly getting bigger no matter what Bitcoin itself did. New data from CoinGecko's second-quarter report complicates that. Total stablecoin market cap fell 1.6%, about $4.8 billion, to $305.1 billion in the second quarter. That's the sector's first quarterly contraction since the third quarter of 2023. So the plumbing didn't just stop growing — it got smaller. We said dollars were migrating into this system. The system just shrank.

And the contraction gathered pace as the quarter closed. The broader drawdown from May through mid-summer ran to roughly $10 billion, with June alone down $7.7 billion — the steepest single month since the Terra-Luna collapse in May 2022. Keep that comparison in proportion, though. This was about a 3% pullback, not the 26% implosion of 2022. What we're watching is capital reallocating, not a stablecoin run. But the direction is the point, and the direction reversed.

Look at the composition and the story sharpens. Circle's USDC posted the largest absolute outflow in the sector, down 4.8%, or $3.7 billion, to $73.5 billion. Meanwhile Tether's USDT barely moved, up 0.2% to $184.4 billion, and grew its market share to 60%. That's a problem for the narrative Wall Street has been selling around USDC — that the regulated, disclosed, bank-partnered stablecoin was the one built to win as institutional rails came online. Instead the more opaque, offshore incumbent gained ground while the poster child for regulated stablecoins bled the most dollars in absolute terms. Transparency and market share are moving in opposite directions, and that's worth sitting with before you accept the regulated-rails-are-winning pitch at face value.

And the bleed doesn't look like a one-quarter blip. Circle's own live stats page now shows USDC supply around $72 billion, a touch below that $73.5 billion quarter-close figure. The page isn't cleanly date-stamped, so treat it as direction rather than a precise new number. But the direction says the outflow kept going after the quarter ended, not that it reversed. If anything, the regulated winner is ceding a little more ground, not stabilizing.

Now, why did the float shrink where it did. The sharpest declines weren't in the payments-focused stablecoins at all — they were in the yield-bearing ones. Sky's USDS fell 16.4%. Ethena's USDe fell 24.4%. CoinGecko attributes both to yield compression: once these tokens' returns dropped below the risk-free rate available elsewhere on-chain, mainly in tokenized Treasuries, the capital left. That's consistent with the argument this show made earlier in the week about capital discriminating on whether a yield actually clears the risk-free rate. Here's the read: the money probably didn't leave crypto. It likely rotated out of stablecoins and into tokenized Treasury products paying a cleaner, more competitive rate. The float got smaller because a chunk of it graduated to something else on-chain, not because dollar demand for digital assets collapsed.

That has a second-order implication worth naming plainly, though not over-quantifying. Stablecoin issuers hold their reserves predominantly in short-dated Treasury bills and repo. A shrinking float is, directionally, a marginal negative for that specific channel of Treasury-bill demand — even if the dollars didn't leave the crypto ecosystem, they may have moved to a different reserve or custody structure with a different demand footprint for bills specifically. I want to flag that we don't have a reliable current, reconciled total for the stablecoin market as of today — one dataset shows $305 billion at quarter-close, another circulating figure puts it closer to $287 billion, and those numbers come from different methodologies and dates that don't cleanly reconcile. So treat the direction as real and the precise current size as unsettled. The broader point still holds: the stablecoins-are-a-structural-ever-growing-buyer-of-Treasury-bills bull case looks more rate-sensitive, and less permanent, than the pitch implies. The cohort that shrank fastest was the cohort chasing yield — which means a chunk of that float was always mercenary capital, not sticky infrastructure.

So if the float itself isn't the growth story right now, where is new revenue actually showing up. Coinbase's second-quarter numbers, which we covered on the headline loss and market-share record earlier this week, had one line we didn't get to: prediction-market contracts. That business more than doubled quarter over quarter, up 106%, and crossed a $100 million annualized run rate. That's a genuinely new, non-cyclical revenue line for the exchange, distinct from trading fees that rise and fall with Bitcoin's price. And it sits on top of an already-diversified base — subscription and services brought in $555 million last quarter, 48% of net revenue. The transaction-fee business is no longer the majority of what Coinbase earns, and prediction markets are the fast-growing edge of that shift.

Treat the $100 million figure carefully, though. It's an extrapolation off one quarter of rapid growth, not an audited annual number. And Coinbase's own reported transaction revenue through late July, only about $130 million, is soft enough that a single weak quarter could swamp the run rate. That fee softness isn't a Coinbase-specific problem either. Spot Bitcoin trading volume just hit its lowest level since 2019, per Glassnode, so the whole industry is running on thinner transaction economics. But directionally, prediction markets are the clearest example this week of activity building something adjacent to crypto trading rather than depending on it.

Last, the regulatory clock, because it frames everything above. The CLARITY Act, the market-structure bill that would formally divide SEC and CFTC jurisdiction over digital assets, picked up a wave of new establishment endorsements in late July — BlackRock, Fidelity, Franklin Templeton, Goldman Sachs, and SoFi all came out publicly in favor, with Goldman's CEO David Solomon saying he's very supportive of moving the CLARITY Act forward. And it still isn't moving. Senate Majority Leader John Thune says he doesn't expect a floor vote before the chamber leaves for its August recess, and the bill still needs at least seven Democratic votes it doesn't yet have. Broad Wall Street backing without a legislative result is itself the data point. The SEC's own administrative fallback, a package of rules dubbed Regulation Crypto, is still only targeting proposed rules this month, with final rules not expected before mid-2027 even in a clean scenario. So institutional demand for a rulebook is real and now loudly bipartisan among asset managers. The rulebook itself keeps slipping.

Put it together: the float shrank, the yield-chasing capital rotated toward the risk-free rate, the regulated stablecoin lost ground to the unregulated one, and the clearest new revenue growth showed up somewhere adjacent to trading altogether. Institutionalization in digital money is still happening. It's just moving slower, and through different channels, than the consensus story keeps assuming.

That's the briefing for August 2nd. Have a good Sunday.
