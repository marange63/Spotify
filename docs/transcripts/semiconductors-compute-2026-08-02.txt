Good morning, it's August 2nd, and here's your semiconductors and compute briefing.

This week the chip tape and the chip supply chain told two different stories. Equities spent late July pricing a peak-in-AI-capex scare. The supply chain, meanwhile, kept guiding shortage. Today's briefing is about the equipment layer settling that argument, with hard numbers instead of sentiment.

Start with Lam Research, because its fiscal fourth quarter, reported July 29th, is the cleanest primary-source data point in the sector right now. Lam posted record revenue of six point seven two billion dollars and record diluted earnings per share of one dollar eighty-two. Management then guided September-quarter revenue to eight point one billion dollars, plus or minus four hundred million, which implies more than twenty percent sequential growth in a single quarter. That's not a company coasting on backlog. Alongside that print, Lam raised its full-year 2026 forecast for wafer fab equipment spending, the industry's core measure of fab capacity investment, to the low one-hundred-fifty-billion-dollar range. That's up from a prior forecast of one hundred forty billion, and it's the second time Lam has raised this number within a year. So what does that mean. It means AI-driven capital spending is not just showing up in hyperscaler press releases and capex slide decks, it's showing up as booked equipment orders at the company that sells the tools fabs need to build capacity. That is the distinction the whole "is this cyclical or structural" debate hinges on, and Lam just answered it from the supply side.

The clearest piece of that raise is in advanced packaging specifically. Lam lifted its calendar-2026 advanced-packaging revenue growth forecast to more than seventy percent year over year, up from a prior estimate of just over fifty percent. Advanced packaging, the process that stacks memory and logic together for AI accelerators, has been the tightest structural bottleneck in this whole buildout, and it's the reason GPU shipments have been running behind order books all year. A packaging growth guide raised by twenty points in one earnings call is equipment-side evidence that gate is actually being funded, not just described in industry reports. That reading gets independent corroboration from TSMC's own advanced-packaging line, where capacity is on track to reach roughly one hundred seventy thousand wafers a month by 2027, according to supply-chain trackers. Put those two together and the packaging bottleneck looks like it's widening in real dollars, even if it isn't closed yet.

Because wafer fab equipment spending is a whole-market number, not a Lam-specific one, Lam's raise is also a forward signal for its peers. Applied Materials, KLA, and Tokyo Electron all sell into the same fab-buildout wave, so a rising industry-wide forecast should read through to their upcoming quarters too. That's an inference from Lam's disclosure, not a confirmed number from those companies themselves, so treat it as directional rather than booked. But the direction is the useful part. If the buildout is real, the cleaner way to own it may be the equipment cohort as a group, because those tool vendors capture the capacity spend no matter which GPU, which custom accelerator, or which memory maker ultimately wins the socket. They sit upstream of that fight. When the whole-market number rises, they all get paid.

Now turn to the thing this equipment data actually resolves: the whipsaw that ran through the last week of July. In the week of the 27th and 28th, twenty of the world's most valuable chip stocks lost a combined one point three trillion dollars in market value, according to CNBC's aggregated tracking. Nvidia alone was down two hundred thirty-eight billion dollars, SK hynix lost one hundred seventy-six billion, Samsung one hundred seventy-three billion, Micron one hundred thirteen billion. The trigger wasn't a single earnings miss. It was a fear that AI infrastructure spending is peaking faster than expected, after the sector had already rallied sharply, by the reporting's own framing roughly one hundred thirty percent, over the prior twelve months. Two days later, on July 30th, the sector reversed hard, in the same window Lam reported its results. Lam itself rose about seventeen and a half percent, Micron seventeen percent, AMD thirteen percent, lifting the broader semiconductor ETF roughly eight percent in a single session. I'd be careful not to draw a straight causal line from Lam's print to every stock in that rebound, since AMD's move that day looks more like sector sympathy than a reaction to its own numbers, which weren't confirmed as reported that session. But the direction of the fundamental data released that week, the equipment forecast raised, the memory shortage guided further out, runs opposite to a demand break. That's the more defensible read: the initial selloff looks like a repricing of an extended multiple, not evidence that the order book cracked.

The second-order risk here is worth naming plainly. If chip equities keep swinging this violently around every capex data point, while the order book itself keeps guiding shortage years out, the stocks start trading on sentiment about AI return on investment rather than on actual fab and foundry utilization. That's a decoupling investors should watch for, because it means the next scare, whatever triggers it, tells you less about real demand than the last one did.

One more callback on the demand side, briefly, because it was heavily covered last week and doesn't need re-litigating. Samsung and SK hynix both guided that the memory shortage, driven by AI server demand pulling capacity away from PCs and phones, persists through 2028, since new DRAM lines announced for 2027 won't add meaningful output until the following year. The update worth flagging today is that this shortage signal is no longer coming only from memory makers talking their own book, it's now corroborated from the equipment layer, through Lam's own capacity and packaging guidance. And this is where the two threads meet. High-bandwidth memory is a rising share of every AI accelerator's bill of materials, so memory prices that stay elevated for two more years flow straight into the cost of building AI compute. That squeezes hyperscaler margins on their own infrastructure, or it gets passed through as higher GPU-cloud pricing. And it lands hardest on PC and phone makers, who absorb memory inflation they can't fully push onto consumers. So the same shortage that keeps the equipment order book full is also a persistent cost tax on everything downstream of it.

Worth noting too that the rate of price increase is cooling even as the absolute shortage persists. TrendForce's own tracking shows conventional DRAM contract prices decelerating from roughly sixty percent quarter over quarter growth in the second quarter to something closer to the mid-teens in the third. Persistent scarcity, slowing rate of increase. Both things are true at once, and they are not a contradiction. Prices climbing more slowly from a very high base is exactly what you'd expect when buyers hit an affordability ceiling but supply still can't catch demand.

Pulling this together: the core question all week has been whether the market is extrapolating a cyclical AI-capex wobble into a structural downturn. The equipment data says no. Lam's twice-raised wafer fab equipment forecast, its outsized packaging guide, and the corroborating packaging capacity buildout at TSMC are hard evidence that the underlying demand signal remains intact, even as the equity risk premium attached to AI capex re-rates lower. The selloff was the market repricing how much it will pay for that growth. It was not the growth itself breaking.

That's the briefing for today. Have a good one.
