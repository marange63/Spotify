Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Credit & the Corporate Credit Cycle

Transcript · October 8, 2026

Good morning, it's Thursday, October 8th.

Today's thread is one firm standing on both sides of the valuation problem. Apollo is offering investors more frequent marks on its loans. In the same week, it is in talks to hold some of the hardest-to-mark exposures private credit has yet been asked to take on.

Start with the lead. The Wall Street Journal reported yesterday that Broadcom has spent recent weeks arranging more than fifty billion dollars in financing. The money is for the custom AI chip Broadcom is developing with OpenAI. Apollo Global Management and Blackstone are among the lenders in early discussions. Per the reporting, the facility would fund several gigawatts of OpenAI's computing capacity, and the target is to close by the end of this year. Separately, Oracle is in talks with Apollo and Goldman Sachs to fund a large chip purchase of its own. The likely structure is a separate entity that buys the chips and leases them to Oracle. Nobody has put a dollar figure on the Oracle piece, and the reporting says plainly that the number of chips involved is unclear.

Treat all of this as early talks, not deals. Nothing is signed, and no lender has confirmed a commitment. Every detail traces back to a single Journal story, relayed secondhand. And crucially, no outlet says who actually borrows the fifty billion, or what legally secures it. Broadcom is described as arranging the money, not as owing it. That omission is the point to bank, not a gap to paper over.

For scale, Goldman Sachs puts AI-related issuance by low-rated firms at eighty-eight billion dollars so far this year. That figure comes from a Reuters analysis at the end of September. It counts a different universe, low-rated issuers rather than one private facility, so treat the comparison as rough. But a single fifty-billion-dollar program would be more than half the size of that entire year's tally.

Here's why that matters beyond the headline. Carlyle's credit team published a white paper on October 1st. It warned that AI lending is becoming a correlated bet, with most of the financing concentrated in a handful of counterparties. A fifty-billion-dollar program tied to one chip and one buyer is that concentration made concrete. No direct-lending fund diversifies around a counterparty that size.

Oracle is a repeat character in this story. Last week it was the tenant that served a force-majeure notice on Blue Owl's Project Jupiter data center in New Mexico. This week it is going to Apollo and Goldman as a would-be borrower. The same name is showing up as a stressed counterparty and as a fresh credit ask within days.

So whose credit is this, really? The obligor could be Broadcom, a special-purpose vehicle, or OpenAI itself. Whoever signs the note, the cash that services it runs back to OpenAI's ability to keep paying for compute. If these deals close at anything like the reported size, the end holders would likely include the insurance and wealth-channel capital that funds these platforms. The so-what is this. Private credit is being asked to become the balance sheet behind one counterparty's compute bill. And its lenders can't yet say publicly what stands behind their claim if that bill goes unpaid.

And the firm at the center of those talks just changed how it tells investors what its loans are worth. Apollo announced on October 1st that it has extended daily pricing across its entire eight-hundred-fifty-billion-dollar credit platform. It first launched daily pricing for its investment-grade fixed income replacement products on July 1st. It has now pushed it out to direct lending, asset-backed finance, multi-credit, and opportunistic credit vehicles. Pricing on individual assets is promised starting October 30th.

The caveat is the whole story. Apollo describes these prices as an estimated fair value, calculated with its own internal methodology and benchmarked against public market data. It says explicitly that this is not a market-clearing price. No one has yet checked these marks against realized secondary trades in public. Now set that against the regulatory backdrop. In late September, SEC staff told funds to check their private-credit marks against market evidence. And Semafor reported this week that Fed examiners have been reviewing the banks that lend to these funds since the spring. The industry's answer is more frequency, not more price discovery.

There are two honest readings. The bull case is that daily marks benchmarked to public data narrow the lag between private and public credit. They give retail holders something to watch between quarterly tenders. The bear case is that they hand holders a daily trigger to get nervous, while exits stay quarterly and capped. A weakening mark becomes visible long before anyone can act on it. That can build a redemption queue rather than calm one. And once the largest credit platform marks daily, the other big BDC sponsors will be pressed to match, which spreads that same trigger across the sector.

The deeper problem is where the marks are weakest. The asset-level prices due October 30th are where it matters most, because individual direct loans are where a public benchmark is thinnest. Those are the same kind of exposures Apollo is now discussing with Broadcom and Oracle. They would be multi-billion, single-counterparty, and without a secondary market to check a model against. The takeaway is simple. Raising the frequency of a guess doesn't make it less of a guess.

Untested marks matter most where realized losses are already deeper than the headline suggests. Fitch reported in April that senior creditors of companies exiting bankruptcy in 2025 got the lowest average recoveries in ten years. Fitch tied that directly to liability management exercises.

That bears on the number that matters most after a liability management exercise. It's what holders actually recover, not how many defaults get counted. And the answer points the wrong way. Two limits apply. The data covers last year's emergences, so it is months old. And it measures rated, syndicated bankruptcies, not direct-lending books. The honest read is narrower. Loss-given-default is worsening in the part of the market we can measure. Private recovery assumptions have no equivalent public print. And those assumptions are precisely what daily marks won't validate.

The forward indicator sits in payment-in-kind income. The Financial Stability Board's May report on private credit cites a study on PIK toggles. It found that using one is associated with a one-to-two percentage point rise in the likelihood a loan goes delinquent the following quarter. That's one cited study, not a regulatory finding. But it makes PIK share of BDC income a leading signal, with non-accruals the lagging one.

Blue Owl gives that signal a date. Alongside its third-quarter tender disclosures last week, Blue Owl flagged that refinancing risk could become more important as loans mature, particularly around 2028. That bears most on Blue Owl Technology Income Corp, known as OTIC, because its book is concentrated in software lending. OTIC's holders asked to redeem thirty-nine percent of its shares this quarter. But the 2028 timing is the manager's own, and it conveniently puts the risk two years out. The alternative reading is that refinancing pressure on software loans arrives sooner. It would surface first as amend-and-extend and PIK, well before any maturity date. Put the pieces together, and the read is this. Credit erosion shows up in recovery value and PIK mechanics before it ever shows up in a default count.

So where does the cycle actually stand? Realized credit is a slow grind. The one hard data point we have says severity is worsening. There's no fresh read on default frequency this week, and private recoveries have no public print at all. And all of it sits underneath marks that no one outside the manager can test. The biggest unresolved risk is concentration meeting illiquidity. The newest, largest, least diversifiable exposures, AI compute at hyperscaler scale, could land in vehicles funded partly by capital that can only leave quarterly, under a cap.

Watch four things. First, whether the Broadcom and OpenAI facility actually signs by year-end, and who turns out to be the borrower. Second, what Apollo's asset-level marks look like when they arrive on October 30th. Third, the alternative managers' third-quarter earnings, the first chance to press Apollo and Blackstone on these talks on the record. Fourth, November's third-quarter BDC filings, where PIK share and non-accruals get their first update since the second quarter.

That's the state of the lending machine. Talk tomorrow.
