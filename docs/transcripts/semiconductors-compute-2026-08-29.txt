Good morning. It's Saturday, August 29th, and this is your semiconductor and compute briefing.

For most of this week, this show has argued that the demand question in AI infrastructure is settled and the value-capture question is not. This week's news is Nvidia trying to answer that second question by buying time rather than by winning an argument. And one disclosure from the earnings call changes how we should read the move.

Start with the number that is genuinely new. Alongside its second-quarter results, Nvidia gave a fiscal-2028 revenue growth outlook of roughly 70 percent. Analysts, per CNBC's tally, had penciled in about 44 percent. The trajectory context is straightforward: the quarter itself came in at $96.2 billion, up 106 percent from a year ago, and the October-quarter guide of $108 billion cleared the roughly $104 to $105 billion the Street expected. Those two prints are last week's story. The out-year guide is not.

It is not, because Nvidia does not normally guide a fiscal year that has not started. So the first question is what class of statement this actually is. It is not a reported result. It is not a booked backlog. And note the scoping: management again held China data-center compute at zero in the outlook, so this is a growth claim on US and allied buyers alone. Worth holding in mind, too, that a 26-point gap to consensus does double duty. It may reveal information analysts do not have. It may also be management setting the anchor for this fall's hyperscaler capex debate before anyone else gets to.

Here is the part that matters more than the headline. On the call, Nvidia's own management characterized the 70 percent as a supply-constrained outlook, not a demand-constrained one. Their words: demand is much greater than 70 percent, and supply is what lets them confidently deliver that number. Unconstrained, they said, it would be a lot higher. That reframes the entire guide. It is not a claim about how many customers have signed up. It is a claim about how many systems Nvidia believes it can physically build.

That is a different kind of forecast, and in one sense a sturdier one. It does not depend on customers exercising options. But it is more fragile in another sense. It now rests entirely on Nvidia's own component supply chain executing, and that chain runs straight through memory, where capacity is reported sold out across all three major suppliers through 2026.

The clearest evidence of that dependency is a figure disclosed in the same report. Nvidia's supply commitments — the purchases it has promised its own suppliers — rose to $279 billion, from $119 billion the quarter before. The company said the increase was primarily related to procuring memory. Nvidia did not present that figure as the basis for the 70 percent guide, and we should not either. But the adjacency is the point. The company that just told Wall Street to expect 70 percent growth also more than doubled its forward purchase obligations in a single quarter to lock up memory. So the swing factor for whether Nvidia hits fiscal 2028 is no longer hyperscaler budgets. It is whether SK Hynix, Samsung, Micron and TSMC's advanced packaging lines can deliver at that rate without a price spike that eats Nvidia's own margin.

There is a second-order risk in that for everyone downstream. If a 70 percent growth outlook becomes the industry's planning anchor, then hyperscaler capex guidance this autumn, HBM supply contracts and CoWoS reservation decisions all get set against a number no filing yet underwrites. That is an anchoring exposure running through the whole supply chain, not just through one stock.

An out-year guide is only as good as the customers willing to date themselves to it, and one of them just did. AWS and Nvidia announced an expansion covering 2 million additional GPUs, spanning Blackwell Ultra, Rubin and Rubin Ultra, to be deployed across AWS infrastructure in 2027 and 2028. That takes Nvidia's total footprint introduced at AWS this year above 3 million by the companies' own framing. The announcement also earmarks 100,000 GPUs for US government and national-security workloads, and folds in Nvidia's new Vera CPU.

Two things are worth separating. First, no dollar value was disclosed anywhere. This is a unit and capacity commitment, not revenue booked, and a unit commitment on future silicon is the cheapest kind of promise to make. There is no disclosed penalty structure, so ask what happens to it if AWS's own AI revenue disappoints in 2027. Second, on whether Rubin is real or roadmap, Nvidia's call disclosed that production shipments of Vera Rubin commenced earlier in August, with purchase orders already in hand from every major hyperscaler, AI cloud and system OEM. Management called it the fastest product ramp in the company's history. That is a shipment claim, not a slide. It does not extend to Rubin Ultra, which the AWS deal also names; that variant's production status is undisclosed. So AWS has dated itself to hardware that is partly shipping and partly still ahead of Nvidia's own stated production timeline.

The Vera CPU attach is the more interesting competitive detail. Nvidia is selling rack-scale compute into the one hyperscaler with the most mature in-house Arm server program. That reads as a concession about integration convenience, not about CPU performance. But if vendor rack-scale integration keeps winning on convenience, the cost case for custom CPUs gets weaker everywhere, not just at AWS.

Dating the hardware is one way to buy duration. Owning the layer above it is the other, and that is what is reportedly happening with Hugging Face. Multiple outlets report Nvidia is close to a $12.9 billion acquisition of the open-source model hub. That is nearly double the roughly $7 billion figure that anchored talks late last year. Neither company has confirmed a signed agreement, so treat it as reported, not done.

The price move is the analytically useful part. A company earning at Nvidia's current run rate, paying almost double its own prior negotiating anchor for a model-distribution layer, is not expressing confidence in CUDA alone as a durable moat. It reads more like a company that suspects the next chokepoint is migrating up the stack, above the silicon, into where models actually get distributed and deployed. That is a bearish inference wearing a bullish headline. And if it closes, expect antitrust questions, given how much AI compute and now model distribution would sit under one roof.

The same forward-dating shows up on the supply side, in concrete rather than in contracts. SK Hynix is reported to have spent more than a month studying potential US and other sites for a front-end memory fab, and separately to be moving into co-packaged optics alongside HBM. Nothing is committed: no site, no timeline, no capital figure. What is notable is the driver. This is described as American customers pressing for wafer production closer to home, not as a response to a subsidy program. That is a different reshoring mechanism than the policy-driven one this show usually tracks. It is supply-security pricing, and it changes who ends up paying for the duplicated capacity.

So what would tell us this read is wrong? The old test was whether any disclosed purchase obligation sat behind the guide, and the $279 billion figure partly answers that — though Nvidia never linked the two. The better test now is upstream. Watch memory and packaging output, and watch HBM pricing, because on management's own framing that is the binding constraint. If supply loosens and the guide does not rise, the supply-constrained story was cover for something else.

What today's read rests on is worth stating plainly. A growth outlook nobody has seen justified in a contract. A unit commitment on next-generation silicon with no disclosed price. And an acquisition nobody has signed. The Vera Rubin shipment and the $279 billion of supply commitments are real, disclosed facts. The bigger numbers driving this week's narrative are still promises about a year that has not started.

That's the briefing for August 29th. Have a good weekend.
