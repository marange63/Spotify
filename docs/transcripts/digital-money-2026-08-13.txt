Good morning. It's Thursday, August 13th, and this is your digital money briefing.

Start with where things stood coming into this week. US spot Bitcoin ETFs pulled in eight hundred fifty-three point five million dollars over five straight inflow sessions from August 3rd through the 7th, already nearly five times July's full-month total of one hundred seventy-two point four million. BlackRock's IBIT captured about eighty-one percent of that category-wide inflow. And yet Bitcoin itself hasn't broken out. It's been parked near sixty-four to sixty-five thousand dollars, inside a sixty-two to sixty-six thousand dollar band it's held for weeks. Yesterday's July CPI print came in exactly in line with forecast, headline at three point four percent year-on-year, core at two point five, both a touch softer than June. Bitcoin's reaction was almost nothing: it sat near sixty-four thousand one hundred into the release and stayed largely unchanged after it. The coin remains down about twenty-seven percent for the year and nearly forty-nine percent below its October twenty twenty-five all-time high of one hundred twenty-six thousand eighty dollars. So the puzzle we've been sitting with all week stands: real money is still coming in the front door of these funds, and the price isn't moving. What's absorbing that bid?

On August 7th we got what looked like an answer. The gross annualized basis on CME Bitcoin futures jumped to seven point eight nine percent for the August contract, six point two five for September, five point six nine for December — all above that day's two-year Treasury yield of four point one nine percent. That ended a hundred fifty-seven consecutive days in which the crypto cash-and-carry spread had trailed the Treasury benchmark, the longest such stretch on record outside the post-FTX period. A richer basis makes cash-and-carry attractive again: buy the ETF, short the future, pocket the spread. That's a mechanism that could explain flows without price follow-through — delta-neutral arbitrage rather than a directional bet.

But here's the part that matters more, and it didn't hold. By August 8th, just a day later, the three-month annualized basis had already compressed back down to around two percent, below the two-year Treasury yield again. One tracked reading put it at two point zero eight percent against the four point one nine percent yield; another, cited separately for the same window, put the basis near three percent against a three point eight percent Treasury yield. The two readings don't reconcile to a single number, but they agree on the direction: whatever richening opened up on the 7th was gone within a day or two, not a new regime. For context, that basis had run twenty percent during the twenty twenty-one bull market and three point eight percent as recently as this past February — so two percent is thin.

And the more interesting fact isn't just that the basis snapped back. It's what hedge funds reportedly did next. Multiple outlets report that CME hedge funds flipped net long on Bitcoin futures in the days after August 7th — a rare reversal after years of structurally net-short cash-and-carry positioning. The reporting frames this as funds abandoning the short leg of the arbitrage, because a compressed basis no longer clears financing, margin, and execution costs, and instead taking outright directional long exposure. That's the opposite of what the setup seemed to promise. A richer carry was supposed to pull more arbitrage capital in. Instead, it looks like funds tested the trade, watched the edge evaporate in a day, and unwound the short leg rather than expand it.

So the honest read is this: the basis flip was real but short-lived, and it doesn't confirm that the ETF flows are hedged arbitrage. If anything, the hedge-fund positioning data points the other way, toward more directional conviction building in the futures market, not less. What it doesn't do is resolve the underlying puzzle. There's still no trader-level data linking ETF creations to futures shorts, and no source could produce a verified figure for whether futures open interest actually rose alongside the ETF inflow streak, or what the arbitrage nets out to after costs. Treat any claim that a specific share of these inflows is arbitrage as unprovable, in either direction. What changed this week is the confidence level, not the mystery. Flow, right now, is a bad standalone signal for where price goes next.

That same gap between headline growth and underlying economics shows up one layer down, in the stablecoin plumbing. Tether posted one point five billion dollars in net operating profit for the second quarter, a genuinely strong number. But its excess reserve buffer — the equity cushion sitting above what's needed to fully back USDT — fell by half, from just over eight point two three billion dollars three months earlier to four point one one billion. USDT issuance kept growing anyway, up about four hundred forty-six million dollars to a hundred eighty-four point six billion outstanding. Where that roughly four billion dollars of reduced buffer went isn't disclosed in Tether's reporting. So you've got record profit, growing supply, and a shrinking shock absorber, all at once. A thinner buffer doesn't threaten day-to-day operations, but it does mean less capacity to absorb a redemption run, exactly as the dollar-stablecoin footprint in Treasury and repo markets keeps getting larger. Growth in the headline number is not the same thing as growth in the thing that actually protects holders.

Now to the rulebook, where the same theme plays out again: Congress stalling, agencies filling the gap. The Senate adjourned for its August recess without voting on the Digital Asset Market Clarity Act. Majority Leader Thune filed a cloture motion on August 8th, but unresolved disputes over ethics provisions and stablecoin yield rules kept it from advancing. The bill needs sixty votes, including at least ten Democrats. The Senate returns September 14th, with the next procedural vote — a motion to proceed, not final passage — expected around September 15th. Meanwhile the agencies aren't waiting. CFTC Chair Michael Selig said on August 4th that the commission has crypto rule proposals ready and plans to finalize them before this administration ends, regardless of what Congress does. And on August 11th, the CFTC invoked emergency authority to keep Kalshi operating after New York's Attorney General filed a thirty-six billion dollar lawsuit alleging its prediction-market contracts amount to illegal gambling under state law. That's a live federal-versus-state jurisdictional fight with no resolution in sight. The pattern across both: statutory clarity keeps slipping, and whatever certainty exists is being built through agency rulemaking instead — which is inherently more fragile, reversible by a future administration or a court, in a way statute isn't.

Put it together and the thread running through today is the same one from the futures curve to the stablecoin balance sheet to the rulebook: headline growth everywhere, and the underlying support for it thinning, stalling, or reversing underneath. The ETF flows keep coming but the mechanism we thought explained them didn't survive forty-eight hours. Tether's profit is real but its cushion is half what it was. And the legislative path to real market-structure certainty keeps sliding a month at a time while agencies improvise around it. None of that is a crisis signal. It's a reminder to discount the headline number until you've checked what's holding it up.
