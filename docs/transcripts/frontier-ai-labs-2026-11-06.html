Good morning, it's Friday, November 6th, and this is your frontier AI labs briefing.

A standing disclosure before we start. Anthropic makes the model behind this show. Anthropic's own filing is a major part of today's briefing, and a benchmark we cite is led by an Anthropic model. Weigh what follows with that in mind.

Here's the thesis. Thursday's selloff hit the one place the market can price AI commitment risk today, and that's the chip suppliers. The labs carrying the biggest commitments are either private or not yet listed. And at the very top of the capability stack, what the leading labs now sell looks less like raw capability and more like permission to use it without the brakes on.

Start with Thursday. Nvidia closed down more than nine percent, erasing almost $450 billion of market value in a single session. The Nasdaq had its worst day since April of 2025. CNBC's reporting ties the move to midterm results that raised concerns on Wall Street that the AI buildout is poised to slow. That's market commentary, not a company disclosure.

This is the next rung on an escalation we've traced all week. Financing has been getting tighter, with a smaller OpenAI round, Oracle issuing stock and bonds, and Meta paying up on long-dated debt. Then, on election day, neocloud stocks fell as voters restricted data centers in nearly every ballot contest. Now the squeeze has reached the labs' anchor chip supplier, just ahead of Anthropic's pending listing.

There are two ways to read the tape. One is a durable re-rating of AI infrastructure risk, on political and financing grounds. The other is a one-day shock in a crowded trade. On election day itself, there was no way to separate political risk from profit-taking, and that's still true. There's also a twist for the labs themselves. If the buildout genuinely slows, capacity that's already running gets scarcer, which could support pricing for labs holding locked-in contracts. And nothing in Thursday's move shows any lab walking back a commitment. What would separate the two readings is Nvidia's next earnings report, later this month, and whether any lab revises a commitment. So the takeaway is this. Capital just got more expensive for everyone in this race, but the first bill landed on the supplier, not the labs.

So where does that commitment risk actually sit? Anthropic's S-1 gives us the clearest counterparty map any frontier lab has disclosed. CNBC's reading of the original filing puts Anthropic's future compute obligations at roughly $417 billion.

One of those counterparties is SpaceX, which has owned xAI since February. SpaceX rents Anthropic compute on Nvidia GPUs that SpaceX itself owns. On October 27th, we flagged that the filing's 90-day cancellation clause sat in a general passage that didn't name SpaceX. Reporting on SpaceX's own filing now ties it to this deal. The noncancelable periods end in January and March of 2027. After that, either company can cancel on 90 days' notice.

Here's why that matters more than it sounds. SpaceX's filing lists an unnamed "Customer B" at 34.5 percent of its third-quarter revenue, and Bloomberg, citing a person familiar with the matter, reports that Customer B is Anthropic. That means more than a third of SpaceX's revenue comes from a lab that competes directly with SpaceX's own xAI. When SpaceX reported earnings this week, its release named no customers for its Super Intelligence segment, which houses this compute business. Bloomberg's identification is the first answer to who sits behind it.

So the 90-day option cuts both ways. A slowdown lets Anthropic shed capacity it no longer needs. But it also lets xAI's parent pull those GPUs back for xAI. A rival's owner holds half of that option. And for SpaceX shareholders, a third of revenue riding on a contract either side can exit in 2027 is concentration risk in plain sight.

Now back to Anthropic's own totals, because the amended filing changes the scale. Per a FilingInsights summary, the November 2nd amendment puts Anthropic's aggregate committed spend at roughly $895 billion, more than twice that earlier $417 billion reading, and substantially all of it for compute and infrastructure. About half falls before the end of 2030. We can't yet tell whether the jump reflects new deals or a reclassification. So treat it as the new headline total, not proof of new contracts.

Set that against the price tag, which is a different kind of number. Anthropic's last private round, in May, valued the company at $965 billion. That mark is now the reference point any public listing gets judged against, in a tape that just repriced AI infrastructure lower. Our inference is that getting public buyers to pay above it just became a harder ask.

Now, if capital markets want proof that this spending buys something durable, look at what the labs sell at the top of the stack. On October 29th, after Google gated Gemini 4 Carbon to cyber defenders, we asked whether OpenAI would answer with cyber claims of its own. It now has. On November 5th, OpenAI published a system card for GPT-6 Cyber, a cybersecurity-tuned variant of GPT-6 Astra for authorized vulnerability discovery and exploit work. It's rated "Critical in Cybersecurity," the same tier as Astra itself. Access is limited to vetted users in OpenAI's Daybreak Red program.

One correction to how this has been described elsewhere. It is not OpenAI's first access-gated model. The system card itself says, quote, "like past models, GPT-6 Cyber is available only to approved Daybreak Red users and organizations." And the measured gains are narrow. On SEC-Bench Pro, a vulnerability-discovery benchmark, GPT-6 Cyber scores 87.02 percent against Astra's 85.38, a gain of under two points. Its perfect 100 percent on ExploitBench carries OpenAI's own contamination caveat. The model may simply have seen those historical vulnerabilities in training. All of these numbers are OpenAI's own, and none has been independently replicated. So the Critical-tier capability already lived inside the general model. What OpenAI is productizing is the vetting layer around it.

That fits a finding from SemiAnalysis's GDPx benchmark this week. SemiAnalysis says Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra have, in its word, "sandbagged" overall scores because of their own safety classifiers. That's the firm's assertion, not a measured refusal rate. But put it next to Daybreak Red, and the product comes into focus. It's vetted access to the model, with the classifier tax removed. There's a fair competing reading, that gating is simply responsible deployment of a dangerous capability. Both can be true at once.

Either way, the moat in cyber is shifting toward trust infrastructure. We've seen this procurement logic before. Sweden's Government Offices kept Microsoft's Copilot approved because its security agreement was already signed, while ChatGPT and Claude were blocked. OpenAI is now building that kind of paperwork for itself. Our own inference goes one step further. A Critical-rated offensive capability inside a commercial product line invites customer-vetting regimes that look like export controls. That favors whichever labs already run government-grade vetting.

One more front where labs compete outside the model itself is developer tooling. On November 4th, the same day OpenAI announced it was buying Pierre Computer for Codex, Meta confirmed it had acquired Conductor. Conductor's app lets developers run multiple AI coding agents in parallel. Its CEO, Charlie Holtz, will work with Nat Friedman, head of product at Meta Superintelligence Labs. No terms were disclosed, and Conductor's cloud product shuts down November 20th. Killing a product within about two weeks reads more like an acqui-hire than a platform buy. So two labs, on one day, bought their way into the coding surface rather than winning it on the model.

Pulling the dimensions apart. Capital access tightened for everyone, with Anthropic most exposed on timing, given its pending listing. On compute, suppliers carry the near-term mark, and the option to walk away cuts both ways. Safety posture is becoming a distribution asset, not just a constraint. Developer tooling is being bought rather than built. On raw capability, there's no new independent read since GDPx. And from the Chinese labs and xAI, nothing verified surfaced in this window.

Three things to watch. First, Nvidia's next earnings report, later this month. Second, whether Anthropic's next filing sets a price range or restates its commitments. Third, whether Anthropic answers with a vetted cyber variant of its own, and whether Google widens Carbon beyond cyber defenders.
