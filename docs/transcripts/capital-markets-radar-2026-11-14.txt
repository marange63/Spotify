Good morning, it's Saturday, November 14th, and this is your Capital Markets Radar.

Here's the thesis for today. Friday's producer-price report didn't start this bond selloff. It ratified one already under way. A December hike is now fully priced, and the ten-year Treasury yield is through five percent. The bill for that repricing is landing first on the assets that pay you nothing to hold them, Bitcoin and gold. It's also landing on Nvidia, the one mega-cap now carrying a governance discount. Meanwhile, the S&P 500 just posted its best week since August. That gap is the story.

Start with the print, because the word "hot" needs an asterisk. October producer prices rose 0.3 percent on the month. That actually missed the 0.4 percent economists expected, per a Dow Jones poll. The hawkish case rests on two other numbers. Core PPI, which strips out food, energy, and trade services, rose 0.5 percent. And headline producer prices are up 6.5 percent from a year ago, the largest annual increase since November 2022.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics' own breakdown explains the gap between that soft monthly headline and the firm core. Energy prices edged lower in October, and trade margins fell. Those are exactly the pieces core strips out, and both dragged the headline down. Underneath, goods prices rose 0.5 percent, on top of a 2.5 percent jump in September. Services rose for a fifth straight month. So the headline miss came from the volatile parts. The pressure in goods and services is the part that tends to persist. Chris Rupkey, chief economist at FWDBONDS, said there was "no silver lining" in the report. He also argued that producer prices lead consumer prices, so households may face a new burst of factory inflation. The composition supports him more than the headline miss undercuts him. The bond market sold on the right numbers, not on the headline.

Now the attribution, which matters more than the print. On PPI day itself, the ten-year yield rose only about four basis points, per CNBC. The real move came on Thursday, before the data. That day the ten-year jumped more than thirteen basis points to above five percent. The two-year jumped more than fifteen.

So what triggered Thursday? The obvious suspect is Treasury's $25 billion thirty-year auction that same day. Demand there was softer. The bid-to-cover ratio fell to 2.36, from 2.54 at October's reopening, and landed below its recent average. But softer is not failed. The auction coverage doesn't tie Thursday's jump to the sale. No Fed speaker or data release has been identified as the trigger either. So PPI confirmed a move whose cause isn't established yet. Don't hand PPI the credit for the whole week.

The shape of the move matters for anyone holding duration. On CPI day, this desk called the selloff a bear flattener. The two-year rose about half again as much as the ten-year, which read as a policy story rather than an inflation story. Thursday broke that pattern, because the front end and the ten-year moved close to parallel. Over the full week, per Investrade, the two-year climbed about 35 basis points and the ten-year about 29. The ten-year finished the week at 5.15 percent. The curve still flattened slightly on the week, but the long end is no longer sitting this out. Duration holders who expected the front end to absorb the hike are now paying part of the bill themselves.

That brings us to the Fed. CME's FedWatch tool, via CNBC, now prices a hike at the December 9th meeting as a certainty. Roughly one-in-seven odds sit on a half point rather than a quarter. Track the arc. After the November 5th rout, hike odds sat near seventy percent. After CPI, a quarter point was fully priced. Now it's locked in.

Yet the half-point tail has barely grown. That's notable, because Fed Governor Christopher Waller said on the record that a half point should be considered. The market is pricing standard tightening, not a credibility-restoring shock. That leaves risk on both sides. If Waller carries the room and the Fed goes fifty, the front end is under-priced. If the Fed delivers a hawkish quarter point and nothing more, the long end may already have overshot.

Now the calendar into that meeting. A PCE report is due November 25th, and November payrolls land December 4th. Both arrive before the decision. November CPI doesn't land until December 10th, the day after the meeting. So the committee decides without a fresh CPI print in hand. That gives the pipeline signal in Friday's producer prices more weight into December than usual.

So who pays for a ten-year above five percent? Start with the assets that pay you nothing to hold them. Bitcoin traded around $69,100 at 8:30 Friday morning, Eastern time, per Fortune. That's down about five and a half percent from the prior morning. It extends a slide this desk has tracked all week, from about $74,200 on Monday.

Two readings compete for that move. One is the discount rate. When cash pays more, a zero-yield asset costs more to hold. The other is plumbing. Leverage unwinds and ETF outflows have been pressing on Bitcoin all week. Either way, the "digital gold" decoupling thesis is failing in this drawdown, not confirming it. In an acute rates shock, Bitcoin is trading as a risk asset, not as a hedge.

Gold tells the same story. Kitco had spot gold near $3,885 an ounce at midday Friday, close to a thirteen-month low. The dollar took the other side. The Dollar Index finished Friday at 103.38, per Investrade, capping its best week in two months. When yields rise on inflation fear rather than fall on growth fear, both zero-yield hedges lose their usual job at once. Bottom line, in this tape the dollar is doing the defensive work that gold and Bitcoin are supposed to do.

The same discount rate now meets the market's most important single stock. On Thursday, Jensen Huang resigned as Nvidia's CEO. The resignation followed reporting of a relationship with an alleged Chinese intelligence asset. The stock fell more than 4 percent that day and another 2 percent on Friday, per CNBC. Nvidia's market value is now below $4 trillion for the first time since July of last year.

The follow-through came from the Street and from Washington. HSBC downgraded Nvidia to hold and cut its price target to $170, from $365. That's one broker's call, but it cuts the target by more than half. Meanwhile, three House committees have summoned Huang to a closed-door interview on December 1st. It's unclear whether he'll attend. A shareholder class action has also been filed.

Now revisit yesterday's read honestly. In Friday's briefing, this desk said the broad index barely blinked at the resignation. We called it a key-person discount, not a verdict on AI demand. That read holds for the index. It doesn't hold for the stock. Nvidia's discount is compounding, not fading. The split of blame is contested. Some put the drawdown on the CEO exit, others on AI-capex worry and profit-taking. But the timing is unkind either way. A five percent ten-year raises the hurdle on every long-duration AI multiple. Nvidia meets that hurdle on November 17th, when interim CEO Colette Kress, who remains CFO, gives guidance in that role for the first time. Nvidia's multiple is being reset by rates and governance at once. Tuesday's call is the first chance to separate the two.

And yet the index had its best week since August. The S&P 500 rose 2.5 percent, the Nasdaq 2.3, and the Dow 2.1, per CNBC. Look at where that gain came from. As we reported on Tuesday, Monday's session alone did almost all of the S&P's lifting. That rally ran on falling oil and on positioning, not on AI. It means the other four sessions roughly netted to flat. Those four sessions held CPI, a five percent ten-year, PPI, and Nvidia's CEO exit. So the best week since August is really one day of oil relief, followed by an index treading water through a rate shock.

That leaves the over-read versus under-read question for equities. Either the index is correctly treating a telegraphed quarter-point hike as a contained event. Or it is the last asset in this market still pricing the old discount rate. Our lean is that the burden of proof sits with the index. Bonds, Bitcoin, gold, and Nvidia have all repriced this week. The index hasn't, and one session of oil relief is thin evidence that it doesn't need to.

Three things to watch. First, Nvidia's results and call on November 17th, Kress's first as interim CEO. Second, the December 1st closed-door House interview, and whether Huang shows up. Third, the December 9th FOMC decision, and whether that half-point tail grows as the PCE and payrolls data arrive.

That's the Radar for November 14th. Trade carefully out there.
