Good morning. It's Thursday, August 27th, and this is your briefing on the space and orbital economy.

Start with the auction that tells you where the real scarcity in this sector sits right now. SpaceX and AST SpaceMobile are both chasing roughly six billion dollars of 800 megahertz spectrum. The licenses are held by Grain Management, freed up by a T-Mobile spectrum swap. Bloomberg reported last week that preliminary offers are due in the first week of September, and that the FCC has set November 5th as the deadline for the bidding process to be completed. Be precise about what that is. It is reported bidding interest, not a filed transaction. No winner exists and no price has been agreed. But hold the thesis for the rest of the briefing. The scarce asset here is not a satellite. It's a license. A satellite is financeable, replaceable, improvable. An 800 megahertz license is none of those things, and there is exactly one set of them for sale on this clock.

Ground that in AST SpaceMobile's actual position. AST reported second-quarter revenue of 31.5 million dollars against a consensus estimate of 34.4 million. That is a miss versus expectations, not a decline, and the distinction is worth keeping straight. Remember Monday's briefing, where we finally put a dollar figure on launch unreliability. AST took a 125.9 million dollar charge on a satellite that a New Glenn upper stage left in too low an orbit to sustain operations. So this is a company that has been executing on the physical layer, however imperfectly. It builds satellites, it launches them, and it absorbs the loss when somebody else's rocket misses. And it now has to win a multi-billion-dollar licensing fight against the best-capitalized company in the sector.

Now the balance sheet, because that is what a bidding contest actually tests. AST disclosed pro forma cash, cash equivalents and restricted cash of over 3.7 billion dollars after closing a 1.15 billion dollar convertible notes offering in July. That is a real war chest by the standards of any listed space name. It is also small beside a counterparty whose June initial public offering alone raised roughly seventy-five billion dollars in primary proceeds. Here is the mechanism that matters. SpaceX does not need to win this auction to hurt AST. It only needs to bid hard enough to lift the clearing price to a level AST would have to raise fresh capital to match. Winning at a punitive price and losing after diluting shareholders are both bad outcomes. So what: the binding constraint in direct-to-cell has moved from satellite count to spectrum access, and AST is now negotiating from a position where showing up with billions is necessary but not sufficient. One thing we cannot tell you is what testing rights AST already holds on that band. There is press reporting of an FCC special temporary authority granted in mid-August, but we could not confirm its scope or duration against the order itself, so we are not going to score the auction on it.

That same asymmetry — whoever can simply outspend wins by default — shows up again when the buyer is the government rather than the market. Space Force General Michael Guetlein said this month that the contractors building space-based interceptors for the 185 billion dollar Golden Dome program have completed their first key hardware tests. Those contractors include SpaceX and Northrop Grumman. All of them now advance to the next phase, which is demonstrating that the technology can operate in space. Flight demonstrations are targeted for 2027, according to that same reporting. So nothing here has flown, and nobody has specified which subsystems were tested. This is a gate cleared, not interceptor hardware validated.

Then, at the Space and Missile Defense Symposium in Huntsville, Guetlein warned that the program could be forced to halt development as early as October without additional money from Congress. No dollar figure behind that shortfall has been published, so treat it as an unstated number rather than a specific one. And take the warning with the appropriate salt. A program chief talking about money at a symposium is doing budget advocacy as much as status reporting, and it is the standard move ahead of a continuing resolution. The informative part is the pairing. A 185 billion dollar headline program cannot fund its next phase without new congressional action, which tells you that number was always an ambition rather than an obligation. Note also where a lapse would land. The ground-based and sea-based layers of the architecture have their own funding lines and their own contractors. It is the space-based interceptor track that stops, so the appropriations risk is concentrated precisely on the orbital segment this briefing covers. So what: passing a hardware test and having the money to continue are independent variables, and Golden Dome currently has one and may lose the other in about five weeks.

Notice who keeps appearing on the government side of that story. The same two names, SpaceX and Northrop Grumman, and one of them is also the only American company flying medium-to-heavy payloads reliably right now. That vendor concentration gets concrete when you look at launch supply. Rocket Lab this month sold Kepler Communications a dedicated Neutron mission — the first time it has sold an entire Neutron flight rather than a rideshare slot. Read that as a vote of confidence, but read the schedule alongside it. Rocket Lab has flagged that its window for a 2026 Neutron debut is narrowing. Production is tracking toward delivering the vehicle to the pad in the fourth quarter, which leaves almost no margin, and the company itself has raised the prospect that Neutron doesn't fly until 2027. Be clear on what a pad-delivery target is not. It is not a launch date. And selling a dedicated flight on a vehicle that has never flown raises the stakes of a slip rather than lowering them. A rideshare delay disappoints several customers a little. A dedicated-flight delay strands one customer's entire program.

Neutron also isn't sliding into an empty field. Blue Origin's New Glenn and ULA's Vulcan are both still grounded on the BE-4 engine fix we covered on Monday. That was a main oxygen valve issue, traced after May's static-fire explosion at the Cape. Because both rockets use the same engine, both companies are gated on the same repair. Each is targeting a return to flight before the end of the year, but that is a company target, not a licensed launch date. Add it up. With New Glenn and Vulcan waiting on one supplier's fix, and Neutron possibly a 2027 vehicle, non-SpaceX American medium-to-heavy capacity for the remainder of this year is effectively nil. That is arithmetic, not opinion, and it is what turns a Rocket Lab schedule slip into a market-structure event.

The counterexample sits in the same beat. Intuitive Machines closed the quarter with 1.8 billion dollars of backlog, and separately won an authorization to proceed on a multi-satellite communications program worth more than 600 million dollars across a 30-month execution window. The customer is undisclosed, so don't file it as either government or commercial. An unnamed counterparty is a weaker durability signal than a named government award, and backlog is not revenue. But the structural contrast with Rocket Lab holds. This is an order book that converts against a stated schedule. Rocket Lab's converts only after a first flight that keeps moving right. Backlog quality, not backlog size, is what now separates the public space names.

Step back and the pattern is the one this beat has been drifting toward for weeks. The binding constraints are no longer rockets. They are a spectrum license and a continuing resolution. What is genuinely inflecting is control of access rights. What is still a demo is every piece of hardware waiting on a first flight. And notice SpaceX sitting on every side of this at once. Dominant launcher. Holder of about six billion dollars of new Space Force awards through Starshield last quarter. A Golden Dome interceptor developer. And now the rival bidder for the spectrum its listed competitor needs. That is concentration risk ceasing to be a launch-market question and becoming a national-security-space question. It is also the kind of thing that eventually gives Congress a reason to write single-vendor limits into an authorization bill.

So watch the calendar rather than the launch pads. Preliminary spectrum offers in early September. A Golden Dome funding decision in October. The FCC's November 5th deadline. And whether Neutron actually reaches the pad in the fourth quarter.

That's the briefing. Talk tomorrow.
