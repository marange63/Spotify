Good morning. It's Sunday, August 23rd, and this is your frontier AI labs briefing.

Six briefings running, the capability leaderboard hasn't moved. What arrived instead is the first outside, non-vendor number this briefing has had all week, and it lands directly on the question we've been arguing since Friday: whether Anthropic's growth is as clean as its run rate implies.

Start with Ramp, the corporate card and bill-pay company, whose numbers TechCrunch published on August 20th. Ramp sees spend across more than seventy thousand American businesses. By its count, Anthropic's share of that business AI market rose from 41 percent in May to nearly 44 percent in July, while OpenAI held roughly flat, at 39 to nearly 40. On the level, Anthropic is still winning, and winning by more than it was two months earlier.

Say that plainly, because the headline running through the press this week inverts it. TechCrunch also reports that OpenAI is growing faster within this same segment in the quarter to date. But TechCrunch attaches no magnitude to that claim. No share-point gap, no dollar figure, no named metric. And it is measured against a quarter with a month still to run. So the honest read is two-sided. Anthropic's lead is real and it grew. The marginal trend inside an incomplete quarter has weakened. That is not the same thing as the lead flipping.

Now the caveat, before any of this becomes an argument. Ramp only sees businesses that transact through its own card and bill-pay products. TechCrunch says so directly: this is not a measure of the total market, and it excludes large enterprises that run their spend through other providers, the American Expresses of the world. That leaves out the negotiated enterprise contracts where the biggest AI commitments actually sit. And as our enterprise technology briefing made clear yesterday, Ramp is counting businesses transacting, not dollars committed. Keep both limits in mind for everything that follows.

Here's the part worth building on. An economist cited in the same report put OpenAI's momentum down to two things: strong uptake of its own GPT-5.6 Sol model, and Anthropic's Fable 5 disappointing buyers on two named grounds, price and data retention requirements. It's the second of those that is strategically loaded. Anthropic's safety and governance posture is supposed to be its selling point in the enterprise. Here it shows up on the buyer's side of the table as a cost line instead. Safety terms have become a price, not just a brand.

It also lines up with a separate cut of the same vendor's data, which ran on our enterprise technology briefing yesterday. That one put Fable 5 at roughly six percent of the tokens businesses buy from Anthropic, but eleven percent of the dollars. Two different slices of Ramp's numbers, landing in the same place: an expensive model that a lot of buyers are choosing not to route much work through.

There's a more benign explanation, and it deserves airtime. A premium model taking a small share of tokens and a larger share of dollars is also exactly what disciplined routing looks like. Buyers reserve the expensive model for genuinely hard tasks and send everything else downward. A hard price ceiling and rational routing produce identical spend data. Ramp cannot tell them apart, and neither can we. But notice that the benign reading carries its own sting. A buyer who routes a small, expensive slice to the frontier model and defaults everything else to cheaper ones has already built multi-model procurement. That is the architecture that makes switching cheap, and it is the opposite of lock-in.

Either way, the timing raises the stakes. Anthropic's annualized revenue run rate is reported at sixty-five billion dollars, and it is reportedly seeking a public listing this fall at two trillion dollars or more. A valuation priced off a revenue line whose premium tier has documented buyer resistance is a different security from one priced off broad-based demand across the lineup. This doesn't touch the growth rate. It questions the mix, and mix is what a public market will price.

Worth noting that Anthropic already made one visible move in this direction this month. On its own pricing page, the company permanently cancelled a scheduled increase on Claude Sonnet 5, holding it at two dollars in and ten dollars out per million tokens rather than stepping up to three and fifteen on September 1st. That is Anthropic responding to price pressure somewhere in its lineup. It is not evidence that it has touched Fable 5's price or its retention terms. Sonnet 5 is the mid-tier model. The objection was raised against the premium one, and those are different products.

That same tension, safety posture as a cost, is running in the opposite direction at OpenAI. This one broke just over two weeks ago, on August 7th, so date it plainly. OpenAI disclosed that preliminary evaluations of its upcoming Astra model meant it could not rule out a Critical capability level for cybersecurity under its own Preparedness Framework, and it suspended parts of Astra's development in response. Where Anthropic is reportedly losing enterprise share over a safety-adjacent term, OpenAI is paying for its safety posture in withheld capability rather than lost share. Same competitive dimension, opposite currency. Which is why safety belongs on the scorecard next to price and placement, not in a separate ethics bucket. On Wednesday we argued that rivals would face pressure to match Anthropic's disclosure detail. OpenAI had arguably already gone further, twelve days before we made the argument. Take the claim with appropriate distance, though. It is self-reported, with no release date for Astra and no specifics on what was paused, which makes it as unfalsifiable as it is cheap to announce.

And hold one thought on the other side of Anthropic's retention terms. The requirement that is costing it share among small and mid-market buyers today is the same requirement that wins regulated buyers tomorrow. Whether that is a good trade depends entirely on which segment turns out to be worth more, and nobody has that answer yet.

From safety as a price to price as a ceiling. We've covered Alibaba's Qwen and Moonshot's Kimi closing the gap with Western models twice this week, so one number here, not the whole story again. Qwen3.8-Max scored 86.1 on the OSWorld-Verified agentic benchmark, against Fable 5's 85.0. That is Alibaba's own published comparison, including the score it assigned its rival, and a 1.1-point edge is inside the noise on any single suite. The license on that largest Qwen model still isn't confirmed open either. The number that actually matters is the price attached to it: two dollars in, six dollars out per million tokens. Anthropic's own pricing page lists Fable 5 at ten dollars in and fifty out. That is five times the input price and roughly eight times the output price of the model Alibaba claims to be matching. Whatever Anthropic's serving costs look like, the ceiling on what it can charge at the top tier is increasingly being set from outside its own building.

Now from price to placement, where xAI just bought what the others are building. Grok Bot, xAI's AI teammate agent product, moved beyond beta on August 21st into SuperGrok's paid tiers and, more importantly, into Cursor's Pro Plus, Ultra and Teams plans. That puts xAI's agent directly inside a third-party developer tool Anthropic and OpenAI also compete for, on a model, Grok 4.6, held flat at that same two-and-six-dollar pricing. That is real distribution, rented rather than built. It is not adoption. No seats, no revenue and no retention numbers were disclosed, and being an option on someone else's menu is the cheapest kind of win to announce.

The same compute advantage looks very different once you leave the code editor for the physical world. At a JPMorgan-hosted meeting on August 20th, Tesla pushed its next-generation AI5 chip to mid-2027 and said external sales of Optimus wouldn't begin before the second half of that year at the earliest. Both are further slips on a pattern of prior slippage, and both are company guidance from an investor meeting rather than commitments. The number that doesn't depend on any forecast is the mileage. Tesla's Austin robotaxi service has logged 380,000 unsupervised miles, against Waymo's more than 200 million. Note what the AI5 delay does to the sequencing. The compute step-change now arrives after the period in which that mileage gap has to start closing, so the near-term autonomy case has to be made on the fleet Tesla already has. In software, placement is purchasable. In hardware it still has to be earned mile by mile, and Musk's compute edge has not closed that gap.

Put it together. Capability at the top is frozen for a sixth briefing running, so the contest has moved entirely to price, placement, and the terms buyers are quietly attaching to safety. And a share position that moves three points in two months, in either direction, is evidence against durable lock-in and for a commoditizing infrastructure layer. That is the wrong backdrop for what would be the largest market debut on record. Two things to watch. Whether Ramp's completed third quarter confirms that OpenAI's marginal gain is real rather than a partial-quarter artifact. And whether Anthropic touches Fable 5's price or its retention terms before that filing goes public.

That's the briefing. Have a good Sunday.
