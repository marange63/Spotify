Good morning. It's Friday, September 18th, and this is your robotics and physical AI briefing.

For eight sessions running, this beat has asked the same question of every humanoid vendor: show us a controlled number, not a demo. Yesterday we said plainly that nobody had shown Figure's video-pretraining data actually improves its robot's fielded success rate. Twenty-four hours later, Figure published a number that claims exactly that. So today's job is to grade it.

Figure's new model, Helix 2.5, was tested in thirty previously unseen homes around the Bay Area on three household tasks: tidying a living room, folding towels, and making a bed. No data was collected in any of the test homes. Be precise about what zero-shot means here. It means the model saw none of those houses, those rooms, or those objects in training. It does not mean the model was untrained on the tasks themselves. This is a claim about transfer to a new environment, not about a robot improvising a job it has never been shown.

Inside that setup sits the part that matters. In a controlled comparison, a policy pretrained on Figure's Index dataset of human behavior video hit 56 percent success. An otherwise identical policy trained from scratch hit 9 percent. Figure says the gap is mostly attributable to the pretraining itself. The new model also matched its predecessor's success rate while using half the per-home adaptation data.

Give Figure real credit for the structure here. This isn't an aggregate press-release number, it's an ablation. The from-scratch control is otherwise identical, so the jump from 9 to 56 percent isolates pretraining as the causal variable. That is more rigorous than what this beat usually gets handed. It's the same shape of claim Skild AI made last week with its single-video, in-context learning result, roughly 66 percent per step against 9 percent for a comparable system. But Figure's version has a cleaner control behind it.

The evaluation protocol deserves credit too. Each task ran on a single fixed checkpoint across all thirty homes. No weights were adapted to the evaluation homes or objects, and no evaluation performance was used to pick the checkpoint. Most importantly, any run requiring a human safety intervention was scored as an outright failure. Not excluded from the sample, not rescued by a teleoperator. Interventions only ever hurt the score. That is a genuinely disciplined design choice, and it is the opposite of how most humanoid footage reaches the public.

Now discount it appropriately. Figure's post does not disclose the trial count behind that 56 percent. No total rollouts, no per-task breakdown across the three tasks. Thirty homes and three tasks could be ninety rollouts or several times that. Without the denominator, the precision implied by a clean percentage isn't actually supported, and there is no way to tell how much of the number is noise. And 56 percent still means a 44 percent failure rate on tasks that are short-horizon and low-consequence by design. Folding a towel badly costs nothing. Nothing in this evaluation touches a task where failure is expensive or unsafe, and every figure in it is Figure's own, with no outside observer and no replication.

There's also a scaling-law claim in the release, and it needs to be read narrowly. Figure says pretraining data volume predicts test loss, meaning next-action prediction error, to within about half a percent across an eightfold range of data. Figure calls that the first human-to-robot transfer scaling law measured on a humanoid, and on its own terms it is a real result. But it is a loss-forecasting relationship, not a task-reliability one. Predicting how well a model will predict the next action is not the same as predicting how often the robot finishes the job. That is precisely the gap this beat flagged yesterday. "Bound by compute" is a convenient framing for a company that committed 3.5 billion dollars of Nscale compute two weeks before this release. A tight loss-scaling curve is exactly the evidence that commitment needed. It does not yet close the reliability question.

Two second-order effects follow, and they point in different directions. The first is on the compute side. A published scaling relationship, even a narrow one, is the justification robotics labs need to keep bidding for next-generation GPU supply against the frontier language labs. That is a demand line on that silicon generation which barely existed a year ago, and it now has a citation behind it. The second is on the services side. If pretraining transfer holds up outside curated homes, the systems integrators and task-specific engineering shops lose their core argument, which is that every deployment needs bespoke engineering. That business gets compressed from the model layer down, the same way it happened in vision.

Which brings up the commercially interesting number, and honestly it isn't the 56 percent. It's the halved adaptation data. Per-site commissioning cost, not peak dexterity, is what actually gates humanoid deployment economics. A 50 percent cut in the data needed to adapt to a new home is a direct hit on that cost line. Watch whether Figure ever states it in dollars or hours rather than in data volume, because that is the translation that would make it checkable.

So where does hard operating data with an actual denominator already exist in physical AI? Not from general-purpose humanoids. Aurora's driverless trucking has logged roughly 440,000 driverless miles through the end of June, with zero Aurora Driver-attributed collisions and 100 percent on-time performance. That's company-reported, and attribution is Aurora's own call, so treat both terms as defined by the party being graded. The reason the number is legible is the same reason it doesn't generalize: a mapped Sun Belt freight lane is about the most structured environment in this sector. Aurora also states a goal of exceeding 200 driverless trucks in commercial operation by year-end. That is a roadmap number, not a fielded count, and it gets the same treatment this beat gave Agility's order book.

Pony.ai adds a second filed denominator. Its SEC disclosures put the China robotaxi fleet at 1,975 vehicles as of June 30th, with a stated target above 3,500 by year-end. In August it expanded a partnership with Uber to deploy more than 2,000 robotaxis across Europe, entering the West through a demand aggregator rather than an owned-brand launch. A fleet count is an asset count, not utilization or revenue per vehicle, and the Europe deal is an intention rather than delivered cars. But 1,975 is a disclosed number in a filing, which is more than any humanoid maker currently produces.

Closing the loop, a Japanese entrant called ugo introduced ugo Nova this week. It's a wheeled, dual-arm teleoperated robot whose entire purpose is data collection rather than autonomy. Operators demonstrate tasks through bilateral controllers, with synchronized video, joint torque, and end-effector position captured to train future models. Mass production isn't planned until 2027, and the company makes no autonomy claim at all. That's exactly why it's useful: it's the honest control case against Figure's bet. Figure is wagering that passive human video substitutes for bespoke per-robot data collection. ugo is building the opposite infrastructure. If Figure's transfer result holds up outside curated homes, purpose-built teleoperation fleets start to look like a stranded capital bet.

Pulling the threads together, nine sessions in, the thread bends today but it doesn't break. We now have the first controlled capability number from a humanoid vendor, and the evaluation protocol behind it is better than the industry norm. We still have no fielded reliability number from any general-purpose humanoid: no uptime, no intervention rate, no cost per completed task. And this beat was partly wrong on its own terms, because that kind of data does exist in physical AI. It just comes from narrow-domain operators, and narrow-domain operators are not where the multiples are being paid. Three things to watch: whether Figure or a competitor publishes an intervention rate or a cost-per-task figure, whether this transfer result replicates outside home environments, and Tesla's sworn response to NHTSA, still due September 30th.

That's the briefing. Have a good weekend.
