Good morning, it's Thursday, August 13th, and this is Cautious Optimism Briefings. All week this show has tracked a "model race stalled at the top" thesis. Today it gets its first real crack.

The crack is OpenAI's own disclosure about an unreleased model called Astra. On August 7th, OpenAI said its latest internal evaluations of Astra, over the preceding few days, showed what it called "significant advancements in agentic coding and cybersecurity." That phrasing matters because it's the trigger for OpenAI's own Preparedness Framework, the internal rubric that grades models on catastrophic-risk potential. OpenAI says Astra's results were strong enough that the company "cannot rule out Critical capability level at this time." Critical is the top tier of that framework. By OpenAI's own definition, a model crosses it if it "can identify and develop functional zero-day exploits of all severity levels in many hardened real-world critical systems without human intervention." No released model has ever been assessed at that level. OpenAI says it has paused internal Astra work that doesn't meet a new set of controls: isolated testing environments, restricted network and tool access, tighter weight protection, and continuous monitoring for risky or misaligned behavior. It's also arranging additional testing with outside government and safety bodies. One clarification worth stating plainly: OpenAI says Astra was not involved in the Hugging Face compromise reported earlier this summer. Those are two separate stories.

So what does this actually tell us. It tells us capability at the frontier is not frozen everywhere — it's advancing hard specifically in the domains that most alarm regulators and enterprise security teams: autonomous coding and offensive cyber operations. That's a genuine data point against the "plateau" narrative.

But hold the skeptical read at the same time. This is a self-evaluation, run under OpenAI's own framework, on OpenAI's own model, with no independent verification and no confirmed release date for Astra. "Our latest internal evaluations" is OpenAI's own language, not a third party's. A disclosure like this is also, transparently, a branding move — it lets OpenAI tell regulators and enterprise buyers that it polices itself before shipping, at a moment when trust and safety posture increasingly function as sales collateral, not just risk management. Both things are true at once: the capability signal is real, and so is the positioning.

There's a competitive edge buried in that move worth naming. Once one lab publicly flags a model near the top cyber tier of its own framework before shipping, it effectively raises the bar for everyone: pre-release red-teaming, isolated eval environments, and continuous misalignment monitoring become table stakes, not optional rigor. That's expensive. It quietly favors the labs with the most compute and the deepest safety headcount, and it raises the cost of admission for smaller players and open-weight shops that can't run that kind of pre-release gauntlet. Safety, framed this way, is not just a cost center — it's a moat.

That governance angle is worth following, because Anthropic just made its own move on the same axis. On August 4th, Anthropic named Mariano-Florentino "Tino" Cuéllar — a former California Supreme Court justice and former head of the Carnegie Endowment — as its first Chief Global Affairs Officer, reporting directly to president Daniela Amodei, to run policy and government relations globally. Anthropic is separately in a dispute with the Pentagon over a "supply chain risk" label the company received in connection with the terms it placed on military access to Claude; the current status of that matter isn't independently confirmed as of this recording, so take that only as background, not as settled fact. Put together, the message from both labs is the same: safety-governance posture, pre-release testing rigor, and government relationships are now a distinct competitive front, not just public relations.

Now to the money behind all of this, where the numbers deserve more care than they usually get. You may have heard, in various forms this year, that Anthropic is generating meaningfully more revenue than OpenAI. That claim traces to a specific Information report citing Anthropic near forty-five billion dollars in annualized revenue against an estimated thirty-three billion for OpenAI — a gap of roughly thirty-five percent. Here's the catch: that reporting is dated to late May of this year, nearly three months old, not a fresh update from this week. It also sits awkwardly next to two other figures already in circulation — a roughly nineteen-billion-dollar Anthropic run-rate cited in early-August financing coverage, and the roughly thirty-billion figure this show has referenced in recent days. Those numbers don't form a clean trend; they look like different methodologies measuring different things, possibly including or excluding reseller revenue Anthropic books through AWS, Google, and Microsoft marketplaces. One piece of reporting flags exactly that ambiguity, noting some analysts believe Anthropic reports more gross marketplace revenue than OpenAI does. The only truly comparable, company-disclosed anchors on record are OpenAI's twenty billion dollars in annualized revenue as of December 31st and Anthropic's fourteen billion as of February 12th — and at that last confirmed comparison point, OpenAI was still ahead. So treat any claim that Anthropic has overtaken OpenAI as unresolved, not confirmed.

What is on firmer ground is how Anthropic is financing its compute. Reporting corroborated across multiple trade-press sources describes Anthropic special-purpose vehicles stacking seventy-one billion dollars in chip-lease debt within a sixty-day window, structured so the hardware sits off Anthropic's own balance sheet while still being leased back to the company. Broadcom is backstopping the senior tranches of that debt: if Anthropic defaults on lease payments, or the resale value of the chips falls short, Broadcom covers the gap. Zoom out further and analysts peg Anthropic's confirmed floor of compute commitments at roughly two hundred fifty-four billion dollars, built from named deals including a Google cloud commitment, an AWS commitment, and Broadcom's own filed TPU purchase commitments — with a looser, less certain upper-bound estimate around four hundred fifty billion once every overlapping commitment is stacked in.

So what's the takeaway there. Whatever Anthropic's actual revenue lead or gap versus OpenAI turns out to be, the company is underwriting an enormous compute buildout through structured credit rather than its own balance sheet — a template Google, Microsoft, and other hyperscalers are watching closely, because it determines who actually bears the risk if AI revenue growth decelerates faster than compute commitments do. That's a genuine capital-markets story independent of who's technically "ahead" on revenue this month.

Zoom out and the pattern across all of today's stories is the same: the labs are now competing on three fronts simultaneously — raw capability, which Astra shows is still moving fast in dangerous directions; safety-governance credibility, which OpenAI and Anthropic are both actively building into a moat; and capital structure, where Anthropic is pioneering off-balance-sheet compute financing at a scale nobody has stress-tested. Watch whether Astra actually ships, whether a cleaner Anthropic revenue disclosure ever surfaces, and whether other labs start leasing chips the same way Anthropic just did.
