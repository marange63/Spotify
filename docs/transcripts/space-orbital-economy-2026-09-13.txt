Good morning. It's Sunday, September 13th, and this is your Cautious Optimism Briefing on the orbital economy.

This sector constantly asks you to grade a reservation as if it were a delivery. Today we can put both kinds of claim on the table side by side, and price the difference between them.

Start with Starship, because it's the cleanest example of a reservation about to settle one way or the other. A September 15th Starship date has been floating around unconfirmed for about a week. What's new isn't the date. It's the content of the filing behind it. SpaceX's Special Temporary Authorization request to the FCC describes a mission profile built around an actual orbital insertion. Every prior Starship flight has flown a suborbital arc. That's the threshold this vehicle has never crossed, and the company has now had to commit to it on paper.

The manifest matters as much as the milestone. Flight 14 is set to carry roughly twenty Starlink V3 satellites. Each one is rated for more than ten times the downlink capacity of a current Starlink satellite — a terabit per second against roughly a hundred gigabits on today's V2 Minis. That's a rated spec, not throughput measured from orbit.

And V3 isn't a Falcon 9 payload at all. Each satellite weighs about two thousand kilograms, more than three times a V2 Mini. Starship is described as the only vehicle in SpaceX's fleet that can deploy them at scale.

The hardware looks ready for the attempt. Booster 21 completed a full thirty-three-engine static fire on August 28th, and Ship 41 finished its own six-engine test the week before.

Say plainly what this briefing is not doing. The flight has not happened as of this recording, and we are not predicting or characterizing its outcome. September 15th is a filed target, not a confirmed launch date, and Starship schedules have slipped repeatedly.

One more piece of proportion. Published estimates put Starship's ceiling near sixty V3 satellites a flight. If that's even directionally right, a twenty-satellite manifest is a partial load. Flight 14 is a test of orbital insertion, not of Starship's maximum capacity per launch.

Here's the part worth banking. What the filing establishes, independent of whether the flight works, is a stated intent to a regulator: an orbit, not an arc. And here's the second-order effect nobody prices. If Starship does eventually deliver V3s at rate, launch stops being the constraint on Starlink's capacity growth. What remains are ground-segment capacity, spectrum, and subscriber density in the places that capacity lands. None of those get relieved by a bigger rocket.

That's capacity priced by the rocket. Now here's what the same capacity costs when you buy it one satellite at a time.

AST SpaceMobile's BlueBirds 12 and 13 fully deployed their communications arrays in early September. Each array is twenty-four hundred square feet, more than three times the size of the company's original Block 1 satellites. They're rated for peak data rates near two hundred megabits per second, against ninety-eight point nine megabits previously achieved — an expected peak, not a demonstrated service rate. But the arrays themselves are real working hardware in orbit today, not a filing.

Now the money, and the arithmetic here is ours, not a company disclosure. AST puts its average capital cost at roughly twenty-one to twenty-three million dollars per satellite. Its stated target is forty-five satellites in orbit by early 2027. Multiply those and you get to about a billion dollars of constellation capital spending.

Set that against what the company has actually booked. Revenue backlog is about one point three billion dollars of aggregated contracted revenue. That's an order book with no disclosed recognition schedule, and a record backlog de-risks nothing on its own. The one realized number is thirty-one and a half million dollars of second-quarter revenue. That more than doubled the first quarter, which is genuine progress.

Now hold it against guidance, which was reiterated at a hundred and fifty to two hundred million dollars for the full year. Against thirty-one and a half million in the second quarter, and less than half of that in the first, the back half of the year has to produce more than double what the front half did. That is not an extrapolation of the current run-rate. It's a step-change, and nothing disclosed so far explains where it comes from.

The real test, though, moves to the fourth quarter. That's when US Mobile launches AST-based direct-to-cell coverage on its Verizon-based Warp network. It's the first time this story gets graded on subscriber adoption instead of satellite hardware milestones. One honest caveat on the comparison to Starship: direct-to-handset service and terminal-based broadband are different products, with different spectrum and different pricing power. The per-bit comparison illustrates capital intensity. It isn't head-to-head competition.

Both of those are capacity that's been promised. Here's capacity that's been sold and still won't arrive for two years.

Rocket Lab has signed its first dedicated Neutron launch contract, with Kepler Communications, deploying satellites that add optical connectivity and on-orbit compute to Kepler's constellation. Flag the timing honestly: this was announced on August 10th, so it's recent rather than new. It's a real commercial vote of confidence — a customer choosing schedule control on a rocket that hasn't flown yet.

But the launch is targeted no earlier than 2028, from Rocket Lab's Wallops Island pad. Read that date correctly. A customer committing to 2028 has already priced in slip. And it puts Neutron's revenue recognition years outside the window in which Neutron's own capital spending peaks. Demand for a second medium-lift vehicle is genuine. The cash from it is not close.

Against three reservations, one realized number.

SpaceX flew the classified USSF-153 mission for the Space Force on September 10th. The booster, B1081, was on its twenty-seventh flight, and its landing was the six hundred and sixtieth Falcon booster landing to date. That's the yardstick every other item today gets measured against: hardware that has already flown, landed, and flown again, repeatedly.

Two cautions on it. The payload is classified, so no mission-specific implication can be drawn. And flight count is not cost. No refurbishment figure has ever been disclosed for a booster like that one, so the number proves the hardware survives reuse, not what each reflight saves.

Set it against what we covered on Tuesday, though — LandSpace landing a Zhuque-3 booster that then caught fire and tipped over. The spread between landing a stage once and reflying the same one twenty-seven times is where the sector's real cost-per-kilogram gap sits.

So where does this actually stand? One thing settles within days: whether Starship holds an orbit and separates operational payload for the first time. One thing settles by the fourth quarter: whether US Mobile's direct-to-cell launch produces real subscriber numbers rather than another spec sheet. And one thing tells you nothing here moves fast: Neutron hardware still has to reach a pad before its 2028 customer date means anything. Grade every headline in this sector by which of those three buckets it belongs in.
