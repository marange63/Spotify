Good morning, it's Thursday, October 15th, and this is Cautious Optimism Briefings, private credit edition.

Here's the thesis. The official sector has started pricing private credit's AI-concentration risk before the market has. Regulators now want capital charges on the bank lending that funds private credit, while lenders are charging almost nothing extra for the risk and recoveries are hitting record lows.

Start with the lead, and a quick bit of context. All week we've followed Carlyle's warning about AI lending. The firm's paper argued that AI lending is more speculative than SaaS ever was. Then Carlyle's head of global research, Jason Thomas, suggested on television that investors cap their exposure to the AI ecosystem at roughly fifteen percent. Yesterday we covered the Financial Stability Board's letter to the G20, which named private credit and AI as linked concerns. Now the IMF has turned that concern into an instrument. In the blog accompanying its October Global Financial Stability Report, published this week, the Fund says leverage and liquidity mismatches in AI, private credit, and other nonbank sectors should be limited. The tool it names is sectoral capital and concentration requirements on banks' exposures to these sectors. That's not a warning anymore. It's a policy design.

The IMF also sized the pipeline it's worried about. It reviewed fourteen hyperscaler AI financing transactions since 2023, and found about three hundred billion dollars of equity against six hundred fifty billion of debt. So debt is financing roughly two-thirds of that stack. The Fund also warned that faltering AI investment and profitability could challenge optimistic expectations. And in its adverse stress scenario, banks holding twenty-two percent of global banking assets would emerge with capital ratios below regulatory limits.

Hold three caveats before you run with that. First, the instrument targets banks' exposures, not private credit funds directly. On our read, it bites on the funding layer underneath the funds. That means subscription lines, NAV lines, back leverage, and warehouse facilities, rather than fund-level leverage caps. Second, the six hundred fifty billion covers every financing type across those fourteen deals. The IMF doesn't say how much sits with private lenders versus banks or bond buyers, and our follow-up research found no breakdown. So don't assume private credit holds that whole number. Third, the twenty-two percent stress result is a broad banking-system outcome with several drivers, including AI, bond yields, and cyber risk. It is not a private-credit loss estimate. And all of this is advisory. National supervisors would have to act, and no US proposal has been reported.

Here's why the instrument matters more than the rhetoric. Yesterday we argued that a private-credit concentration cap doesn't shrink AI credit risk. It just decides who holds it, and we named banks as the weakest home. The IMF now proposes making banks a costlier home too. That has a direct consequence for the funds, and this is our inference, not the IMF's. If bank leverage to BDCs and credit funds gets more expensive, levered returns fall at exactly the vehicles already gating redemptions. And if you squeeze both the funds and the banks that lever them, the residual AI exposure has to migrate. The likeliest next stops are insurers and the investment-grade and asset-backed markets, which face a lighter prudential ask today. So the regulatory squeeze lands on the funding layer first, and pushes the risk outward from there.

So the regulators are pricing this risk. Is the market? For scale, TokenPost reports a thirty-five billion dollar vehicle, led by Apollo and Blackstone, that bought chips for lease to Anthropic. Its closing date is unconfirmed, so treat it as a measure of the category, not this week's news. One disclosure: Anthropic builds the model behind this show, and it's the lessee in that deal.

Now the pricing. The same TokenPost report puts average spreads on AI-related private loans at six-point-two percentage points over benchmark. Other private-credit loans averaged six-point-one. That data is secondhand, and we couldn't check it against LCD, PitchBook, or a rating agency. Taken at face value, it's about ten basis points of extra pay. That's for a risk Carlyle calls more speculative than software lending, and one the IMF now flags as a financial-stability threat.

There are two ways to read that. The complacency read says lenders aren't being paid for obsolescence, or for counterparty concentration in compute. The credit-support read says many of these deals carry hyperscaler leases and hard collateral. On that view, a similar spread could reflect comparable protection rather than mispricing. Both averages also blend very different deals, so neither read is clean. Still, the evidence leans toward complacency, because the mismatch shows up in deal design. Meta's Hyperion data-center financing has bonds running to 2049. Yet some of the underlying leases require renewal every four years. A renewal isn't a default, and it can roll on unchanged terms. But it means the real risk horizon is the four-year lease cycle, not the 2049 maturity. Spreads at today's levels aren't paying for that gap.

Spreads are one half of compensation. The other half is what you get back when something goes wrong. On October 12th and 13th, we flagged that record default rates sat on top of almost no recovery data. J.P. Morgan Asset Management's fourth-quarter heatmap, published at the end of September, now supplies a number, and it's bad. Loan recoveries have fallen to a record low of roughly thirty-six cents on the dollar. Meanwhile, the US leveraged-loan default rate is still a moderate three-point-oh-four percent. Put those together, and loss severity has deteriorated even while the default count looks contained. J.P. Morgan also puts the leveraged-credit distressed universe at two hundred eighteen billion dollars, the highest since mid-2023. And its house forecast has the default rate rising toward four and a half percent into 2027. A moderate default rate is not the same thing as a moderate loss rate.

One caveat matters here. This is leveraged-loan and high-yield data, not private credit, so the read-across is our inference, and it runs both ways. Direct lenders argue that tighter documents and fewer creditors give private loans better recoveries. The syndicated market, by contrast, has been scarred by liability-management exercises and creditor-on-creditor violence. The opposing view is simpler. Private marks just haven't been tested by a real downturn. Here's the practical test, and again it's our inference. If realized public recoveries sit at a record low, any private book carrying stressed loans at model marks well above that level will eventually have to justify the gap.

The first chance to look comes on October 30th. As we noted on Monday, that's when Apollo expects to start publishing asset-level prices to its investors. That means a mark for each individual loan across its credit platform. Apollo says these are fair-value estimates from its own internal models, not market-clearing prices. But for the first time, outsiders will see loan-by-loan marks from a major lender, and can compare them with other managers holding the same credits. Here's what to listen for. If Apollo's stressed loans are carried close to par, that supports the mark-lag worry. If they sit nearer where recoveries are actually running, it cuts against it.

So where does the cycle stand? Defaults are moderate, but loss severity is worsening. AI concentration risk isn't visible in spreads, and it hasn't been tested in private marks. Yet it's now the explicit target of prudential policy aimed at the bank-funding layer. That's the biggest unresolved risk heading into year-end. AI exposure is concentrating in vehicles and bank lines that lenders haven't repriced, and that supervisors have so far only described.

Watch four things. First, today's G20 finance ministers' meeting, though we don't yet know what, if anything, it will say on private credit. Second, the alternative managers' third-quarter calls later this month, for any disclosure of AI and data-center exposure. Third, Apollo's first loan-by-loan prices on October 30th, for where its stressed credits are marked. And fourth, Goldman Sachs BDC and Kayne Anderson BDC, scheduled to report November 5th and 6th. They'll be among the first calendar third-quarter reads on non-accruals and payment-in-kind.
