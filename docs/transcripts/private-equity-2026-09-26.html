Good morning, it's Saturday, September 26th, and this is your private equity briefing.

Start with the inventory problem, because it's the number everything else in this business has to clear. Bloomberg's reporting this week, citing PitchBook data, puts unsold private-equity-backed companies at 33,575 as of June 30th. That's up from 32,451 at the end of 2025.

The delta matters more than the level. On Sunday this show used Bain's figure of roughly 33,000. That was a different source, and the gap between two surveys is not a trend. This is. The same PitchBook series rose by about eleven hundred companies in six months. The pile is still growing, not shrinking.

The same reporting puts unrealized value in those portfolios at 3.8 trillion dollars. Hold periods sit near the seven years we flagged Sunday. And the reporting gives the extreme case. Hellman and Friedman still owns UKG, formerly Kronos, after nineteen years, with no imminent plans for a sale or IPO. That's one anecdote, not the average. But it's the direction the average is moving.

Now the number that settles the exit question. Only seventy PE-backed companies have gone public on US exchanges since 2022. Set that against 33,575 companies waiting for an exit. The IPO window is not the valve. Whatever clears this backlog, it isn't a reopened Nasdaq.

Distributions tell the same story, and it isn't new. Bain's distributions-to-NAV measure, which this show cited Monday, still sits near its lowest levels since the 2008 crisis. So the point that holds is structural. If the backlog clears anywhere, it clears through secondaries, sponsor-to-sponsor sales, or simply holding longer.

So if the backlog clears through secondaries, that valve has its own capacity problem. Jefferies' review of the first half of 2026, published in July, shows dedicated secondaries capital falling to 290 billion dollars at midyear. That's down from 327 billion at the end of 2025, roughly an eleven percent drawdown in six months. It happened while deal volume ran at the record pace this show covered Sunday. Put those two facts together. Buyers are consuming committed capital faster than new capital is being raised to replace it. One fair counter-reading is timing. Several large secondaries funds are in market now, and the drawdown may just be deployment ahead of those closes. But unless those funds actually close, the price of liquidity should rise. Demand for exits keeps growing against a shrinking pool of dedicated buyers.

So who's refilling that pool? Increasingly, it's the wealth channel. Jefferies puts evergreen vehicle fair market value at 120 billion dollars. Secondaries now make up 46 percent of those portfolios, up from 41 percent in 2025. Think about what that means. Semi-liquid, retail-facing funds that price off appraised NAV and promise periodic redemption are becoming the marginal buyer of the least liquid assets in private markets. That's a liquidity-mismatch risk, not yet a realized problem. But the private-credit side showed us this week what the wrapper looks like under pressure, with Apollo's flagship retail credit fund capping redemptions again.

At the small end, there is a new entrant. Chicago's Twin Bridge Capital Partners is raising a debut secondaries fund targeting up to 600 million dollars. Only 300 million is committed so far, from an anchor investor in December, and the final close isn't targeted until the first half of 2027. It will focus on middle-market single-asset continuation vehicles. But even the full 600 million is a rounding error against a 37-billion-dollar drawdown in dedicated capital. It tells you who's showing up at the edges, not that capacity is being restored. So the backlog risk isn't clearing. It's migrating toward holders with less tolerance for illiquidity than the institutional limited partners who've historically carried it.

So what price is anyone actually paying for that liquidity? Jefferies' answer is the most useful check in the whole picture. LP-portfolio stakes traded at an average of 87 percent of net asset value in the first half. That's LP-portfolio pricing, not continuation-vehicle pricing, which still isn't published reliably. Read the 87 percent two ways. One reading is that marks across the industry are running roughly 13 percent rich, and secondary buyers are correcting for it. The other is that marks are basically fair, and 13 percent is the premium buyers charge to take illiquid exposure off someone else's hands. The number alone can't tell you which is right. It's also an average across the funds LPs chose to sell, so it may understate the discount on the weakest tail.

What would settle it is direction. If that discount widens as dedicated secondaries capital keeps shrinking, the market is pulling marks down toward reality. If pricing holds at 87 percent despite less buyer capital chasing more volume, that looks more like a stable liquidity premium on sound marks. Either way, this is the most observable market check on private-equity marks we have.

The largest managers have clearly noticed where the money and the fees are. EQT completed its combination with the secondaries specialist Coller Capital on August 31st. That closed a month ago, but it matters here. EQT paid 3.2 billion dollars in base consideration, plus up to 500 million in contingent payments tied to performance through March 2029. It paid mostly in newly issued stock, roughly 7 percent of its share count. In return, EQT gets 10 percent of the carried interest in Coller's current flagship fund. It also gets the right to invest in 35 percent of the carry in every future closed-end Coller fund.

Think about what that buys. On Thursday this show argued that the biggest managers are building income that doesn't depend on a portfolio company actually selling. Lending spreads and deal origination keep paying while the exits wait. EQT-Coller is the same logic, one step further. EQT now owns a fee stream and a carry stream in exactly the part of private markets that grows because exits are frozen. That's a large buyout manager hedging its own cycle by owning the mechanism that clears everyone else's backlog.

It also raises a governance question worth watching. A firm that originates buyout deals and now runs a secondaries buyer has an obvious incentive to buy aging assets at a price it helps set. Nothing in the record shows that happening. And there's a timing risk. EQT is buying this franchise near the top of a record volume cycle. If secondaries capital keeps shrinking and pricing slips below that 87 percent line, EQT bought the valve at its peak, not its floor.

Pull it together. The exit logjam isn't clearing. It's being managed through a valve that's shrinking, increasingly funded by retail capital, and increasingly owned by the largest managers. The biggest unresolved risk is the gap between what funds say they hold and what they can turn into cash. And part of that gap now sits inside wrappers that promise liquidity they may not deliver under stress.

Watch four things from here. First, whether the big secondaries fundraises now in market actually close and refill dedicated capital. Second, whether second-half pricing data shows that 87 percent figure slipping. Third, the secondary sale Blackstone is arranging for investors in a fund inside its Blackstone Property Partners real estate strategy. No price has been reported yet, and it's a live test of whether buyers pay near net asset value. And fourth, third-quarter manager earnings in late October, where fee-related earnings versus realized carry is the split to read.
