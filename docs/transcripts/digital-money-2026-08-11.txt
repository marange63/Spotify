Good morning. It's Tuesday, August 11th, and this is your digital money briefing.

The tape looks quiet this week. Bitcoin is pinned near $64,000. But underneath that calm is a structural stress story, not a calm one, and it starts with the largest corporate bitcoin holder on earth turning into a seller.

Strategy, the company Michael Saylor built on a decade-long promise to never sell bitcoin, has now sold bitcoin in back-to-back weeks, below its own cost basis, to keep a preferred-stock obligation current. In the week ending August 2nd, Strategy sold 1,638 BTC for $104.7 million, an average price of $63,957 a coin. That's well under the company's blended cost basis of $75,419 per bitcoin. A further sale the following week brought total holdings down to 840,447 BTC, from 842,138 the week before. Year to date, Strategy has now sold 6,948 bitcoin, a pivot that began in late May when Saylor shifted the company's stated framework from pure accumulation to what it calls a Digital Credit Capital model. In plain terms, that model exists to service the dividends on Strategy's preferred shares, and right now it isn't optional.

The mechanism matters here. One of those preferred issues, STRC, carries a variable dividend that ratchets up every time the stock trades below $95: up half a point each time, and that increase does not reverse even if the price recovers. STRC closed at $89.46 on July 31st, comfortably below that trigger, and the rate has now climbed to 12% annually. So this isn't a one-time cash squeeze Strategy can wait out. As long as STRC stays depressed, the dividend bill keeps climbing, which means the pressure to sell bitcoin keeps climbing too, independent of where bitcoin itself trades. Strategy is currently sitting on a $10.9 billion paper loss across its full position. The skeptical read isn't insolvency — this is disclosed, disciplined selling to meet a known obligation, not a fire sale. But it is the first concrete evidence that the levered corporate-treasury model cannot ride out a flat market without touching the coins it swore it would never touch.

There's a tell in the filings that underlines the point. Alongside the sales, Strategy has been building a dedicated dollar reserve, cash set aside specifically to cover preferred dividends and debt interest. That reserve reportedly ran around four billion dollars in early August and topped four-point-six billion a week later. Think about what that signals. A company whose entire thesis was that you never need cash because you just hold bitcoin is now stockpiling cash as a buffer against having to sell more of it. That's not a footnote. It's a quiet admission that the pure never-sell model needed a backstop it never used to need, and building that backstop is itself a drag on the accumulation story.

And this is bigger than one company. A whole cohort of corporate treasuries raised money the same way Strategy did, issuing preferred stock and convertible debt to buy bitcoin, on the same implicit promise that they would never have to sell. Every one of them now faces the identical mark-to-market math, on the same kind of preferred stack, in the same flat market. Strategy is simply the largest, and the first to show the strain in the open.

That selling helps explain something that's been puzzling this market for weeks: why bitcoin ETF inflows keep coming back without moving the price. US spot bitcoin ETFs took in $626 million over the first three trading days of August, after bleeding $265.4 million on the very last session of July. A sixth straight day of inflows followed, $101.7 million on August 7th alone. But look at where that money is going. BlackRock's IBIT alone captured $479 million of that three-day total, roughly 76% of everything that came in. This is not broad institutional adoption. It's one fund, absorbing supply from one seller. Strategy has effectively become the counterparty to BlackRock's flows, and that's a very different story than the one being marketed to allocators. Concentration like that is its own risk: one fund, one custodian, sitting at the center of the whole complex's marginal bid. And we still don't know how much of even that IBIT flow is fresh allocation versus basis-trade and index-rebalancing plumbing, which would make the demand look thinner still.

It's also why bitcoin is failing its two biggest narrative tests at once this week. Gold climbed above $4,200 an ounce, its highest level in six weeks, on Chinese ETF and central-bank buying. The S&P 500 rallied to a fresh record after a weak July jobs report — initial estimates near 23,000 jobs lost — cut the market's odds of a September Fed hike from roughly 57% down toward the mid-40s. Equities loved that news. Bitcoin's reaction was muted. So this week, bitcoin is behaving like neither a clean equity-beta trade nor gold's digital hedge — it lagged both. If you're holding bitcoin for either of those theses, neither one is showing up in the price action right now. The next real test is Wednesday's CPI print: a hot number could snap bitcoin's correlation back to equities fast, since most of this setup is rate-expectation driven rather than anything idiosyncratic to crypto.

Now to the rulebook, where the picture is bifurcating in an important way. The Senate opened the formal cloture process on the CLARITY Act — the bill that would hand the CFTC clear jurisdiction over bitcoin and ether as commodities while leaving the SEC in charge of investment-contract tokens. But that happened right before the August recess, and Majority Leader Thune has confirmed the real vote won't happen until September, when the chamber will need what he called only a handful of session days to finish the job. That's a procedural placeholder, not progress toward passage — market-implied odds of getting this done in 2026 fell sharply on the delay, and passage still needs roughly ten Senate Democrats to clear the 60-vote bar. Meanwhile, on a completely separate and less contested track, Treasury opened a formal comment period on illicit-finance detection rules under the GENIUS Act, the stablecoin framework that's already law. Comments are due by October 17th. So stablecoin issuers are getting real regulatory certainty on a fixed calendar, while the much bigger question of how bitcoin and ether themselves get classified and traded risks sliding into 2027. That's a meaningful gap for institutions deciding whether to move into direct token custody: the money-movement rails are getting clarity first, the assets underneath them are not.

Put it together and the through-line for today is this: the tape's calm is an illusion built on offsetting forces, not equilibrium. The single largest bitcoin holder is now a forced seller because of a dividend ratchet that only tightens. The single largest ETF buyer is absorbing that supply almost alone. Bitcoin is failing both its hedge narrative and its risk-asset narrative in the same week. And the rulebook meant to give this whole complex legitimacy is splitting into a fast track for stablecoins and a slow, uncertain track for everything else. None of that shows up if you're only watching the spot price sit still near $64,000.
