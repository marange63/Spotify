Good morning, this is your enterprise technology briefing for Thursday, October 22nd, 2026.

Two incumbent software vendors reported yesterday, and both are building AI-linked backlog that their own cash generation can fund. Underneath them, the infrastructure layer is still signing backlog ahead of the money needed to build it. So today's organizing question isn't how big a vendor's AI backlog is. It's who finances the gap between the signing and the delivery.

Start with SAP, because it's the cleanest version of the story. SAP posted its first-ever quarter above ten billion euros on Wednesday, with total revenue up 12% at constant currency. Cloud revenue grew roughly twice as fast, up 25%. And the forward book grew faster still. Current cloud backlog rose 26%, to about 24 billion euros.

That gap is the signal. When backlog outgrows recognized revenue, signed but undelivered business is accumulating faster than SAP is converting it. That points to durable demand rather than a one-quarter beat. It is still a leading indicator, though, not recognized revenue, and the conversion timing is unproven.

The cash side held up too. SAP's own release says operating profit rose 18% year over year, on both an IFRS and non-IFRS basis. Free cash flow rose 36%, per a secondary summary of the results. On the earnings call, CEO Christian Klein made the causal claim explicit. In his words, "In Q3, AI was the main driver behind our customers' move to the cloud." Guidance was firmer, but not raised. Cloud revenue is now trending toward the top of SAP's prior range. Backlog, operating profit and free-cash-flow guidance were reaffirmed.

Now the skeptical turn, because Klein's causal claim deserves scrutiny. SAP disclosed that nine of its fifty largest deals this quarter included AI or Business Data Cloud components. Read that the other way: by SAP's own disclosure, most of its biggest deals didn't. SAP gave no breakdown of whether AI was decisive to those nine signings, or simply attached to migrations already under way. The more honest read is that AI is being bundled into cloud migration SAP would likely have sold regardless. It is not yet a proven standalone revenue line.

One more line matters for positioning. Klein said, "Unlike some of our peers, SAP is not committing to a preferred model. We give our customers choice." That's a deliberate contrast with vendors tying their AI roadmap to a single lab or hyperscaler. Its value as a selling point rises the more concentrated everyone else's AI supply chain gets. We'll come back to that concentration problem shortly. My inference is that SAP's disclosure also raises the bar for rivals like Oracle and Workday, who will face questions next cycle about AI-attributable backlog of their own. So what to take from SAP: its AI narrative is monetizing through migration it would probably have won anyway, but the forward book really is building faster than the revenue it will become.

The second incumbent print resolves a catalyst we flagged on Tuesday. We called IBM's results the first filed test of whether enterprise AI spending is actually showing up in the numbers. One caveat up front: every IBM figure here comes from a single secondary aggregator, not IBM's own release. IBM beat modestly. Revenue came in at 17.1 billion dollars, about 1.5% above the estimate. Adjusted earnings per share beat by about 2.7%. Those are ordinary-quarter-sized beats, not evidence of an AI inflection.

The mix underneath is uneven. IBM's mainframe business, IBM Z, fell 20% in the quarter. Some of that likely reflects a tough comparison in the mainframe product cycle, so don't build a structural-decline thesis on one quarter. Full-year constant-currency revenue guidance of 4% to 5% was only reaffirmed, not raised. What did move was the cash target. IBM raised its full-year free-cash-flow outlook to an increase of more than one billion dollars. That points to margin and cash-conversion discipline, not demand suddenly accelerating. IBM's branding for its AI push, what it calls "Super Intelligence," has no verifiable adoption metric behind it this quarter. Treat it as a relabeling of the existing agent strategy. So what to take from IBM: it's turning its AI story into cash before it's turning it into faster growth.

Now the other side of the ledger: backlog that can't fund itself. Oracle's free cash flow was negative 5.4 billion dollars last quarter. Capital spending of 28.5 billion dollars on buildings and equipment outran total revenue of 19.3 billion. That's a software incumbent spending more on construction than it brings in. Last Saturday we framed Oracle's problem as duration: cash goes out now, and the anchor contract pays later. The July downgrade to one notch above junk is old news and only backdrop.

What's new is a theory about who's standing behind the lenders. Oracle's remaining performance obligations total 664 billion dollars in signed commitments not yet booked as revenue. This week, TD Cowen analyst Michael Elias wrote that Nvidia may be helping Oracle secure financing from its lenders. His mechanism is either a lease-payment guarantee or a sublease structure. That view rests on channel checks, not confirmation. Nvidia has disclosed guarantee obligations capped at 108.5 billion dollars, without naming Oracle as a beneficiary. Neither company has confirmed the link.

If it's real, the circularity is the point. A chip vendor would be financing demand for its own chips. That should make lenders and enterprise buyers discount the quality of Oracle's backlog, whatever its size. There's a bull case worth hearing: signed commitments from large counterparties are exactly what lenders are supposed to finance capex against. But my inference is that if Nvidia credit support becomes a common thread across several providers, risk concentrates on one chip vendor's balance sheet. A stumble there would travel across the whole infrastructure layer, not just one name. So what to take from Oracle: the question has moved from whether lenders will fund the buildout to who's standing behind the lenders.

The same pattern shows up one level down the stack. Bloomberg reports that Anthropic and the neocloud Nscale are in talks for a second compute deal worth roughly 45 billion dollars. It would cover a second building at Nscale's Monarch campus in West Virginia, delivering 460 megawatts on Nvidia's Vera Rubin chips. That would roughly double Anthropic's existing commitment to Nscale. Nscale's own IPO filing puts the first contract, signed in August for the same site, at 44.6 billion dollars.

Adding the two reported values gives something like 90 billion dollars of commitments to one supplier. That's my own sum, not a number either company has stated. It only holds if the second deal actually signs. The talks are not finalized, and neither company has commented. And here's the catch. Nscale itself has said it hasn't secured binding financing for the GPUs and data centers its existing contracts already require. In the interest of disclosure, Anthropic makes the model behind this show.

There is a rational reading of this. A lab that can't get enough hyperscaler capacity anchors a supplier, and the anchor contract is what makes that supplier financeable. That only works if the financing closes. For Anthropic's enterprise customers, the risk this creates is capacity delivery and reliability, not model quality. So what to take from this one: a frontier lab's enterprise capacity increasingly rides on one unlisted supplier's ability to raise the money to build what it has already promised.

Pull it together. SAP and IBM are building AI-linked backlog that their own cash flow can support. Oracle and the Anthropic-Nscale chain are building backlog that depends on financing nobody has fully arranged yet. In Oracle's case, that financing may trace back to the same chip vendor supplying the demand. SAP's refusal to commit to one AI model looks less like a feature and more like a hedge against exactly this kind of concentration. We've said before that buyers should weigh a provider's balance sheet. The escalation now is that the provider's financing counterparties belong in AI-infrastructure diligence too, right alongside price and capability. That's an inference, but it favors self-funding incumbents over levered capacity providers.

Worth watching from here. ServiceNow reports on October 28th, the next incumbent data point on AI budget flow. Watch whether Nvidia or Oracle confirms or denies any credit support. And watch whether the Anthropic-Nscale talks actually sign, and whether Nscale closes the binding financing it says it still needs.
