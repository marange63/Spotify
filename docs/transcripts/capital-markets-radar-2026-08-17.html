Good morning, it's Monday, August 17th, and this is your Capital Markets Radar.

No new macro print moved the tape over the weekend — the next scheduled catalyst is the FOMC minutes from the July meeting, due around August 19th. So today the tell isn't a new number, it's what's underneath the numbers we already have. And the clearest example sits inside last week's headline equity story.

The S&P 500 closed at a record 7,798.99 last week, its 27th all-time high of the year, riding what FactSet's August 7th earnings update calls the strongest earnings season since 2021. Blended second-quarter growth for the index hit 50.4 percent. Eighty-six percent of companies beat estimates, and the aggregate earnings surprise came in at 29.2 percent — a record since FactSet began tracking the number in 2008. Read at face value, that's about as broad and unambiguous a risk-on signal as markets produce.

Now strip out two names — Alphabet and Amazon — and the picture changes. FactSet's own breakdown shows that excluding just those two, the aggregate surprise falls from 29.2 percent to 10.9 percent, and blended growth falls from 50.4 percent to 32.0 percent. Roughly half the headline beat, and most of the record surprise, is coming from two AI-capex-linked mega-caps. The forward multiple on the index, for context, is 20 times earnings, only marginally above its five-year average of 19.9 — so this isn't a story about an obviously stretched valuation. It's a story about concentration. The record close is real, but the breadth behind it is thinner than the headline growth rate implies.

One caveat on the numbers themselves: that FactSet cut is dated August 7th, so a handful more companies have reported since, and the exact figures will have drifted a little. But the direction — two mega-caps doing most of the work — doesn't reverse in a week. Treat it as an early-August read on composition, not a same-day print.

So what does that mean going forward. It means the durability of this rally now rests disproportionately on the next big AI-capex print — Nvidia's upcoming earnings are the obvious test — rather than on broad corporate participation. If that print disappoints, the index doesn't have the cushion of eighty-plus companies carrying the load; it has two. And if you're reading the index level alone as a broad risk-on signal, you're reading past the composition that actually produced it. There's a factor-rotation corollary here too. For the rally to keep melting up without leaning on those same two names, breadth has to improve — cyclicals and value have to start carrying more of the load. If they don't, the rotation that allocators keep waiting for stalls out even as the headline index climbs, and you get a rising tape that is quietly getting more concentrated, not less. That's the opposite of what a healthy broadening looks like.

That same pattern — a strong surface with a thinner reality underneath — shows up again in digital dollars. Tokenized U.S. Treasury products have grown to $16.21 billion across 87 distinct products as of August 9th, according to rwa.xyz data, and for the first time they're growing faster in absolute terms than stablecoins themselves. Circle's USYC leads the category at $3.00 billion, with BlackRock's BUIDL close behind at $2.68 billion. Total tokenized real-world-asset value across all categories, not just Treasuries, reached $38.17 billion the same day.

Here's the correction worth making explicitly, because the popular framing gets it wrong. The common story is that the GENIUS Act already bars Circle and Tether from paying yield on stablecoins, and that this ban is actively pushing capital out of stablecoins and into yield-bearing tokenized Treasuries right now. That's not quite right. The statute's language is clear that no payment stablecoin issuer may pay yield to holders — but legal analysis of the Act is equally clear that it doesn't take effect until January 2027 at the earliest, and only sooner if regulators finalize implementing rules ahead of schedule. So as of today, that ban isn't yet binding. What we're actually watching is yield-seeking capital moving toward tokenized Treasuries ahead of a rule that isn't live yet — call it anticipatory positioning rather than a completed regulatory reallocation. Stablecoins, to be clear, remain far larger in absolute terms; this is a growth-rate crossover, not a size crossover.

But the direction of travel is the point. On-chain dollar demand is starting to migrate toward instruments with direct interest-rate sensitivity, and that changes the plumbing. A stablecoin pays its holder nothing; a tokenized Treasury pays the front-end policy rate. As collateral shifts from the first toward the second, the crypto-native funding base becomes rate-sensitive in a way it simply wasn't before. That tightens the link between Fed policy and crypto collateral markets — a cut or a hold at the front end would transmit into on-chain yields far more directly than it did a year ago — and it does so well before the legal driver even switches on. The structural shift the headline points to is real, but it's in its early, forward-looking phase, not a done deal.

The same shape — strong on top, already repricing underneath — is showing up a third time, in credit. Private credit spreads have widened by roughly 50 to 100 basis points since late 2025, even as public high-yield spreads sit pinned near their richest decile in history around 271 basis points. That's a genuine decoupling. Public high-yield, the more liquid and more closely watched tier, is still priced as if very little can go wrong. Private credit, the less liquid tier where lenders hold loans to maturity and have to mark them more deliberately, is already moving the other way as loan supply normalizes and underwriting conditions tighten.

So what's the takeaway there. The tier of the credit market that's supposed to move first on deteriorating risk — the liquid, publicly traded tier — is the one still priced for calm. The tier that usually reacts slower is the one already repricing. That's backwards from the textbook sequence, and it means the cushion in public high-yield against any genuine growth scare is thinner than the spread level alone suggests. And thin cushions in liquid markets don't unwind gently. If a real growth scare does hit, high-yield doesn't have room to bleed wider gradually the way it would from a normal starting point — the repricing from the richest decile in history is more likely to be fast than orderly. The place that looks calmest is the place with the least margin for error.

Pull the three threads together. The S&P's record is a breadth illusion resting on two names. On-chain dollar demand is beginning to shift toward rate-sensitive instruments ahead of a regulatory driver that isn't even active yet. And private credit is already marking risk wider while public high-yield refuses to follow. In every case, the marketed number — the index level, the stablecoin float, the tight public spread — looks stronger than the number sitting just beneath it. With no fresh macro catalyst until the FOMC minutes later this week, that composition, not a new print, is the story worth carrying into the next few sessions.
