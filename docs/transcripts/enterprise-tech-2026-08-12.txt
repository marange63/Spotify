This week the binding constraint on enterprise AI keeps climbing the stack. First it was whether demand was real. Then whether capacity could serve it. Then whether usage converts to revenue. This week's evidence says the frontier has moved one level further: can enterprises even measure the return. Adoption keeps scaling, but proof that it pays remains the scarce commodity.

Start with Atlassian, which reported fiscal fourth-quarter and full-year 2026 results last week. Total revenue grew 28% year-over-year to $1.8 billion, and cloud revenue rose 31% to $1.213 billion, both accelerations. Subscription annual recurring revenue reached $6.6 billion, increasing 23% year-over-year, and the company swung back to GAAP profitability. On the surface, a strong print. But guidance tells a different story. Atlassian says total revenue growth next fiscal year is expected to be approximately 13.0%, roughly half this year's 28%. Subscription ARR growth is guided to approximately 18.0%, down from 23%. That is a sharp deceleration, and it could mean two very different things: either Atlassian is sandbagging, which is its habit, or the tailwind from moving big customers off Data Center onto cloud is finally running out. The company isn't saying which, and neither should we.

But the distinction matters beyond one vendor. If the slowdown is migration saturation, it's an early read-through for every on-prem-to-cloud software company still riding that same one-time tailwind. Growth that comes from moving existing customers onto cloud is a different, more finite thing than growth from selling those customers something genuinely new. The first kind runs out the moment the migration is done. The second kind doesn't. Which of the two Atlassian is now left leaning on is exactly the question its own guidance quietly raises.

Here's the part that matters most. In the release announcing those results, CEO Mike Cannon-Brookes said Atlassian's MCP server and Teamwork Graph CLI, the connective tissue that lets AI agents plug into a company's Jira and Confluence data, "hit 1 million MAU, making it the fastest-growing integration surface in company history." More than a million monthly active users, and it more than doubled in a single quarter. That is a real adoption signal. But nowhere in the earnings release does Atlassian attach a dollar figure to it. No revenue, no ARR, no monetization language at all, just usage. Compare that with what Salesforce disclosed one day earlier this week, where agent usage and a real, growing revenue line moved together. At Atlassian, the same agent-platform story is playing out with the proof half missing. Usage is scaling. Whether it's worth anything to the business is still an open question.

That gap, between what's being adopted and what's been proven to pay off, is exactly the constraint IBM is now trying to sell a solution to. On August 6th, IBM's Apptio unit put a new product called AI Value and ROI into public preview, a FinOps-style tool meant to tie AI spending, including token costs, back to measurable business outcomes. General availability is planned for the third quarter. IBM cited Gartner research to make its case for why this category needs to exist at all: "84% of finance leaders have not been able to measure the ROI of AI initiatives. Among those who tried to measure ROI, roughly two in five succeeded in quantifying returns." Read that again. Not that returns were poor, that most finance leaders can't even measure them. That's the hardest data point yet for the idea that proof of concept, not capability, is what's actually scarce in this market right now.

Worth being clear-eyed here: Apptio AI Value and ROI has no named customers and no disclosed pricing. It's an announcement, not a deployment, exactly the kind of distinction worth holding onto rather than crediting as traction. The real news isn't the product, it's the market condition it's responding to. When a legacy vendor builds an entire FinOps category around the admission that finance can't measure AI payback, that tells you where the budget conversation inside enterprises has actually moved. And IBM won't have that category to itself. Cloud-cost incumbents like Flexera, and Datadog's own cloud cost management line, already sell into exactly this budget, and they'll move to contest an AI-ROI layer the moment it looks like real spend. The category is being drawn now, before anyone has proven they can actually measure the thing it's built to measure. It cuts both ways for next year's software and cloud spending: credible ROI measurement could justify and accelerate budgets, or it could expose weak returns and choke them off. Either way, accountability is now doing as much work in the sales cycle as capability was doing a year ago.

Even with that measurement gap wide open, deployment keeps pushing into harder territory. On August 5th, the U.S. Army Human Resources Command became the first Department of War organization to run Salesforce's Agentforce inside an Impact Level 5 environment, the government's high-security authorization tier, through a new offering called Missionforce National Security. The system is meant to support 9.2 million soldiers, veterans, and family members. That's a genuine trust milestone in the most conservative buying environment there is, federal defense procurement, and it says enterprises aren't waiting for the ROI question to resolve before they push agents into the highest-governance corners of their operations. But be precise about what this is: an authorization to operate, not evidence of measured value. It belongs on the adoption side of the ledger, not the proof-it-pays side. The two are advancing on separate, uneven tracks.

One more thread worth a sentence, because it ties directly back to that Atlassian MCP number. As MCP adoption scales, so does the security debt underneath it. Anaconda acquired a company called Enkrypt AI on August 4th to add MCP security and governance to its platform, its third acquisition in about three months. Enkrypt's own pre-acquisition research is the source of the headline claim, so treat it as directional rather than audited, but the scale of what it found is striking on its face: scans across 25,000 MCP servers turned up vulnerabilities affecting the large majority of servers checked. Adoption is outrunning security review, the same pattern showing up on both sides of the agentic-platform story this week.

So pull it together. Adoption is no longer the question. A million monthly users connecting agents to Atlassian's data, agents authorized to run inside U.S. Army systems, agent platforms multiplying across vendors, none of that is in doubt anymore. What's still unresolved, at Atlassian, inside enterprise finance departments per Gartner, and now inside the security layer under MCP itself, is whether anyone can prove the deployment was worth it, and whether it's safe. This week, on every axis, the proof lagged the rollout.
