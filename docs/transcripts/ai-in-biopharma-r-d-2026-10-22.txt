Good morning. It's Thursday, October 22nd, 2026, and this is your briefing on AI in biopharma R&D.

This week, the AI drug-design layer repriced itself on numbers no outsider can check. The clearest case is Isomorphic Labs. It's the right place to open, because it answers a question this show has been asking all week.

On Friday, and again yesterday, we asked whether Isomorphic would put an AI-designed drug into humans before year-end, as Demis Hassabis pledged. That deadline is now about ten weeks out. Yesterday the Boston Globe reported that executives confirmed the company is still in preclinical development. They declined to say whether they still expect to meet the goal. That is not a missed deadline. No one has announced one, and declining to confirm is not the same as abandoning. But it is the company going quiet on its own target. And this target has already slid once. In January 2025, Hassabis told the Financial Times that Isomorphic planned to begin human trials for its first AI-designed drug by the end of that year. That date passed with no disclosed dosing. Coverage reports he reset the goal to end-2026 at Davos this January, though we couldn't verify that against a transcript.

We also found no IND, no CTA, and no ClinicalTrials.gov listing under Isomorphic's name. That is an absence, not a documented refusal. But it is conspicuous for a company this prominent. One outlet did claim this spring that an Isomorphic compound had cleared for human testing. That molecule, ISM8969, is actually an Insilico Medicine asset, IND-cleared for Insilico in January. The two similar names appear to have been conflated. So the clean read stands: no disclosed Isomorphic candidate in humans.

Meanwhile, the money is moving the other way. Bloomberg, as relayed by the Globe, reports Isomorphic is raising at a forty-billion-dollar valuation. The UK outlet The Cap Table puts the early talks at forty to fifty billion, with no agreement reached. The comparison point is the two-point-one-billion-dollar Series B that closed in May. Isomorphic never disclosed that round's valuation. The only figure in circulation is a single hedged estimate of roughly seventeen and a half billion. So call the step-up somewhere between a bit more than double and nearly triple, in five months. Both ends of that comparison are secondhand.

What underwrites that markup is a capability claim. In February, Isomorphic published a technical report on its drug-design engine, IsoDDE. Outside scientists have informally nicknamed it "AlphaFold 4." Nature quotes Columbia's Mohammed AlQuraishi calling it a major advance. Here's the company's headline number. On a held-out set of 334 novel complexes, IsoDDE hit 39 percent high-fidelity accuracy, against 17 percent for AlphaFold 3. That's more than double its predecessor. But it comes from Isomorphic's own report, and nobody has replicated it. Nobody can. IsoDDE is fully proprietary, with no weights and no architecture disclosed. Nature reports that rival developers are "left guessing" how to reproduce its results. So the most-cited capability claim in AI drug design has no independent verification path. And the valuation is being marked up on that same unaudited claim.

The bear case has a limit, though. Structure prediction was never supposed to fix toxicology, ADMET, or manufacturing. So a slipping first-in-human date is weak evidence against the binding model itself. It is strong evidence about what actually gates the run from preclinical to IND. Benchmark superiority hasn't touched that gate. That's why a better model hasn't yet compressed the timeline.

One campaign this week did publish its numbers, including the ones that failed. That's the useful contrast. Full disclosure: Anthropic makes the model behind this show. On Tuesday, Anthropic reported a design campaign run inside Claude Science against five scorpion neurotoxins. An internal research model, more capable than the public product, worked largely autonomously for about 64 hours. It started from one prompt and four follow-ups. It generated more than 318,000 candidate protein binders. The team sent 211 of them for synthesis and binding assays at commercial partners Twist Bioscience and Adaptyv Bio. Seventeen cleared Anthropic's binding bar. That's roughly eight percent. The best two, against the Mexican Cn2 toxin, bound at single-digit nanomolar affinity. That's a strong result for a cold-start campaign. One sourcing note: we couldn't retrieve Anthropic's original post, so these figures come from detailed secondary write-ups of it.

Now the limits. No design bound more than one toxin. The attempt at a pan-toxin binder failed outright. And Anthropic was explicit that the assays measured binding only, not neutralization in cells or animals. There's no antivenom claim and no therapeutic claim. Because the model was an internal version, customers of the shipped product can't reproduce the run either. So the signal isn't the headline count. It's the eight-percent hit rate and the failed generalization. Ideation is now cheap and fast. Turning a design into a usable hit still runs through physical synthesis and assay cycles. That's the gating cost. It's the same thing Isomorphic's silence is telling you, from the other direction. One research shop, Citrini, has already turned the result into a call on contract research and tools names. Treat that as a market reaction, not a demand signal.

These two stories also show a split in architecture. IsoDDE is a purpose-built structure engine. Claude Science is a general-purpose language model orchestrating tools and wet-lab logistics. They aren't competing on the same axis, which is why both can post results in the same month. The third model in view is a different kind of specialist. Xaira Therapeutics launched in 2024 with more than a billion dollars of committed capital. Its team includes developers of RFdiffusion and RFantibody from David Baker's academic lab. Earlier this month, after two and a half years in stealth, Xaira disclosed its X-Design antibody platform. Its Vega model generated 182 nanobody sequences de novo against a specific epitope on its lead oncology target, XA-1. A second program targets a GPCR.

That's the whole disclosure. There's no binding rate, no preclinical data, and no candidate stage. CEO Marc Tessier-Lavigne told Fierce Biotech the goal is antibodies that are "drug-ready from the start." But developability is a claim only toxicology and manufacturing can settle. And the core architectures trace to openly published academic work. So Xaira's differentiation rests on a proprietary layer on top, which it didn't describe. That's the pattern across this layer. Model IP starts in academia and ends up walled off inside well-funded startups. For more than a billion dollars of capital, Xaira has so far shown a methods post, not a result.

All three stories point at the same bottleneck, and it's physical. So who sells that bottleneck? Danaher is one answer. Earlier this month it announced an AI-automated lab at its Abcam unit. It projects up to eight times faster reagent discovery, and up to tenfold annual output, once the lab is at scale in early 2027. Those are targets, not measured results. And the product is research reagents, not therapeutics. That's a much lower bar. A reagent either binds or it doesn't, and you find out fast and cheap. That's precisely why automation is furthest along there. If agentic design loops spread, the incremental demand lands on synthesis and assay capacity. It lands on tools, reagents, and contract research, more than on model licences.

So pull the threads together. The question that would settle any of this isn't a benchmark. It's a dosed human patient. Across this show's recent episodes, we haven't had a single fresh prospective clinical readout from an AI-discovery platform. Today is no different. That has a capital-markets consequence. Closed weights at the frontier degrade the field's ability to benchmark. That raises the cost of capital for any platform without an independently replicated result. It favours companies with clinical assets over companies with papers. And if Isomorphic reaches year-end with no disclosed trial, the read-through hits every preclinical design platform marked on benchmarks, Xaira included.

For R&D spend, the implication isn't contraction. It's reallocation, toward data, and now specifically toward synthesis and assay capacity. And the probability-of-success lever, the thing that would justify these valuations, is untouched by anything disclosed this month. Watch three things before year-end. First, whether Isomorphic confirms or quietly drops its human-trial target. Second, whether Xaira or Anthropic discloses a developability or neutralization result, rather than a binding count. Third, whether anyone in this space publishes a number an outsider can replicate.

That's your briefing. Have a good day.
