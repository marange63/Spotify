Good morning. This is the Capital Markets Radar for Friday, November 20th, 2026.

Yesterday we laid out three competing explanations for this week's tape. Supply absorption from the Anthropic IPO, a de-grossing wave, or genuine doubt about AI valuations. We said the debut was the next test. Thursday ran that test. The evidence now leans away from an AI story and toward market-wide de-risking, with no hedge working except the dollar.

Start with the debut itself. A standing disclosure, stated once: Anthropic makes the model behind this show. Per Yahoo Finance, Anthropic opened at $111.50, a 34% jump from its $83 IPO price. It then cooled through the session and closed at $97.17, up 17.1%. That handed back roughly half the opening pop. So it was a solid debut, not a euphoric one.

Meanwhile, the incumbents didn't ease. Per CNBC, the Dow fell 2.19% and closed below 50,000 for the first time since June. The S&P 500 fell 2.02%, and the Nasdaq fell 2.25%. When the Dow falls about as hard as the Nasdaq, the selling isn't concentrated in tech. That's an index-wide drop, not a rotation from old AI into new AI.

Now score yesterday's three hypotheses against that tape. Supply absorption predicted the incumbents would ease once the debut traded. Instead, the selling broadened. Valuation doubt predicted the debut would struggle. It didn't, even though it faded from the open. De-grossing predicted broad selling regardless of AI exposure. That's what showed up. So the evidence leans toward broad de-risking.

Two cautions before you bank that. First, one session can't crown a hypothesis, and these three aren't mutually exclusive. More than $81 billion of new stock still has to be funded from somewhere. So supply absorption can still be running underneath a broader de-grossing. Second, don't over-read the debut. A first-day gain on a scarce float, with a large strategic backstop at the IPO price, is weak evidence on AI valuations generally. That's the same scarcity argument this desk made yesterday. So the debut argues against AI doubt driving the selloff. It doesn't prove that AI valuations are fine.

One wrinkle cuts against a pure breadth story. Per Motley Fool, Nvidia closed at $145.98, down 3.30%. That's more than a point worse than the S&P. So even inside a broad selloff, the AI bellwether carried something extra. Our read is that it's the leadership discount Nvidia has worn since its CEO exit, layered on top of the market-wide move. The breadth argument holds at the index level. It just doesn't make Nvidia clean.

For the month, per Bespoke, all three major indexes were down more than 3% through November 18th. That would make this the first November since 2008 with all three that deep in the red. The count predates Thursday's drop, and the month isn't over, so call it on track, not settled. The so-what: this stopped being a story about whether AI capex pays off. It's now a story about who is still levered into a market that's de-risking across the board.

If this is broad de-risking, the next question is where the money went. On Thursday, almost nowhere. Start with bonds. The 10-year Treasury closed around 5.06%, per Investing.com data. That's essentially flat on a day equities fell 2%. No duration bid showed up at all. The rate impulse didn't intensify either. A December hike was already almost fully priced before Thursday, so this wasn't a fresh discount-rate shock.

Gold didn't help. December gold futures fell 1.51% to settle at $3,685.50. There are two readings of that, and they imply different things. One is a real-rate and dollar repricing of a zero-yield asset, which lasts as long as hike pricing does. The other is winners being sold to meet margin in a de-grossing tape, which reverses once the deleveraging ends. Nothing in hand separates them yet.

Bitcoin didn't help either. One report put it at $61,273, its lowest since the start of July. Spot bitcoin ETFs shed $306.4 million on November 18th alone, per crypto dot news, and the monthly outflow tally kept growing. In Sunday's briefing, this desk warned that if the weekend leverage hadn't cleared, the weekend low wasn't a clean base. It wasn't. The rebound we reported on Wednesday has now reversed to a lower low. Parts of the crypto press blame rotation into the Anthropic IPO, but that's reporter inference, not traced flow. The simpler reading is that bitcoin traded as one more long-duration risk asset, not as a hedge.

The only asset that moved the right way was the dollar. Per BabyPips, the dollar index notched a sixth straight up session, to its highest level since April 2025. On Tuesday, this desk said the traditional hedges weren't hedging, and that cash and the dollar were the real shelter. That pattern just held through a larger selloff.

So why didn't bonds hedge? Thursday's Philadelphia Fed manufacturing survey offers a mechanism. The headline index came in at 6.3, against a forecast of 18. That's a big activity miss. But the prices-paid measure rose to 58.2. Weak activity with firm prices is a stagflation-adjacent mix. In that regime, bad growth news can't rally bonds, because the shock runs through inflation and a hawkish Fed. The stock-bond correlation turns positive, and duration stops diversifying. That's our inference. It also rests on one regional survey, so don't build a regime call on it alone.

There's a risk here the market may be under-reading. A Fed priced to hike into a soft activity print raises the odds of a policy error. And the second-order effects compound. When stocks and bonds stop offsetting each other, balanced and risk-parity books lose their diversifier. They have to de-lever both legs at once. That can turn a de-grossing episode into a self-reinforcing one. Meanwhile, a dollar that keeps climbing tightens funding for the most leveraged borrowers. The takeaway: in an inflation-driven selloff, cash is the only real diversifier.

That funding squeeze leads straight to credit. Credit is where broad de-risking either stays contained or compounds. And credit just moved past where we said it would stop. On Wednesday, this desk called broad high yield contained, with stress confined to the CCC tier. FRED's data now says otherwise. The ICE BofA high-yield index spread hit 3.47% on November 18th, up from 3.20% on November 12th. That's about 27 basis points of widening in roughly four trading days.

Keep that in proportion. At 347 basis points, the broad index is still at a modest level, not a stress level. Our research found no named November catalyst, such as a large CCC default. So this may be an existing trend continuing rather than a fresh shock. Treating it as a credit break would be an over-read. Dismissing it would be an under-read. For the first time in our coverage, stress has moved beyond the CCC tier into the broad index. For reference, FRED's CCC-and-lower spread stood at 13.29% on November 17th. Take that as a level only, since our earlier CCC figure came from a different source.

One important limit applies. This FRED data runs only through November 18th. It doesn't capture Thursday's 2% equity drop, so whether credit followed equities on Thursday is simply unknown. The other open thread is the ING data-center loan auction. This desk has flagged it since last week as the only credit-priced test of AI risk. Bids were due Thursday, and no result has surfaced in our sources. We're not inferring an outcome from that silence. The so-what: the next few spread prints decide whether Thursday's selloff bleeds into credit. That's the signal that would turn a de-grossing episode into something structurally worse.

That also revises a line from yesterday. We said the signals to watch were flows and issuance, not FedWatch. Flows still matter. But if this is macro de-grossing rather than AI doubt, AI news won't stop the tape. A strong debut didn't, and neither did Nvidia's beat. That holds until the rate and inflation impulse turns. So the catalysts that matter most now are macro.

On the calendar, with no outcomes assumed. The ING auction result, Anthropic's second session, and the next high-yield spread prints. Then PCE on November 25th, payrolls on December 4th, the Fed's decision on December 9th, and CPI the day after that. Until then, the test is simple. Does anything other than the dollar start acting like a hedge again?
