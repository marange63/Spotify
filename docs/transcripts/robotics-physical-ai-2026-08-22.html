Good morning. It's Saturday, August 22nd, and this is your robotics and physical AI briefing.

This week on this show has been a story told in valuations — Unitree's Shanghai debut, Anduril reportedly raising near a hundred billion dollars, Figure's roughly thirty-nine billion dollar private mark. Today the story is different. It's about who physically builds the machines, and the answer sits awkwardly against the trade policy Washington has been writing.

Start with Waymo. We covered the California Public Utilities Commission's eighteen-county approval in full on the fifteenth, so treat that as background, not today's news. What's new is operational. On August 19th, Waymo opened its sixth-generation robotaxi, the Ojai, to all riders in Los Angeles, Phoenix and San Francisco. Roughly three hundred Ojai units are in the commercial fleet today, against a company projection of five thousand by year-end. So more than nine in ten of the vehicles Waymo's year-end plan depends on have not been built yet. What is real is the import pace. Seven hundred twenty-five Ojai units entered the United States in July alone, and cumulative imports of the platform now run past thirty-two hundred vehicles.

Here's the part that matters. The Ojai is built on a Zeekr platform — that's Geely, the Chinese automaker. Waymo calls the vehicle cheaper to build, operate and maintain, but that claim is qualitative. The company has published no per-vehicle cost, no cost-reduction percentage, and no cost per mile. What we have instead is customs data and third-party math. US import declarations value the bare Zeekr chassis at roughly thirty-eight thousand dollars before it ever touches a Waymo integration line. Add the reported tariff and the autonomy hardware, and one outlet's calculation lands near a hundred and three thousand dollars per vehicle before final US assembly. Set that against analyst estimates putting Waymo's older Jaguar I-Pace retrofit as high as two hundred thousand dollars, and the Chinese platform still wins even carrying a triple-digit tariff.

Now the caveat that keeps this honest, because it's larger than it looks. The trade press cannot agree on the tariff rate itself. Some outlets cite about a hundred and two and a half percent on these shipments. Others cite a hundred and twenty-seven and a half percent on the same shipments. Neither figure has been traced to a published customs ruling. So the customs classification of America's most important robotaxi is not publicly settled, which means the cost base underneath Waymo's fleet plan isn't either. The direction of the cost advantage is credible. The magnitude is undisclosed by the company and contested by everyone else.

Set that against policy. This show has already reported Washington walling out Chinese robots this summer — the FCC banning new authorizations for Chinese-made humanoid and quadruped robots in late July, and the Section 232 drone tariff signed on August 13th. And yet in the same stretch, hundreds of Chinese-built robotaxi chassis kept clearing the port, tariffs and all, because they still land cheaper than the alternative. Whether the emerging connected-vehicle restrictions reach a chassis that reporting says arrives stripped of Chinese connectivity hardware and gets its electronics fitted here is a genuinely open legal question. Our follow-up research could not obtain the scope language of the governing rule, so I'm not going to tell you the answer. What is fact is the asymmetry. The same policy season that is pricing Chinese humanoids and drones out of the American market has not stopped America's leading robotaxi fleet from being built on a Chinese vehicle.

That asymmetry is a risk on the asset side of the robotaxi business, and the bull case doesn't price it. Waymo's cheapest path to five thousand vehicles runs through a supply route whose legal durability nobody can currently verify. If a rule change closes it, the hit lands on the fleet cost curve and on the year-end scale target at the same time. That's a policy risk sitting inside a capex plan, which is not where investors usually go looking for one.

The same industrial base shows up one layer down, in components. Chinese rare-earth magnet makers just put hard numbers on a trend this show has tracked since the eighteenth, when the NdPr benchmark was up more than twenty-one percent in a month. JL MAG Rare-Earth disclosed first-half net profit up thirty-one to fifty-one percent, and its robotics and servo-motor revenue alone up about ninety percent. Two caveats. Those are earnings-preview ranges, not audited finals. And separately, Beijing tightened dual-use export licensing in late June on elements including samarium and gadolinium — but nothing in the sourcing links that move to this profit growth. Resist the tidy causal story. The simpler read is a robotics demand boom that Chinese suppliers were already positioned to serve, with licensing a lever Beijing is holding rather than one it has pulled.

Either way, the so-what is the one this show argued on the twenty-first. If the robot brain commoditizes, advantage relocates down to actuator and magnet cost — and that line runs through China, whether the machine on the end of it is a humanoid or a robotaxi.

Now the counter-strategy, because not every American autonomy program is importing its way to scale. Aurora Innovation held its first investor town hall on August 20th and said it's targeting at least two hundred driverless trucks running commercially by year-end, up from twenty to twenty-five at the end of the third quarter. That's nearly the entire year's growth compressed into a single quarter, so treat it as a target, not a count. Aurora's manufacturing answer is domestic: production with Roush in Michigan aimed at twenty trucks a week by October, about a thousand trucks a year of capacity.

Put the two American autonomy leaders side by side. Waymo imported seven hundred twenty-five vehicles in a single month. Aurora is targeting the equivalent of roughly eighty a month, and hasn't reached it yet. That's about an order of magnitude, in favor of buying rather than building, and it's the cleanest read available on what domestic manufacturing actually costs in this cycle. There's a labor read here too. If Aurora is even close to its number, autonomous trucking reaches a larger fielded fleet this year than humanoids do, on a far narrower operating domain. Near-term displacement in physical AI shows up in freight before it shows up on a factory floor.

Last, briefly, the breadth of the base underneath all of this. The World Humanoid Robot Games opened in Beijing today with more than two thousand robots from six hundred sixty-six teams, and team participation up a hundred and thirty-eight percent year on year. The field is overwhelmingly Chinese-domestic — Chinese companies and universities fielded nearly all of those robots on their own. That's a real data point on the depth and iteration speed of China's humanoid ecosystem. It is not a data point on reliability. This show made exactly that distinction on the twentieth, when Beijing's marquee humanoid turned out to be teleoperated. And the games opened today, so there are no results yet — only entry counts.

Pull the threads together. The machines actually reaching fleets at scale this year are wheeled, not bipedal: robotaxis and trucks. Today's humanoid news is a competition, not a customer. The binding constraint on Western physical AI right now is not model capability. It's who can build the hardware cheapest and under which trade rules — and this week the cheapest path ran straight through a Chinese supply chain that policy has not closed. Watch two things next. Whether Waymo's weekly ride number, stuck at past five hundred thousand across two consecutive quarterly prints, finally moves. And whether any rule change actually reaches an imported robotaxi chassis.

That's the briefing. Talk to you next time.
