Good morning. It's Saturday, September 5th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

On August 30th this show called chip tariffs a live policy option. Something the administration was reportedly weighing, with no rate, no timing, and no country treatment set. On September 3rd, Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick said the mechanism out loud, on camera, on the sidelines of the G20 Innovation Ministers Meeting. Before we go further, one correction to the frame. This is not the first semiconductor tariff.

A Section 232 duty has been in force since January. Presidential Proclamation 11002, signed January 14th and effective the next day, put a 25 percent levy on a narrow set of advanced logic chips. Narrow is the operative word here. Qualification runs off specific processing-performance and memory-bandwidth thresholds, with Nvidia's H200 and AMD's MI325X named as examples that clear them. There are standing carve-outs for data centers, for R&D, for startups, and for public-sector buyers. So a tariff instrument already exists. Today it reaches a handful of accelerator parts and very little else.

What Lutnick described on Thursday is an expansion of that regime, not a new one. Two things change. The scope widens from a list of chips to finished goods that contain chips, which means laptops, game consoles, and data-center servers. And the relief structure gets formalized around where you build. His own words: if you build in America, you get tariff relief, and if you don't build in America, you pay tariffs. That is the real news. A duty that currently touches a few accelerator SKUs is being redrawn to touch the entire bill of materials of anything with a chip inside it. Customs treatment becomes a function of where you site capital spending. That is industrial policy delivered through the tariff schedule, company by company, rather than through a CHIPS-style grant with published rules for everyone. Lutnick also said every company already knows the tariffs are coming. Which tells you the announcement effect is mostly spent. The news from here is the structure, not the surprise.

One of his supporting claims is doing real argumentative work, and it doesn't hold up. He cited a US-Taiwan agreement requiring Taiwan to bring 40 percent of its chip production to America, and used it as the template for how a relief threshold might be set. Taiwan's government has publicly rejected that number. Taiwanese trade negotiators told Washington that Taipei would not relocate 40 percent of its semiconductor production, and the vice premier called the idea impossible. The signed framework does something quite different. It lowers the US tariff on Taiwanese goods from 20 percent to 15 percent. In return, Taiwanese companies invest 250 billion dollars in the US on their own plans, and Taipei provides a further 250 billion in credit guarantees. No relocation clause appears in the deal terms. So when you hear 40 percent cited as precedent, hear it as Lutnick's characterization, disputed by the counterparty. The so-what is that the template underneath the coming policy is shakier than the confident delivery suggests. And for the expanded version there is still no published rate, no HTS product scope, and no effective date. This is an interview, not a proclamation.

Now the premise the whole structure rests on. Building a fab in America is treated as a decision about capital. Commit the money, earn the relief. TSMC's own leadership just said that isn't how it works. At SEMICON Taiwan on September 3rd, deputy co-chief operating officer Cliff Hou said the company's equipment purchasing requirement rose to 1.5 times an earlier baseline after a single quarter, and reached 1.9 times by July. That is roughly a 90 percent increase in six months. Two cautions before that number travels anywhere. These are conference remarks relayed by trade press. No TSMC release or transcript is available, so treat the percentages as reported rather than independently verified. And nobody has published the denominator. This is not a claim about 90 percent more capital spending. It is a claim about the shape of the order curve.

The constraint claim is the part that matters. TSMC is advancing construction on nearly 20 fabs worldwide right now, 13 in Taiwan and five to six overseas. Hou said construction labor, equipment installation, and supply-chain resources all face practical limits. In plain terms, the queue is not money anymore. It is people and installation crews. Note the incentive before you bank it. A foundry with pricing power benefits from telling customers at a Taiwan trade show that capacity is physically rationed, so read the emphasis with that in mind. But the direction is defensible, and it is a claim about sequencing. Orders are arriving faster than anyone can install them.

That forces a correction to how this show has been framing the AI buildout. All week we have located the binding constraint in advanced packaging slots and HBM allocation. On Tuesday we said it in so many words: logic wafer starts were never the queue. TSMC's deputy co-chief operating officer has now named a constraint sitting upstream of both, and it is construction throughput. So the honest picture is three constraints stacked on each other. Packaging slots, memory bits, and physical build-out. A tariff that rewards onshoring cannot touch the third one. You cannot lobby your way to more cleanroom crews. Worse, a fab pulled onshore to clear a relief threshold bids for the same scarce construction labor TSMC says is already maxed out. So the policy's first-order effect on total supply could be to slow the aggregate build rather than speed it up.

For the equipment makers, that is a timing problem rather than a demand problem. ASML, Applied Materials, Lam Research, and KLA keep taking orders. If crews cannot install the tools, backlog builds while recognized revenue lags behind it. An order that cannot be installed is deferred revenue, and lead times stretch without any change at all in the underlying demand signal. The tell to watch is whether those companies start describing backlog growth and shipment timing as two separate stories on their next calls, because that is what this constraint looks like when it reaches a financial statement.

So if capacity is capped physically, where is the capex dollar actually going? TrendForce's memory report, published August 25th, has an answer, and it is price rather than volume. Take these as projections, not realized contract prices. TrendForce expects DRAM and NAND flash combined to account for 47 percent of major cloud providers' total capital spending this year, rising to 68 percent in 2027. Now set that against the supply line for the same year. Combined server DRAM and HBM bit supply grows just 27 percent in 2027. Put those two numbers together and the story inverts. Hyperscalers are not buying two-thirds more memory. They are paying two-thirds of the budget for roughly a quarter more bits. On the price side, and off a different base period, TrendForce projects server DRAM contract prices jumping about 270 percent this year. So this is not a capex reallocation. It is a margin transfer from cloud buyers to the three memory makers, Samsung, SK hynix, and Micron. And it is a ratio that would collapse the moment pricing normalizes, rather than a permanent change in what a data center is made of.

Put the three threads together and be precise about what the tariff actually moves. It can move where assembly and capital spending get sited, and it can move landed cost. It cannot move yield, it cannot move packaging throughput, and it cannot move memory bit supply, which is where every constraint this show has tracked actually lives. Meanwhile a duty on finished goods lands on server OEMs and on hyperscalers importing assembled systems, not on the fabs the policy is meant to influence. You can reprice a bill of materials. You cannot relocate a fab on a tariff clock. And the two cost channels stack hardest on the buyers with no AI revenue to offset them. A cloud buyer can pass memory and tariff cost into the price of a GPU instance. A laptop or console maker mostly cannot. The forward test is whether this Section 232 expansion ever arrives as a published rate with a defined product scope and an effective date, or whether it stays a negotiated, company-by-company threshold. Because a discretionary standard rewards whoever can lobby it, not whoever actually builds.
