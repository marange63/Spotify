Digital Money, Crypto, and Market Structure

Transcript · November 12, 2026

Good morning. It's Thursday, November 12th, and this is your Cautious Optimism briefing on digital money.

Here's the frame for today. The on-chain dollar is shrinking at a pace this market hasn't seen since the 2022 bear market. At the same moment, a group of the world's biggest banks just put money down to build a new one. That's not a contradiction. It's two clocks running on two different horizons.

Start with Bitcoin, briefly, because the real story today is the cash underneath it. Bitcoin sat out yesterday's stock market rebound entirely, according to CoinDesk. That matters because just yesterday, this desk reported Bitcoin's correlation with the Nasdaq at a 99th-percentile reading. If that coupling were symmetric, Bitcoin should have caught a bid along with equities. It didn't. One day doesn't make a regime. But the pattern now looks asymmetric. Bitcoin seems to transmit equity selloffs without joining the rallies.

Meanwhile the ETF tape kept bleeding. U.S. spot Bitcoin ETFs lost ninety-five million dollars on Tuesday, a fourth straight day of net outflows, per SoSoValue. BlackRock's IBIT accounted for seventy-eight million of that, its eighth straight session of redemptions. Over the past twenty trading sessions, net outflows total roughly one point six billion dollars. After the hot October inflation print we covered yesterday, CME FedWatch now prices December hike odds at ninety percent. Crypto futures liquidations surged two hundred eighty-eight percent, to seven hundred twenty-five million dollars in a day, per CoinGlass. Trackers disagree on that total, so read it as directional. And the causal chain, from hot inflation to hike odds to liquidations, is CoinDesk's own synthesis, not a named strategist's call. Treat it as plausible, not proven.

There's a cohort angle too. On-chain analysis finds that roughly twenty-seven Bitcoin recently arrived at exchanges at a loss for every one moved at a profit. About sixty-five percent of short-term holder supply is now underwater. That cohort's average cost basis is seventy-five thousand six hundred dollars, inside the band this desk watched fail on last week's close. Two caveats. These are exchange deposits, not confirmed sales, and some may be collateral posting. And loss-heavy deposits can mark either a local capitulation low or the start of a deeper leg. The data doesn't say which. So read it as pressure building, not pressure resolved.

But the bigger exit isn't running through Bitcoin. It's running through the cash.

Glassnode reports that major stablecoin supply fell fourteen point two billion dollars over the past thirty days. That's the largest decline since the 2022 crypto bear market. It leaves total supply just below two hundred eighty billion dollars, a level not seen since September of last year. That's a different basket from the higher tracker total we cited on Tuesday, so don't subtract one from the other. All week we asked whether the float would find a floor. It hasn't. It has hit a 2022-era benchmark instead.

Zoom into the mechanics. Per CryptoQuant, Tether's USDT market cap fell six point six billion dollars over the sixty days through November 7th. That's more than double the three point two billion dollar drop over a comparable window in December 2022. By our arithmetic, nearly half of that sixty-day decline came in the final week, when USDT dropped three point one billion. So the drain is accelerating, not spreading evenly. USDC lost one point three one billion in that same week, to seventy point six billion.

Now the skeptical read, because the 2022 comparison can smuggle in the wrong connotation. December 2022 was a solvency and credit shock, in the wake of FTX's collapse. Tether today is a much larger base, about one hundred seventy-seven billion dollars. By our arithmetic, that sixty-day drop is under four percent of supply. That's a real contraction, not a run on the bank. When this desk checked on Sunday, both major pegs had held at par. This looks like orderly de-grossing, not panic.

What's causing it remains unproven. One candidate is market-makers and basis desks deleveraging. Another is plain risk-off redemptions. A third, and this is our inference only, is opportunity cost. A zero-yield on-chain dollar gets costlier to hold with a December hike ninety percent priced. The data can't separate them.

Here's the comparison that frames the stakes. By our arithmetic, that fourteen point two billion dollar stablecoin drop is roughly nine times the one point six billion dollar ETF outflow. On Monday, using a single week, this desk put that ratio at more than five times. The windows and sources differ, thirty calendar days against twenty trading sessions, so treat the ratio as approximate. But the direction is clear. The cash door is leaking far faster than the ETF door.

Now the other side of that ledger. While the float shrinks, the banks are building.

Bloomberg reports, citing people familiar with the matter, that twenty-one financial institutions plan a joint U.S. dollar stablecoin. Named members include Goldman Sachs, Citigroup, Santander and Deutsche Bank, and the group has committed around two hundred seventy million dollars. We're working from secondary relays of that reporting, and there's no press release or filing. Reporting describes the money as an operational investment, not the reserves that would back tokens in circulation. The issuing company is due to form in the second half of this year, with a target launch in the first half of 2027. Stated uses span wholesale, institutional and retail, specifically cross-border payments and the settlement of digital assets.

Two things we can't tell you. Nobody has disclosed whether the token runs on a public blockchain or a permissioned one. One outlet says flatly the venture "has not disclosed its preferred blockchain networks." And no source specifies which GENIUS Act issuer pathway it will use. Treat both as open questions.

The strategic map is more tangled than a clean split. JPMorgan is not in this twenty-one-member venture. Instead it's doubling down on its own proprietary JPM Coin and Kinexys rails. But JPMorgan is also in a separate effort, a shared tokenized-deposit network with Citi, Bank of America, Wells Fargo and more than a dozen other banks. That network, run through The Clearing House, also targets the first half of 2027. Notice that Citi sits in both camps. So these are overlapping coalitions, chasing the same settlement business in the same launch window. That's a fragmentation risk, with several bank rails splitting network effects instead of building one.

The analytic point is that the banks are betting on the payments rail, not on the trading float that's draining. The float can shrink cyclically while the rail gets built structurally. Keep the size in proportion, though. Two hundred seventy million dollars is an operating budget for a company that hasn't launched. Next to these members' balance sheets, that's an option on the rail, not a bet. A consortium of competitors also brings governance and speed problems a single issuer doesn't have. And the defensive reading is as strong as the bullish one. Banks may be protecting their deposit and payments franchises from non-bank issuers, not expressing conviction in on-chain dollars.

Either way, it opens a third front for Circle. This desk has already flagged USDC's economics as squeezed on both rates and float. A credible bank-distributed dollar token, even on a 2027 horizon, competes for the same settlement business. That threatens Circle's share, not just its yield.

And there's a reason banks are moving on stablecoins first. The Senate's cloture vote on the CLARITY Act, the crypto market-structure bill, failed in September. Democrats are reported to have won both chambers for the Congress that starts in January. Post-midterm reporting says they'll have little incentive to cut deals before then. That's single-sourced, through a secondary relay, so hold it loosely. Meanwhile, the GENIUS Act's stablecoin framework remains the one settled piece of federal crypto law. Our inference is that this keeps institutional build-out concentrated in stablecoins, not in products that hinge on unresolved SEC and CFTC jurisdiction.

So what's the strongest bull case, built only from what we actually know? The float is contracting in an orderly way, with pegs at par when last checked. Major banks are committing real money under a settled legal framework. And short-term holders depositing at a twenty-seven-to-one loss ratio is what capitulation can look like. That's the case. What it lacks is any reversal in the ETF outflow streak, and any floor in the stablecoin float.

Three things to watch. First, whether the thirty-day stablecoin contraction slows or deepens. Second, whether the ETF and IBIT outflow streaks finally break. Third, any disclosure from either bank effort on charter, chain or membership, and any on-record word from JPMorgan.

The thesis in one line. The cycle is draining the dollars already on-chain, while the institutions build the rail for the next ones.

That's the briefing for November 12th. Talk to you tomorrow.
