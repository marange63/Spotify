Space & the Orbital Economy

Transcript · September 20, 2026

Good morning, and welcome to Cautious Optimism Briefings for Sunday, September 20th, 2026, on the space and orbital economy.

This week the counterspace argument stopped being a speech and became a purchase order.

On Friday, September 18th, the Defense Innovation Unit and Space Systems Command picked Northrop Grumman and True Anomaly to build prototypes under a program called GHOST-R. That stands for GEO High-Resolution Optical Space-Based Tactical Reconnaissance. The goal is to prove that small satellites built with low-cost commercial technology can track both friendly and adversary satellites in geosynchronous orbit.

The schedule is specific. The prototypes are expected to launch in 2028. They would become operational RG-XX platforms by 2029. RG-XX is the Space Force program meant to replace the GSSAP spacecraft that do this job today.

Here is the callback. On September 16th this show argued that in a disclosure race, where every side's counterspace claims are unverifiable, the scarce good is not a weapon. It is knowing what is actually up there. We called that a demand argument for space domain awareness. Two days later, the Pentagon wrote that demand into prototype contracts.

The most revealing requirement is not image quality. Both teams must provide a plan for scaling their satellites to a full constellation that can track all publicly catalogued objects in GEO. That is a unit-cost requirement dressed as a technical one. A handful of exquisite inspectors cannot watch every object. Cheap, replaceable ones might.

The pairing matters too. A prime and a venture-backed entrant are building to the same requirement. That is price discovery. The Space Force learns what this mission should cost by watching two very different cost structures try to meet it. For True Anomaly, it is a government-funded reference customer. One caution: nothing in the reporting shows True Anomaly has flown a comparable mission. Treat the model as promising, not proven.

Now the skeptical turn. This is a prototype demo, not a production award. The 2028 launch and the 2029 operational date are plans, not commitments. And no dollar value appeared for either award in the reporting we reviewed, nor any per-satellite cost for GSSAP to benchmark against. The prototype price is the missing number.

Other numbers are public, and they are worth having. The Space Force's fiscal 2027 request includes 355 million dollars for RG-XX, the follow-on program, and the projections peak near a billion dollars in fiscal 2029. So the program has a budget path even though the prototypes do not have a published price.

The parallel contract vehicle is called Andromeda, a pool of fourteen vendors competing for task orders. Air and Space Forces Magazine reported in May that the Space Force raised that ceiling from 1.8 billion dollars to more than 6.2 billion. Those are ceilings for competed work, not obligated money. But the ceiling more than tripled before any GHOST-R prototype flew.

So the checkable test is this. Does that fiscal 2029 peak of roughly a billion dollars shrink because cheap satellites worked, or does it simply get filled? The Space Force is sizing demand faster than it is publishing prototype prices.

That brings us to the posture this hardware serves. The new material is thin but useful. A Space Force spokesperson said the on-orbit capabilities Air Force Secretary Troy Meink acknowledged on September 14th can be employed for offensive and defensive purposes at the direction of combatant commands. That matters for GHOST-R. Satellites whose declared job is to inspect adversary spacecraft in GEO raise the stakes of proximity-operations norms just as Washington has dropped its ambiguity. Expect reciprocal Chinese and Russian inspection claims. Commercial GEO operators will be the incidental subjects of both.

Meink also said the Golden Dome space-based interceptor program went from initial contract to flight-ready hardware inside a year, with initial capability by 2028. And he said the first space-based moving-target-indicator satellites, the AMTI birds, launch this month. Those follow SpaceX's May 29th award, headlined at 4.16 billion dollars. Be careful with that number. It is the headline value of a contract already awarded back in May, not fresh money announced this week.

Every one of these is a podium claim from the official whose program it is. Flight ready in under a year is either a real break in the acquisition model or a redefinition of the phrase. The reporting cannot tell us which. As of this recording, no launch of those moving-target-indicator satellites has been confirmed, and there are ten days left in September. Whether one flies is the cheap test. The declared items still outrun the graded ones.

Now the money. If position and inspection in GEO are contested, the ability to move there becomes a scarce input. That is where capital went this week.

Impulse Space extended its Series D by 308 million dollars, bringing the round to 808 million. Payload reports the valuation rose to 5.4 billion dollars. The company plans to spend it chiefly on hiring and manufacturing, after selling out its Caravan 2 and 3 Helios rideshare missions for 2028. The Helios kick stage is designed to move payloads from low Earth orbit to geostationary orbit in under a day.

Two cautions. That transfer time is a design specification, not a flight result. And the mark was set by the people buying the shares. The demand behind it is sold-out manifests two years out, which is not revenue. Still, the read-through is real. A 5.4 billion dollar private comparable now sits above the valuations of listed spacecraft-bus and orbital-mobility businesses, including Rocket Lab and Redwire. Those public names inherit a multiple that no public trade has tested.

The other way capital answers a launch bottleneck is to spend on the constellation you already have. Eutelsat signed Airbus for an investment of about one billion euros, roughly 1.16 billion dollars, to replenish and expand OneWeb. That fleet today comprises more than 600 satellites. Reuters notes Eutelsat operates the only low Earth orbit network outside Starlink.

This is a different payback model from Starlink's. Eutelsat is not racing a consumer subscriber base. It is replacing satellites in a fleet that already earns revenue, leaning on sovereign and government demand. That changes the arithmetic on revenue per satellite and on the replacement cycle. It is also why a billion-euro order is rational for a fleet just past 600 rather than a fleet of thousands. The trade-off is that it concedes the consumer mass market to Starlink and Amazon.

Notice what these two bets share. Neither needs a new rocket to exist.

That leads to the layer everything above still waits on. Rocket Lab's Electron launched a radar-imaging satellite for Synspective on September 18th. SpaceNews calls it the fifth Electron flight in a month and a half. That is genuine manufacturing discipline. But Electron cannot lift a megaconstellation or a GEO inspector. And on the vehicle that could, Neutron, Rocket Lab's own management has said the window for an end-of-year launch date is narrowing. There is no firm public first-flight date. The cadence is real, and it is in the wrong vehicle class.

Rocket Lab's other move this week fits the pattern I just described. The company filed an 8-K covering September 15th. In it, Rocket Lab says it raised about 1.9 billion dollars in gross proceeds by issuing new shares, and then terminated in full the 3.6 billion dollar bridge loan it had arranged to fund its acquisition of Iridium, the satellite operator. So Rocket Lab is buying Iridium with stock rather than borrowed money, and the deal is expected to close in mid-2027. Here is why it belongs in this paragraph. What that money buys is an operating satellite network that already earns revenue, not a bigger rocket. Rocket Lab, Eutelsat and Impulse are all spending around the launch bottleneck rather than through it.

Now, where the orbital economy is inflecting, and where it is still paper.

What is inflecting is funded. GEO awareness has prototype contracts and a public budget path. In-space mobility has a private mark of 5.4 billion dollars. Fleet replenishment has a billion-euro order. What is still paper is the medium-lift vehicle, the orbital Starship, and Golden Dome as an operating capability. Every dollar committed to replenishment and to moving existing assets, rather than to new lift, concedes the launch bottleneck rather than solving it. If Neutron slips into 2027 and Starship slips again, the sector's 2027 capacity plan is Falcon 9 and Ariane 6, and pricing power stays exactly where it is.

Three catalysts to watch. First, Starship Flight 14, its first intentional orbital flight, carrying 26 Starlink V3 satellites. It is now targeted for as early as Monday, September 28th, pending regulatory approval. That flight has not happened. The FAA has said it will make a license determination only after SpaceX provides all outstanding information, so treat that date as a filing, not a schedule. Second, whether a space-based moving-target-indicator launch actually occurs before September ends. Third, whether anyone ever publishes a GHOST-R contract value.
