Good morning, it's August 28th, 2026, and this is your space and orbital economy briefing.

Start with the scoreboard, because for the first time this beat has one that updates every trading day. SpaceX is now a stock. It priced its IPO at 135 dollars a share in June. That was the largest IPO in history. The stock opened at 150 and closed its first day at 160.95. As of Thursday it was trading at 139.63. Sit with those three numbers rather than a headline percentage. Issue price, first-day close, and where it sits today. I am deliberately not giving you a drawdown figure, because the only sources for a post-IPO high are retail aggregators that contradict each other. The raw levels are enough. They tell you the market has handed back the entire first-day pop and now prices the company a few dollars above where the underwriters set it. What sits in that gap is something that used to happen entirely behind closed doors. It is Starship's execution risk.

Here is what that risk actually is. On SpaceX's first earnings call as a public company, on August 4th, Elon Musk said Flight 14 would be Starship's first flight to actual orbit. It would deploy version-three Starlink satellites to operational orbit rather than merely testing them. That is a meaningfully different claim from Flight 13. Flight 13 flew on July 24th and was a suborbital test. It dropped twenty functioning V3 satellites for brief checks, and then the vehicle came back down. Suborbital deployment and operational orbit are not the same accomplishment. That distinction is exactly what is now being priced every trading day.

A follow-up SpaceNews report put a date on it for the first time. Citing an August 20th briefing given to airspace managers, it lists Flight 14 no earlier than September 15th. The same report clarified what actually got scaled back from Musk's original plan. It is the attempt to catch the upper stage at the launch tower, not the orbital-deployment goal itself. Musk had said on the call that he would try the tower catch on this flight. He later walked that back on social media, saying the catch would probably come a few months later. NASA administrator Jared Isaacman put down his own marker as well. If SpaceX launches again in early September, he said, we're going to feel very good about their timing. So what is on the table in mid-September is an orbital deployment attempt without the tower catch. That is a narrower and more achievable test than the one Musk originally described. And a trillion-dollar-class public equity is now waiting on it.

There is a second-order effect here that has nothing to do with rockets. SpaceX now files quarterly. Over time that should put subscriber, revenue and margin data for the dominant constellation operator on a fixed public cadence, which this sector has never had. Every competitor's business case has until now been underwritten against modelled Starlink economics. Increasingly it will be underwritten against a disclosed one. That is the most durable consequence of the listing, and it will outlast any single flight outcome.

The launch bottleneck is the thread running through everything else in orbit right now. The clearest proof is AST SpaceMobile. Yesterday we walked through AST's balance sheet, and the number was over 3.7 billion dollars of pro forma cash after July's convertible offering. So its constraint is not money. It is flights. AST had targeted roughly 45 satellites in its constellation by the end of this year. That target has now slipped to early 2027, and the company's own reporting attributes the slip specifically to launch availability. That is the thesis sentence of this briefing. A fully funded company is being paced by somebody else's manifest.

The engineering is moving even where the manifest isn't. AST launched three new BlueBird satellites, numbers 11, 12 and 13, on a Falcon 9 on August 5th. It is targeting speeds that would roughly double the peak downlink its earlier satellites demonstrated to a standard smartphone. That earlier peak was 98.9 megabits per second. Keep both halves of that honest. The 98.9 figure was a measured peak to a single handset, not a sustained rate across a loaded cell. And the doubling is a target for the new satellites, not a result yet. On the regulatory side, AST picked up a 30-day FCC test authority covering up to 100 handsets, plus separate approval in Japan alongside Rakuten Mobile. The FCC piece is a test permit, not a commercial green light. That matters, because AST's president is guiding toward beta service later this year, which is company guidance rather than a confirmed date. None of it changes the constraint. Continuous coverage needs satellites, satellites need rides, and the rides are what slipped.

Now look at the system where capital is, by construction, not scarce at all. China's answer to Starlink is SpaceSail, which runs the Qianfan constellation. It has just closed a state-backed funding round. The South China Morning Post puts the valuation at roughly 50 billion yuan, with the new money representing about 14 percent of the company's equity. I won't give you a single dollar figure for the raise. The reporting conflicts. One outlet says roughly 7 billion yuan, another cites a much larger dollar total, and picking one would be guessing. What is solid is the investor list, which includes state-backed vehicles such as Shanghai Alliance Investment.

The deployment math is solid too, and it is the more interesting number. Qianfan stood at roughly 238 satellites as of early August. Its own Phase 1 plan calls for 648 active satellites by the end of this year, on the way to 1,296 in total. Money did not close that gap. Four months of launch cadence has to. So the question about China's megaconstellation push may not be about capital or spectrum rights at all. It may be whether Chinese launch vehicles can fly often enough to hit a target the company itself set. Treat that as the question the numbers pose rather than a conclusion, because nobody has published a Long March cadence plan against that 648.

Same story, smaller dose, at Amazon. Amazon's Leo constellation, the renamed Project Kuiper, has put up more than 375 satellites across twelve missions. That count is Amazon's own, and it is a moving target. It flew those missions on three different rockets: Atlas V, Ariane 6 and Falcon 9. Amazon says it now has enough satellites up to begin service later this year. Treat that as a marketing claim rather than a coverage claim. There is no subscriber number, no price, and no firm service date attached to it. The more telling detail is that Amazon still has more than a hundred additional launches merely secured, not flown.

Look at that launch mix, because it is a structurally different risk from Starlink's. Atlas V is being retired. Falcon 9 belongs to Amazon's chief rival in satellite broadband. Ariane 6 is early in its own ramp. SpaceX launches its constellation on rockets it owns, so it sets its own deployment schedule. Amazon's schedule is set by three suppliers, one of whom competes with it directly. Twelve missions flown against more than a hundred still owed is the same shape of problem as AST's and Qianfan's. Contracted flights, not deployed hardware, are what most of these constellations currently consist of.

So pull the threads together. Yesterday this briefing argued that for the sector's rights-holders, the spectrum bidders and the Golden Dome contractors, the binding constraint had shifted away from rockets and onto access rights. Today's evidence says that is true for the rights-holders and false for the builders. AST, Amazon and SpaceSail are all capital-adequate and flight-constrained. And the company whose rocket is supposed to relieve that bottleneck for everyone has not got Starship to orbit yet. That sets up the uncomfortable version of the same point. If Starship works, the marginal price and availability of flights improves for every operator queued behind it. But the party deciding who gets that capacity, and in what order, is the operator of the largest competing constellation.

So watch three dates. Flight 14, no earlier than September 15th. AST's beta service guidance for later this year. And Qianfan's year-end target of 648 active satellites, four months out with 238 in orbit today. Whichever of those slips least tells you who is actually solving the launch problem, and who is just talking about it.
