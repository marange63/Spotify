Good morning, it's September 11th, and this is your private equity briefing.

No fresh deal tape landed this week. No megadeal signed, no fund closed, no manager issued a new disclosure since the last time we spoke. So today's argument is built the other way round, out of what the quarter's disclosures actually say, against the question this show has been circling for days. Does any of this liquidity engineering reach a limited partner, or does it just move the paper around.

Start with KKR's second quarter, because it contains the fact that argues against our own running thesis, and that deserves to be stated first and on its own terms. KKR called it the largest monetization quarter in its history, with eight hundred forty-eight million dollars of realized performance income. That is real cash, converted out of paper marks, in a single quarter. Now size it. KKR still carried eighteen point two billion dollars of remaining unrealized gains as of June 30th. So the best realization quarter in the firm's history converted a low single-digit share of the pile it is sitting on. The honest read is not that exits are frozen solid. It is that the overhang is the large thing here, not the realization.

Two caveats before we lean on any of this. These are second-quarter figures, period ended June 30th and reported in early August, so they are roughly six weeks old and nothing fresher exists from any major manager until the third-quarter prints in late October. And the per-share numbers come to us through a transcript aggregator rather than KKR's own release, which we could not parse. Treat them as high confidence, not as independently cross-checked.

With that said, turn to the other half of the same print, because that is where the more interesting story sits. KKR's fee-related earnings per share rose thirty-four percent year over year, at a seventy percent margin. That is the fee machine compounding cleanly while realized carry stays lumpy.

The fundraising side did the same thing. KKR raised three hundred five billion dollars from the start of 2024 through June 30th, beating its own three-year, three-hundred-billion-dollar target in two and a half years. And its K-Series wealth vehicles, the private-wealth channel, reached forty-two billion dollars in assets, up almost seventy percent in a year. Put that beside the realization math and the so-what is direct. The fundraising machine kept growing while distributions did not. Something other than cash returned to limited partners is funding it, and the K-Series number points squarely at the wealth channel. We should be precise about the limit of that claim. No disclosure we could find splits KKR's raised capital between wealth money and institutional money, so this is an inference from the growth rates, not an attribution of dollars.

There is a valuation consequence in that split, and it is the sort of thing the market re-rates on quietly. If fee-related earnings keep compounding on wealth inflows while carry stays episodic, then the alternative managers get priced on fee multiples rather than on carry visibility. That is a change in valuation methodology, not a cyclical recovery. And it has an uncomfortable implication. The manager's own stock becomes less sensitive to the exact exit problem that is straining the funds underneath it.

That sets up who is actually leaving the table. Bain's midyear report, published June 8th and three months old now, is standing structural context rather than news, but it remains the best-sourced quantification of the exit problem and nothing has superseded it. Bain finds that one in five limited partners are actively reducing their allocations to buyout funds. Hold periods have stretched to roughly seven years. And distributions as a share of net asset value have been running at record lows for four years. These figures come from the report's press release rather than the full methodology, so treat the percentages as directional.

Bain also puts the industry's unsold portfolio company count at roughly thirty-three thousand. That one is worth pausing on, because this show flagged a factor-of-two disagreement on that exact number on Sunday, with McKinsey cited at something closer to sixteen thousand. Bain's figure now has a name, a date and a primary source behind the high end. McKinsey's low end has not been withdrawn. Two respected houses disagreeing by a factor of two on the industry's own unsold inventory is not a rounding error. It is the honest state of the evidence, and it should make you skeptical of any single clean number quoted on this topic, including ours.

So the marginal dollar in private equity is changing hands. The investor stepping back is the one who grades a manager on cash actually returned. The investor stepping in, through vehicles like K-Series, does not yet have a distribution record to grade. And the two bear liquidity risk very differently. A pension fund can wait seven years without complaint. A semi-liquid vehicle sold on a periodic repurchase offer cannot promise the same thing, and no large private-equity evergreen vehicle has disclosed a redemption request at or near its cap this year. We looked. That is an open question, not a disclosed stress, and we are not going to pretend otherwise.

There is a third consequence, and it is the sharpest one. A manager whose fundraising has decoupled from its distributions has less reason to clear its inventory at an honest price. It can wait. That is the adverse-selection problem this show described last week arriving through the fundraising door rather than the exit door.

Now the less-discussed reason institutional money is stepping back, which is not about liquidity at all. It is about returns. Bain notes that a buyout deal needing only five percent annual EBITDA growth to produce a two-and-a-half-times return a decade ago now needs closer to ten to twelve percent for the same result. That is roughly double the operating growth for the identical outcome. The implication is that leverage and multiple expansion were doing most of the historical return work, not operational improvement. Both of those tailwinds are fading at once. A reopened IPO market does not fix a value-creation model that needed cheap debt and rising multiples to clear its hurdle. It just lets you find out sooner.

Institutional money is not gone, though, and the counterweight matters. Advent International is reportedly nearing the final close of a roughly twenty-six-billion-dollar flagship fund, modestly above its predecessor vehicle, helped by more than twelve billion dollars of realization proceeds generated so far this year. Be careful with the sourcing. The size is reported, not announced by the firm, the realizations figure comes from unnamed people speaking to Buyouts, and the fund is described as nearing close rather than closed. Set it against Blue Owl, whose GP Stakes Fund Six slipped roughly a full year against its own timetable, as this show covered on Monday. Two managers, opposite outcomes, and the variable separating them is cash returned. Institutional money is not absent. It is priced, and the price is distributions to paid-in capital. The awkward footnote is that Advent's success adds roughly twenty-six billion dollars of fresh dry powder to an industry whose binding constraint right now is exits, not capital to deploy.

Briefly on the release valve, and this show used the same Jefferies first-half review on Tuesday, so take only the one figure that still earns its place. Secondaries volume hit a record one hundred twenty-one billion dollars in the first half. GP-led volume rose thirty-five percent year over year, while limited-partner-led volume rose only about four percent. Growth is coming almost entirely from the side the manager controls and prices, not the side the limited partner initiates. And note that is a volume statistic, not a pricing one. It measures manager activity, not limited-partner satisfaction. Liquidity is being engineered, not simply realized.

So where does the cycle stand. Fee earnings and fundraising are compounding cleanly, increasingly on wealth money that no redemption cycle has tested. Realized carry is real but episodic, converting a fraction of a genuinely large paper overhang. Institutional limited partners are trimming into return math that now demands close to twice the operating growth it used to. And the manager who can show cash returned still raises fastest, which is the one clean piece of good news here.

Three things would confirm or refute that read. Third-quarter manager earnings in late October, for whether realizations broaden beyond one good quarter. Confirmation of Advent's final close and its actual size. And the first disclosure from any large semi-liquid private-equity evergreen vehicle of a repurchase request landing at or near its cap. That would be the equity side's version of what the private-credit market is already showing, where Cliffwater's lending fund has now capped redemptions three quarters running. Nothing like it has been disclosed on the equity side yet. When it is, this story stops being about paper marks and starts being about who actually gets their money back.

That's the state of the machine. Talk tomorrow.
