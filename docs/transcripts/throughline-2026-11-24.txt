Today's headline: CrowdStrike signed an all-stock deal to buy Zscaler at an enterprise value of about 43.9 billion dollars, and its shares fell 9 percent on Monday despite a record quarter.

We'll start with the macro backdrop, where Strategic Power argues that the lever this week is the license, not the supply. Beijing's suspension of its ban on exporting gallium, germanium and antimony to the US runs out this Friday, November 27th, and the desk's point is that those metals stay license-controlled whatever Beijing announces. The desk infers that even a Friday extension leaves Western chip-packaging and defense-optics supply chains carrying discretionary risk, a durable reason to source outside China. The same logic is reaching the grid, where Spain now makes large data centers show 80 percent hourly-matched renewable power before they get the right to connect.

From minerals to markets, Digital Money finds that bitcoin's marginal buyer still hasn't shown up. Strategy's latest filing shows just 29.6 million dollars of bitcoin bought last week, against 1.79 billion dollars of outflows from US spot bitcoin ETFs, the heaviest weekly exit since June. Strategy spent roughly five times as much buying back its own preferred stock as buying coin, so the largest steady corporate bid in this market is now a rounding error against the exit.

Frontier AI Lab Competition has OpenAI's chief scientist proposing a shared capability brake keyed to pretraining perplexity, which Sam Altman endorsed but no other lab has been reported backing. The desk's caution is that much recent capability gain is attributed to post-training and inference compute, so a perplexity line may not bind where the frontier is actually moving. The reinstatement restores roadmap continuity for labs that run on Nvidia's chips, but the reported scrutiny of Nvidia's accounting is still open.

From a proposed brake to a signed deal, Enterprise Technology carries today's headline, pairing the Zscaler announcement with a record quarter for CrowdStrike's net-new annual recurring revenue and sharply raised guidance. The desk's read is that the market paid up for CrowdStrike's demand but charged it for the deal's price, dilution and integration risk. Beyond the deal, Microsoft and Databricks are splitting on agent governance, with Microsoft giving agents employee-style identities and Databricks letting its agent act only through human-approved pull requests. On the desk's reading, if identity-bearing agents multiply, governing them becomes a security budget line that a combined CrowdStrike and Zscaler would be positioned to capture.

That's the compute stack. Now the applied frontier, where standards and permissions are moving faster than deployments.

Robotics and Physical AI has Nvidia and Beijing each standardizing one end of the humanoid stack in recent weeks. Nvidia opened more of its Isaac robot software and unveiled a reference humanoid built with Agile Robots, while China's industry ministry issued state standards for electric-drive joints and dexterous hands. Neither move is a deployment, and the desk's inference is that if both brain and body standardize, the scarce layer shifts to integration and proven field reliability. That's uncomfortable for mid-tier Western humanoid makers, who would be left to compete on integration, data and uptime.

Space and the Orbital Economy reports that Amazon missed its July deployment milestone for the Leo constellation. The FCC's waiver keeps Amazon's license alive, but satellites it launches late now sit behind future entrants in priority, until as late as March 2028. So every launch slip now costs Amazon regulatory standing as well as revenue. Even Rocket Lab's Neutron, which hasn't flown yet, is booked into the 2030s, so new Western medium-lift capacity won't arrive in volume before the end of the decade.

AI in Biopharma R&D closes the tour with the first number this show has seen linking AI to clinical success, rather than to speed or benchmarks. Oliver Wyman finds 45 percent of AI-native biotechs' Phase II programs advanced, against 29 percent industry-wide, but the sample is just 46 programs, with half still running. Capital isn't waiting: about 89 billion dollars was committed to AI drug-discovery deals across 2025 and the first half of 2026, for what is still a sliver of the clinical pipeline.

One thread does run through several of today's briefings: control is being exercised through permission, not supply. Beijing's mineral licenses, Spain's grid decree and the FCC's priority queue are all gates that someone else opens or shuts. OpenAI's proposed brake would be one the labs set for themselves, if any rival signs on.
