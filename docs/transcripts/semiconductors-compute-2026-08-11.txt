Good morning. It's Tuesday, August 11th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

We're leading with Intel, because yesterday it did something it hasn't done since it went public in 1971: it sold stock to the market. Intel announced a $15 billion underwritten common stock offering on August 10th, with underwriters holding an option to buy up to $2.25 billion more. Intel says the proceeds are for general corporate purposes, which may include capital expenditures and working capital. That's the headline. The more interesting story is what happened next. Reporting from the same 24 to 48 hours, corroborated across multiple outlets, indicates Intel was already moving to upsize the deal toward $20 billion, roughly a third larger than the initial target, on investor demand reported above $100 billion. That detail isn't yet confirmed by a priced filing, so treat it as a live, credible development rather than a settled number. But if it holds, it changes the math. Analyst estimates built off Monday's closing price put the base $15 billion raise at around 3.1% shareholder dilution and coverage of roughly 75% of Intel's 2026 capital budget. A $20 billion raise would push dilution closer to 4% and could cover close to the entirety of that capex guide, which Intel itself has now raised to more than $20 billion for the year.

So what is this money actually funding, and does it prove Intel can compete at the leading edge? Not yet. Intel Foundry posted roughly a $2.1 billion operating loss last quarter, even as total foundry segment revenue rose to $5.8 billion, up 31% year over year. That segment figure includes internal transfers to Intel's own chip groups, not just outside customers, so it overstates external traction. The previously reported $293 million of external foundry revenue is the more relevant number, though it comes from secondary reporting rather than a sentence we could directly verify in Intel's own release, so hold it loosely. On the technology side, Intel's 18A process entered high-volume manufacturing for a subset of Core Ultra Series 3 chips, and the next-generation 18A-P variant entered risk production. Those are real engineering milestones, but neither is quantified in wafer or unit terms, and Fortinet remains the only named external foundry customer of consequence. So here's the distinction that matters. Intel has now demonstrated capital access at a scale investors weren't sure was available to it. It has not yet demonstrated manufacturing leadership. Those are separate competitive dimensions, and conflating them is the mistake to avoid this week.

Why is Intel raising equity instead of borrowing or waiting for government money? Because the ground shifted under the whole sector last month. Chip stocks have lost more than a trillion dollars in market value since their July peak, with the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index down over 20% from its high. Nvidia alone shed roughly $238 billion of that in the initial leg, with SK hynix, Samsung, and Micron all down sharply too. The prevailing read, and this is interpretation rather than settled fact, is that this is a repricing of an overheated rally rather than confirmation that AI demand is actually slowing. Hyperscaler capex disclosures haven't shown a break. But a repricing still raises the cost of equity for exactly the companies that need it most right now. And that can be self-reinforcing: the more the group sells off, the more expensive it gets to fund the very capacity the demand still requires. Intel diluting shareholders into a battered market, rather than waiting for calmer conditions, is the clearest evidence yet that this repricing has started feeding back into how the AI buildout gets financed.

Now, the counterpoint, because the physical demand signal hasn't actually cracked. TSMC reported record July revenue of roughly $14.49 billion, up 44.7% year over year, released the same day as Intel's offering. That's not a company seeing AI capex peak. But look inside the number before you extrapolate it. TSMC's leading-edge N2 node still accounts for only about 3% of revenue, even though the company is racing to lift N2 output from roughly 20,000 wafers a month today to about 100,000 by year end. The five-nanometer and three-nanometer nodes still carry the business, at 33% and 30% of revenue respectively. And TSMC itself says the steep N2 ramp will drag second-half gross margin down by three to four percentage points. So record revenue is not record margin. An early leading-edge ramp costs money before it pays for itself, and investors treating "TSMC printed a record" as confirmation that two-nanometer demand has fully arrived are getting ahead of the data.

Stay on TSMC for one more beat, because the binding constraint on AI accelerators isn't the logic wafer, it's the packaging. TSMC's CoWoS advanced-packaging capacity is on track to reach 120,000 to 140,000 wafers a month by year end, up from about 13,000 at the end of 2023, and industry estimates have the supply-demand gap narrowing from around 20% to about 10% over the same window. That's an analyst range, not a TSMC-confirmed figure, and we flagged it yesterday, so treat it as context. The point is directional: the packaging bottleneck is easing but not gone, and it still gates how many Nvidia, AMD, and hyperscaler ASIC parts actually ship, regardless of how many logic dies come off the line.

And there's a second bottleneck sitting right behind it, which brings us to memory. This is the loudest fundamentals story of the month, even though the specific prints are now weeks old. Micron guided its fiscal fourth quarter to $50 billion in revenue. The prior quarter it did $41.46 billion, against just $9.3 billion in that same quarter a year earlier. SK hynix posted a 76% operating margin in the second quarter on record revenue, driven by HBM4 shipments that began ramping in the period. Both companies say their entire calendar-2026 HBM supply is essentially sold out on fixed price and volume. That matters beyond the margin line: if HBM is spoken for through next year, then HBM, not just packaging, caps effective accelerator supply independent of how many good logic dies the foundries yield. It's a real structural reallocation of DRAM capacity toward AI infrastructure, and it's part of why conventional DRAM prices for PCs and phones keep climbing too.

That reallocation has a capital-markets tail worth naming. When memory margins rival or exceed logic-foundry margins, capital allocation incentives across the industry shift. Incremental capex gets pulled toward DRAM and HBM fabs rather than logic capacity, and that tilts the equipment order mix at ASML, Applied Materials, Lam Research, and Tokyo Electron toward memory-specific tools. But margins this extreme are also exactly the kind of number that invites a repricing like the one the sector just took, so treat the memory story as a tailwind with a ceiling, not a one-way bet.

Put it together and the picture for early August is this. The physical fundamentals of the AI buildout, TSMC's wafer volumes and memory's sold-out HBM backlog, are still accelerating. But the cost of capital to fund the next leg of that buildout just rose, and Intel's equity raise is the clearest sign that even an incumbent with an existing fab base now has to dilute shareholders to keep building factories, rather than proving those factories can win business first. Watch three things. Whether Intel's offering actually prices near $20 billion. Whether TSMC's margin drag deepens as N2 scales. And whether Nvidia's August 26th earnings show any crack in the demand that this whole thesis rests on. Those are the threads that tell you whether this is a financing story or the start of something structural.

That's the briefing. Have a good one.
