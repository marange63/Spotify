Good morning, it's Monday, September 7th. This desk was dark yesterday, and the weekend changed the object we're tracking, not just the level.

Start with the escalation. On Saturday, September 5th, Iran's Revolutionary Guard fired ballistic missiles at a US aircraft carrier and a destroyer on patrol. Both evaded the attack, and the US military said no personnel were hurt. The US response was to strike three Iranian oil tankers. Two were, in the military's words, permanently disabled, and a third, unladen one was destroyed. Iran's state broadcaster claims it hit three American vessels in return. That claim is not independently confirmed, and the US account remains that both warships came through untouched. Set the claim aside and look at what's agreed. This is a direct state-on-state military exchange, missiles at a carrier group met with strikes on tankers. That is a different category of event than the six months of deniable attacks on shipping this conflict has produced since February. So the tail risk nobody was being paid to hold got materially fatter over a weekend when almost nothing traded.

Here's the problem for anyone trying to price it this morning. The physical evidence doesn't exist yet. The last oil settle we can source is September 1st, when Brent rose four dollars sixteen cents, or four point six percent, to ninety-four sixty-five, and WTI rose five point two percent to ninety point twenty-two, both on the prior week's fighting. Brent was reported near ninety-five seventy-three by Friday, though that's a report rather than a settle I can verify. Nothing verifiable exists for Sunday's reopen or this morning's session, so I won't hand you a print I can't source.

The flow data is worse. The most recent Strait of Hormuz transit count is six tanker crossings a day, against an eighty-five-per-day pre-crisis baseline. That reading is dated August 30th, a full week before Saturday's exchange. The one available war-risk insurance data point — tankers paying three to ten percent of hull value to transit, versus roughly a quarter of one percent before the war — is seven weeks stale, from mid-July. And the EIA's throughput estimate, Hormuz flows averaging four point nine million barrels a day in the second quarter against twenty-one point six million before the conflict, is a quarterly average describing the war in general. It says nothing about Saturday's strikes. So the market is trading a headline this morning with essentially no confirming data behind it. What you see on the tape today is sentiment, not evidence.

That gap is why the first place this shows up is leadership, not the index. Crude sustained in the mid-nineties is a transfer of margin, not a subtraction from it. Refiners, chemicals, airlines and freight pay the input bill, producers and energy services collect it, and the two roughly net out at the index level while diverging violently underneath it. So an S&P that goes nowhere this week would not be evidence that the market shrugged off the weekend. It would be evidence that the rotation happened beneath a flat print, which is what a supply shock looks like before it becomes an earnings event. Watch the spread between energy and transport, not the headline number.

Which is where the calendar becomes the problem. This escalation lands on a Fed that was already leaning hawkish. Chair Kevin Warsh's Jackson Hole speech on August 28th surprised to the hawkish side. He said summer inflation readings, while better than expected, don't tell him underlying trends have meaningfully improved — an explicit rebuff of the White House's push for lower rates. Then Friday's August jobs report blew past consensus. Payrolls rose a hundred sixty-two thousand against expectations near fifty-six thousand, unemployment held at four point one percent, and June and July were revised up a combined fifty-five thousand. September hike odds on CME's FedWatch jumped to fifty-eight point four percent from forty-nine point four the day before, and the ten-year Treasury yield reached its highest level since November 2023 on September 2nd. I won't retell that print, since we covered it in full on Saturday. The point today is what it means now that oil has jumped again. A supply shock landing on a labour market that just added a hundred sixty-two thousand jobs doesn't split the Fed's mandate the way it would against a cracking one. It frees Warsh to tighten into resilience. So the energy leg of this story is hawkish in a way it wasn't two weeks ago.

But push on that, because a rate hike can't produce a barrel of oil. If Warsh does hike on September 16th with crude in the mid-nineties, the objective isn't cooling demand. It's defending inflation expectations against a supply shock the funds rate cannot fix. That is a much harder thing for markets to price than a standard demand-side hike. There's a second input in the long end that has nothing to do with the inflation outlook. A sitting chair publicly at odds with the White House on rate direction is a credibility question, and credibility questions get paid for in term premium. Some of the move in the ten-year is that, not the price of oil or the pace of hiring. And none of it is resolved by anything on the calendar yet. August PPI lands September 10th and CPI on the 11th, and neither has been released. Those two prints, not this weekend's headlines, are the binding constraint on the September decision.

So look at what the market is charging to insure against any of it. The VIX touched roughly fourteen point two in late August, its lowest level of 2026, even with the war and the hawkish pivot both already on the table. High-yield credit spreads were two point six five percent as of September 3rd, on the ICE BofA index, against a twenty-year average near four point nine percent on the comparable Bloomberg high-yield index. That is among the tightest compensation in two decades. Both of those marks predate Saturday's exchange entirely. Citadel Securities put the asymmetry plainly before the weekend even happened. With earnings behind and the catalyst calendar turning back toward macro, there is more room for volatility to reprice higher than for another compression to keep supporting equities. So the price of protection you'd buy this morning was set in a world where no carrier had taken missile fire. That's an asymmetry available to buy. It is not a forecast that it pays.

I'll score this desk's own record honestly here, because it bears on how much to trust the framing. We argued on August 29th and again on September 3rd that if the hawkish repricing accelerated, credit would widen first, given how little cushion two point six five percent of spread provides. It didn't widen through the sharpest part of that repricing. The fair counter is compositional. Today's high-yield index is materially higher quality than the pre-2008 vintage, so a tight spread on better credit isn't automatically mispriced. What survives that counter is narrower, but it's still real. The compensation on offer is thin enough that the first move in any genuine scare is likely to be fast and mechanical, a de-rating across the whole index rather than an orderly repricing of individual credits. And low realised volatility has let systematic and vol-control strategies run more equity exposure than they'd otherwise carry. That means a repricing gets amplified by forced de-risking rather than absorbed by it.

Here's the falsifier, so you know what would prove this framing wrong. If the Hormuz transit count recovers toward normal and next week's PPI and CPI prints come in cool, this weekend becomes another escalation that resolved into nothing. Credit and volatility stay right where they are, and the whole hawkish-oil-shock framing runs in reverse. Watch the flow data and the inflation prints, not the headlines, for the real answer.

That's the Radar for September 7th. Trade carefully out there, and I'll see you next time.
