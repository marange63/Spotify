Good morning. It's Friday, July 31st, and this is your private credit briefing.

Start with the number that matters most this week: sixteen cents on the dollar. That's where First Brands Group's senior, first-lien rescue loan is being quoted in broker markets, even though the company is pitching creditors on a full recovery through two billion dollars of clawback litigation. A billion-one of rescue debt, first in line to be repaid, trading like a junior claim. That gap is the story.

Here's why seniority didn't save this loan. First Brands' restructuring advisors have confirmed that two point three billion dollars of the company's receivables were fabricated. The collateral behind the lending wasn't just impaired, it was fictional. First lien only protects you if the lien attaches to something real, and the market is now pricing the possibility that it didn't. A June 8th court filing lays out the debtors' own recovery math, and it's damning on its own terms: the litigation trust needs to recover about one point eight five billion dollars just to pay DIP and administrative claims in full, and about one point nine two billion before priority claims are made whole. Push the recovery all the way to three billion dollars, above both of those thresholds, and general trust-interest holders still net only about eight point four percent against eleven and a half billion of allowed claims. That's the company's own illustrative waterfall, not a bear case. Sixteen cents isn't a distressed-market overreaction. It's a rational read of the debtors' own numbers.

So who's holding this. Roughly fifteen BDCs, eleven private and four public, disclosed a combined two hundred thirty-seven million dollars of First Brands exposure, a small slice of total industry AUM. But the marks are the tell. Palmer Square Capital BDC and AGL Private Credit Income Fund carried First Brands positions marked between ninety-one and ninety-five cents as of June 30th, well above where the loan trades today. Some reporting frames those near-par marks as dating from before the fraud was uncovered, which would make them stale rather than current. Either way, the next time these funds report, the market gets a clean, named test of whether private marks catch up to price discovery or keep lagging it. Watch that reconciliation closely — it's the concrete version of an argument that's usually made in the abstract.

Now the mood swings hard in the other direction. The same week First Brands is humiliating a recovery pitch, Blue Owl Capital reported second-quarter earnings on July 30th and the stock jumped ten percent. AUM hit three hundred nineteen billion dollars. Redemption requests at Blue Owl's flagship direct-lending fund came in at nineteen percent of assets, and thirty-eight percent at its software-focused lending fund, both down from twenty-two and forty percent the prior quarter. The company honored just five percent of those requests, the standard industry cap that avoids forced asset sales. And on the credit side, only two-tenths of a percent of the software fund's book is flagged as unlikely to be fully repaid, with zero delinquencies in the quarter. The market read that as the private-credit scare being over.

Be skeptical of that read. Redemption requests easing from a Q1 peak is moderation, not resolution — most investors who want out are still trapped, since honoring five percent of requests means the queue barely moves. And the clean credit numbers are self-reported by Blue Owl, untested by any real stress event, in exact contrast to First Brands' loan, which has an actual, price-discovered mark. There's a second tell buried in the fundraising data: over seventy-five percent of Blue Owl's trailing-twelve-month equity capital came from non-direct-lending strategies, real assets and GP stakes, not credit. Headline AUM keeps growing, but the direct-lending fundraising engine specifically looks like it's slowing. That's not the same story as "resilience."

Here's what reconciles the relief trade with the First Brands proof point. Octus's BDC-industry roundup, still the freshest cross-sectional read even though it's a quarter stale, shows total BDC non-accrual debt jumped forty percent sequentially in Q1 to just under ten billion dollars, taking the reported industry rate to two percent. But because many borrowers carry loans from multiple BDCs simultaneously, Octus adjusts for cross-holdings and gets an exposure figure sixty-one percent higher — sixteen billion dollars — putting the true, adjusted non-accrual rate at three point two four percent, not two. That's Octus's own analytical construct, not a regulatory figure, but it's the most honest available estimate of borrower stress, and it says single-manager reported metrics like Blue Owl's understate what's really happening across the book. Pair that with a hundred thirty-five borrowers taking fair-value markdowns over fifteen percent in Q1, up from eighty-eight the prior quarter and concentrated in software — the same sector where AI disruption is squeezing recurring-revenue credits. So the honest synthesis is: First Brands shows what happens when marks finally meet a real price; the cross-holding data says a lot more of the book is quietly in that same position, just not yet tested.

One more piece of machinery worth naming briefly. Private credit CLO issuance is running near record pace this year, roughly nine and a half billion dollars year to date in private-credit CLOs alone, with the broader CLO market on track for about two hundred twenty billion dollars in 2026, a new high. Don't read that as a confidence signal. CLOs are permanent, non-redeemable financing — once the deal is done, equity holders can't force a sale the way BDC or interval-fund investors can request redemptions. That lets managers keep holding stressed loans without being forced into price discovery. It's a rational treasury move by managers bracing for more First Brands-style surprises, and it's also exactly the mechanism that lets marks stay disconnected from reality for longer.

So where does the cycle actually stand. Price discovery, in the one instance where it's genuinely happening, is brutal — a senior loan at sixteen cents against a marketed full recovery. Everywhere else, reported metrics — Blue Owl's clean self-reported numbers, single-manager non-accrual rates — are flattering the picture relative to the cross-holding-adjusted read. The biggest unresolved risk isn't the default rate itself, it's the mark-to-model gap: how much of the industry's book looks like Blue Owl's clean disclosures today and First Brands' sixteen cents tomorrow. Watch two catalysts. Apollo reports second-quarter earnings on August 4th, the next big manager to put fresh non-accrual and fund-performance numbers on the table. And the First Brands plan confirmation process, still working through the docket, will tell us whether the debtors' own eight-point-four-percent recovery math holds up or gets worse. The read to refute: if those catalysts show marks converging toward price without a wave of new non-accruals, the relief trade earns its ten percent. Until then, treat it as premature.

That's the briefing. Have a good weekend.
