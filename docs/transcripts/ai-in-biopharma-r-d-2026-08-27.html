Cautious Optimism Briefings

AI in Biopharma R&D

Transcript · August 27, 2026

Good morning, it's Thursday, August 27th, and this is your briefing on artificial intelligence in biopharma R&D.

For five straight days this beat has landed on the same conclusion. The checkable AI wins in drug development have slid down the stack. They have moved away from picking targets and designing molecules, and toward operations and paperwork. That is the only layer anyone would attach a hard number to. Yesterday that layer got its first independent test. It failed.

The test came from Northeastern University's Institute for Experiential AI, reported August 26th. Over the past academic year, three student cohorts ran real drug-discovery tasks against leading open-source agentic research frameworks. Two are named in the write-up: GPT Researcher and Agent Laboratory. The first cohort gave eight of these frameworks one job, which was to reproduce a published algorithm developed at Northeastern under professor Lei Xie. All eight failed. All eight also failed to draft a usable scientific report describing what they had done. A second cohort ran five more advanced frameworks against a tougher task, replicating the findings of a Novartis drug-discovery study. A third tested whether the models could independently review scientific papers, large datasets, and formulas. The study's own summary line is blunt. Today's models continue to make dangerous factual mistakes, are incapable of drafting well-sourced scientific reports on their own, and need constant hand-holding. Lei Xie's verdict is sharper still. The claim that AI can solve every problem including drug design is, in his words, very far from the truth.

Now the honest pushback. This tested open-source, general-purpose research agents. It did not test the frontier bio-tuned systems pharma sponsors actually deploy, like OpenAI's GPT-Rosalind or Anthropic's Claude Science. And it is a student capstone, not a peer-reviewed trial. That is a real limit on how far the finding generalizes, and nobody should stretch it onto a named commercial platform. But here is the other side of that same coin. A targeted search for an independent, non-vendor evaluation of those closed bio-tuned systems turned up nothing. The performance figures circulating for GPT-Rosalind all trace back to OpenAI's own launch materials. So the honest scorecard for the entire workflow layer, right now, is one negative independent data point and zero positive ones.

That matters because of the frame around it. Roughly eight years into the AI drug-discovery boom, no medicine discovered or designed with AI has been approved by a regulator in any major market. An analysis presented at ASCO this year counted 117 AI-enabled assets across 63 companies that have entered human trials. Sixty completed Phase I. Only eight completed Phase II, and we do not know how many of those eight actually hit their endpoints. Hold the definition in view, because it does a lot of work: AI-enabled means any machine-learning touchpoint anywhere in the program, not a molecule an AI designed. What the field has accumulated by now is capital, trial starts, and a thin first layer of human data. So the field has no layer with an independent, positive result. Not target selection, not molecule design, and as of yesterday, not even the paperwork.

The immediate consequence is financial, and it lands on the enterprise deployments rather than the labs. The case for rolling a language model out to tens of thousands of pharma employees rests on headcount leverage in regulatory writing, literature synthesis, and coding. If that layer needs constant human correction, the savings do not disappear, but they change shape. You are no longer replacing a scientist. You are speeding up a scientist who still has to check every line. That is a smaller number and a slower-compounding one, and it arrives later than the deployment schedules assume.

The second consequence is about how sponsors will scope these agents, and it cuts the other way. Expect deployment gated by liability rather than by capability. Drafting and synthesis get used with mandatory human sign-off. Data interpretation and anything headed into a regulatory submission stay hard-gated. That is bad news for the "the model eats the workflow" thesis, and quietly good news for the regulatory-affairs and clinical-operations software vendors this beat expected to get bundled away. A tool that enforces the human check has a longer life than one that promises to remove it.

If the operations layer is unproven, the design layer carries the field's prestige. And its flagship just missed its own deadline. Isomorphic Labs is the Google DeepMind spinout led by Demis Hassabis. It raised six hundred million dollars in March 2025, in a round led by Thrive Capital, specifically to push its AlphaFold-based design engine into the clinic. Hassabis had promised human trials by the end of 2025. The company now expects its first clinical trial by the end of 2026 instead. That is a one-year slip, with still no named candidate, indication, or filing date disclosed. Resist the easy read that AlphaFold simply does not translate. A one-year slip from a pre-IND platform company is unremarkable by ordinary biotech standards, and the company has not said why it happened, so any mechanism you attach is inference. The real lesson is narrower and more useful. The gap between a modeling breakthrough and an actual IND is bigger than the modeling breakthrough itself.

So what can the field actually collect right now, if not proof of benefit? Designations. Insilico Medicine's ISM6331 is a pan-TEAD inhibitor for advanced mesothelioma, built on its Chemistry42 platform. It received FDA Fast Track designation on July 29th, the company's first. It already carries Orphan Drug status from 2024, and Phase I data is set for an oral presentation at the European cancer conference ESMO this fall. Say plainly what Fast Track is. It is a timing benefit, granted on unmet medical need and mechanistic plausibility. It is not a finding of efficacy. And read it as evidence about the indication chosen rather than about the molecule designed, because mesothelioma is close to the ideal setting for designation stacking. The readout that would actually settle something is still ahead. Insilico's more advanced asset, rentosertib, has a Phase II interim readout in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis expected this quarter. It has not been reported. Treat it strictly as a pending catalyst, not a result.

That leaves the equity read where it has been all week, only firmer. With no validated layer anywhere in the stack, the AI-native platform names get priced as faster, cheaper option factories, not as higher-hit-rate ones. Every quarter without a positive pivotal readout widens the gap between capital deployed and approvals, and the market has no way to close it from the outside.

Behind all of this, the capital keeps compounding anyway. Nvidia's five-year, one-billion-dollar partnership with Eli Lilly, announced back in January, built a joint AI lab spanning discovery through manufacturing. Bristol Myers Squibb expanded its own Nvidia partnership in July to scale an internal AI factory, and Novo Nordisk struck a parallel compute deal. Those are capacity and access deals, dated months ago, and I am citing them as scale rather than as news. Billions committed to infrastructure, and not one disclosed productivity number attached to any of it.

Pull the threads together. Cycle-time compression is increasingly real and measurable. Designing a molecule faster, drafting a filing faster: those you can count. Improving the probability that a drug actually works has, after roughly eight years and zero approvals, no cohort-level evidence behind it at all. And now the fallback claim about the paperwork layer has taken its first hit. So the rational forecast for pharma R&D spend is still not a reduction. It is reallocation toward earlier, cheaper failure, and today's evidence removes one of the few offsetting savings that reallocation case was leaning on. The open question worth watching is simple. Will anyone run an independent test of the closed, bio-tuned systems the way Northeastern just tested the open ones? Until someone does, treat every vendor benchmark as a claim, not a result.

That's the briefing for August 27th. Talk to you tomorrow.
