Cautious Optimism Briefings

Strategic Power, Energy, and Institutional Change

Transcript · September 8, 2026

Good morning. It's Tuesday, September 8th, and this is your strategic power briefing.

All week this beat has run the same test on every claim of power: what got announced against what got delivered. Every time, the delivered side came up short. ERCOT's interconnection queue dwarfed the generation actually energized. OPEC's quotas dwarfed the barrels that could actually be lifted. Today that inverts. Something was genuinely delivered this week, and it was destruction.

Start with Russia's refineries. Ukrainian drones have now struck eight of Russia's ten largest oil refineries, several of them more than once. Only two remain undamaged, and both sit far beyond the Ural Mountains, at the far end of the country from Ukraine. The latest hit came over the weekend, against the isomerization unit at the Ryazan refinery. Treat that strike as the latest data point, not the story. The story is cumulative: a systematic campaign that has now reached almost the entire top tier of Russian refining capacity.

The evidence that it is working shows up downstream, not at the plant gate. Gasoline prices inside Russia are rising at their fastest pace in two decades. Fuel-sale restrictions have been imposed across dozens of regions. That includes the Khanty-Mansi district, which produces roughly forty percent of Russia's total oil output. Keep that forty percent precise: it is that region's share of crude production, not its share of the shortage and not its share of refining. And Moscow has gone as far as requesting an emergency shipment of fifty thousand tons of gasoline from Kazakhstan — a request reported in the press, not a confirmed delivery.

There is a second channel here that is easy to miss, and it is fiscal. In late July, Russia extended its ban on gasoline exports, for all producers, through the end of this year. That ban had originally been set to lapse on July 31st. So a measure sold as temporary got stretched across the rest of the calendar. Read that as the government's own revealed estimate of how long the shortage lasts. And read the cost: every ton held back for the domestic market is a ton not earning hard currency abroad. That is a separate hit from the discount Russian crude already takes at export, and it lands on the same budget.

Now the honest limit on all of this. Every figure I've given you is a price, a restriction, or a ban — not a volume. There is no independently verified barrels-per-day figure for how much Russian refining capacity is offline right now. We went looking specifically for one and came back empty. The only quotable capacity-loss number that exists anywhere is nine hundred thousand barrels a day, attributed to Reuters. That figure is from March of 2024. It describes a completely different phase of the campaign, and stretching it over today would be manufacturing a number rather than reporting one. So the strategic magnitude here is being read off consumer symptoms, not measured at the source.

Two things temper the crisis narrative further. First, a state that rations fuel is also a state that can prioritize military logistics over civilian supply. Scarcity at the pump does not automatically translate into pressure on the war effort. Second, the most recent official Russian statement on record cuts the other way. Deputy Prime Minister Novak said in late August that several refineries were already back in operation and that fuel supply was expected to increase. That is a government claiming stabilization, not collapse. It also predates this month's strikes, and a state with every incentive to manage the story is not a neutral witness in either direction. Take it as a caveat on the crisis reading, not a rebuttal of it.

And resist the tidy conclusion that pump pain shortens the war. The same evidence supports the opposite branch: hardened air defense around refineries, and deeper import substitution from Kazakhstan and Belarus. What would distinguish the two is a change in Russian behavior you can actually see — a negotiating concession on one side, or a visible reallocation of air-defense batteries away from the front and onto refinery perimeters on the other. Neither has shown up yet.

Even fully hedged, the strategic content here is the cost-exchange ratio, not the tally. Cheap one-way drones are inflicting repeated damage on fixed, capital-intensive, essentially irreplaceable refining infrastructure. That is a template every state with an exposed energy complex is now studying — Gulf producers, Taiwan, NATO planners alike. And it forces an uncomfortable conclusion. Right now, destruction is proving a more reliable instrument of pressure on Russia than sanctions or diplomacy have managed.

Which brings us to diplomacy, because the two are now directly linked. This beat has said for two straight days that the settlement track has nothing to anchor to — no disclosed terms, only adjectives. Kyiv has now said the same thing out loud. Envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner met Vladimir Putin in Moscow, then met Volodymyr Zelensky in Kyiv. Both legs were described as substantive — but note who is doing the describing. That word comes from the envoys and from a Kremlin adviser. There is no joint communique and no official US readout. Witkoff's own formulation was narrower: they brought new ideas to Kyiv. No ceasefire and no framework was announced. Territorial questions were pushed to a future leader-level meeting, and that meeting is unscheduled — no date, no venue.

And Zelensky is now on record expecting the war to run through another winter. He is treating diplomacy as Plan A while actively building a Plan B: reinforced air defense, protection for the power grid, and American liquefied natural gas.

That LNG line is the real news buried in that story. It converts American export capacity into a direct instrument of this war. It ties US gas infrastructure to a conflict with no scheduled end, and it does so independent of whatever happens at any future summit. No dollar value, volume or timeline has been published for that package, so nobody should be sizing it. But notice what stays frozen while terms remain undisclosed: sanctions sequencing, the still-immobilized Russian central bank assets sitting in Europe, and European defense budgets built around an outcome nobody has defined. A winter survival package is not diplomacy failing loudly. It is diplomacy being quietly de-prioritized by one side while it prepares for the alternative.

So both halves of this war are now fights over energy infrastructure — Russia's refineries under attack, Ukraine's grid needing defense. Turn now to where the fight over energy is happening without a single drone: the largest electricity market in the United States.

PJM's latest capacity auction cleared sixteen point four billion dollars in total costs. Data-center load accounts for about forty percent of that. And PJM's own market monitor — not outside critics, the market's internal watchdog — says data-center load growth is the primary reason for the tight supply-demand balance and the elevated prices.

Here is the part that should stop you. The monitor books that data-center number as forecast cost. It is forward-looking load growth, not load that exists and is metered today. That is the same kind of forward-looking number this beat has spent all week discounting when it showed up as an announcement. Here it is showing up as a bill instead.

Now a different auction in the same series. In December, PJM cleared more than six thousand five hundred megawatts short of its own reliability target — even at those elevated prices. Those are different sittings in the same auction series, so don't read them as a single delivery year. Read them as a single direction.

The skeptic's turn matters, though. High capacity prices are the market working as designed. They are the signal meant to call forth new generation. So the real puzzle is not the price. It is why that six-thousand-five-hundred-megawatt gap still did not clear even at that price, and the likely answer is turbine and switchgear lead times rather than insufficient incentive. Either way, the cost is socialized across all ratepayers, while the load driving it is concentrated in a handful of hyperscale campuses and largely still forecast. That mismatch is exactly the mechanism turning AI capex into state-level political risk. It is a regulatory exposure that never shows up in a chip order. Expect two responses from here. More data centers will contract generation directly and behind the meter, outside the wholesale market altogether. And siting will migrate toward regions with more headroom, away from PJM's footprint.

So the through-line for today. In Russia, the energy base layer is being destroyed faster than it can be defended. In America, it is being consumed faster than it can be built. Neither story is being settled by anyone's press release. What would actually change this read is a real barrels-per-day figure out of Russia, a disclosed peace term out of Moscow or Kyiv, or a PJM auction where the reliability target finally clears.

That's the briefing for September 8th. Stay grounded out there.
