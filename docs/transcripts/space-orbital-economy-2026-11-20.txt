Good morning. It's Friday, November 20th, 2026, and this is your space and orbital economy briefing.

On Wednesday this desk left two questions open. Was SpaceX's reported four-billion-dollar bond a leverage bet, or just financing at the margin of a bigger plan? And where, exactly, does Vulcan's failure trace? Both have moved since, and the answers pull in opposite directions for SpaceX.

Start with the money. That four-billion-dollar bond was never the real number.

According to a BigGo Finance summary of the reporting, SpaceX is getting Nvidia's Vera Rubin chips through roughly forty billion dollars of financing, led by Goldman Sachs and Apollo Global Management. An Apollo-backed special-purpose vehicle buys the chips and leases them to SpaceX. The debt sits on that vehicle's books, not on SpaceX's balance sheet. And according to reporting relayed by TipRanks, the debt is backed by five-year lease payments.

The chips feed SpaceX's AI computing segment. As this desk reported on Wednesday, that segment brings in about half of SpaceX's revenue but earned just 431 million dollars last quarter. So the rent lands on a business with a big top line and thin earnings.

This is a template much of the AI buildout has leaned on. Buy the hardware in a separate vehicle, lease it to the operator, and keep the liability one step removed. So Wednesday's first reading was the right one. The leverage is real. It is just housed in a different entity.

Has the debt actually priced? Several outlets, citing Bloomberg, report a completed sale of about thirty-two billion dollars at yields above eight percent. How that sale fits inside the forty-billion-dollar package, the reporting doesn't say. This desk also could not verify the print against Bloomberg's own text or any filing. Treat it as widely reported, not settled.

Here's the thing about off balance sheet. It changes who legally holds the debt. It does not remove SpaceX's obligation. Five years of rent on the chips behind that AI segment is, economically, a fixed claim on SpaceX's cash flow. That's this desk's read, not a disclosed accounting treatment.

The lenders' own behavior points the same way. The Financial Times, as relayed by TipRanks, reports that SpaceX's lenders sought a clause preventing the company from sending the financed chips into space. Their stated concern is asset recovery. Chips in orbit could be difficult to recover in a default. Whether that clause made the final documents is unclear. But the ask tells you how lenders see this deal. It is asset-backed finance in the literal sense, and chips they can physically reach are the backstop.

There's an irony in that for a space company. If the clause survived, the financing would keep this generation of SpaceX's compute on the ground. That cuts against any plan to put data centers in orbit, at least for these chips. So the first real test of space-based compute may be decided in a loan covenant, not on a launch pad.

The credit market has already formed a view on all this. Start with SpaceX's own balance sheet, separate from the chip vehicle. According to that same BigGo summary, SpaceX's total debt went from about twenty-three billion dollars at the end of last year to nearly thirty-nine billion by September 30th. That's up roughly two-thirds in nine months.

Keep that number apart from the chip financing. It is dated September 30th, before this month's chip deal came to market. And the chip debt sits in the separate vehicle anyway. So this is SpaceX's own borrowing, climbing sharply before the forty billion even arrived.

Now look at what it costs to insure SpaceX's debt. Its five-year credit default swap spread widened from about 110 basis points at the June IPO to about 265 basis points on November 16th. That's more than double in five months.

By market convention, a spread that wide implies roughly a one-in-five chance of default over five years. That reading assumes a standard recovery rate. It is not a rating action or a company forecast. But it is the price the market is actually trading on.

Two explanations compete here, and nothing in the reporting cleanly separates them. One is idiosyncratic. Credit is repricing SpaceX specifically, as it pivots into AI compute. The other is systemic. SpaceX is one more data point in the AI-infrastructure credit stress this show has tracked all week. That thread includes a shrunken data-center debt sale and a first-ever section on data-center leverage in the Fed's Financial Stability Report. Either way, here's what to bank. SpaceX equity and credit are now a claim on whether AI compute revenue outruns the rent, not just on Starlink and launch cash flow.

Here's what makes that spread strange. The launch franchise underneath it got stronger again this week. ULA's investigation into the November 15th Vulcan failure, which destroyed all forty Amazon Leo satellites aboard, has narrowed. Its preliminary finding, reported by Spaceflight Now, is "inadequate fuel supply from the vehicle to the BE-4 engines." ULA separated the thrust shortfall from the engines' own performance. So the problem points at the vehicle side, not the engines.

That matters because of what came earlier in the week. ULA had already said Vulcan's six solid rocket boosters worked well. That left the core stage and its two BE-4 engines as the obvious suspects. The fuel-supply finding now points away from the engines themselves.

The finding also separates two failures that looked alike. Last weekend, Starship's Super Heavy booster also came up short, and Elon Musk blamed that on its own engines. Vulcan's shortfall now looks like fuel delivery on the vehicle side. Those are different failure classes. That matters for Blue Origin, which builds the BE-4, because the same engine family powers its own heavy lifter. One inference follows, and it's ours. A vehicle-side cause decouples the BE-4's reputation from ULA's integration problem.

But don't hear "not the engines" as "quick fix." Inadequate fuel supply is a symptom, not a mechanism. ULA itself says "work remains to determine cause." There's no named component and no return-to-flight date, and the FAA still has to sign off on the final report. A vehicle-side fault could be a single valve or a redesign of the core stage. So the search has narrowed, but nobody can yet size the grounding.

That leaves Amazon exposed. Its next Leo launch isn't American at all. Amazon's own mission page lists an Ariane 6 carrying 36 more satellites, set for November 23rd. Its other domestic heavy-lift option, Blue Origin's New Glenn, is still grounded after a ground-test explosion in the spring, as this desk has reported. So until Vulcan returns, Amazon's near-term choices are a European rocket or Falcon 9. Falcon 9 belongs to the company Amazon is racing in low-Earth-orbit broadband. That hands SpaceX scheduling leverage over a direct competitor's build-out.

There's a quieter implication for every launch customer, the Pentagon included. By this desk's read, SpaceX's launch franchise is now effectively the collateral behind its AI bet. Its marginal dollar and its credit headroom are going to compute. That isn't a claim that Falcon flights are at risk. It's a claim about where the one reliable American heavy-lift provider is putting its balance sheet.

The same launch scarcity is forcing capital decisions downstream. Earlier this month, AST SpaceMobile said it will build its own twenty-ton-class rocket, called Blacklift, using Miranda engines from Firefly Aerospace. The point is to deploy its own BlueBird satellites. That's a tell. For an operator like AST, launch access, not spectrum or demand, looks like the binding constraint. But Blacklift is a press release, with no hardware shown. And AST can ill afford a second discipline. Its third-quarter revenue was 42.3 million dollars, against 211 million dollars of operating expenses. A direct-to-cell operator still without a commercial service is now adding rocket-building risk to a business far from self-funding.

So where does that leave the picture? What's genuinely inflecting is SpaceX's leverage over every competitor and customer in American heavy lift. It widened again this week as rivals stayed grounded. What's still unproven is whether SpaceX's AI compute can carry forty billion dollars of chip financing, and whether Vulcan's fix is a part swap or a redesign. The paradox worth sitting with is that the moat and the spread are widening at the same time. Watch three things from here. First, Amazon's Ariane 6 flight, no earlier than Monday. Second, ULA naming an actual component and a return-to-flight plan. Third, any primary confirmation of what SpaceX's debt actually priced at.

That's the briefing. Stay cautiously optimistic, and have a great weekend.
