Good morning, it's Friday, September 25th, and this is your enterprise technology briefing. This week's evidence comes down to one question: once agents are actually running in production, who gets paid?

Start with Salesforce, because it just published the strongest production-grade evidence on agent adoption this beat has seen. The company's new Agentic Enterprise Index sets a rule most vendor data doesn't bother with. To qualify for the dataset, Salesforce says, quote, "businesses needed to have activated agents in production every month across the analysis period." That excludes pilots by construction. Against that screen, Salesforce reports that, quote, "organizations increased activated agents by nearly 3x by the end of this fiscal year." The operational number underneath it is just as telling. In Salesforce's words, "2 days is the average time from agent creation to use." Building an agent and running it live are now separated by a weekend.

Three caveats matter here. First, Salesforce discloses no base. We don't know whether "nearly three times" means a handful of agents becoming a dozen, or thousands becoming tens of thousands. Second, the production-only screen carries survivorship bias. Any customer whose agent failed or got switched off mid-period simply drops out of the sample. That flatters the growth figure. Third, timing: the data runs through April 2026, so it's recent, but it isn't live.

Even so, it remains a count of agents, not a dollar figure. Back on September 20th, this beat asked whether any agent vendor would publish realized revenue instead of a count. This is a better count. It is still a count.

The named proof point is Telstra, and it's a real production deployment, not an announced partnership. Salesforce says the rollout, quote, "already covers millions of customer interactions a year, with new AI agents in production supporting more than 1,000 contact centre representatives." The agents handle refund and eligibility automation, plus security-permission monitoring. Salesforce also says the agents, quote, "are expected to reduce operational costs and are projected to resolve hundreds of thousands of routine enquiries annually." Notice the tense: expected, projected. That's a forecast, not a reported result. And "millions of interactions" measures breadth, not depth. It tells us nothing about what share of those interactions the agents actually resolve without a human. No contract value, no realized savings, and no headcount effect appears anywhere in the release.

So here's the takeaway on Telstra. Adoption breadth is real and large. Dollar materiality is still unshown. Hold that against this beat's earlier estimate that Agentforce is roughly three percent of Salesforce's revenue base. Adoption is visibly outrunning revenue.

Which raises the obvious follow-on question. If an agent goes from creation to live use in two days, the configuration and implementation labor that systems integrators have historically billed for gets a lot smaller. That's my interpretation, and no revenue figure sits behind it yet. But if it holds, where does that services dollar go?

One answer showed up in Anthropic's own backyard. On September 18th, Anthropic named Accenture as its first "embedded evaluator." That's a team working inside Anthropic alongside its internal safety staff. It red-teams models and tests safeguards, with employee-like access. Per Accenture's announcement, "the companies each expect to invest at least $1 billion over five years in AI safety." To be clear, that's each firm's own stated spending on AI safety. It is not a price tag on the evaluator work, and what Anthropic pays Accenture for that work is undisclosed. The billion-dollar figure is also an expectation, not a filed contract value, and its scope may be wider than the evaluator team. The arrangement puts into practice a proposal Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei made on September 12th. He called for independent evaluators with that kind of access, as a check on the pace of frontier development.

Now look at who pays. For now, Anthropic is funding Accenture's work directly. It also says, quote, "long-term, we think funding should come from pooled or government sources." That line cuts two ways. It means the evaluator is being paid by the very lab it's supposed to evaluate, a conflict this show flagged on September 22nd. And it means Anthropic itself calls the self-funded model a stopgap. For an integrator, that makes this revenue line provisional. If funding later moves to a pooled or government source, the buyer, the terms, and the margin could all change.

Here's the arc, though. Just yesterday, this beat reported Accenture as a Global Premier partner in Anthropic's Claude Marketplace. That's a commercial channel role. Now Accenture is also the lab's paid safety evaluator. That's two lab-attached roles in quick succession. And it cuts against the argument we've been making for days, that embedded AI is simply a demand headwind for integrators billing by the hour. The more accurate read is migration rather than shrinkage. Services revenue looks to be moving away from configuration hours, toward channel and assurance work attached to the lab itself.

One structural detail supports that read. The Accenture deal is explicitly "non-exclusive." That leaves room for Deloitte or another integrator to sign a similar lab-funded evaluator arrangement. If that happens, safety evaluation becomes its own consulting category rather than an Accenture franchise. That's an inference, not a confirmed trend. But it's the one to watch, because it would turn a one-off deal into a budget line.

So the Accenture point lands like this. The integrator isn't losing the agent economy. It's being repriced toward the jobs that don't compress.

The other job that doesn't compress to two days is ripping out legacy systems entirely, and this week's evidence there comes from Palantir. Defense News reports that the U.S. Army awarded a Palantir subsidiary a $48.1 million contract. The dollar figure isn't the point; it's small next to Palantir's government book. The point is what the software replaces. Per that report, it "will streamline ammunition management by consolidating nine separate legacy platforms." Nine systems become one. That's software displacing software, not a feature bolted on top.

Set that against a counter-example from the same week. The FAA's SMART air-traffic platform went live in limited Washington operations on September 21st. It runs under a twelve-year, $875 million prime contract, and Palantir didn't win it. Per FinanceFeeds, "Air Space Intelligence — not Palantir — is the prime contractor on the 12-year, $875 million award, with Palantir and Thales both bidding and losing." So legacy-replacement budgets are genuinely competed. An established data platform doesn't win them by default, and on the bigger prize this time, both Palantir and Thales lost.

Then there's the stock. Palantir shares, quote, "climbed above $190 on September 23, 2026, marking its highest level in nearly a year." That echoes the pattern this beat flagged in cybersecurity stocks earlier this month, where equity pricing ran ahead of evidence in the numbers. That raises the bar for Palantir's next print. The test is whether legacy-replacement wins are actually outpacing the losses, or whether the market is pricing a narrative.

Pull the three threads together. Agent build time is compressing toward days, which commoditizes the configuration work integrators used to bill for. What replaces it isn't disappearance. It's migration toward two harder jobs: ripping out legacy systems outright, and getting paid to police what the agents do once they're live.

Three things would settle it. First, a Salesforce dataset with a disclosed base and an actual dollar figure attached. Second, a second integrator signing its own lab-funded evaluator deal. Third, Palantir's next earnings print, showing whether its displacement wins are outrunning its losses.

That's the briefing for today. Have a great weekend, and I'll be back with more next week.
