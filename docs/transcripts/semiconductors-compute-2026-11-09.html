Good morning. It's Monday, November 9th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

All last week, this desk tracked Washington's and Beijing's grip on where Nvidia's chips can go. Over the weekend, that story reached Nvidia's chief executive himself.

Nvidia's board has opened an investigation tied to Jensen Huang's relationship with a woman named Alice Huang. Start with the allegation, and with who is making it. CNBC reports that, quote, "U.S. intelligence agencies have expressed concerns that the woman, Alice Huang, has been passing information to Beijing, according to reports in The New York Times and the Financial Times." So the claim runs from US intelligence, through the Times and the FT, into CNBC's write-up. It is not a confirmed government finding. And no evidence has surfaced that any chip was diverted or any export rule broken.

Now Nvidia's side. The board says, quote, "While the Board has no reason to believe that either national security or NVIDIA secrets were compromised, the Board's inquiry will examine all available information." That's the company's own board speaking. Treat it as a starting position, not a verdict.

The company link matters more than the personal one. Alice Huang is affiliated with Megaspeed. In CNBC's reporting, Megaspeed, quote, "has been accused of helping restricted Chinese companies acquire Nvidia AI chips." That is an accusation, not a finding. But it's the reason this story belongs in a semiconductor briefing. Jensen Huang disclosed the relationship to staff himself. In an email he wrote, quote, "I was unfaithful to my wife." A spokesperson says he is committed to continuing as CEO without interruption. The affair matters here only because it explains why there was a disclosure at all. The substance is channel compliance.

Here's the piece that's new to this desk. Capitol Hill was moving before the weekend's news broke. Three House committee chairmen sent Nvidia a formal letter dated November 4th. The House Foreign Affairs Committee's release is titled, quote, "Chairmen Mast, Moolenaar, and Crawford Request Jensen Huang Brief Congress on Link to Chinese Intelligence Asset." That letter predates the board probe and the affair disclosure by several days. So congressional scrutiny of Nvidia's China ties was already running through committee process. One limit on that. We haven't been able to read the letter's full text, so we can't say how explicitly it ties the matter to GPU export licences.

Now connect it to Saturday. That day, we reported Nvidia imposing resale conditions on cloud resellers and stepping up chip tracking in Southeast Asia. We offered two readings. The defensive one was that Nvidia is protecting its export licences ahead of enforcement scrutiny. The commercial one was that it is closing remote-access routes into China. This weekend's news raises the stakes on the defensive reading. In our judgment, whatever the board's inquiry finds, Nvidia now has a sharper reason to be seen policing its China channel aggressively.

Score it by competitive dimension, because this story is easy to overweight. It doesn't touch Nvidia's architecture lead, its manufacturing access, or its software moat. It touches two things. The first is customer relationships in Nvidia's China channel. The second is Nvidia's standing in Washington as the industry's leading advocate for China access. Our inference is that a national-security cloud over a China-linked customer makes any further loosening of advanced-GPU exports politically harder. There's a spillover too, and it's also inference. Scrutiny of China-linked resellers like Megaspeed is likely to raise compliance costs for every US accelerator vendor selling through Asian channels. The counter-reading deserves a hearing. If China chip policy is set by trade bargaining at the executive level rather than by Congress, a governance scandal may not change a single licensing decision. Either way, what's at risk is Nvidia's political position and its sales channel. Nothing reported so far reaches its operations.

The market hadn't priced any of this as of Friday's close. It was busy pricing a different fear. And in Korea, that fear was also a Washington permission story.

First, recall where memory stood. Yesterday this desk laid out a split. Memory contract prices are still forecast to rise, and SK hynix guides to demand outrunning supply for years. Yet memory stocks keep falling. That rising memory bill is a cost squeeze on the device makers who pay it, and we'll come back to it with Qualcomm.

Friday's drawdown in Korean memory stocks is now sized. SK hynix fell 10.44 percent at the Seoul close, against a 6.02 percent drop for the Kospi as a whole. Samsung Electronics lost 6.35 percent. Foreign investors sold a net 2.77 trillion won of Korean shares that day. Korean press tied the drop to fears of, quote, "tighter U.S. rules on AI data center construction after the midterm elections." Notice what that is. It's a US politics headline, not a memory contract print. Yesterday we offered three readings of the memory sell-off: front-running a peak, a beta move, or a genuine mispricing. The politics-driven attribution leans toward the beta reading. But be careful with the flow number. Heavy foreign selling fits a positioning unwind, and it fits a genuine global re-rating of memory just as well. Flow data can't tell those apart. So the Korean tape tilts the debate. It doesn't settle it.

Set against that sell-off is one forward data point from a major chip buyer. AMD's Lisa Su told investors last week that she expects server revenue to double year over year in 2027. That's management's expectation, and it blends CPU and GPU server lines, so it doesn't measure HBM consumption directly. Still, a large buyer of memory and advanced packaging is guiding up in the same week the stocks that supply those inputs got hit. The takeaway is that memory's equity multiple fell on a politics headline, while the demand guidance pointed the other way.

Now the cost side of the memory squeeze, where it may be surfacing in hard numbers: a buyer's income statement. Qualcomm's chip division, QCT, saw its pre-tax margin fall to 24 percent, down from 29 percent. That follows the 26 percent handset revenue drop we covered yesterday, which Qualcomm's executives tied in part to memory pricing. But Qualcomm hasn't split the cause of the margin decline. The Apple modem transition and product mix are equally plausible drivers. So memory is one contributor among several. What Qualcomm is doing about it is diversifying. Automotive chip revenue rose 89 percent, to about 2 billion dollars, though that comes off a smaller base. Management also points to a new data-center business that hasn't yet disclosed any revenue. The takeaway is that a buyer without pricing power is restructuring around the memory-cost squeeze, rather than out-earning it.

Back to permission, now from Beijing's side of the ledger. China has drafted export controls of its own, aimed at advanced chip-design technology. The draft itself is several weeks old. What's live now are its deadlines. It would add 40 technologies to China's export catalogue, more than half of them AI-related. Per The Next Web's reporting, it covers, quote, "AI chip designs needing 14 nanometre or finer manufacturing or 2.5D and 3D packaging, and the networking behind clusters of more than 10,000 AI accelerators." It does not restrict finished chips.

Now separate what's proposed from what's already in force. The catalogue of controlled technologies is still a draft. Comments are due November 15th, and the final text isn't settled. But one piece already applies. Per the same reporting, anyone exporting the listed technologies must register now. That includes deals that have started but aren't yet complete. So the paperwork is live today, while the restrictions themselves are still proposed.

Keep the skepticism on this one. The sourcing is secondary reporting, not a verified translation of the Chinese government text. No enforcement case has been reported. So its practical bite on EDA or IP licensing deals is unproven. In our judgment, it may be leverage-building as much as a binding constraint. It also lands beside the rare-earth deadline we flagged yesterday. China's suspension of its expanded rare-earth export controls is due to lapse tomorrow, unless Beijing extends it. Put that next to Nvidia's exposure in Washington, and the point is this. The permission constraint on this industry now runs in both directions, on chips flowing into China and on design know-how flowing out.

So here's the state of play. The operating evidence this week does not show the demand collapse that Friday's tape priced. AMD is guiding up, and Qualcomm is diversifying around the memory-cost squeeze. What has changed is permission. Nvidia's China channel is now a national-security question in Washington, and Beijing is building a mirror-image constraint on its own exports. Watch three things. First, any formal congressional follow-up to the November 4th letter. Second, the November 15th comment deadline in Beijing. Third, how the Korean memory names trade once this news cycle settles.
