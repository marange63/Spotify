Good morning, it's Friday, October 16th, and this is your private equity briefing. This week's big sponsor deals are capital going in, not coming out, and the one trade that would look like a real exit is still just talks. Let's work through why that distinction matters more than the deal values do.

Start with Thoma Bravo's Proofpoint agreeing to buy Varonis Systems, signed yesterday. The price is forty-nine dollars seventy-five cents a share in cash. Signed is not closed, so nothing has changed hands yet. Now the premium problem. That price is fifty-two percent above Varonis's close on June 22nd, the last session before the first sale rumors. Measured against the September 1st close, it's only seventeen percent. That makes three signed take-privates this week where the headline premium leans on a stale base. On Thursday we showed that CCC's headline premium was mostly a measure of how far the stock had run on leak speculation. Bumble's premium said more about a depressed starting price than about a buyer paying up. Varonis repeats the pattern. Seventeen percent over a price the market had already moved to is ordinary. It is not a sign that sponsors are suddenly paying up again.

Now the ownership logic, which matters more than the price. Thoma Bravo, Proofpoint's owner, is providing committed equity financing and has secured debt financing commitments. The split between debt and equity hasn't been disclosed, and neither has which Thoma Bravo fund writes the equity check. So take no view on leverage here. What is clear is the platform's history. Thoma Bravo took Proofpoint private in 2021, in a deal that valued it at about twelve point three billion dollars. That's the entry value, not today's mark. So this isn't a new platform, and it isn't an exit. It's fresh sponsor equity and new debt going into a five-year-old holding. No cash goes back to Thoma Bravo's limited partners today.

There's a benign reading too. Proofpoint may simply have wanted Varonis's data-security capability and paid a fair forward price for it. Both things can be true. But the fund-economics consequence is the same either way. A bigger Proofpoint needs a bigger exit. At that size, the realistic buyers narrow to the IPO market or a very large strategic. That lengthens the expected hold and defers distributions on a 2021 position. The value-creation case is consolidation, not multiple expansion, and it only pays if the combined company exits at or above its blended entry. So Varonis is deployment, not realization, and the exit it eventually needs just got larger.

The financing side of that same pattern shows up in Thursday's lead, CCC Intelligent Solutions. Its buyer group, GTCR and Elliott Investment Management, accepted no financing condition. The new number is the termination fees. CCC would owe about a hundred forty-nine and a half million dollars if the deal collapses under specific circumstances. The buyer entity would owe about three hundred fourteen million, more than double. Neither fee is a guaranteed payout; each applies only in defined scenarios. Still, a reverse fee that lopsided is the measurable form of "no financing condition." It shows these buyers underwriting deal certainty on a high-quality, financeable software cash flow. It does not show that the broad buyout debt market has reopened. That's a narrower claim, and a more useful one.

Now the other direction, capital that might actually come out. On Thursday we closed by asking whether any sponsor would exit this quarter without an insider sitting on both sides of the table. The first candidate has appeared. London Stock Exchange Group has confirmed it's in talks to buy Octus, the data business owned by Permira. Permira acquired control of Octus from Warburg Pincus in 2022, at a valuation of one point three billion dollars. Reuters reports Octus is expected to generate around a hundred forty-five million dollars of EBITDA this year, rising to roughly two hundred million in 2027. Those are reported expectations, not audited results. If a deal happens, it would be a third-party strategic buyer taking out a sponsor-owned asset. That's the kind of realization that actually becomes distributed capital, unlike a continuation vehicle or an add-on like Varonis.

But keep the caveats front and center. LSEG's own statement says no final agreement has been reached and no transaction is guaranteed. Any deal would also need regulatory approval. There's no disclosed timetable, and no disclosed form of consideration, cash or stock. LSEG shares closed four point six eight percent lower the day the talks were confirmed. That tells you the market isn't cheering the idea, though it doesn't tell you what price is on the table. Even if Octus signs, any gain against Permira's 2022 entry is gross and at the enterprise level. It's before fees and carry, and it isn't a fund distribution figure. And Octus is a scarce data asset that a data-and-analytics strategic specifically wants. That's a narrow case. It says little about the long tail of aged sponsor holdings, and it isn't evidence that the IPO or trade-sale window has reopened broadly. One unsigned deal doesn't make a thaw.

Why doesn't one potential exit move the needle? Look at the allocator side, starting with CalPERS. These aren't this week's numbers, but they haven't been on this show. A June staff report shows CalPERS' private equity net asset value climbing from ninety-two point two billion dollars to a hundred nineteen point three billion in the year to March 31st. Separately, Preqin puts private equity at nineteen point three percent of the CalPERS portfolio as of May. The board's target is seventeen percent, raised from thirteen percent only in March of 2024. The two figures may not be measured on the same basis, so call it running above target on Preqin's numbers rather than a confirmed breach. The NAV growth also blends marks, contributions, and new commitments, and the data doesn't separate them.

Two readings follow for CalPERS. Running hot against a recently raised target limits fresh commitments at the margin. But Preqin reports that CalPERS has adopted a Total Portfolio Approach, which may loosen hard asset-class caps rather than force any selling. Either way, paper value is growing faster than the policy limit built to contain it.

Set that against the industry-wide cash yardstick. Distributions as a share of net asset value have stayed below fifteen percent for four years running, an industry record, per Bain data as cited by Moonfare. Fundraising, meanwhile, hasn't collapsed. NEPC puts U.S. fundraising for the first half of this year at a hundred fifty-nine point six billion dollars across a hundred seventy-eight funds. That's broadly consistent with 2025, even as the number of successful closes keeps falling. PwC, on a different dataset, has aggregate dollars up nine percent on the first half of last year, with fund count slipping as established managers took a growing share. The two numbers don't splice, but they point the same way, toward concentration. Capital keeps flowing to the same scaled platforms that can do Varonis-style add-ons. Meanwhile the cash coming back to limited partners sits at a record low. Paper is compounding faster than cash is returning.

So where does the cycle actually stand? On our read, sponsors with scale are extending holds and deploying through committed equity and debt inside existing platforms. That can make deployment look healthy without any real improvement in exits. Genuine third-party exits remain rare, and the one in view, Octus, is still unsigned. Distributions, not deal headlines, remain the binding constraint on this cycle.

Watch three things from here. First, whether the Octus talks produce a signed agreement, and at what value against Permira's carrying mark. That's a direct test of whether sponsor marks lag or lead real clearing prices. Second, whether Varonis and CCC close on schedule. Third, the managers' third-quarter reports, starting with Blackstone, with KKR confirmed for November 9th. No third-quarter figures from either firm are in yet, so treat anything you hear before then as speculation.

That's where we stand heading into the weekend; thanks for listening, and I'll catch you at the next briefing.
