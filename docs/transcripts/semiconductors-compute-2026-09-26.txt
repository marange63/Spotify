Good morning, it's Saturday, September 26th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

A quick word on freshness. The lead story is five days old, and a couple of the other pieces run back a month or more. I'll date each one as we go. The angles, though, are new to this show.

Start with the CPU socket inside the AI rack, because it just stopped being an x86 default. On September 21st, reporting flagged that Nvidia is folding its own Vera CPU into GPU platform sales. The argument was that Nvidia is eating into CPU revenue that used to default to Intel or AMD. That framing undersells what's happening. Nvidia's own newsroom release says Vera is also sold standalone. It ships in air-cooled two-socket servers through Dell, HPE, Lenovo, and Supermicro. Nvidia's own words are "the first standard CPU option beyond x86." So this isn't just a bundle that sweetens a GPU order. Nvidia is putting a freestanding Arm-based CPU into the same OEM channel Intel and AMD sell through. It is pitched at enterprise, cloud, and data-processing work, not only AI racks. That's a direct socket challenge.

Now the size of the prize. One forecast making the rounds has AI CPU revenue growing from 38 billion dollars to 155 billion by 2030. That's a compound rate of about 42 percent a year. Handle that number with care. The firm behind it and its methodology couldn't be confirmed, so I won't attach a name to it. I also won't compare it with accelerator growth, because no accelerator figure was verified. But take the shape of it seriously. If that market roughly quadruples, Intel and AMD can lose a large share of the socket and still grow AI CPU revenue in dollars. So the real threat isn't necessarily to their revenue line. It's to their pricing power and their control of the socket. Margin, not revenue, is the variable to watch.

Nvidia is attacking that socket from the platform side. Arm is attacking it from underneath. Arm unveiled its own finished server chip, the AGI CPU, back on March 24th. It then disclosed detailed specs at Hot Chips in late August. The chip runs up to 136 Neoverse cores at 300 watts. Arm claims more than double the performance per rack, at the same power, versus x86. That is Arm's own launch claim, and nobody has independently benchmarked it. Mass production is slated for the second half of this year. And there is no named customer yet for the finished part.

What matters is who Arm is now competing with. Arm's business has been licensing its architecture to hyperscalers who design their own Arm chips, like Amazon's Graviton and Google's Axion. Now Arm is selling a finished rival into the same market. That puts it in channel conflict with some of its biggest licensees. It also raises the question the launch coverage skips: who buys it? The hyperscalers best placed to run an Arm server CPU already build their own. My inference, not anything reported, is that the likeliest buyers are operators without a custom silicon program.

So we have two structurally different attacks on one socket. Nvidia contests it through platform control and OEM distribution. Arm contests it by moving from architecture licensor to finished-silicon vendor. Score those as separate competitive dimensions. They don't reinforce each other, and it's an open question whether either one lands a real customer.

Both attacks eventually run into the same wall, which is TSMC's advanced-packaging queue. Mizuho Securities Asia has raised its forecast for TSMC's CoWoS capacity to 190,000 to 200,000 units a month by 2027. That's a broker's estimate, not a TSMC target. What's new is the reasoning. Mizuho's note names the drivers outright: "stronger demand outlook for AI and server CPU in 2027, including Nvidia Vera CPU, Intel and AMD server CPU, and CSP players' server CPU." Mizuho also expects AI-related server CPU demand, both x86 and Arm, to grow more than 50 percent year over year in 2027. So GPUs are no longer the only bidders for packaging capacity. The very CPUs fighting over that socket are now in the same queue. Yesterday we reported that TSMC is said to be telling Nvidia and Broadcom it can't supply everything they want. That allocation squeeze just picked up a new class of bidder.

If this is a genuine squeeze on Intel and AMD, the market hasn't priced it that way. Chip stocks sold off sharply on September 14th on pacing worries. By September 17th, the complex staged what one report called a broad and unusually powerful rebound. Intel, AMD, Arm, Qualcomm, and Nvidia all advanced together. And as of Wednesday, AMD was up 178 percent over the trailing three months. That is not how investors treat a company losing its core socket. Either AMD's value now rides on something beyond x86, or the market is betting the AI CPU pie grows fast enough to cover everyone. I'd hold that as an open tension, not a resolved call.

Whoever wins that socket still has to feed it, and that brings us to memory. Intel detailed its Crescent Island inference chip at Hot Chips. It is built around 160 gigabytes of LPDDR5X rather than high-bandwidth memory, and it targets air-cooled inference servers. Read that as Intel routing around the HBM queue. That's my reading of the memory choice, not a strategy Intel has stated. It isn't necessarily cheaper, because conventional DRAM is tight too. And the commercial launch date is disputed, with trade press doubting Intel's timeline. So treat Crescent Island as a design choice, not a shipping product. It does connect Intel's two fronts, though. Squeezed on the CPU socket, Intel is trying to enter inference through the one memory door that isn't rationed like HBM.

Which brings us to Micron, the name this show has flagged as the next hard test all week. According to DigiTimes, Micron plans to roughly double its high-bandwidth memory capacity by the end of this year, to about 100,000 wafers a month. That is reported, not a Micron disclosure. If it's real, it revises yesterday's read that Micron's construction-heavy capex mostly pays off later in the decade. Some HBM supply could arrive much sooner. How Micron gets there is unconfirmed. My inference is that it comes partly from converting existing DRAM lines. If so, conventional DRAM gets tighter, not looser. That squeezes exactly the kind of memory Intel's Crescent Island depends on.

Micron reports fiscal fourth-quarter results after the close this coming Wednesday, September 30th. Nothing about that print is known yet. What we do have is the bar. Micron's June guidance called for revenue of 50 billion dollars, plus or minus one billion. It guided non-GAAP earnings per share to 31 dollars, plus or minus one dollar. A print above the top of that range would say memory pricing power is landing with the memory makers themselves. A print below the range would suggest more of the surge is being absorbed upstream by Nvidia, AMD, and the hyperscalers. Either way, it won't settle the structural-versus-cyclical debate on its own.

So here's where that leaves the picture. The AI rack's CPU socket is contested for the first time in years. Nvidia is coming through its platform and through standalone OEM servers. Arm is coming through vertical integration. But the market for that socket may be growing fast enough that incumbents lose share without losing revenue. Every one of those chips still clears the same packaging and memory gates. Watch two dated tests from here. First, whether Micron's print on Wednesday shows pricing power sticking with the memory makers. Second, whether Arm or Nvidia names a paying customer, or an attach rate, for their new CPUs.

That's the briefing for today. Thanks for listening, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
