Good morning. It's Saturday, August 22nd, and this is your private credit briefing.

A new dataset landed this week on a book we have tracked every day for the last five sessions, and the interesting number in it is not the one in the headline. PitchBook LCD published its second-quarter tracker of the ten largest publicly traded business development companies on Wednesday. The top-line print is that debt tranches in non-accrual status at those ten BDCs rose to three point nine five percent of total debt at cost, up twenty basis points on the quarter, with the dollar balance up eighty-nine million dollars to three point three billion. Before you file that next to anything this show has cited earlier in the week, know that it is not the next point on any of those lines. It is a fourth measurement basis.

This week alone we have used Octus's hundred-and-seventy-four-fund aggregate, where non-accrual debt at cost jumped forty percent in a single quarter. We have used a median non-accrual ratio across the twenty largest BDCs, which went from two percent to two point eight percent. We have used Fitch at six point one percent and Kroll at one point five percent on trailing twelve-month private credit defaults. LCD's three point nine five is a cost-weighted aggregate across a top-ten cohort. None of those four bases add together, and none of them is the continuation of another. Four different rulers, all reading warmer. That is the setup.

Here is the actual content of Wednesday's print, and it sits underneath the headline. LCD also reports an adjusted, borrower-level measure. Instead of counting only the loan facility that stopped paying, it reclassifies a borrower's entire debt balance as non-accrual the moment any single tranche from that borrower trips. LCD's own language is explicit about why. In BDC reporting, it says, it is not unusual to see one loan facility from a borrower placed on non-accrual while another loan in that same borrower's structure isn't. On that adjusted basis, exposure at cost reaches five billion dollars, or five point nine five percent of total debt, up fifty-four basis points in the quarter. That is nearly three times the twenty-basis-point move in the headline number.

Be careful about what that adjusted figure is. It is an exposure measure, not a loss estimate. Nobody is telling you that six percent of the book has stopped paying. What it tells you is how much of these portfolios sits at borrowers where something, somewhere in the capital structure, has already stopped paying. And that share is growing nearly three times faster than the share the industry actually headlines. The gap between the two measures, not the level of either one, is the leading indicator here.

The second thing under the headline is breadth. The count of distinct borrowers with at least one tranche on non-accrual rose by eleven in the quarter, to a hundred and one. Set that against a non-accrual balance that moved only eighty-nine million dollars on a three point three billion dollar base. More names, barely more dollars. That is more credits getting into trouble a little at a time, not a handful of large blowups, and most of the twenty-basis-point move in the headline ratio is denominator and mix rather than a wave of new failures. That shape matters, because it is what an early turn in a cycle looks like rather than a crisis. It also argues against a single forced-sale event that reprices everything at once. What you get instead is a slow grind in net investment income.

Now the layer that makes the headline measure the slowest one to move by construction. LCD states plainly that much of the debt owed by these non-accrual borrowers is payment-in-kind, and that PIK income does not show up in the cash interest income figure. So a fund can keep reporting a stable portfolio yield while the cash actually arriving quietly shrinks. That is a mechanism, not a magnitude. There is no clean industry-wide PIK percentage for the second quarter, so I am not going to attach one to it. But the direction is unambiguous. Reported yield and earned cash income are separating. And that separation shows up first in distribution coverage, which is the number the wealth channel actually watches, long before it shows up in NAV. A gated fund with a covered distribution is a very different sales story from a gated fund leaning on return of capital to pay one.

One more honesty note about the data itself. LCD says outright that only the ten largest publicly traded BDCs had reported second-quarter results when it published. That cohort is a proxy for the broader market, not the market. Its wider view, covering more than two hundred managers, is still a first-quarter reading, and on that first-quarter basis adjusted non-accrual loans rose six point one billion dollars to seventeen point three billion. Different quarter, different universe. Do not hear those two numbers as one series either. The useful point is that the fresher, broader figure does not exist yet, and will not until the rest of the BDC universe reports.

Which brings me to a correction. This briefing closed yesterday by saying the dispersion across default measures was definitional, not credit. Different counting conventions producing different numbers, none of them more true than the others. Wednesday's data revises that. The tranche-versus-borrower split is itself carrying credit information, and it is decomposable. LCD is showing you precisely how a single credit deteriorates in stages, one facility at a time, well before it ever reaches an aggregate default rate. The definitional gap was never neutral. It was measuring something real, and I was not reading it that way.

So where does that leave the marks. This show has made the generic point about valuation opacity four times this week, so here is the specific version. The place to look is not the tranches already on non-accrual. Those are flagged, and everyone can see them. It is the still-performing tranches sitting at those hundred and one borrowers where something else has already stopped paying. Those loans are carried at or near par. If the borrower-level measure keeps outrunning the tranche-level one, that is exactly where the stale mark lives, because a fund has every incentive to keep valuing the performing piece at cost until it cannot.

One closing thread on funding. With non-traded BDC gross sales down eighty-two percent year over year in the second quarter, and the existing book widening at the borrower level, new origination capacity increasingly has to come from somewhere other than the wealth channel. That means insurance balance sheets, CLO liabilities, or standing bank co-origination programs. The Wells Fargo and Centerbridge venture, Overland Advantage, is the cleanest example with a hard number attached. As of January of this year it had deployed more than seven billion dollars since launch, including eighteen transactions worth about four billion in 2025. That is old plumbing rather than news. It matters here because it is precisely the channel that lets a bank keep the fee and the client relationship while the credit risk sits inside a fund. Headline bank balance-sheet exposure understates how wired the two systems already are to each other.

Where this leaves the cycle. Four measurement bases, all pointing the same way, and this is the first one that explains why they disagree instead of just adding another data point to the pile. Deterioration is broadening faster than it is deepening, which is a turn, not a break. The unresolved risk is still valuation, but stated properly. It is whether the performing tranches at already-troubled borrowers are marked at a price anyone would actually pay today. Two near-term catalysts. Cliffwater's third-quarter repurchase window closes August 31st, and that gives a read on how much redemption pressure is still building behind the gates. Then the third-quarter non-accrual prints later this year, which tell you whether the borrower-level measure keeps outrunning the headline or whether this quarter was the widest the gap gets.

That's the briefing. Have a good weekend.
