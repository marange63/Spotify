Private Equity & the Deal Economy

Transcript · September 8, 2026

Good morning. It's Tuesday, September 8th, and this is your private equity briefing.

All week this show has argued that liquidity in this asset class is being engineered rather than realized. What it has never had is the number showing which exit route actually broke. PitchBook's second-quarter US PE Breakdown supplies it. One sourcing note before the figure, because it matters. PitchBook's own report page is not publicly retrievable, so these numbers reach us through summaries of that report rather than a direct read of the primary text. Treat them as a high-confidence secondary read of a primary dataset, not as a verbatim print.

With that said, here is the headline. Total US private equity exit value fell to a hundred and two point six billion dollars in the quarter, down roughly 46 percent from the first quarter. That number understates the story rather than overstating it. Inside the total sits the figure that carries the argument. Asset sales to corporate strategic buyers, historically the single largest exit channel for sponsor-owned companies, fell 63 percent, to thirty-eight and a half billion dollars.

Now the discipline this data requires. The quarter ended June 30th, more than two months ago. It is the last complete read available, not a live signal. No third-quarter print exists yet, and nothing here tells you whether the strategic channel reopened after June. PitchBook itself names genuine one-off drags in the quarter, an energy price spike layered on tighter financing conditions and geopolitical uncertainty. And a 46 percent decline off a strong first quarter is partly a base effect. So do not read a trend out of one quarter.

What survives all of that is the gap between the two numbers. The market as a whole slowed by roughly 46 percent. The strategic buyer withdrew by 63. That difference is not a base effect and it is not an energy price. It is a composition shift. The buyer who used to write the biggest check for a sponsor's portfolio company stepped back faster than everyone else. And when one exit route closes, the realization burden does not disappear. It migrates to whatever channel is still open, and that channel is the sponsor's own continuation vehicle.

Follow that downstream and you land on the fundraising machine, which is where a quarterly exit print eventually gets paid for. A strategic sale is a cash event. A transfer into a sponsor-controlled vehicle mostly is not. So vintages that underwrote a 2026 strategic exit are now carrying unrealized value rather than cash returned, and cash returned is the number a limited partner uses to fund the next commitment. I want to be careful here, because there is no quotable 2026 distribution figure to attach to that claim, and I am not going to invent one. But the mechanism was visible on this show on Friday, in Blue Owl's GP Stakes Fund the Sixth, targeting thirteen billion dollars and now running roughly a year behind the close the firm itself had guided to. An exit problem in one quarter becomes a calendar problem for the next raise.

The same quarter shows deployment behaving the same way, and this is where the shape of the cycle gets clearer. US private equity deal value fell to a hundred and seventy-seven billion dollars, down 37.5 percent quarter on quarter. Set that next to a separate global series from PwC, on a different methodology that should not be blended with PitchBook's US figures. Globally, deal volume fell 34 percent while average deal size rose nearly fourfold. Read together, that is not a retreat. That is a concentration. Fewer transactions, each far larger. The fundraising side rhymes with it, since the quarter's totals leaned on a handful of giant closes, KKR's twenty-three billion dollar North America Fund the Fourteenth among them. The practical consequence is that headline dry powder overstates how deployable that capital actually is. Most of it is waiting for one of a small number of trophy trades, not spreading across the market.

That concentration has an address, and it is the UK. Takeover activity there is running at its highest level since 2015. Sponsors are chasing it for a specific reason. The FTSE 100 trades at roughly 13 times earnings against 15.3 times for the broader MSCI Europe index. That persistent discount is doing the work that leverage and multiple expansion used to do. Two qualifications. First, this is not purely a private equity story, because trade buyers still account for more than half of announced deal value, so sponsors dominate the take-private subset rather than the headline aggregate. Second, where sponsors are active, they are paying up. Deals are being struck at an average 44 percent premium to the undisturbed price, up from 40 percent a year earlier. A rising premium eats directly into the entry-multiple discount that is supposed to justify the whole trade. If premiums keep climbing while exits stay shut, sponsors are spending the very edge they came for.

Which brings us to the harder question. If the strategic buyer has stepped back, what does the replacement bid actually pay? This show established earlier in the week that sponsor-led continuation vehicles are now the dominant growth engine in secondaries, dwarfing traditional sales of fund stakes by limited partners. I use that only to size the stakes. The new information is how that market prices, and it prices in two directions at once. Single-asset continuation vehicles are clearing around 14 percent above net asset value. Meanwhile 71 percent of multi-asset vehicles sold by limited partners are still pricing below par. Flag those two figures as single-source. They come from an aggregator summarizing a bank's first-half secondary market review, and neither could be cross-checked against a second report.

Take them at face value and the implication is sharp. There is not one secondaries market. There are two, priced on opposite sides of net asset value. The premium goes to the single trophy asset a sponsor chose to keep and marked itself. The discount goes to the diversified portfolios limited partners are simply trying to offload. That distinction matters because a premium on a continuation vehicle gets sold as validation of a manager's marks, and it is nothing of the kind. The denominator is the sponsor's own reported value, on an asset the sponsor selected. That is a selection effect, not an independent fair-value test. The honest signal runs the other way. When the market gets a diversified basket to price, its verdict is that the average mark is too high.

Two consequences follow. The first is an incentive problem. If sponsor-controlled vehicles are the only route clearing above net asset value, sponsors have every reason to route their best remaining assets into vehicles they price themselves, improving the reported outcome while leaving the weaker residual in the original fund. That is how a distribution problem quietly becomes a markdown problem. The second is capacity. The replacement bid is finite. Secondaries dry powder was down 10 percent, to a hundred and ninety-four billion dollars, on the figures this show used last week. If new closes do not refresh that pool, the bifurcation widens rather than heals.

One last channel to watch, and it pays off a flag planted on this show on Friday. The argument then was that raising the process cost of continuation vehicles, through ILPA's draft guidance, would not remove the need for liquidity. It would relocate it somewhere with less disclosure. Net asset value lending is the obvious destination, and it is scaling. The market stands at an estimated one hundred to a hundred and fifty billion dollars outstanding. In March, 17Capital closed a seven and a half billion dollar fund dedicated to this lending, the largest ever raised for the strategy. That close is six months old, so treat it as structural evidence rather than news. Critics argue this is leverage on leverage dressed up as portfolio management. That characterization is contested and I have no numbers to settle it. What is not contested is that this channel discloses materially less than the one the limited partners are now busy policing.

So where does the cycle stand. On the last complete quarter of data, the industry lost most of its largest exit channel, and what has replaced it is a bid that pays a premium only for the assets sponsors already wanted to keep. Everything else, the diversified portfolios, the broad deployment, the traditional strategic sale, is priced at a discount or is simply not happening. Watch three things from here. PitchBook's third-quarter print, for whether the strategic channel stayed shut past June. The next round of manager earnings, for any guidance on the pace of realizations. And whether any of this year's announced sponsor exits actually converts to cash before year-end. That is the tape that confirms or breaks this read.

That's the briefing. Talk to you next time.
