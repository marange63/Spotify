Space & the Orbital Economy

Transcript · September 21, 2026

Good morning, and welcome to the Space and Orbital Economy briefing for Monday, September 21st, 2026.

The thesis today is simple. Defense demand in orbit is graduating from speeches, to prototypes, to standing operating lines. But the launch base underneath that demand is narrowing, not widening. So the value is accruing to whoever owns persistent sensing and satellite buses, not to whoever is building the next rocket.

Start with the change at the margin. On September 18, the Space Force said it is expanding its use of commercial satellites to detect and locate GPS jamming and other electronic interference. SpaceNews describes it as turning what began as an experiment into a standing global operation. The remarks came at the AMOS conference in Maui, from Barbara Golf, who leads the Space Force's Joint Commercial Operations Cell.

Here is why the word standing matters. An experiment is a one-off task with a one-off payment, if it has any payment at all. A standing operation implies a repeating task, and a repeating task implies a repeating buyer. That is the first genuinely recurring commercial data line we have seen on this beat.

It also completes an arc. Last week, that same demand showed up as prototype contracts, with Northrop Grumman and True Anomaly picked to build the GHOST-R geosynchronous reconnaissance satellites. Now it shows up as an operating line. Rhetoric, then prototypes, then a service.

Now grade that claim down. This is a posture statement, not a purchase order. Nothing I can verify names a vendor, a contract vehicle, a duration, or a dollar value, and the report is paywalled past its opening. No GHOST-R award value has been published either.

The consensus read is that this re-rates the commercial RF-geolocation operators, the constellations that listen for the jammer rather than photograph the ground. I have to stop short of naming one, because nothing I can verify ties a specific operator to this operation, and that silence is itself the story. The consensus assumes the government will now pay subscription prices for data it has been getting cheaply through an experiment. The alternative is that standing simply means an operations cell tasks existing feeds at no incremental cost. Nothing retrieved settles which it is.

So the takeaway is about revenue quality, not revenue level. If this becomes a real buy, whoever wins it sells a subscription against a budget line rather than one-off imagery tasks, and that backlog deserves a lower discount rate. But today it is a demand signal, not sized revenue.

An operating line still has to get to orbit on someone's rocket. And that brings us to the supply side, where the choices are shrinking.

On September 16, SpaceX flew USSF-259 from Vandenberg, a classified Space Force payload headed to a low Earth orbit inclined roughly 80 degrees. Spaceflight Now reports it was SpaceX's third launch in a month procured by Space Systems Command under the National Security Space Launch Phase 3 Lane 1 contract. The booster, B1097, was on its thirteenth flight.

Two cautions. Lane 1 is the on-ramp lane for lower-risk missions, so this says nothing about the heavy Lane 2 missions. And no source I have gives a 2026 Phase 3 total or a split among providers. What the fact does say is that the cheapest lift and the most concentrated lift are the same thing. Read USSF-259 as evidence the Pentagon has few others to fly with, not only as evidence of SpaceX strength.

Consider who else was supposed to be flying. The alternative-lift cohort promised for 2026 is quietly becoming the 2027 cohort, and the newest entry is Astra. SpaceNews reported on September 18 that Astra now projects early 2027 for Rocket 4.0, and framed the company as aiming to reenter a market increasingly desperate for launch capacity.

Notice the tension there. This market has been desperate for capacity for two years. It has still not pulled a single new Western medium-lift vehicle onto a pad. That suggests the binding constraint is engine and stage qualification, not demand. A shortage of launch is not automatically an opportunity for challengers.

The standing cases tell the same story. Rocket Lab's Neutron has nothing newer than August 10. That day the company said production of the Stage 1 tank is aligned with delivery to the pad in the fourth quarter. It also said it is balancing that first launch against production readiness. Aligned to reach the pad is not a launch date. And Blue Origin's New Glenn is still grounded after the May 28 pad explosion, traced to the main oxygen valve on one of its BE-4 engines. Return to flight before year-end is a company target, nothing more.

The counterweight is China. On August 19, LandSpace's Zhuque-3 made what Spaceflight Now called China's first successful recovery attempt of an orbital-class first stage using landing legs. But NASASpaceflight reported the stage toppled afterward, so its post-landing condition is disputed. One legged landing is not a booster in rotation. I would not yet price this as China closing the reusability gap.

The so-what is that Western assured access is narrowing while demand for it grows. That raises the shadow price of a second supplier. It is the strongest available argument for allied buyers to overpay for Vulcan, Ariane 6, or Neutron capacity. It also means launch market share data understates what buyers would actually pay for optionality.

So where does the capital go, if not into rockets? Into the layers that do not require a new one.

Take EnduroSat. We covered its 205 million dollar round on September 19, co-led by Riot Ventures and Atreides Management, and its contract to build 24 software-defined buses for Vantor, the Earth-observation operator. The company says it has launched 103 satellites to date. The number to interrogate is its stated goal of driving the cost of space data to roughly one dollar per gigabyte, from hundreds of dollars today.

That figure comes from a press release by the party raising money. And it implicitly assumes launch cost keeps falling, which is exactly what the last section says is not arriving. If buses commoditize at that price, the margin in an Earth-observation constellation migrates to whoever owns the sensor and the model, not the platform. The bus becomes the commodity and the sensor keeps the pricing power.

Then there is Starcloud, the orbital-compute company. TechCrunch reports it raised a 250 million dollar extension to its Series A in August. Its chief executive said launch is, in his words, pretty constrained right now because SpaceX's Falcon 9 program is scheduled to end in 2028. That is single-source, and no SpaceX announcement I can find sets any such retirement date. Falcon 9 was flying a classified Space Force payload five days ago, on a booster making its thirteenth flight. I cannot resolve that tension, so hold it as a flag on the pitch. Orbital compute needs cheap heavy lift to work, and it cites the absence of cheap heavy lift as the reason to fund it now.

One more signal. SpaceNews reports early signs of a resurgence in commercial geostationary communications satellites. The order counts are paywalled, so this may be a handful of replacement buys rather than a cycle. But it fits the pattern of buses and replenishment getting funded while new lift does not.

Notice the three standards of proof today. A Space Force posture statement, a company press release, and a venture round are three different things. The government's newest commitments arrive with no dollar values. The checkable numbers keep coming from companies raising money.

One catalyst to hold in mind. Starship Flight 14 is targeted no earlier than Monday, September 28, pending regulatory approval. SpaceX gave no public reason for the roughly six-day slip from September 22, so any licensing explanation is inference. The plan is to deploy 26 Starlink V3 satellites, with the booster landing offshore in the Gulf rather than being caught by the tower.

The new information is that this show has now carried the same date in four straight editions, after a long run in which it moved every time. A clean orbital flight would validate the vehicle as a deployment platform. It would tell you nothing about cost per kilogram, because the booster is not being caught and the ship is being expended in the Pacific. And the flight has not happened, so nothing here is a result.

Pulling it together. What is genuinely inflecting is recurring demand for commercial sensing and bus manufacturing at scale. What is still paper is every non-SpaceX medium-lift vehicle, the sized value of the standing interference operation, and orbital compute.

Put that on the tape. The narrowing launch base prices up scarcity value for the second suppliers, meaning ULA's Vulcan, Arianespace's Ariane 6, and Rocket Lab's Neutron option, and it prices up further the longer New Glenn stays grounded. The standing sensing line prices up the picks and shovels instead, meaning bus builders like EnduroSat and the prime-plus-entrant pairing of Northrop Grumman and True Anomaly that won GHOST-R.

The open questions are who gets paid for that standing operation, and whether any second Western launcher reaches a pad before 2027. The catalysts to watch are Starship Flight 14, any Space Force disclosure of vendors or values, and the first hard dates from Neutron or New Glenn.

That is the briefing for September 21st. Thanks for listening, and see you tomorrow.
