Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Equity & the Deal Economy

Transcript · October 31, 2026

Good morning. It's Saturday, October 31st, and here's where the private-equity cycle stands.

Start with the largest deal in play this week, and start with the caveat: these are talks, not a deal. The Financial Times reported on Friday that Clayton, Dubilier and Rice is working with GTCR on a joint take-private bid for Global Payments. The FT says the deal could value the company at more than forty billion dollars, including debt. Nothing is signed, there is no announced price, and the talks could still collapse. William Blair has put a plausible take-private price near one hundred one dollars a share. That is the bank's own estimate, not terms. And William Blair's caveat is the one worth repeating: anything higher "feels rich" against better-positioned fintech peers. The shares jumped on the report.

The structure matters more than the price. GTCR is Global Payments' largest shareholder. That stake traces back to the Worldpay deal, when Global Payments bought Worldpay from GTCR and FIS. So one of the firms bidding to take the company private already sits inside the cap table. That gives the bid an informed insider and a block of support. It also means the price is being set by a party on both sides of the table.

That insider structure is part of a wider shift this month. Sponsors are finding ways around the clean sale from one buyout fund to another, a channel that has nearly shut. Earlier this week, Trian proposed taking Wendy's private for about one point four billion dollars. Trian is already Wendy's largest shareholder, and it plans to roll its stake rather than cash out. And on October 8th, CD&R agreed to take Option Care Health private alongside McKesson, a strategic partner, at a five-point-eight-billion-dollar valuation. Global Payments now makes CD&R a partner in two such deals in a single month. Why does it matter? An insider or a strategic gives the deal a buyer that doesn't depend on another sponsor's bid. The cost is price discovery. Each of these prices is set by someone with an edge, not by an open auction. Elliott Management also built a Global Payments stake after the Worldpay deal was announced. But its size and intentions were never disclosed, so there is no activist-campaign story to tell yet.

Here's the part that matters for the exit math. A forty-billion-dollar take-private is capital going in, not cash coming out. Whether it returns anything to GTCR's own limited partners depends on whether GTCR rolls its stake into the new structure or sells. No reporting we found resolves that. So file this as megadeal ambition returning, not as proof the exit backlog is clearing. If anything, it deepens the backlog. Every dollar committed to a new take-private eventually needs one of the same scarce exit routes. And a sponsor paying up for a de-rated processor under fintech competition is buying a turnaround, not a compounder. If the price lands above William Blair's "rich" line, that future exit gets less forgiving.

A forty-billion-dollar check needs a fund that size behind it, and this week one closed. Advent International's eleventh flagship buyout fund, GPE Eleven, closed at about twenty-seven billion dollars. That's the largest buyout fund to close so far in 2026. Alongside it, Advent raised more than three billion dollars for a new mid-market vehicle aimed at smaller checks.

That pairing is the bifurcation this cycle keeps producing. Brand-name managers clear mega-closes while the broader fundraising market stays slow. Capital is concentrating into fewer, larger hands rather than recovering across the board. A manager that can raise a mid-market fund off the back of its flagship has a hedge across deal sizes. The sub-scale managers without a flagship to lean on don't, and their squeeze tightens.

The more interesting number is what managing partner John Maldonado said. Advent has generated about thirteen billion dollars in realizations so far this year, against roughly ten billion invested. That's a manager-stated figure, not an audited distributions-to-paid-in number. Its composition isn't disclosed either, so we can't tell arm's-length sales from recaps or continuation vehicles. But the inference is worth stating. Limited partners may be re-upping with Advent precisely because it can show more cash out than in this year. Brand alone doesn't buy that kind of close. Demonstrated cash-back might.

Now set Advent beside Blackstone. On its third-quarter call, Blackstone reported two hundred twenty-nine billion dollars of dry powder. That is firm-wide, across real estate, credit, infrastructure and private equity, not a buyout number. Even so, it is more than eight times the size of Advent's new flagship. A meaningful share of it is aimed at large take-privates like Global Payments. More money chasing scarce large assets pushes entry prices up, which makes today's vintages harder to exit well. Blackstone also told investors that carry realizations already under contract for the fourth quarter should exceed the firm's entire third-quarter net realizations. That promise is a test we'll come back to.

All that capital eventually has to get out, so look at how exits are actually clearing. The freshest comprehensive secondaries data covers the first half of this year, published in July. Single-asset continuation vehicles cleared at an average discount to net asset value of about three percent, per Campbell Lutyens. That's essentially par. High-quality buyout interests sold by limited partners cleared at about a ten percent discount, per Evercore. The two firms measure different things, so hold the comparison loosely. But a seven-point gap is the price of selection, not proof that private-equity marks broadly hold up.

The reason is simple. General partners choose which trophy assets to roll into a continuation vehicle. Near-par pricing on hand-picked assets tells you little about the unsold back-book behind it. If that pattern persists, the non-trophy assets stay at marks that only the manager's own valuation supports. That's where mark opacity concentrates.

Continuation vehicles made up fourteen percent of global sponsor-backed exit volume in the first half. Part of the reason is that the other exit channel, the public listing, keeps disappointing. Firmus, an AI-infrastructure operator backed by Nvidia and Blackstone, abandoned a planned Australian IPO. The offering had targeted roughly five billion dollars and failed to find enough demand. That comes from Dealroom, a single source. It follows AirTrunk, the Blackstone-backed data-center platform, which this desk reported last week had pushed its Singapore listing into 2027. Two Blackstone-linked digital-infrastructure listings stalling in October is a pattern to watch, not a closed window. The IPO route stays selective, even for the asset class investors say they want most. So the exit that works best right now is the one the general partner controls end to end. That is also the weakest form of price discovery available.

The same liquidity question is reaching individual investors, and this desk owes a correction. On Thursday we said semi-liquid retail vehicles hadn't yet faced a real redemption test. Blackstone's own third-quarter call, a week earlier, already showed otherwise. Repurchase requests at its private-credit retail vehicle, BCRED, hit ten percent of net asset value against a five percent quarterly cap. Blackstone filled only about half of them. Management attributed much of the overage to requests carried over from the second quarter. That's partly reassuring, because it isn't fresh panic. It's also partly worse, because the queue has persisted across quarters. BCRED is a credit vehicle, so the mechanics belong to the private-credit desk. But the equity-side lesson travels directly to semi-liquid private-equity products. When redemptions are gated, the holder bears the mismatch, not the manager.

So where does this leave the cycle? Capital and deal ambition are concentrating hard at the top of the market. You can see it in a possible forty-billion-dollar take-private and the year's largest buyout close in the same week. But the cash that reaches limited partners still depends heavily on exits the general partner controls. Those run through continuation vehicles priced inside a closed circle, while the public listing route keeps stalling even for the hottest assets. The exit-and-distribution logjam remains the binding risk, not fundraising or dry powder.

Here's what would confirm or refute that read. Apollo reports on November third, Carlyle on November fifth, and KKR on November ninth. Watch their realizations, not their fee growth. Wendy's also reports on November fifth, which is the next checkpoint on Trian's insider bid. Watch whether the Global Payments talks become a signed deal, and at what price relative to William Blair's hundred-and-one-dollar marker. And watch whether Blackstone's promised fourth-quarter realizations arrive, and whether they come from real sales or from vehicles it controls. That's where the next real information lands.

That's the state of the machine. Talk tomorrow.
