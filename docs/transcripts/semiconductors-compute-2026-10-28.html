Good morning. It's Wednesday, October 28th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

For a week this desk has followed a bottleneck climbing the AI supply chain: first the back-end suppliers, then the equipment makers, then the memory specification sheet. Today the pressure point moves again, from making the chips to holding the financial risk on them.

The lead is a roughly $63 billion financing that Broadcom arranged through four banks for Anthropic's chip purchases. A quick disclosure: Anthropic makes the model behind this show. According to Bloomberg's reporting, Bank of America, Blackstone, Citigroup and Morgan Stanley agreed to fund Anthropic's purchases of Broadcom-built Google TPUs, with deliveries expected to start in 2027. The detail that matters is what Broadcom itself is doing in that deal. It isn't just brokering the financing. The money runs through a newly formed special-purpose vehicle. Broadcom is providing residual value support to that vehicle, sized to the first-lien portion of the financing, protecting lenders if Anthropic defaults. The dollar size of that first-lien slice has not been disclosed in anything we could verify.

Here's why that's a step change. Last Friday, this desk flagged a single-source report that Broadcom was exploring financing for OpenAI's custom chip. Now it has actually done one, for Anthropic, and done it as guarantor, not just arranger. Contrast that with NVIDIA's move last week, putting capacity-reservation money into six of its own back-end suppliers. NVIDIA paid upstream to lock in supply. Broadcom is now underwriting downstream to lock in demand. Both put scarcity risk on a chip vendor's own balance sheet.

What's actually committed here is narrower than the headline number. Reuters, citing Anthropic's IPO prospectus, reported that Broadcom could lend up to $42 billion against roughly a third of a $125.2 billion five-year TPU lease commitment. Broadcom has separately disclosed that Anthropic can access about 3.5 gigawatts of TPU capacity starting in 2027. Whether the new $63 billion is additional capital or an expansion of that earlier $42 billion facility isn't established in the reporting, so don't add the two figures together. One more sourcing caveat: every figure on this deal comes from press reporting citing Bloomberg and Reuters, and no filing laying out the new financing has been seen. What the lease and the gigawatt figure do show is customer-relationship strength and real financing scale for the Google-Broadcom TPU ecosystem. They don't show architecture or software-ecosystem leadership over NVIDIA's GPUs, and none of it ships before 2027.

There are two ways to read Broadcom's backstop. The generous reading is that Broadcom is confident in TPU resale value, confident enough to put its own balance sheet behind it. The more cautious reading is that the banks wanted that guarantee because they weren't willing to take Anthropic's or the TPU's residual-value risk on their own. The banks also reportedly secured higher interest rates. That detail is where the quoted evidence leans: toward lender caution, not just vendor confidence.

Now add the memory backdrop. Micron's CEO says a large part of 2027 HBM volume is already sold out, and HBM prices, as this desk noted yesterday, are expected to more than double next year. A tight, inflating memory market makes accelerators look valuable today. That makes a residual-value guarantee look cheap right now. But the TPU lease underneath this deal runs five years, well past whatever this memory cycle does next. So, on our read, the chip vendor is holding part of its customer's credit risk for longer than the current pricing environment is likely to last.

The other giant GPU financing story this week shows the opposite problem: an announcement running ahead of the money. On Saturday, this desk described SpaceX's roughly $40 billion GPU financing as early-stage talks, with Bloomberg reporting they could end without a deal. Elon Musk has now said the deal is closed. SpaceX's own investor-relations release tells a different story. It describes a binding commitment to enter into an approximately $40 billion master finance lease. That lease remains subject to specified closing conditions, and it's expected to close only before year-end. That's SpaceX's own primary language, not outside reporters, directly undercutting Musk's "closed."

The plan aims to keep the borrowing off SpaceX's balance sheet, and Semafor reported proposed rates of about seven and three-quarters percent on a safer tranche and about eight point three percent on the lowest-rated one. The chips are expected in the first half of 2027. SpaceX's release doesn't even state a price, deferring lease terms to a forthcoming regulatory filing. So the deal is claimed closed, not closed. That gap between announced capital and committed capital is exactly where any real economics constraint on this buildout would show up first. It wouldn't appear as cancelled chip orders. It would appear as financing terms nobody has agreed on yet.

Put together, and this is our inference, both deals are pushing AI-compute credit risk off buyer balance sheets and into banks, private credit, and now chip vendors themselves. If utilization or lab revenue disappoints down the line, that stress reaches lenders and Broadcom, not just Anthropic or SpaceX. Investors pricing Broadcom's AI revenue should start asking how much of it now has a contingent guarantee attached.

If buyers are leaning on vendors and lenders to carry risk, Intel, the one Western challenger in leading-edge manufacturing, is leaning on its own shareholders. Intel says Tesla will use its upcoming 14A process for future chips. Yesterday this desk carried that as a reported line; it's now framed as Intel's own statement. That matters because Intel's annual filing says plainly that if it can't land a significant external customer for 14A, it may pause or discontinue leading-edge process development altogether. A named Tesla commitment buys time against that threat. But Tesla's volume and revenue on 14A haven't been disclosed, and Tesla isn't a buyer at hyperscaler scale.

Keep the rest of Intel's foundry book in view. Before Tesla, only Amazon, Microsoft and the US Department of Defense had confirmed plans to use Intel's 18A node. Intel's CFO describes only inbound interest in the 18A-P variant, not signed deals. And an unconfirmed Tom's Hardware report says Intel might stop offering 18A to foundry customers and focus on 14A instead. That report sits in tension with the CFO's comment. If it holds, the Tesla win signals a narrower external strategy, not a broader one.

Intel also announced a $20 billion equity raise earmarked for new fab buildouts. It said 18A output exceeded internal targets, but that's a comparison to an internal benchmark nobody outside Intel has seen, not a published yield number. The equity raise itself is a tell. On our read, Intel still can't fund leading-edge capacity from its own operating cash flow, unlike TSMC, which funds its capex internally. That's shareholders underwriting the option on a second Western leading-edge source. If Thursday's earnings show no real volume behind the Tesla logo, this raise starts looking more like survival funding than growth capital.

Intel's foundry bet isn't its only front, though. On the software side, AMD announced last Tuesday, October 20th, that it is folding its ZenDNN optimizations into oneDNN, which the two companies are making the default path for x86 CPU acceleration in PyTorch. AMD is also joining the UXL Foundation as a steering member alongside Intel. The two are co-developing a next-generation x86 instruction set aimed at matrix multiplication. No timeline or benchmarks were given, and this is about CPU inference, not a challenge to CUDA on accelerators. But it's a rare explicit alliance between the two x86 rivals, and it shows the ecosystem fight running in parallel with the manufacturing one.

So, the watch list. Thursday brings Intel's third-quarter earnings, the first real test of whether volume sits behind the Tesla win. AMD reports November 3rd. Watch November 10th too, when China's pause on expanded rare-earth export controls is due to expire. As of mid-October no extension had been published, and that's a hard date for specialty materials feeding packaging and substrates. Today's Fed decision is the rate backdrop for every one of these financing structures, and its outcome isn't known as this goes out. Finally, watch for any filing that actually sizes Broadcom's backstop or confirms SpaceX's financing terms. Right now the dollar amounts behind both remain the biggest open question in this entire financing wave.
