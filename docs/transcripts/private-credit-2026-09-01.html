Good morning. It's September 1st, and this is your private credit briefing.

For most of the past week this show has argued the same thing. When private credit gets priced, the price that matters is usually set inside the family. The mark, the CLO equity tranche, the AI mezzanine coupon. Today three outside prices arrived at once. Every one of them prices collateral, not a borrower.

Start with the lead. On Sunday we noted that banks were winning the marquee AI credits, with CoreWeave's loan syndicated through the banking system. We said then that the subordination premium in AI financing was the one number the market wouldn't print. Blue Owl just printed one. Blue Owl Capital's managed funds, with PIMCO participating, led a two-point-four-billion-dollar debt deal for IREN, the cloud and AI compute provider. The proceeds fund Nvidia Blackwell Ultra GPUs for IREN's Mackenzie data-center campus in British Columbia. A separate outlet put the all-in number as high as three-point-four billion once PIMCO's piece is folded in. But the two-point-four-billion figure is the one Bloomberg and Blue Owl's own release stand behind, so that's the number to anchor on.

The structure matters more than the headline. It's a one-point-two-billion-dollar senior secured term loan plus one-point-two-billion of senior secured notes. Both carry a nine percent coupon. Both mature two and a half years after funding, drawn in stages as IREN buys the equipment. And both tranches are described only as senior secured. The disclosures don't specify, in any sentence we can quote, whether the actual security is the GPUs themselves, the Mackenzie real estate, or recourse to IREN's parent. So don't assume it's the chips. Here is what we can say. A two-and-a-half-year maturity on a facility that funds equipment in stages is a structure built around residual value. What it is not is a structure built around contracted cash flow at that site. IREN's one disclosed anchor offtake is a roughly nine-point-seven-billion-dollar, five-year Microsoft cloud contract. That contract sits at a different campus entirely. Childress, Texas, not Mackenzie. So this loan isn't backstopped by the one offtake IREN has actually shown the market. The entire credit decision lands instead on a single refinancing moment two and a half years out, against a residual value nobody has tested in a distressed sale.

Call nine percent the cleanest quoted price we have on what a lender thinks that kind of collateral is worth today. But hold it loosely. A separate, less-verified report describes an earlier IREN GPU facility, roughly three-point-six-billion dollars, that closed back in June at a materially lower blended cost. We could not confirm that figure against a primary source, so we won't state it as fact. But if it's real, then this week's nine percent isn't IREN's first quote on its own collateral. It's just the newest one, and the direction of travel would be pricing getting more expensive, not less. Either way, the coupon that did print becomes the reference point every other AI-infrastructure lender marks against. If the next comparable deal clears wider than nine, every existing mark on this structure is already stale. That's the mechanism to watch. One printed coupon doesn't just price one deal. It resets the assumption every lender in the space is working from.

If the IREN deal is about what collateral is worth, the next question is whether a lender can actually reach it. That's Aston Martin. Two bondholders, Arini Capital Management and Tresidor Investment Management, have filed for US discovery. They're targeting a five-hundred-fifty-million-pound financing that HPS Investment Partners led back in July, alongside Authentic Brands Group. As part of that deal, Aston Martin agreed to transfer just over half of its non-automotive intellectual property to Authentic Brands. Fifty-point-one percent. The bondholders say that moved valuable collateral out of their reach and weighed on their bond prices. Be precise about what this filing is. It's a procedural step ahead of an English court proceeding. It is not a finding against anyone, and nothing here establishes that HPS did anything the loan documents didn't permit.

That's actually the more useful takeaway than any allegation of bad faith. European direct-lending documentation now supports the same intellectual-property carve-out technique US sponsors have used for years, and existing creditors are finding out about it after the money has already moved. Private credit here isn't the victim of a liability-management exercise. It's the counterparty benefiting from one. So the security package you were promised is only worth what the documents let you keep. And increasingly, what the documents let you keep is negotiated away from you before you ever get a vote. If this carve-out survives contest, existing European bonds get repriced without any change at all in the borrower's underlying credit quality.

And when the documents run out, you find out what the collateral actually liquidates for. That's the update on First Brands. This show flagged the Chapter 7 conversion ruling back on the twenty-fifth and said explicitly, hold the recovery number open, because nobody yet knows what a broken senior claim actually pays. We now have a first, partial answer. First Brands exhausted substantially all of a roughly one-billion-dollar debtor-in-possession facility. The combined recovery against it came to a hundred and ninety-four million dollars, against one-point-one billion borrowed. Treat that as reported and incomplete, not as a final recovery rate. The estate isn't wound up.

But sit with what kind of money that is. A debtor-in-possession facility is the most protected claim in any capital structure, senior to everything else in the stack, and it primed the creditors who came before it. When super-priority money recovers a fraction of what went out, the failure isn't in the priority waterfall. It's in what the collateral was actually worth. That's the transferable lesson, and it points at asset-based finance and receivables-and-factoring strategies specifically, where the whole credit decision rests on collateral marks that no one has stress-tested through an actual liquidation. Rating agencies and CLO managers build their recovery assumptions off exactly the kind of model this case just tested against reality. Reality came in far lower, and those assumptions now have one real observation to argue with.

None of what we just covered, the nine percent, the intellectual-property carve-out, the recovery on the debtor-in-possession loan, shows up in any default rate or non-accrual ratio this industry reports. That's worth one line of backdrop. Fitch's private-credit default measure and the step-up in business development company non-accruals were both on the record earlier this week, so we won't re-run them. The point today is simply that those metrics count payment events, and every price we just walked through was a collateral event. They're measuring a different thing, and they were never going to agree. That gap is structural rather than a matter of managers being slow to mark things down. Collateral quality just isn't a line item in a non-accrual ratio until a loan has already gone bad.

So where does that leave the cycle. The binding risk has moved again, and it keeps moving one layer further from the reported statistics. A week ago it was a question of how fast managers recognize stress. Then it was vintage and deployment. Now it's whether the collateral underneath these loans is worth what it was underwritten at, and whether a lender can actually enforce its claim on it. Underwriting a borrower and underwriting an asset are different disciplines, and this industry's track record is overwhelmingly in the first one.

Watch three things from here. First, whether a second GPU-collateralized deal prices near nine percent or wider. That tells you whether today's coupon holds as the market's benchmark or gets repriced within weeks. Second, whether the Aston Martin discovery action actually advances into the English proceeding, which would start setting precedent for how carve-outs like this one get treated in European restructurings. And third, the third-quarter non-accrual and payment-in-kind prints from the business development companies, landing in November. Those will tell you whether this week's collateral stories were the leading edge of a broader repricing, or just the loudest ones so far.
