Good morning, it's October third, and this is your briefing on artificial intelligence across biopharma R&D.

For the last two days, this show has stayed in the clinic, with Roche's Phase 3 claims and Insilico's rentosertib. Today the story moves one layer upstream, to the design bench. AI antibody design just got a blinded wet-lab scorecard, and the verdict is parity, not superiority. Who owns the model is becoming the less interesting question. Who owns the validation is the one to watch.

Start with the clearest sign of that shift, at Anthropic. One disclosure first: Anthropic makes the model behind this show. The company has been assembling a life-sciences stack all year. In April it bought Coefficient Bio, a stealth drug-discovery biotech, in an all-stock deal valued at roughly four hundred million dollars. In June, AlphaFold co-creator John Jumper announced he was leaving Google DeepMind for Anthropic, after nearly nine years there. Those were the inputs. September brought the structural moves.

On September seventeenth, Anthropic launched its Life Sciences Verification Program. It gives vetted life-science professionals access to its Mythos, Opus, and Sonnet models, under what the company calls safeguards more permissive for biology-related work. The program has a Standard Use tier and an add-on grant called High-risk Use. According to coverage of the launch, that add-on removes all safeguards that block life-science requests. Other protections, such as Anthropic's cyber classifiers, stay in place.

Days later, Anthropic's head of life sciences, Eric Kauderer-Abrams, confirmed to TechCrunch that the company runs a physical wet lab in the San Francisco Bay Area. His rationale was blunt. Quote, to do biology, the final test is still, and will be for a while, in real lab work. End quote. But Anthropic has not disclosed the lab's size, staff count, opening date, or biosafety level.

Here's the twist worth sitting with. According to the same coverage, Kauderer-Abrams described the lab's focus as, quote, fundamental biology rather than drug discovery. That sits awkwardly next to a drug-discovery acquisition. And no Anthropic statement we found says whether the company will ever own a drug candidate, rather than serve partners and generate training data. So this is not confirmed vertical integration into drug development. It is a model company buying its way into validation, for reasons it has chosen not to spell out. One plausible reading is that a company whose business is selling models is buying credibility and proprietary data as much as it is chasing drugs.

There is also a regulatory angle. Loosening biology safeguards is commercially rational. But OpenAI is courting the same customers, and a race to widen frontier-model access to biology is the kind of dynamic that invites scrutiny, whatever the lab actually does.

Now contrast OpenAI's route. In April it launched GPT-Rosalind, a reasoning model built for life sciences, offered through trusted access and tool integrations. OpenAI says it beats its own earlier GPT-5 models on chemistry, biochemistry, and experiment design. Those are internal benchmarks, with no outside check. So one frontier lab is building a bench, and the other is shipping a model into other people's benches.

There's a third posture, and it answers the question of who protects what. Isomorphic Labs, the DeepMind spinout, told a Semafor forum last month that, quote, all of our AI models are locked down in-house. It still declined to give a timeline for its first clinical trial. So the frontier labs are opening access to sell their models, while a design specialist like Isomorphic treats its weights as the crown jewels.

Why would a model vendor want a bench at all? The best answer this fall came from a blinded test. On Wednesday, this show said the design layer was producing throughput, not yet success rates. Here is the first blinded scorecard we've been able to report. It's called AIntibody, and its results were published on August twenty-sixth in Nature Biotechnology. The competition made and measured five hundred eleven antibody designs from twenty-nine organizations.

The headline result came in affinity maturation. Aureka Biotechnologies took first place with a design measuring 94.7 picomolar. That's roughly a two-thousand-fold improvement over its starting antibody. The best conventional result took about three months of phage-display work and reached 113 picomolar. According to coverage of the paper, the two results count as a statistical tie, with the AI design numerically slightly ahead.

Aureka's own press release says AI design surpasses the best experimental result. That overstates it. The competition had three tasks, including the hardest one, designing binding loops from scratch. Across all three, computational design did not outperform experimental methods on a field-wide basis. And the coverage we have doesn't say who led that de novo task. One strong entrant out of twenty-nine is not an industry capability. Affinity maturation starts from a known binder, which makes it the most forgiving task. So on a narrow, well-defined problem, one strong model can match three months of bench iteration. It cannot yet make the bench optional. That is the case for a model company owning a lab. Validation, not the algorithm, is becoming the scarce asset.

If design is only at parity, pharma's other option is to rent it. Chai Discovery, which builds generative design models, signed paid deployments with Eli Lilly, Pfizer, Novartis, and the immunology specialist argenx, all within seven months. Days after the last two deals, Chai closed a four-hundred-million-dollar Series C at a three point eight billion dollar valuation.

No deal values were disclosed, so four logos are not necessarily four large commitments. And the valuation is a private-round price. Still, the business-model contrast is worth naming. Chai gets paid without taking clinical risk, but it is exposed if design models commoditize. Anthropic is taking on the cost of a bench itself. Roche, as this show covered on Thursday, wants to own its models outright. There's also a less flattering reading of Chai's run. Pharma may be multi-sourcing design tools precisely because no single model has proven decisive, and AIntibody's field-wide result supports that. For now, pharma rents the model and keeps the data and the molecule.

All of this sits upstream of the number that matters: whether any of it moves clinical success. Here the evidence splits by phase. According to a reported synthesis of industry analyses, AI-discovered molecules clear Phase 1 safety trials 80 to 90 percent of the time. The historical norm is about 50 percent. That is a real edge. But there's a selection caveat. AI platforms often pursue validated mechanisms and conservative chemistry, so a safety pass is partly what you'd expect.

In Phase 2, where a drug has to actually work, the same synthesis puts success back at the industry's ordinary rate of roughly 40 percent. Boston Consulting Group estimates that AI roughly doubles a drug's odds of reaching market, from about 5 to 10 percent to 9 to 18 percent. Treat that as a single-source, optimistic estimate. Most of it is the Phase 1 gain carried forward, not evidence of better efficacy.

Here's why that Phase 2 number is a hint, not a finding. One review counted 117 AI-enabled assets that had entered human trials by mid-2026. Only 8 of them had completed Phase 2. That differs from the tracker count of more than 173 programs we cited yesterday, which shows how loosely the industry defines AI-designed. Either way, the denominator is tiny. Yesterday this show called the clinical effect parity. The more precise call is this: Phase 2 looks like parity so far, but with eight completed programs, neither parity nor an edge can be proven yet. Better binders were never going to settle that. Efficacy failure is a translational and biological problem.

Pull the threads together. If design is at parity, the moat for AI drug-discovery platforms moves from having a model to having proprietary data, assay throughput, and clinical execution. That is exactly what a bench buys. For tools vendors and contract research organizations, design compresses months of phage-display iteration without removing the bench. That favors high-throughput assay and validation capacity over plain wet-lab labor. For investors, the demonstrated lever so far is Phase 1 and cycle time. A success-rate lever is unproven in either direction. So price speed and cost per program, with no success-rate premium until the Phase 2 count says otherwise. And demand blinded, external benchmarks like AIntibody before crediting any AI-designed claim.

On spend, follow the visible money. It is going to design tools, like this year's Chai deployments. It is going to data, like Tempus AI's large multi-year real-world-data collaborations with Merck and Gilead. And it is going to benches. Yesterday's cost evidence pointed the same way: Recursion credits AI agents, automation, and capital prioritization for a roughly 40 percent cut in its cost base since absorbing Exscientia. So our read holds: flat total budgets, reallocated toward data, validation, and cheaper programs. The Phase 2 wall remains a biology problem that no model has yet solved.

The open questions for next week. Will Anthropic say whether it intends to own a molecule? Will anyone publish who led AIntibody's de novo loop-design task? And when does the completed Phase 2 count get large enough to call, in either direction?

That's your briefing. Talk tomorrow.
