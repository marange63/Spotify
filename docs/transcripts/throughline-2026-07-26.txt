Good morning, this is The Throughline for Sunday, July 26th, 2026. The single biggest development across today's briefings is that money has stopped being the scarce input for the AI buildout — turbines, memory, grid capacity, and financing quality now are, and today's briefings show that constraint showing up in five different places at once.

Start with the macro backdrop. Strategic Power leads with the clearest evidence: GE Vernova's gas-turbine capacity under contract jumped from 100 to 116 gigawatts in a single quarter, and the company is mostly sold out through 2030. A hyperscaler announcing a new data center today, without an existing turbine allocation, likely can't get gas-fired power before 2031 — no amount of capital fixes that. The same briefing tracks FERC ordering all six US grid operators to justify their large-load interconnection rules, and a new Taiwanese export rule that could cap TSMC's offshore manufacturing regardless of Washington's subsidy dollars. Physical and legal constraints, not financial ones.

That tension between abundant capital and a wary discount rate carries straight into Capital Markets Radar. SK Hynix's Nasdaq listing raised $26.5 billion and was oversubscribed seven times over, even as Nvidia posted record revenue and went nowhere — a sign capital is rotating into fresh AI paper while getting warier of the incumbent's backward-looking numbers. Underneath that, private credit's software borrowers are now the largest stressed cohort in BDC portfolios, a plausible sign that AI spending is squeezing the very borrowers most exposed to it. And Kevin Warsh's Fed has scrapped forward guidance entirely, keeping higher-for-longer alive as a base case into Tuesday's decision — the discount rate that makes both of those other stories riskier.

That question of who can actually afford this buildout is exactly what Private Credit and Private Equity address from the funding side. Private Credit finds insurers telling Marsh's own survey they know the illiquidity premium is shrinking and underwriting standards are loosening, and increasing their private-credit allocations anyway — a classic late-cycle signature, with CLO issuance down 23% year over year confirming the same spread compression. Private Equity shows the same liquidity strain by other means: easyJet's $7.3 billion take-private is genuine, rare price discovery, but underneath it, GP-led secondaries now account for 16% of all sponsor exits, manufacturing distributions rather than delivering the cash realizations LPs actually need.

Turning to the AI and compute stack, where the physical-constraint story sharpens further. Semiconductors and Compute reports that memory scarcity has spread beyond high-bandwidth chips into commodity DRAM and NAND, with DRAM contract prices up 13 to 18% quarter over quarter — a cost now landing directly in consumer electronics margins, not just hyperscaler capex budgets. Equipment makers are the clean beneficiary: Applied Materials now expects more than 30% growth in 2026, and ASML booked a record €6.86 billion order tied to SK Hynix's listing. The caveat that connects back to Strategic Power: SK Hynix's own chief executive calling 2027 the industry's "worst supply year" is a forecast from someone raising capital, not an audited fact.

AI Products and Applications shifts the lens from hardware scarcity to who controls the software layer built on top of it. Jack Dorsey's Block launched Buzz, giving AI agents cryptographic, auditable identity rather than bolting AI onto human-first tools — an early bet, but one aimed at a real coming fight over agent standards. And Fireworks AI's billion-dollar revenue run-rate, at a $17.5 billion valuation, shows enterprise AI spend concentrating in customized inference infrastructure rather than raw frontier-model calls — the model is becoming a commodity, the deployment layer is where the money actually lives.

Enterprise Technology makes the financing-quality point explicit with three companies in one week. Oracle's stock fell 34% and its credit got cut to triple-B-minus because its AI capex is debt-financed against slow-converting backlog, while Alphabet gets more slack for the same behavior because its capex is funded by cash the business already generates. SAP went a step further, naming token costs directly as a drag on 2026 profit guidance — the first major incumbent to say inference spend is now visible enough to move the number. Three companies, the same AI spending, three different verdicts, and the discriminator is whether the cash exists today or is still years out.

Finally, the applied frontier. Robotics and Physical AI shows both halves of the same gating problem: NHTSA's escalated investigation into Tesla's vision-only Full Self-Driving system is one procedural step from forcing a recall, and Amazon quietly shut down its Blue Jay warehouse robot six months after unveiling it, because staged capability keeps failing to survive contact with real-world cost and reliability. Meanwhile Figure AI's newest humanoid took on a genuinely harder logistics task at BMW's Spartanburg plant, and European industrial money, Bosch and Schaeffler among them, is now writing direct strategic checks into humanoid startups — capital committed years ahead of the reliability data that would justify it.

Here's the thread running through all of it. Every one of today's briefings describes plenty of capital chasing the AI buildout, but the thing actually rationing that buildout has moved outside the balance sheet — into turbine slots, memory fabs, grid interconnection queues, and which companies can pay for their capex out of cash they've already earned rather than debt they've taken on. That's a genuine shift from the claim-versus-evidence gap this show has tracked most of the week; today the gap is between what capital wants to build and what physical and financial capacity will actually let it build.

That's The Throughline. Have a good Sunday.
