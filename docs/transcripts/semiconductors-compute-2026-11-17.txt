Good morning, it's Tuesday, November 17th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

The headline is a new Commerce Department rule on AI compute, reported Monday, and it's a bigger deal than it sounds. The control point in US export policy just moved. The old regime asked where a chip shipped. The new rule asks who ultimately uses the compute, no matter which country's data center physically holds the GPU.

Some context first. All week, pressure has built on Commerce over AI chips reaching China through Southeast Asian resellers. On Sunday we said to watch whether a "growing consensus" in Congress turned into text. It did, but through Commerce rather than Congress. And an executive interim final rule moves a lot faster than a bill.

Here's what the rule does, per TechCrunch's reporting. It requires licenses to export, reexport, or transfer AI chips and servers to data centers anywhere in the world. That targets the route where compute gets rented through offshore clouds. It adds licensing requirements for Malaysia, Singapore, Indonesia, Thailand, and Brazil, which Commerce says present a higher-than-normal risk of diversion. Singapore is the detail to hold onto. It's home to Megaspeed, the reseller at the center of the diversion probe we've been covering. Existing data-center operators get ninety days to obtain licenses. And the rule rescinds the Biden administration's January 2025 AI Diffusion Rule, which had set country-level limits on advanced chip exports. The Federal Register text and effective date aren't confirmed yet, so treat the fine print as provisional.

So is this a blanket tightening? Not quite. Rescinding the old country tiers could loosen access for destinations that were previously capped. Meanwhile, the five newly named countries tighten. That's a targeted redrawing of the map, not a uniform clampdown. The reporting we have doesn't show where the net effect lands. There's also an open enforcement question. The rule asks Commerce to license compute access at data centers worldwide. Nothing on record explains how it will verify who actually sits behind a cloud tenancy. That leads to a new category. This desk usually splits demand into two kinds: constrained by compute availability, and constrained by economics. Now add a third, demand constrained by permission. A data center can have the GPUs and the power and still be blocked from serving a given customer.

Two of our recent calls can now be checked against the rule. We argued that the channel actually delivering chips to China was the grey reseller layer, not the stalled licensed one. We also argued that a Commerce Department accused of under-enforcing had an incentive to over-enforce, raising costs for every merchant vendor selling through Southeast Asia. The rule now names that corridor country by country. One call missed. We expected chip-making tools to be the next layer for controls. The rule went to the compute and end-user layer instead, and nothing reported so far extends it to equipment. So on what's public, the toolmakers' China exposure stays where it was.

The next question is how much revenue sits in that corridor, and the answer is itself a finding. We looked for a current figure on how much of Nvidia's revenue is billed through Singapore. We couldn't find one. Per our research, Nvidia switched its geographic reporting from billing location to customer headquarters, starting with the quarter ended October 2025. The last Singapore billing figure in its filings covers the quarter ended July 2025. Back then, Singapore billings were roughly 22 percent of Nvidia's revenue. That number is now more than a year old, and it isn't being updated. So the corridor the rule targets is one Nvidia stopped measuring publicly before the rule existed. That means nobody outside the company can easily track what compliance in that corridor costs.

So who gains and who loses? Start with the clearest losers, the neocloud and GPU-as-a-service operators in the five named countries. Licenses now attach at the data center, not at the chipmaker. So those operators carry a new gating step before they can serve a given tenant. The ninety-day clock lands that step right inside the 2027 deployment calendar. Our read is that slow licensing review would delay regional buildouts, not just paperwork.

Next come the merchant vendors. Nvidia, AMD, and Broadcom all face the same customer-vetting burden, because the license now follows their silicon into the data center. The rule doesn't single out any vendor. Relative damage depends on how much of each company's volume runs through the named corridor. Nvidia is the only one with a disclosed historical figure, that old Singapore share. None of the three publishes a current one, so the market will be guessing at the ranking.

On the other side of the ledger, the clearest gainers sit in China. If the remote-access route closes, Chinese buyers have less legal path to US silicon, even through intermediaries. That raises the payoff to domestic accelerators such as Huawei's Ascend and Cambricon. We have no Chinese volume figures to size it, so take it as a direction, not a magnitude. A quieter possible gainer is any destination the old Diffusion tiers had capped, if rescission really loosens its access. Put simply, the grey channel just became a licensing problem. The first bill lands on the operators in the middle, not on the chip designers.

Permission governs where compute can run. The other half of the map is where it gets made, and here we owe you a correction. Yesterday we said Intel Foundry still has no confirmed external customer at the leading edge. That was too strong. Intel has said that Tesla agreed to use its upcoming 14A process as a foundry customer. Intel paired it with a twenty-billion-dollar equity raise for new factory buildouts. That disclosure dates to October 20th, so this is a correction, not new news.

Score it as a customer relationship, not production leadership. Per a secondary blog report, 14A is still at the 0.9 design kit, with the 1.0 kit targeted for the first quarter of 2027. No wafer volume, dollar value, or production date has been disclosed for Tesla. The equity raise also dilutes shareholders, so Intel is financing capacity ahead of proven external volume. As Washington tightens control over where compute runs, leading-edge capacity outside Taiwan gains strategic value. Intel's turnaround case now has a named leading-edge customer, but not yet a wafer count behind the name. And yesterday's narrower point still holds: an improving Intel roadmap doesn't change who can buy accelerator capacity in 2027.

Neither a license nor a new fab helps if the memory isn't there. Yesterday we said, as inference, that phones and PCs would face tighter conventional memory supply as HBM absorbs capacity. Qualcomm's chief executive has now put company evidence behind that. Explaining a decline in Qualcomm's handset chip sales, he said, per Seoul Economic Daily, "there is no problem with demand; the problem is supply." That reads as the AI memory boom spilling out of HBM into the ordinary DRAM that phones use.

Hold the caveat, though. The remark dates to early November, and it's a CEO's framing, not a measured supply count. A chip vendor reporting a sales drop has every reason to blame supply rather than its own demand. For context, not as news, Micron said on its earnings call that more than 75 percent of its fiscal 2027 output is already committed. That's standing guidance from an interested seller. Still, a memory seller and a chip vendor squeezed by memory are now describing the same shortage from opposite sides. And this gate doesn't care who holds a license, or which country's fab is running.

So here's the frame for tonight. Nvidia reports its fiscal third-quarter results after the close today. Those numbers don't exist yet, so there's no preview and no forecast here. The test that matters is new. Does management address the remote-access rule directly? And what does it say about its customers in Singapore and Malaysia specifically? Listen for which of the three gates they talk about: permission, memory, or manufacturing.

That's the briefing for November 17th. Thanks for listening, and I'll catch you next time.
