Good morning, it's Friday, September 11th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

The freshest hard number in the industry landed yesterday, and the market is already using it to answer a question it cannot actually answer. Let's get into it.

TSMC reported August revenue of 514.81 billion New Taiwan dollars, a record month. That is up 10.1 percent from July, on the company's own release. Against August of last year it is up 53.3 percent. Reported monthly revenue has now risen four months in a row. However you cut it, wafer demand at TSMC is not decelerating.

Now here's where we have to slow down. Five days ago, on September 6th, this show flagged a trade report that TSMC had delayed placing some of its 2027 advanced-packaging equipment orders. We treated that as a possible early sign that the CoWoS capacity crunch might finally be easing. That crunch is what has rationed GPU and custom-accelerator supply for two years. We said at the time that we needed supply-side confirmation before believing it. Yesterday's revenue print is not that confirmation.

The reason is mundane and decisive. TSMC's monthly release is a single consolidated revenue line. It carries no node breakdown, no segment breakdown, and nothing on advanced packaging specifically. It tells you about blended wafer demand, pricing, and mix. It tells you nothing about whether packaging capacity is loosening.

And the acceleration itself cuts both ways. A fourth consecutive monthly increase, at double digits month over month, is just as consistent with TSMC's own capacity finally catching up to an existing order backlog as it is with end demand still accelerating. Both scenarios bill more wafers. That is the distinction that matters here: demand constrained by compute availability looks very different from demand constrained by economics, and this print cannot separate them. Yesterday we noted that Nvidia's supplier-commitment schedule pointed the other way entirely, with the largest buyer in the industry behaving as though memory, not packaging, is still the binding constraint. So ten days after that trade report first appeared, and five days after we flagged it here, there is still no on-record word from TSMC or from any named equipment vendor either confirming it or denying it. The industry has no dated primary evidence on the packaging question in either direction. That is the honest state of play, and it is worth naming plainly rather than picking the reading that fits the narrative.

There is one thing the revenue line does establish, and it is worth stating carefully. Cumulative revenue for January through August is up 39.3 percent year over year. For a public-market investor, that is the cleanest independent validation of hyperscaler capex guidance available anywhere, because it is billed wafers rather than a stated intention. It is also the single easiest place to make the cyclical-into-structural mistake. Four good months are a sequence, not a run-rate, and nobody should annualize them.

If the capacity question is genuinely unresolved, the more useful place to look is what TSMC's biggest customers are doing about depending on a single fab. That is where the second development of the week matters more than its evidence quality suggests.

OpenAI is now publicly discussing building its next generation of custom silicon at Samsung Foundry, alongside TSMC. Say upfront what this is and what it isn't. It is real, and it is thin. OpenAI Korea's general manager, Harrison Kim, said on the record at a Seoul press conference that the most progress with Samsung has come in joint production and research on, quote, the next-generation chips we are developing. He named no chip generation. He named no process node. He named no volume. Neither OpenAI nor Samsung has issued a written release confirming any of it, and the quote reaches us through wire reporting rather than a company transcript.

It is tempting to reach for the 200 billion dollar memorandum of understanding that Samsung and Broadcom signed in July and call that the vehicle. Broadcom is, after all, OpenAI's chip co-designer on its existing accelerator program. But that reading does not survive contact with the document. Samsung's own release names only Samsung and Broadcom as parties. It scopes the foundry piece to Samsung's two-nanometer and below process technologies for Broadcom's products, and extends it to HBM memory and to advanced packaging, running through 2030. OpenAI is not mentioned anywhere in it. So the 200 billion dollars is a Samsung-Broadcom commitment about Broadcom's own chips. No primary document attaches any dollar figure to OpenAI silicon at Samsung. Treat these as two separate and loosely connected relationships, not one vehicle.

So why bother with a story this thinly sourced? Because it breaks a pattern this show has been tracking all week. We have run a standing test on Intel Foundry: has any named external customer put real volume behind an advanced node yet? Session after session the answer has been no, including as recently as yesterday. This week the second source that actually drew a named frontier AI lab onto the record was not Intel. It was Samsung. The pattern did not resolve. It moved sideways, to the challenger nobody on this show has been scoring.

Which means applying the same skepticism to Samsung that we have applied to Intel all week. Evaluation is not qualification. Qualification is not volume. A country manager's quote about collaboration is a relationship statement, not a foundry allocation. And there is a capacity paradox worth sitting with. Digitimes reported in early August that Samsung Foundry is already running close to full utilization. Flag that for exactly what it is: trade reporting, with no utilization number attached and no confirmation from Samsung itself. If it is right, the optionality a customer is buying here is smaller than the headline implies, because there is limited room to physically absorb a multi-gigawatt accelerator program even if the relationship firms up.

Here is the second-order effect that matters more than any transferred wafer. A credible second source does not need to move a single wafer to change TSMC's leverage. The mechanism runs through price before it runs through volume. If a buyer the size of OpenAI has a real alternative on the table, the first place that shows up is not TSMC's shipment numbers. It is TSMC's gross-margin guidance on the next leading-edge node. So the figure to watch is not a Samsung production ramp. It is whether TSMC's pricing power at two nanometers and below softens at all.

Let's close with a forward test rather than a recap. Note what happens to the scarcity premium if packaging really does loosen while memory stays booked out. It does not disappear. It transfers, from the OSATs and packaging-tool vendors to the three memory makers. And the memory layer is where this same question gets a dated, disclosed answer in three weeks. Micron reports fiscal fourth-quarter results on September 30th. Set the baseline carefully, because these numbers are easy to garble. Take two consecutive quarters on the same basis. Micron's fiscal third quarter, the three months ended in late May, brought in revenue of 41.46 billion dollars. The quarter immediately before it brought in 23.86 billion. Both figures come from Micron's own results release. That is a quarterly revenue line close to doubling in a single step, which is the scale of the HBM-driven upcycle in one comparison. Management has guided the fourth quarter higher still, but guidance is not an actual, and that print has not happened yet. A line that nearly doubles quarter over quarter is not a rate that survives being annualized, and nobody should model it as one. Watch whether September 30th confirms the trajectory or marks where cyclical extrapolation meets structural reality.

So, two things to actually watch going into next week. First, any packaging-equipment vendor disclosing a real change to its 2027 order book. That would be the first primary-source word on whether CoWoS capacity is genuinely loosening. Second, any on-record statement from OpenAI, Samsung, or Broadcom attaching an actual node, volume, or date to the Samsung chip program, rather than a general manager's quote about collaboration. Until one of those lands, both of this week's big stories remain open questions dressed up as conclusions.

That's the briefing for September 11th. Have a good weekend.
