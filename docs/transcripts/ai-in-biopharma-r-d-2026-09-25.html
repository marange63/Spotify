Cautious Optimism Briefings

AI in Biopharma R&D

Transcript · September 25, 2026

Good morning. It's Friday, September 25th, 2026, and this is your briefing on AI in biopharma R&D.

Here's the tension worth holding today. This week an AI-discovery platform asked public investors to price it on a model and a partner. Meanwhile the newest AI clearance a US regulator has granted in pathology went to software that checks the quality of a scanned slide before anyone reads it. That gap between what's being sold and what's being certified is the story.

Start with Iambic Therapeutics. On September 21st, the Nvidia-backed discovery company did three things on the same day. It announced a multi-year small-molecule collaboration with AbbVie across immunology, neuroscience and oncology, structured as an upfront payment, milestones and tiered royalties. It launched its newest model, Enchant v3. And it filed an S-1 to list on Nasdaq under the ticker IAM. The S-1 is the new fact. Unlike a press release, it is filed under securities liability.

Enchant v3 is described in Iambic's own materials as a 41-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts model, trained on more than 6,000 molecular properties across 16 biomedical data modalities. Those are input specifications. No hit rate is attached, no cycle-time reduction, no prospective result. Iambic also positions Enchant as the leading model in the field, and no independent benchmark stands behind that. Treat it as marketing until someone outside the company measures it.

The S-1 was the first place the AbbVie economics could surface, because a registration statement is where material agreements get described. It doesn't surface them. The filing names AbbVie only in a list of partners that includes Takeda, Lundbeck, Revolution Medicines, Jazz and Bayer. In the filing's words, those relationships "reflect meaningful external interest in our platform." No upfront, no milestone total and no royalty rate is itemized. So AbbVie-Iambic stays one of three large-pharma AI deals in about a week with no cash disclosed. The silence is informative. Either the terms are not yet material to the company, or Iambic prefers the partner's name to the partner's number.

What the filing does disclose is the income statement, and the trend is steepening. Iambic lost 50.1 million dollars in the first half of 2026 alone. That's about two-thirds of the 77.3 million it lost in all of 2025, so this year is on pace to be worse.

Now the balance sheet. Cash and equivalents stood at 207.9 million dollars at the end of June. At the first-half pace, that covers a little over two years of losses, before any IPO proceeds. Net loss isn't the same as cash burn, so treat that as a rough bound. The filing itself won't say. Its runway sentence is left with a blank where the end date should go. The price range on the cover page is blank too.

And the clinic hasn't spoken. The lead asset, IAM1363, an oral HER2 inhibitor, is confirmed as still in an open-label, multi-center Phase 1/1b basket trial in advanced HER2-altered solid tumors. The filing discloses no efficacy or safety data and gives no readout timing.

So read the same-day sequence as choreography built from inputs, not outputs. A pharma name, a parameter count and a filing, timed to one date. Add a widening loss, and the company has its own reason to want capital now rather than later. HER2 is also well-validated biology. So even a clean eventual readout from IAM1363 would say more about chemistry execution than about AI finding new biology.

That shapes what the IPO can actually measure. Whatever price Iambic fetches will be a verdict on narrative and losses, not on disclosed contract economics. The second-order effect falls on the other private AI-discovery platforms. If Iambic prices well, expect them to treat it as the reference point and file on partnership narratives of their own. If it prices poorly, expect them to retreat toward contingent-payment pharma deals instead of the public market. The plain takeaway: public investors are being asked to pay for the platform before the clinic has said anything.

Now the other side of that trust question. If the discovery layer is asking investors to take the model on faith, the diagnostic layer shows what earning regulatory trust looks like. In August, Leica Biosystems' Aperio iQC DX received FDA 510(k) clearance. It is described as the first FDA-cleared software for AI-assisted digital pathology quality control in a clinical setting. It sits within a published count of 51 FDA-authorized pathology AI devices through April 2026.

Three caveats matter more than that headline count. First, the Leica software does quality control, not diagnosis. It does not read the slide. Second, the 51 spans scanners and image-management systems alongside actual algorithms, so it is not 51 diagnostic models. Third, the value to pharma, quality-controlled slides feeding AI biomarker work, is an inference, not a demonstrated drug-development saving. So the frontier of regulatory trust sits at the most checkable task in the workflow, not at the diagnostic call. Trust is being built outward from the verifiable step, not inward from a discovery claim. That's the opposite direction from where Iambic is asking the market to start.

There's a business point here for the tools and diagnostics vendors. Hamamatsu's clearance on September 2nd, for its NanoZoomer scanner line, was for hardware rather than an AI model. But it belongs to the same revenue logic as Leica's software. For both companies, clearance-gated pathology products are a revenue line that doesn't depend on any drug succeeding. That's a different risk profile from a discovery platform whose upside rides on milestones and royalties. That contrast is inference from the clearances, not a reported revenue figure. But it explains why the least glamorous layer may be the steadiest one.

The other regulated-data layer worth watching is real-world evidence. Access to that data today mostly runs through linkage intermediaries like Datavant, or through data licensors like Tempus. Truveta is different: the health systems govern the data themselves. It is a collective of 30 US health systems that together provide 18 percent of US patient care. In Truveta's own peer-reviewed description, its privacy-preserving token linkage is designed to comply with HIPAA and IRB requirements. Designed to comply is not the same as a settled legal answer for every secondary use. And 18 percent coverage means most US care still sits outside the collective.

That's real infrastructure, and provider-owned governance answers part of the HIPAA question at the source. What's still missing is any quantified drug-development saving attached to it. The published examples are epidemiological surveillance, not a cheaper external control arm or a faster-enrolling eligibility design. The rails exist. The productivity number does not.

Pulling the threads together, three questions stay open. Does Iambic's price, when it's set, reward a platform story with no clinical data behind it? Do FDA's AI clearances in pathology move from quality control toward actual diagnostic calls? And does anyone attach a measured trial saving to governed real-world data?

Now the bigger question: how far can AI realistically move clinical success rates? My read is that the gains are real but lopsided. AI is most credible where failure comes from the molecule itself: potency, selectivity, exposure, safety margin. That is mostly early-phase attrition, and better design can plausibly cut it. AI is least credible in Phase 2, where most programs die because the target biology doesn't translate into efficacy in patients. A better-built molecule against the wrong target still fails. The real test of whether AI picks better biology is Insilico's rentosertib, the first generative-AI-designed drug in a Phase 3 trial, in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. That readout isn't due before 2027. None of today's sources offers a clean comparison of AI-derived versus historical phase success rates, so treat any headline uplift figure with suspicion. My expectation is a modest improvement concentrated before Phase 2, not a step-change in overall odds of approval.

That also says where the near-term low-hanging fruit is. It's probably outside discovery: governed real-world data used to tighten eligibility criteria and size trials, where enrollment time rather than model capability is the binding constraint. That's a judgment, not yet a measured saving.

On R&D spend, expect a sorting rather than a cut. Money for AI is flowing to the layers that can show verifiable output, like cleared devices and governed data rails. The discovery layer is still being paid mostly in promises: contingent deal terms, and now public equity priced before proof. Until a molecule grades the method, sponsors will keep their AI commitments contingent, and total R&D budgets won't bend on AI's account.
