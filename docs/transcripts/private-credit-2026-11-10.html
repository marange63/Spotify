Private Credit & the Corporate Credit Cycle

Transcript · November 10, 2026

Good morning. It's Tuesday, November 10th. Last Friday we said a market, not a manager's model, was finally getting a vote on AI-capex debt. Now we know who's selling, and it's a bank.

Per Bloomberg, as relayed by CNBC, ING is running a bids-wanted-in-competition auction on twelve data-center loans worth about five hundred million dollars. Bids of ten million dollars or more are due November 19th. One twenty-five million dollar slice is linked to the Stargate project in New Mexico, the venture involving OpenAI, SoftBank, and Oracle. An SMBC strategist, quoted in that coverage, said the buyers can range from private credit investors through asset managers to insurers. So this is the bank-to-nonbank migration we've been tracking, but not through origination. It's a bank handing seasoned risk to the non-bank system. ING's motive isn't stated in the reporting, and five hundred million dollars is small next to the sector's financing needs. So don't read distress into it. Still, mark the direction. Ten days ago, banks were seeking credit insurance on AI debt. Now one is selling the loans outright. Risk management has moved from hedging to sale.

The caution behind that sale is now reported by two outlets. Nigeria's Businessday, citing unnamed sources, says credit committees at banks and private lenders are stalling new data-center deals, even for facilities backed by Alphabet and Anthropic. Reuters, on firmer sourcing, describes hesitation rather than a freeze. It reports some banks telling market participants they would hesitate to fund additional AI deals, including ones backed by guarantors like Anthropic and Google. And Reuters points to Nebius dropping a planned high-yield bond in October as a sign that some planned deals are being shelved. Freeze or hesitation, both reports point the same direction.

Behind both sits a hard number. BloombergNEF data, cited by Businessday and in Bloomberg's own reporting, puts fixed-rate, dollar-denominated data-center debt maturing in 2027 at thirteen point four billion dollars. Rolling it over at current rates would raise borrowing costs by two point eight five percentage points. That's roughly three hundred eighty million dollars a year of extra interest, before any further spread widening. Keep two distinctions straight. First, this is not a private-credit maturity wall. It's the broader data-center debt stack, and the split between bonds and loans isn't disclosed in the reporting. Second, keep financing friction separate from demand destruction. A committee pause on an Alphabet-backed project tells you about a lender's risk appetite and sector concentration. It tells you nothing about whether anyone still wants the data center. The real test lands November 19th, when ING's bids come in. A meaningful discount to par would be the first hard evidence that model marks on similar paper sit above where the market clears. A clean print near par would support the industry's underwriting story instead. Both outcomes are live. So bank this: the sector private credit is being asked to fund faces a refinancing test, and its lenders are pausing in front of it.

So if banks are selling and committees are hesitating, what are the non-bank buyers getting paid to take this on? Blue Owl's listed business development company gave a clean read on its third-quarter earnings call, per a published summary of that call. Its credit metrics, the low non-accruals and the falling PIK share, ran here on Friday. The new detail is pricing. New commitments topped eight hundred ninety million dollars this quarter. New floating-rate loans priced at a five point two percent weighted-average spread, up from four point eight percent last quarter. That's a major BDC stepping its new-issue spread wider in a single quarter. Net asset value ticked up, and income covered the dividend.

There are two honest readings of that forty-basis-point move, and nothing here settles it. One, lenders are regaining pricing power after a year of credit deterioration. That's a healthy sign. Two, the wider spread is buying riskier paper, not more pay for the same risk. That would be a mix shift dressed up as discipline. And this is one manager, not an industry series. Either way, it only touches new loans. The back book, written in tighter vintages, doesn't reprice because this quarter's loans did. Its spreads stay where they were set, and its marks stay with the model. Blue Owl's management also said the vast majority of its PIK was structured at inception, not added through stress-driven amendments. That's management's own characterization. Test it against the Financial Stability Board's May report on private credit. It cited a study finding that PIK toggles in US BDC loans are associated with a one to two percentage point higher likelihood of delinquency the following quarter. That's association, not causation. Weaker borrowers may simply select into the toggle. But it means PIK by design isn't a clean bill of health either. So the margin is being repriced, and the back book isn't.

The syndicated market shows what repricing looks like with a daily price attached. That's the contrast this whole briefing turns on. JPMorgan strategists said more than nine in ten leveraged-loan names traded below face value earlier last week. Later in the week, that share eased to about eighty-five percent. Below par isn't distressed. A loan at ninety-nine still counts, and there's no average price here, so don't read it as a default wave. Meanwhile, new US CLO issuance is running more than sixteen percent behind last year's pace. Carlyle, KKR, and Oaktree are reportedly weighing purchases of that discounted paper to warehouse into new deals. That's reported intent, not closed trades, and it cuts two ways. It could be opportunistic managers buying cheap to build an attractive arbitrage. Or it could be a backlog of loan selling the market hasn't fully cleared.

Here's the second-order effect worth sitting with, and it's my inference. CLOs are the biggest structural buyer of syndicated loans. If formation stays this slow, the refinancing exit narrows. That's the route stronger direct-lending borrowers have used to graduate into the syndicated market. A narrower exit keeps better credits inside private books for longer, which sounds reassuring. But it also closes an escape route for the weaker tier heading into the 2027 maturities. Those borrowers either stay private on extended or PIK terms, or face a market that prices risk in real time. Public credit is clearing at a price today. Private credit's back book is still waiting for its first one.

So where does the cycle actually stand? Repricing is showing up wherever there's a real market to show it. A bank is auctioning seasoned AI loans to non-bank buyers. A large BDC is charging forty basis points more on new paper. And most leveraged-loan names trade below par while CLO formation lags. It isn't yet showing up in the private marks behind those loans. On defaults, nothing new today. Fitch's US private credit default rate still stands at a record six point three percent on a trailing twelve-month basis, up from six percent the quarter before. That's Fitch's private-credit series specifically, not a read on high yield or syndicated loans. And most of those default events are interest deferrals, PIK introductions, or stressed extensions, not bankruptcies. So private-credit stress is being deferred, not resolved. Nothing today closes the gap between prices and marks.

The biggest unresolved risk, in my read, is concentration. AI-infrastructure exposure is migrating out of bank hands into BDC and insurance-channel vehicles. Their marks haven't yet met a real price, and they sit in the same sector public markets are repricing. Watch three things. First, the November 19th bids on ING's loan book, the first clearing price on this paper. Second, whether other BDCs show the same new-issue widening as Blue Owl, which would turn one manager's data point into a market signal. Third, CLO formation into year-end, which decides whether the syndicated exit stays open for the borrowers who need it most. And one carry-forward. KKR was due to report on Monday. Its Global Atlantic numbers are the next test of the insurance-funded lending model, and we'll take them up once the detail is out.

That's the lending machine for November 10th. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
