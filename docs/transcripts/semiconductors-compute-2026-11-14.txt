Good morning, this is your semiconductors and compute briefing for Saturday, November 14th, 2026.

For two straight sessions early this week, this desk tracked one pattern. Nvidia fell while AMD and Broadcom rose. On Friday that pattern broke. Per CNBC, Nvidia closed down 2.01 percent at 162 dollars and 85 cents, a fresh 52-week low. The backdrop is a leadership crisis. On Thursday, Nvidia announced that Jensen Huang had resigned as chief executive, and CFO Colette Kress is now interim CEO. Press reports tie his exit to an alleged affair with a woman suspected of being a Chinese intelligence contact. And Washington is now probing the company. AMD did not hold its gains either. It sold off in what coverage called a sector-wide AI-chip sell-off, and it fell harder than Nvidia. So today's question is what this week's rotation was actually made of. And does Friday tell us anything new about AI demand itself?

Start with a correction. Earlier this week, this desk said the reporting named no company-specific catalyst for AMD's Monday jump of roughly 13 percent, and yesterday's episode said no design win had been reported. Both missed it. On November 9th, AMD announced that its fifth-generation EPYC processors are the host CPUs for Google Cloud's new TPU 8tx system. The two companies call it the first cloud TPU system to deliver hardware-enforced confidential computing. That's their own claim, not an independent test. Contemporaneous coverage made the link to the stock explicit. A GuruFocus headline tied AMD's surge to the Google Cloud partnership. Seeking Alpha credited the Google Cloud deal alongside a broader tech rally. So part of what we read as rotation out of Nvidia belongs in the fundamental column. One half of yesterday's line still holds, though. No allocation shift away from Nvidia's accelerators has been reported. That distinction is the key to reading the Google win correctly.

So get the competitive dimension right. This is a host-CPU win, not an accelerator socket taken from Nvidia. Google's TPUs still do the AI math. AMD's EPYC chips run the host layer around them. AMD frames the win broadly. It says EPYC is the host CPU of choice for more than ten leading hyperscalers and frontier AI labs. And it says those systems span AMD's own Instinct GPUs, Nvidia's GPUs, and custom silicon. Here's the inference worth drawing, and it's ours. AMD's server-CPU franchise gets paid whichever accelerator wins the slot next to it. That makes part of AMD a hedge on the accelerator war, not a pure bet against Nvidia. Investors reading AMD's week as Nvidia's loss and AMD's gain are mixing up two different competitive layers.

There's a second lever in the announcement, and it isn't raw performance. Our inference is that hardware-enforced confidential computing could pull regulated-industry and government AI workloads out of on-premises data centers and into Google Cloud. If it does, Google widens the market its own silicon can address. That gives custom silicon a way to compete with merchant GPUs on something other than benchmarks. And AMD's EPYC sits inside that pitch, on the host side.

Three things we don't know, and shouldn't pretend to. Neither AMD nor Google has sized the revenue. And we found no primary source saying whether TPU 8tx is generally available, in preview, or still future-dated. We also don't know which host CPU earlier Google TPU systems used. So there's no basis to say Intel, or Google's own silicon, lost a socket here. Treat this as an announced design win, not a confirmed deployment. The so-what is simple. Part of AMD's Monday move was real, and it sits on a dimension Nvidia doesn't contest.

If part of Monday was fundamental, Friday is the test of the rest. AMD gave the gains back fast. Outlets disagree on the exact size of AMD's drop, but on every reading it exceeded Nvidia's 2.01 percent. There are two honest readings of Friday, and the tape alone can't separate them. The first is a positioning unwind. Money that rotated into Nvidia's rivals on an event took its profits after a sharp run-up. The second is a genuine reassessment of AI-chip demand, hitting every name at once. Notice what the governance scandal can't do here. It can explain Nvidia's own slide. It can't explain why AMD fell harder.

Either way, Friday carries a second-order lesson, and it's our inference. If host-CPU wins like Google's become a recurring pattern, AMD's AI revenue diversifies beyond GPU share gains. Over time, that should loosen the link between AMD's stock and Nvidia's. Friday's joint sell-off says the market isn't pricing that yet. It's still trading AMD as AI-chip beta. The takeaway: the rotation trade unwound far faster than any design win can show up in revenue.

The tape can't tell those readings apart, but the invoices can, up to a point. We gave you TSMC's October record yesterday. The comparison that matters against Friday's tape is pace. Per TSMC's monthly filing, October revenue grew 59.7 percent year on year. January through October grew 43.3 percent. So October ran well above the year-to-date average. That says foundry demand was accelerating into the fourth quarter, not plateauing. It's a lagging signal, because it bills wafers ordered quarters ago. It can't rule out orders turning before revenue does. And it runs only through October, before this month's sell-offs. But as far as it goes, nothing in physical demand through October explains Friday.

One step down the chain, our read is that this also feeds tool demand. Record foundry billing supports continued spending at TSMC's equipment suppliers, from ASML to Tokyo Electron. But equipment orders lag foundry revenue by quarters. So October's acceleration speaks to the tool cycle quarters out, not to this week's tape.

That's where the extrapolation risk sits, and it runs both ways. This week's equity moves were driven by events: a governance scandal at Nvidia, then a sector unwind. So neither the rotation into AMD and Broadcom nor Nvidia's 52-week low should be read as a structural re-rating of anyone's competitive position. Nvidia's low reflects a CEO exit and a Washington probe, not a lost accelerator socket. The structural evidence this week was narrower. It was one host-CPU design win at Google, and a foundry still accelerating through October. Price the competitive story off those two facts, not off the tape.

What could move the demand story, or the policy story, next is on the calendar. Nvidia reports third-quarter results Tuesday, November 17th, as previously scheduled. Interim CEO Colette Kress keeps her CFO title too. It will be Nvidia's first print since Jensen Huang's exit. It is also the first hard test of the second reading of Friday, the demand reassessment. If the numbers and the guide hold up, the positioning story gains ground. If they don't, Friday was the first sign of something bigger than one company's scandal.

Washington's side of the Nvidia story keeps widening. This desk has tracked scrutiny moving from Jensen Huang's personal conduct to Nvidia's corporate conduct. Now it is reaching the administration's own licensing decisions. Congress and the FBI are reportedly scrutinizing the export-license approvals that let Nvidia sell its H200 chips into China. Reporting indicates that Senate Banking Committee chair Tim Scott is seeking testimony from both Huang and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick. The timing of any testimony isn't set, and coverage conflicts on it. Hold the backstory loosely too. Nvidia has not stated why Huang resigned. The affair allegation, carried by outlets including the Washington Post and CNN, remains press reporting, with no government finding.

Our inference follows from that. Some of Nvidia's drop is about governance, and some is about policy risk. Once the Commerce Secretary is called to testify, the policy piece runs on Washington's timetable. And an export-license fight is a structural question for Nvidia's China channel, not a cyclical one. Neither Nvidia's board nor Tuesday's numbers can settle it.

Three things to watch into next week. First, Tuesday's print, and whether the demand numbers settle which reading of Friday was right. Second, any deployment evidence behind TPU 8tx: a general-availability date, a named customer, anything beyond the announcement. Third, whether a congressional testimony date actually gets set, for Huang or for Lutnick.

That's the briefing. Enjoy your Saturday, and I'll talk to you next time.
