Good morning. It's Sunday, August 30th, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

For the past several days this show has closed on the same complaint. Three enterprise software vendors reported, three of them beat, and not one told you what share of its growth was actually artificial intelligence, in a unit an outsider could reason about. Workday's fiscal second-quarter print, reported Thursday, is the closest thing this cohort has produced to an answer. It is worth being precise about what is actually new in it, because the number itself is not new. Only its scope is.

On the earnings call, Workday's management said AI products drove more than one hundred million dollars of new annual contract value. That was over twenty-five percent of all new annual contract value closed in the quarter. It sounds like a fresh disclosure. It isn't. Back in May, on the fiscal first-quarter call, Workday reported a comparable figure. More than twenty-five percent of new contract value in customer-base expansions came from AI, with AI-included expansion deals averaging more than fifty percent larger in size. So Workday has now published an AI-attribution number in back-to-back quarters. What changed between the two is scope. In May it covered only expansion deals sold into the existing customer base. In August it widened to cover all new bookings, expansions and new logos together. That is a real broadening of the claim, not a first-time reveal, and this show should not credit it as one.

Give the disclosure the credit it does deserve, though, on one specific point. Because management attached a dollar figure to the percentage — over a hundred million dollars of new contract value — the number reads as built from sales of AI-specific products, rather than the full value of any deal that happens to contain an AI feature. Those are very different claims, and the second one would make the metric a labeling convention rather than an attribution. The dollar figure points to the better interpretation. But Workday still does not publish the methodology, so that remains an inference and not a confirmed definition. Hold one more thing about the metric: annual contract value is a bookings-style measure. It does not reconcile against the two point six four nine billion dollars of revenue Workday actually recognized in the quarter.

Here is why the two-quarter history matters more than the headline. Because we now have two consecutive prints at roughly a quarter of new bookings carrying an AI attribution, we can finally ask the harder question. Has that AI attach moved the growth rate at all? Fiscal first-quarter subscription revenue grew fourteen percent year over year. Fiscal second-quarter subscription revenue grew thirteen point nine percent. That is flat, arguably a hair slower, across the exact stretch in which AI has supposedly been driving a quarter of new bookings. Total revenue was up twelve point eight percent, and full-year subscription guidance sits at roughly thirteen percent growth. If AI dollars were purely additive at that scale, the growth line should be bending upward. It is not bending at all. So either AI-labeled contract value is substituting for core human-capital and financials bookings that would have happened anyway, or it is being attached to deals that would have closed regardless. Workday does not publish what would let us tell which. That is the single most important sentence in this print.

There is a genuine adoption signal underneath the arithmetic. More than fifty-five hundred customers now use at least one of Workday's organic AI agents, up more than thirty-five percent from the prior quarter alone. That is real sequential land-and-expand inside the installed base, not just new-logo activity. Workday's own AI annual recurring revenue is approaching six hundred million dollars, more than tripling year over year. But scale that against the full-year subscription guide of just under ten billion dollars and, on our own arithmetic rather than a Workday disclosure, AI is running at roughly six percent of the subscription business. And an activation count does not tell you depth of use. A customer who switched on one agent in expense management counts identically to one running agents across payroll, recruiting and financial close.

One forward-leaning wrinkle cuts the other way, and it deserves flagging rather than burying. Workday's twelve-month subscription backlog — its near-term booked revenue — grew fourteen point two percent, to nine point oh three four billion dollars. That is running slightly ahead of the twelve point eight percent total revenue growth rate. Backlog has not worked through to recognized revenue yet. So it is the one data point that could complicate the "AI attach is not moving the needle" read in future quarters. Worth watching, not yet resolved. Meanwhile non-GAAP operating income came in at eight hundred twenty-four million dollars, thirty-one point one percent of revenue, so none of this AI investment is currently pressuring margin.

Now set Workday's number next to what its peers are willing to say, because the comparison is where the buyer-relevant conclusion lives. Salesforce, reporting the same week, told investors Agentforce annualized recurring revenue exceeded one and a half billion dollars, up more than two hundred forty percent year over year. That is a real growth rate. But it is a run-rate Salesforce itself defines and does not decompose against total bookings. There is no denominator to check it against, the way there is with Workday's share-of-contract-value figure. CrowdStrike, also reporting that week, added a record three hundred thirty-two point eight million dollars of net new annual recurring revenue. It grew its Falcon Flex consumption bundle to two point two nine billion dollars in ending recurring revenue, up one hundred one percent. But CrowdStrike attributes none of that specifically to AI, while leaning hard on AI-driven threats as its demand narrative.

So rank the three by disclosure quality. Workday publishes a share of bookings you can compare against a total, even if the definition is incomplete. Salesforce publishes a self-defined run-rate with no denominator. CrowdStrike publishes nothing at all and lets the AI framing do the rhetorical work instead. ServiceNow, which this show covered on Friday, sits somewhere in between, publishing an absolute AI contract-value figure without a share. Every one of these numbers is still self-reported, with no outside check. What has improved this week is the shape of the disclosure, not its independence.

Two second-order effects follow from the Workday number specifically. The first is pricing. Workday's suite is priced per employee, which was supposed to be the one seat-based meter immune to agent disruption, because headcount is a stable denominator. If AI attach keeps climbing as a share of new bookings, that denominator stops making sense. Workday, and peers like SAP SuccessFactors and Oracle's human-capital business, then face the same drift toward consumption or per-agent pricing that ServiceNow and CrowdStrike have already started. For a Workday buyer that is the sharper version of the problem, because headcount was the one line a chief information officer could forecast.

The second is governance. Fifty-five hundred customers now running agents inside payroll, expense and financial-close workflows creates an approval-chain and audit-scope problem the human-resources suite itself was never built to solve. That is a budget line someone has to own. Either Workday builds the oversight tooling in-house, or identity and security vendors sell into the gap.

So what would settle whether this is substance or relabeling? Watch the next print. If Workday's AI share of new contract value climbs again while subscription growth stays pinned near thirteen percent, that is strong evidence for relabeling. If subscription growth actually moves up alongside it, the AI dollars are incremental. And watch the calendar. Palo Alto Networks and MongoDB report September first, Snowflake September second, Zscaler September third. Now that a large suite vendor has put a share-of-bookings number on the table, the absence of a comparable figure from any of those four stops being a neutral gap and becomes a signal worth reading.

That's your briefing for August 30th. Have a good day.
