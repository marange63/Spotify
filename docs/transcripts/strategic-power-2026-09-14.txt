Good morning. It's Monday, September 14th, and this is your strategic power briefing.

For five sessions this beat has graded announced commitments against delivered capacity, and the announcements kept losing — nuclear deals, turbine backlogs, capacity auctions, enrichment task orders. This week the thing that actually converted was never announced at all. Yesterday that showed up in rare earths, where the most consequential action of the week came with no press release behind it. Today it arrives with something yesterday's version lacked: an official agency has written that unannounced action into its own forecast, with a number attached.

Start with the International Energy Agency. Its September 11th Oil Market Report revised down the baseline outlook for Russian oil processing over the next eighteen months to around four million barrels a day. That is thirty percent below pre-invasion levels. The cause is sustained Ukrainian drone strikes on refinery secondary-processing units — the equipment that turns crude into diesel and gasoline. August saw at least twenty-two such strikes on Russian refineries, the highest monthly total of the war. Those units take six to eight months to replace. The IEA's own language is that persistent strikes and the patchwork nature of the repairs are having a cumulative negative effect and degrading the refining system. That word, cumulative, is what moves this from a raid to a capacity ceiling.

Here is the arithmetic that makes this a product story rather than a barrel story. The IEA cut its 2026 forecast for Russian crude supply by only 125,000 barrels a day, to 8.7 million. Set that against a thirty percent cut to refining. Russia can still pump the crude. What it is losing is the ability to turn that crude into usable fuel. Trade-flow data corroborates the split from the demand side. Combined diesel and gasoil net exports from Russia and the Gulf were 1.6 million barrels a day lower in August than in February, when those two sources accounted for almost forty-five percent of global seaborne trade in the product. US diesel prices broke above two hundred dollars a barrel in early September, ninety-four percent above pre-war levels. That is an observable price, not a projection.

So the so-what is an inflation channel most oil commentary is not set up to see. A crude shock shows up at the gasoline pump and in headline CPI within weeks, and central banks have a well-worn script for looking through it. A middle-distillate shock is slower and stickier. Diesel is the fuel of freight, of agriculture, of construction equipment, and of the backup and bridging generation now running alongside new industrial and data-center load. It enters the price level as a cost of moving and making goods, not as a line in the consumer basket. That channel takes longer to appear and longer to clear, because it is not a single spike but a supply ceiling held in place by an eight-month repair queue.

One assumption deserves to be knocked down before it spreads. The obvious inference is that the crude Russia can no longer refine at home simply gets exported raw instead, putting the same barrels back on the water in a different form. There is no flow data supporting that yet. The reporting describes a near-halt in Russian refined-product exports compounding the losses, and the IEA cut its crude supply forecast rather than raising it. So treat the substitution as a plausible mechanism, not an observed flow.

Keep the other limits in view too. An eighteen-month baseline is a forecast from a consumer-country agency, not a measurement, and the IEA has revised Russian supply repeatedly through this war. The twenty-two-strike count is an August figure with no September equivalent, so the run-rate may already have moved in either direction. And degrading refining is not the same as degrading war-fighting: a state that cannot refine can still export crude and buy finished product.

Now the contrast that organizes the episode. The instrument that inflicted this damage carried no statute, no appropriation and no vote. The instrument written to do the same job is still waiting on one. The Senate passed the Graham Russia and Iran sanctions bill 86 to 11 in early August. It would authorize the White House, at its discretion, to impose secondary tariffs of up to one hundred percent on the top five importers of Russian oil and gas. Five weeks later it still has not had a House floor vote. A member says he was briefed on a plan to bring it up the week Congress returns, which is this week. Treat that as a plan, not a schedule, and certainly not a result. Authorization is also not imposition, and this White House has a pattern of holding such authority as leverage rather than spending it. And the reported House objection is that the bill expands a tariff authority the Supreme Court has already curtailed — which means passage could produce a legally contested instrument rather than a usable one.

That mechanism points at the buyers, and they are unusually concentrated. Trade-flow analysis this year puts China at about fifty percent of Russian crude exports and India at about thirty-six percent, with Turkey and the EU around six percent each. That is mid-year data, not a September snapshot, but the shape is the point: a measure aimed at the top five importers has, in practice, two that matter. And one of them is already moving without any statute. Kpler data show Russia supplied India roughly 2.1 million barrels a day in August, about forty-five percent of Indian imports, down from a record 2.8 million barrels a day and more than half of imports in July. The flows are repricing ahead of the legislation, which is the pattern of this whole episode.

The other of those two buyers is China — and Xi Jinping comes to Washington on September 24th. So what Washington actually has to offer Beijing matters, and the chip file now has a number on it. Yesterday this beat noted that Nvidia is not counting on Chinese data-center revenue. Here is the measurement: sales of the H200 to Chinese buyers were less than one percent of Nvidia's data-center revenue in the quarter ended July 26th, and the company has not been able to ship even its full licensed allocation, because Beijing is objecting to the imports. So the concession Washington might dangle — easing export terms — is one Beijing has already priced near zero. That bounds what the summit can deliver on this file.

That figure is also seven weeks stale, so the 24th could move it in either direction. Which way depends on a rationale no Chinese authority has stated. If the restriction is negotiating leverage, it can be lifted on the 24th and the number recovers. If it is industrial policy — forcing domestic substitution — it does not lift at all, and Huawei and SMIC capture the Chinese hyperscaler capex that the entire US export-control architecture was designed to delay.

If the sanctions track is about removing Russian barrels, the other half of Washington's energy policy is about adding new ones — and Venezuela is splitting the supermajors. Chevron said on September 1st it will invest more than seven billion dollars over five years to push its Venezuelan output toward roughly 600,000 barrels a day. That is a financed, bankable commitment. Separately, the administration awarded a hundred-year concession over seventeen fields, targeting 1.5 million barrels a day. It did not go to Chevron, Exxon or ConocoPhillips. It went to a private, Barbados-headquartered firm with no public operating record, and whether that entity can finance or execute development on that scale is entirely unverified. Taken at face value, the two claims together imply well over two million barrels a day from a country producing a small fraction of that today. No source reconciles that gap, and the gap is itself the finding.

Exxon is the outside check. Its chief executive called Venezuela uninvestable in January, and as of the reporting at the start of this month a spokesman said nothing has changed. Two firms with the same access to the same assets reached opposite verdicts. That divergence is a more honest risk assessment than either government's press release — and political durability is unresolved either way, with Maduro-era officials still running the ministries.

So here is the ledger. What converted this week was destruction, executed with no announcement and no appropriation behind it. What has not converted is every constructive instrument aimed at the same problem: a sanctions bill unvoted five weeks after passing the Senate, a hundred-year concession with no operator, a chip concession worth under a percentage point of revenue. And with Russia hitting Ukrainian power sites again on the 13th, there is still no catalyst to unwind the war-risk premium sitting under European gas and defense budgets. Watch two checkable dates: whether the House actually votes this week, and what September 24th produces. Everything else is still paper.

That's the briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
