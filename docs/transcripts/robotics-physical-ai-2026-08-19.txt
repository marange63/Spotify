Good morning, it's Wednesday, August 19th, 2026, and this is your robotics and physical AI briefing.

Yesterday we flagged Unitree's Shanghai listing as tomorrow's catalyst, and framed the open question as whether the public market would price past the company's profit collapse or actually reckon with it. It printed. Shares opened at 1,100 yuan against an offer price of 150.80 yuan, and closed the day up 629 percent, according to figures reported by the South China Morning Post. That takes Unitree's market capitalization to roughly 445 billion yuan, or about 66 billion dollars, making it the first mainland-listed humanoid robotics pure-play, and the largest single valuation print this category has ever produced. The market didn't reckon with the profit collapse we cited yesterday. It rewarded scarcity instead.

Start with the mechanics, because they explain the size of the pop better than any fundamentals story does. Unitree sold 40.45 million shares, ten percent of its enlarged share capital, raising about 6.1 billion yuan, roughly 905 million dollars. Retail demand was extraordinary: nearly 9.8 million accounts bid for 9.7 million shares, and the final allocation rate came out to just 0.018 percent. That means the overwhelming majority of retail money chasing this stock got none of it, which is exactly the setup that produces a violent first-day pop — a tiny float meeting a wall of demand that couldn't get in at the offer price. So the headline 66 billion dollar figure is less a verdict on Unitree's business than a scarcity price. The company itself only captured that 905 million dollars in proceeds. Everything above it is paper wealth marked to a thin float, not capital that reached Unitree's balance sheet.

Now, is that valuation actually disconnected from the fundamentals, or is there a real number underneath it? There is one, and it's worth stating plainly, because it changes the shape of the story. Unitree's prospectus discloses full-year 2025 revenue of about 1.70 billion yuan, roughly four times the 392 million yuan it booked in 2024, with a gross margin of 60.1 percent and adjusted net profit of about 600 million yuan. At the IPO offer price — the 61 billion yuan valuation before trading even started — that works out to a price-to-earnings ratio of 219 times 2025 earnings and a price-to-sales ratio of 36 times, both figures reported directly by press coverage of the offering. So Unitree was already priced at frontier-AI richness before a single share traded. And here's the tell on sentiment: one outlet, Forbes, called that 36-times-sales offer price "the cheap end of the humanoid trade," a direct comparison to Figure AI's roughly 39 billion dollar private valuation on essentially no revenue at all. When 36 times sales is the value end of your category, the category is priced for a decade of flawless execution.

What happened today layers a 629 percent pop on top of that already-expensive base. The 66 billion dollar debut cap is a reported figure. What no source has published is a recalculated multiple at the trading price — no analyst or filing has restated the price-to-earnings or price-to-sales ratio at 445 billion yuan, and I'm not going to invent one, because scaling the offer multiples by the pop is my own arithmetic, not a quoted number. What we can say with confidence is narrower and sturdier: the disclosed base was already at 219 times earnings, and today's action moved well beyond that base on retail flow alone. The re-rating didn't take Unitree from cheap to expensive. It took an already-expensive stock and multiplied it.

On the operating side, the prospectus does give us real numbers to work with. Unitree shipped 5,500 humanoid units in full-year 2025, part of cumulative 2022-through-September-2025 shipments of more than 30,000 quadrupeds and 4,000 humanoids. A separate Forbes figure puts cumulative humanoid production at roughly 18,000 units by July of this year — that's not reconcilable with the prospectus range without assuming an enormous first-half ramp, so I'm flagging the discrepancy rather than picking a winner. Either way, Unitree is shipping real units at a 60 percent gross margin, which is more than most of the humanoid field can say. That's the crucial distinction today's tape blurs. The base business is genuine and growing. It's the mania sitting on top of it, not the business itself, that's unmoored from anything you can underwrite.

The debut lands in the middle of the World Robot Conference opening in Beijing, running through the 23rd, with more than 300 exhibitors, up 36 percent year over year, and over 150 product launches expected. That's the backdrop worth naming. China's industrial-policy machine and its public markets are now pricing humanoid robotics as a strategic national category, with retail capital doing the underwriting that venture capital does in the US. The listing and the conference are the same message from two directions: the state wants this industry financed, and a STAR Market pop is a faster way to socialize the cost of capital than any subsidy line item.

Which brings us to the other side of the trade. While China prices a humanoid maker at frontier multiples on retail mania, the clearest US data point this window is quieter and comes from Figure AI. The company's BotQ facility built its 1,000th Figure 03 humanoid on July 23rd, about three months after first hitting a sustained rate of one robot per hour — at that point it had built over 350 units, so the cadence held for a full quarter rather than being a one-day demo. Figure 03 units are deployed at BMW's Spartanburg plant on assembly-logistics sequencing tasks. As of the April ramp announcement, end-of-line first-pass yield was above 80 percent, battery-line yield was 99.3 percent, and the company had produced more than 9,000 actuators across ten-plus variants. One caveat to bank: deployed at BMW is not the same as autonomous at BMW, and Figure hasn't disclosed how many of those field hours are supervised versus fully independent. Still, that's manufacturing discipline, funded by private capital rather than retail flow. Set against it, the counterpoint from Tesla: as of its July shareholder letter, Optimus production at Fremont still hadn't started, pushed again to "later this year" — a reminder that vertically-integrated EV manufacturing know-how hasn't automatically translated to humanoid hardware the way Figure's cadence has.

That's the bifurcation this week has made concrete. China's humanoid cohort is now measurable against a public tape, priced by retail mania on top of a genuinely growing but still 219-times-earnings base. The US cohort is being built quietly on private capital and manufacturing yield, with no public price to mark it against yet. And the read-through for private valuations cuts both ways. On the way up, Unitree's tape becomes the bull-case comparable every banker points to when they market the next Figure or Apptronik round. On the way down, a 629 percent pop on a 0.018 percent allocation rate is a textbook setup for a sharp correction once the lockup unwinds and the mania float clears — and if Unitree's price breaks, that same tape becomes the bear case, and the whole humanoid trade gets re-marked lower with it. The comparable that lifts private valuations today is the one that can drag them tomorrow.

So watch two things from here. First, whether Unitree's price holds once real trading volume replaces scarcity-driven demand — that's the signal on whether 66 billion dollars was a price or just an artifact of who couldn't get an allocation. Second, whether any Chinese peer — AgiBot, UBTech, or Fourier — tries to pull an IPO timeline forward to catch this window before it closes. Mania this visible tends to summon supply, and the fastest way to end a scarcity premium is for the scarcity to disappear.
