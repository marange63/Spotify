Good morning, this is The Throughline for Saturday, September 5th, 2026. Today's headline: August payrolls landed at 162,000, roughly triple consensus. One print falsified this show's stagflation scenario, knocked bitcoin off a four-month high, and changed the Fed question from tightening into a slowdown to tightening into resilience.

Start with the macro backdrop. Strategic Power finds the AI buildout's upstream supply chain revising demand sharply upward. TSMC's own estimate of its quarterly equipment purchases has nearly doubled in six months, and it is advancing construction on close to twenty fabs worldwide. But a Congressional Research Service report expects only fifty to sixty percent of scheduled US data-center capacity to energize on time, and no improvement in chip supply fixes a power gap. All of it gets underwritten at a price the Fed sets in eleven days.

Capital Markets Cross-Asset Radar has the print itself, and it is brutal to the scenario this beat had been carrying. June and July were revised up by a combined 55,000 jobs, and July flipped from an outright decline to a gain. So the softening-labour-market leg of the stagflation call is gone. Now the subtle part. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh built his hawkish case on the breadth of price increases, not on labour slack. A firm jobs number therefore does not strengthen his argument. It removes the one thing that could have stayed his hand. Hike odds for September 16th moved from near fifty-fifty into the high fifties. The Russell 2000 fell 1.4 percent against the S&P's four tenths, which makes this a discount-rate event rather than a growth scare.

Digital Money has the same print doing the damage in crypto. Bitcoin had climbed to $82,240 on a dovish hint from Governor Waller, then fell more than two percent within minutes of the release and closed down about 1.2 percent. It stopped just short of the $83,000-to-$86,000 wall of long-term holder supply this show had named as the real breakout test. And bitcoin's correlation with gold, which hit its highest level since 2020 in August and looked like the best evidence yet for the digital-gold thesis, stopped mattering the moment one macro number landed.

If rates are repricing public markets by the hour, Private Credit and the Corporate Credit Cycle shows the slower version in loan books. A cross-filing read of forty-four business development companies finds fair-value marks falling three quarters running. The damage is concentrated. Eighty-one percent of software loans are now carried below cost, against forty percent of everything else. That is a vertical being repriced, not a cycle turning, but the same managers financing the AI buildout hold the loan books of the incumbents it is disrupting.

Private Equity and the Deal Economy has the limited partners writing back. ILPA's draft continuation-vehicle guidance would require competitive bidding, independent third-party price validation, and fee disclosure in actual dollars rather than headline percentages. That is an admission that nobody can verify a fair mark when the sponsor sits on both sides of the trade. One law firm is already telling sponsor clients to get ahead of the standard rather than fight it, which suggests it may legitimize continuation vehicles more than restrain them.

Turning to the AI and compute stack. Semiconductors and Compute Infrastructure has Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick putting a shape on chip tariffs. Build in America and you get relief. Don't, and you pay duties on finished goods. But TSMC's executives say the binding constraint is no longer capital. It is construction labour and installation crews, which no tariff can buy, and which an onshoring push would bid against.

Frontier AI Lab Competition has the price. OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra lists at ten dollars per million input tokens and fifty per million output, line for line the same rate card as Anthropic's Claude Fable 5.1. So the headline price has stopped sorting the frontier. Competition has moved to cache pricing, which nobody publishes on the same basis, and to access. Astra's least-restricted tier is rationed through a vetting program called Daybreak rather than sold at any price.

AI Products and Applications has that convergence pushing the fight one tier down. Meta's cheap coding model comes at roughly a twelfth of its own list price on input, in exchange for permission to train future models on the developer's prompts and code. Google is pulling the other lever and discounting against a clock. Gemini 3.8 Flash carries an introductory rate that doubles on January 1st.

Enterprise Technology ties the stack back to physical reality. HPE says supply, not demand, is what limits converting AI orders into revenue, and guided fiscal 2027 growth down by more than half on memory and NAND shortages. CrowdStrike, carrying none of that hardware exposure, raised its net-new annual recurring revenue growth guidance by 630 basis points. The 2027 guides are sorting vendors by how much memory sits inside their own cost of goods sold.

Into the applied frontier. Robotics and Physical AI has China's AgiBot publishing the sector's first fielded reliability number, a 99.99 percent success rate on an electronics inspection line. The trouble is that the same figure is anchored to two contradictory denominators in different reports, with no definition anywhere of what counts as a failure. Meanwhile Unitree listed in Shanghai at a market value near sixty-six billion dollars, the first public equity mark on a humanoid maker, and it happened in China rather than here.

Space and the Orbital Economy has outside checks arriving faster than deliveries. A Berenberg initiation moved AST SpaceMobile about eleven percent on a differentiation claim, while two thirds of the fleet AST has paid to build still sits on the ground waiting for launch. And two outside analyses published this week argue Golden Dome's space-based boost-phase intercept runs into orbital mechanics that money does not obviously fix. Those are opinion pieces, not a Pentagon finding. But it is the first constraint of the week that a bigger check would not cure.

AI in Biopharma R&D closes the tour in digital pathology. It is the one layer of AI in pharma holding a credential somebody outside the company conferred, an FDA clearance and a drug-development-tool qualification, rather than a self-scored benchmark. It is also the layer pharma is quietly buying rather than loudly funding, as AstraZeneca's purchase of Modella AI shows. Ninety-five percent of this year's disclosed AI-drug-discovery financing went to two molecule-design shops, neither with an external audit of its claims.

One change from yesterday is worth naming. This show spent most of the week complaining that almost nothing in these sectors was checkable from outside. Today the outside checks arrived at once. A payrolls print falsified a macro call. A physics argument questioned a defence architecture. A contradictory denominator undercut robotics' first reliability disclosure. And limited partners asked for a competitive bid instead of a mark. Each of those moved more than a week of self-reported numbers did.
