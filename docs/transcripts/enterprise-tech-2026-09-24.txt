Good morning, it's Thursday, September 24th. This is your enterprise technology briefing.

It's a thin week for fresh numbers on this beat. So rather than a roundup, I'll spend today on two connected moves. The first is that Anthropic is writing the big systems integrators into its model-spend channel. Accenture and Deloitte are now listed as Global Premier partners in Claude Marketplace. The second is new survey data on whether enterprises can actually control the agents that channel will put to work.

Start with the Marketplace, and date it once. It launched on March 6th with six partners and has expanded since. The consulting tier is the latest step, not a debut. Here's the mechanic that makes the consulting tier matter. According to RuntimeWire, eligible customers can put a portion of their committed Anthropic spend toward partner purchases. The purchasable Claude-powered products come from Cursor, CrowdStrike, Harvey, Legora, Lovable and Snowflake. RuntimeWire also counts more than two thousand connectors and plugins at launch. Accenture and Deloitte now sit on top of that catalog, helping organizations plan and carry out deployments.

So why does it matter? Because of what it does to procurement. Once a committed AI contract can buy software, the model vendor sits upstream of the application vendor's sales cycle. The partner gets a pre-approved budget and a shorter procurement path. In exchange, it cedes discovery and the customer relationship to Anthropic's catalog. For vendors like Harvey, Cursor and Lovable, growth starts to depend partly on placement in someone else's storefront. That's interpretation, and no figure attaches to it yet. But it's a real shift in who controls enterprise software budget.

The consulting tier is what turns that catalog into deployments. Integrators are how enterprise budgets become running projects. Embedded AI normally favors whoever already holds the renewal. It also caps what AI-native point tools can charge, because the suite vendor bundles a good-enough feature. And a feature inside a suite carries no integration project, which makes it a demand headwind for integrators. The Marketplace pushes the other way on all three. Point tools get a path into budget that's already approved, just not by the suite vendor. And Accenture and Deloitte get a deployment job attached to the model contract rather than to the suite.

Both forces can hold at once. Embedded features shrink hourly integration work, while a model-contract catalog creates a new kind of deployment job. The upshot is that the renewal holder now has a rival for the budget: whoever holds the AI commitment.

This is also where Anthropic's own competition is heading. With frontier model prices falling, rank on a leaderboard is a wasting asset, and distribution is where the lab now competes. Incumbents are working the same surface from the other direction. Salesforce already ships its CRM into Claude as a plugin, now in beta for all customers. So the budget fight isn't suite against startup anymore. It's suite against model contract, with both trying to be the place where the purchase happens.

Now the skeptical read, because this is a mechanism, not a revenue event. No dollar figure for spend routed through the Marketplace has been disclosed. There are no transaction counts and no customer names. A listed partner is not a deployment, and a Global Premier badge is not a signed project. There's also a real alternative explanation for why Anthropic built this. Suppose enterprises signed commitments larger than their actual token consumption. Letting them spend the leftover on partner software protects Anthropic's committed revenue, and it heads off an awkward renewal. On that reading, this isn't platform strength. It's a sign that consumption lags commitments.

Nothing disclosed so far can tell those two stories apart, and that's by design. RuntimeWire's coverage of the earlier expansion this month said Anthropic doesn't explain marketplace fees, partner revenue shares, or how much of a commitment can be redirected. This week's coverage says the company still hasn't said what share of a commitment is eligible. Two rounds of announcements, same silence. That looks like a choice, not a gap in the reporting.

The fee silence matters most for one partner. CrowdStrike is a named product partner in this catalog. That bears on a live question in security: does the security vendor stay the brain of an enterprise's defenses, or become a channel for a model lab's intelligence? Here, a major security vendor is selling through the model lab's storefront. Without fee terms, we can't yet say who captures the value.

And this isn't a new idea. RuntimeWire notes that Microsoft already allows eligible marketplace purchases to count fully toward Azure consumption commitments. So Anthropic isn't inventing a procurement pattern. It's importing an existing cloud one into an AI model contract. That makes it easier for buyers to accept, and easier for Microsoft and Google to match.

Which brings us to the architecture Anthropic is routing around, and it has a number attached. Per Futurum's write-up of Microsoft's late-July earnings, Microsoft 365 Copilot passed thirty million paid seats, up from roughly twenty million three months earlier. That's the last reported baseline, not news. It describes Microsoft's model: AI sold inside the suite, by the seat. Anthropic's model is AI spend that buys someone else's software. Paid seats aren't active usage, though. And new Copilot Business licenses sold through Microsoft's partner channel default to usage-based billing from November 2nd. So even the incumbent is drifting toward consumption, which is the Marketplace's home turf. Two opposite architectures are chasing the same budget, and both are quietly conceding that the seat is the wrong unit.

Now to the second move, governance. Two thousand connectors means more agents touching more systems of record. And the freshest data says enterprises can't reliably control the agents they already run. That data comes from a survey released on Tuesday by Guild.ai, a vendor that sells agent-governance tooling. Treat it as motivated research, because the gap it finds is exactly the problem its product solves.

Here's the headline contrast. Ninety-six point four percent of IT decision-makers say they're confident their organization has a complete and accurate inventory of its AI agents. Yet two-thirds of organizations with agents report what the survey calls an agent-related operational consequence in the past twelve months. That term is undefined, so I won't call it an incident. And confidence and consequence aren't contradictory if most of those consequences were minor.

The sharper number measures a control rather than a belief. Only thirty-one percent of organizations can immediately stop a malfunctioning agent with an automated kill switch. That's fewer than one in three. No sample size or methodology was published, so read all of this as directional, not authoritative. Even so, a kill switch is the one control you can't bolt on after an agent misfires. Knowing an agent exists is inventory. Stopping it mid-action is operations.

Put the two moves side by side. A catalog of more than two thousand plugins and connectors meets a survey where fewer than a third can stop a misbehaving agent automatically. Agent observability is already a product you can buy; Cisco's Splunk sells one. The likeliest spend to follow a wider connector catalog is spend on watching and controlling what that catalog lets agents do. That's plausibly part of the deployment work Accenture and Deloitte will be selling. And whoever runs the catalog is positioned to become the governance point, or the liability point, when something breaks.

The pattern holds: distribution is being built faster than the governance to operate it.

So what would settle this? First, a disclosed dollar figure or fee term for what's actually moving through the Marketplace. Second, Microsoft or Google matching the mechanic with a named, AI-specific move. Third, a governance survey that publishes its methodology, not just its headline numbers. Until then, the Marketplace tells us where Anthropic wants to sit in the budget, not yet how much budget moves.
