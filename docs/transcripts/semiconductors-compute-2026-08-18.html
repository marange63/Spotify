Cautious Optimism Briefings

Semiconductors & Compute Infrastructure

Transcript · August 18, 2026

Good morning. It's Tuesday, August 18th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

The lead today is a financing story, not a chip story, and it may matter more than any product launch this month. Nvidia has signed memorandums of understanding with six of the largest names in finance — Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs, and KKR — to build dedicated platforms aimed at mobilizing, in Nvidia's own words, "over $500 billion of third-party capital for the buildout of AI infrastructure over time." That capital is meant to fund data centers, chip factories, and the power plants that feed them. Nvidia's stated purpose, again in its own language, is to "create dedicated pools of capital at significant scale at attractive rates for NVIDIA customers." And Jensen Huang went further, telling CNBC that Nvidia's compute is "broadly adopted, flexible across models and workloads, fungible and transferable across customers and operators" — the pitch, in short, that a GPU is now an asset class, something you can lend against the way you'd lend against a toll road.

Two things to hold onto here. First, this is not committed money. Nvidia's own release is explicit that these partnerships "remain subject to execution of the final agreements" — these are memorandums of understanding, not signed, priced, funded deals. Rates, terms, and structure are still being negotiated. Second, separate reporting attributes to Huang a plan for Nvidia itself to backstop as much as a quarter of these financings. That figure doesn't appear anywhere in Nvidia's own press materials — treat it as reported, not confirmed, until it shows up in a filing. And note what is missing entirely: no primary source has yet put a number on the financing rate, the depreciation schedule, or the residual value assumed for a used GPU. The single assumption the whole pitch rests on is, so far, unquantified in public.

So what does this actually tell us? It tells us that even Nvidia — the company that has profited more than any other from this buildout — now believes its customers can't fund the next leg from operating cash flow and equity alone. That's a significant admission. And it's not happening in isolation. It's the same logic behind Intel's move eight days ago to upsize its stock offering to $20 billion, pricing 210 million shares at $95 apiece to fund foundry capital expenditure with no single external customer's name attached to the proceeds. Run those two together and you get a pattern: capacity is increasingly being financed against demand that isn't fully booked yet, whether that financing comes from diluting shareholders or from pulling in private credit and infrastructure funds. The buildout has outgrown the balance sheets that built it so far.

Which raises the obvious next question: if GPUs are going to be treated as collateral, what actually backs their value? The answer runs straight through memory. This is where the picture gets more interesting, because the bill of materials behind every AI accelerator is being reshaped in real time by a memory supercycle, and the competitive order inside that memory market just shifted.

Samsung's HBM4 yield has reportedly reached about 80 percent — what the industry calls a "golden yield," the threshold that minimizes defects and makes a memory product genuinely profitable to ship. That's a real turnaround. As recently as February this year, that yield sat below 60 percent. Six months to go from sub-60 to golden-yield is a legitimate manufacturing achievement, not a marketing claim, and it's corroborated by hard numbers from UBS. UBS now projects Samsung will edge ahead of SK hynix in HBM bit-share in 2027, at 41 percent versus 39 percent, after SK hynix holds the number-one position this year with 48 percent of shipments. If that crossover holds, Nvidia and the hyperscalers gain a credible second source for the single most supply-constrained component in an AI accelerator. That's exactly the kind of development that could cap the pricing power SK hynix has enjoyed as the dominant HBM supplier.

That matters for the collateral thesis in two directions at once. A rising memory bill of materials pressures the unit economics of every GPU built on it, which is a headwind for residual value — and this shortage has already pushed consumer DRAM pricing sharply higher over the past year, by some estimates roughly quadrupling since last September. But a second qualified HBM supplier is a tailwind, because it reduces the risk that a single vendor's allocation decisions determine what an idle GPU is actually worth on resale. Those two forces pull in opposite directions on the exact assumption — residual value — that Nvidia's financing partners will have to price.

There's a third stress on that collateral pitch, and it's geographic rather than technical. Huang's framing was that Nvidia compute is fungible and transferable "across customers and operators." China is the clearest evidence that isn't universally true anymore. Reporting this month puts China's high-end AI chip market on track for close to 90 percent domestic share in 2026. Huawei is targeting 750,000 AI chip shipments this year, and its Ascend 950PR is expected to take roughly 60 percent of domestic AI accelerator demand. Analysts now view Huawei's Ascend 950 line as roughly comparable to Nvidia's H200 — though treat that with caution. It's an analyst characterization, not a benchmark, and Huawei's published specs have historically run ahead of real-world software performance. The 750,000 figure is a target, not a confirmed shipment count.

But directionally, this is consistent with what Huang himself has said in recent months. Nvidia's China AI chip share, once near 95 percent, is now effectively zero. A whole national base of customers and operators has been removed from the pool across which Nvidia's compute is supposed to be fungible. That doesn't move Nvidia's near-term financials much, since both Nvidia and AMD already model China at close to zero. But it does shrink the addressable universe for the collateral thesis Nvidia is now selling to Wall Street. And it's a permanent loss, not a delayed one — China is building its own hardware and software stack around Huawei's architecture, which raises the switching cost back even if controls eventually eased.

Put it together and the through-line is this. The AI buildout has crossed from being primarily an engineering and capacity problem into being a credit problem. Nvidia is trying to institutionalize its chips as a financeable asset class so the next few hundred billion dollars doesn't have to come off hyperscaler balance sheets. Whether that works depends on assumptions nobody has published yet — what a used GPU is worth in three years, what utilization looks like in a downturn, and how concentrated the credit risk becomes if Nvidia really is backstopping a meaningful slice of these deals. The memory market and the China market are both quietly testing those assumptions right now. Memory pushes on residual value from both sides at once — a richer component cost dragging it down, a second supplier propping it up — while China proves that fungible doesn't mean universal. Watch for these memorandums of understanding to convert into signed, priced deals. That's the moment the assumptions stop being marketing and start being numbers on a balance sheet.
