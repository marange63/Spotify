Good morning, this is The Throughline for Friday, September 25th, 2026.

Today's headline: Houthi missile fire aimed at Yanbu, the Red Sea outlet of Saudi Arabia's Hormuz bypass, pushed Brent up more than three percent, into a Fed that is already guiding toward one more hike.

Start with the macro backdrop, and the summit that framed the week. Strategic Power reports that Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent extended the Busan tariff truce by two months, to January 10th, 2027, with no firm new commitments on chips or rare earths. The latest trade data show what Beijing's rare-earth leverage looks like in practice: US-bound shipments fell again in August, Japan got none for a third straight month, and Korea now takes more than it did before the controls. At home, PJM's capacity auction cleared at its price ceiling and still came up 6,831 megawatts short, so an emergency backstop procurement opens for bids on September 30th, the same day FERC's governance deadline lands.

Now to the oil shock itself. Capital Markets Cross-Asset Radar has Brent closing Thursday at 106.39 dollars a barrel, after the Houthis said an Aramco facility at Yanbu was their target. No source confirms lost loadings or damage, so the desk treats this as an attack, not yet a supply loss. The sharper point is timing: September's flash PMI already showed the fastest input-cost rise since October 2022, so oil raises the odds that the Fed has to go beyond its one-more-hike median dot.

The same rate pressure is testing bitcoin's buyers. Digital Money reports that spot bitcoin ETFs still took in 346.9 million dollars on Wednesday, the day the ten-year Treasury yield jumped to 5.11 percent. That is positive, but it is roughly half Tuesday's inflow and a third of Monday's, so the bid survived at shrinking strength. The flow series alone can't separate real demand from fading demand or a hedged carry trade, and the next confirmed print is what starts to settle it.

Staying with money that wants out, Private Credit and the Corporate Credit Cycle finds Apollo's flagship retail fund capping redemptions at five percent for a third straight quarter, with 14.7 percent of shares asking to leave. Apollo estimates that most of those requests are old money re-tendering rather than a fresh run, and that is the manager's own read, not a verified count. Meanwhile ICG and Arini keep raising billions for closed-end European direct-lending funds with no redemption queue at all, so liquidity risk in private credit is splitting by wrapper, not by loan book.

Now to the AI and compute stack, where allocation, not design, decides who ships. Semiconductors and Compute Infrastructure relays secondhand reporting that TSMC has told both Nvidia and Broadcom it can't supply all the capacity they want. Memory shows the same squeeze with a price on it: spot HBM reportedly traded at more than five times the contract price in early September, and Micron's HBM is booked through 2027. The briefing's caution is that TSMC and Micron have both raised capex sharply, so anyone pricing today's scarcity rents as permanent is ignoring the supply already on its way.

From who gets the chips to who writes the rules. Frontier AI Lab Competition reports that OpenAI, Google and Anthropic have approached former White House AI advisor Sriram Krishnan to lead a FINRA-style self-regulator, tentatively called the Frontier AI Standards Agency. Krishnan has said there will not be an FDA for AI, so the pick signals pre-emption of government rules, and a badge written by three labs could become a moat against open-weight and Chinese models. The same briefing notes that nine months after Washington licensed H200 sales to China, zero units have shipped, because Beijing is now the side refusing them.

Enterprise Technology follows agents into production and asks who gets paid. Salesforce's new Agentic Enterprise Index counts only agents running in production, and it shows activated agents nearly tripling, with two days from creation to live use, though it discloses no base. The briefing's read is that faster builds shrink the configuration hours integrators used to bill, and Accenture is migrating toward lab-attached work instead, including as Anthropic's first embedded safety evaluator. Anthropic itself calls funding that role directly a stopgap, so even that new revenue line is provisional.

Turning to the applied frontier, where both hardware and rights are waiting on dates. Space and the Orbital Economy has Starship fully stacked for a Monday launch carrying its first real payload, 26 Version 3 Starlink satellites, but the FAA license was still pending as of Thursday. A September 30th FCC vote could open more than 1,000 megahertz for satellite use. But a draft clause keeps terrestrial incumbents primary for ten years, which makes it a slow-release option rather than an immediate unlock.

AI in Biopharma R&D closes the tour on the gap between what's being sold and what's being certified. Iambic filed to list on Nasdaq the same day it announced an AbbVie deal, but the S-1 itemizes no AbbVie economics and no efficacy data from its lead trial. Meanwhile, the newest FDA clearance in AI pathology went to Leica software that checks slide quality rather than making a diagnosis. So regulators are building trust outward from the most verifiable step, the opposite end from where Iambic is asking investors to start.

One thread runs through today's briefings: very little got resolved this week, it got scheduled. Watch September 30th, when PJM's backstop opens, FERC's deadline lands, the FCC votes and Micron reports, and then January 10th, when the China truce comes due again.
