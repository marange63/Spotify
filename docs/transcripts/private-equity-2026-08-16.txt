Good morning, it's Sunday, August 16th, and this is your private equity briefing.

Today's tension is a familiar one dressed up in fresh numbers: the fee machine just posted its best quarter in years, and the exit door is cracking open in public markets for the first time since 2021. Neither one, on close inspection, proves the industry has actually solved its cash-return problem.

Start with the earnings. Blackstone, KKR, Apollo and Carlyle all reported second-quarter results in the last three weeks, and fee-related earnings hit records or near-records at every one of them. Blackstone's fee-related earnings climbed 22% year over year to 1.8 billion dollars, on assets under management that reached 1.35 trillion, up 11% from a year ago. KKR's fee-related earnings rose even faster, up 37% to 1.2 billion dollars. Apollo's rose 25% to a record 785 million. Carlyle posted its own record at 358 million, up 11%, with distributable earnings the highest in nearly four years. That's four different franchises, four different strategies, all compounding management and performance fees at double-digit rates. So what: the fee-related earnings line runs on assets under management and time, not on the deal cycle — it grows whether or not sponsors are actually selling anything, which is exactly why the market keeps re-rating these firms as fee-compounding annuities rather than as cyclical deal shops.

The plan going into this briefing was to set that fee growth against a slower-growing carry line — the actual cash gains funds realize and send back to limited partners — and argue the two are diverging, that managers are increasingly paid on the mark rather than the exit. That argument turns out to be harder to support than the headline suggests, and it's worth being honest about why. Carlyle is the one firm here with a clean, quoted, apples-to-apples figure: its realized carry-fund proceeds rose 12% over the trailing twelve months to 36.8 billion dollars, per its own reported figures — a hair faster than its 11% fee-related-earnings growth, not slower. For Blackstone and Apollo, no comparably clean, dated realized-carry growth figure could be confirmed; Blackstone did report 31.8 billion dollars of realizations in the quarter and 144.5 billion over the trailing year, but without a verified year-over-year comparison. So the tidy story — fees outrunning carry everywhere — doesn't actually hold up on the one balance sheet we can check cleanly, and it's unproven on the other two. So what: the real finding isn't a confirmed divergence, it's that outsiders still can't cleanly verify whether cash returned to limited partners is keeping pace with fee growth across the group, and that opacity is itself the tell.

What is verifiable, and worth flagging distinctly, is a mix shift inside the earnings. Apollo posted record spread-related earnings of 877 million dollars alongside its fee-related record — that's insurance and retirement-services income, not classic buyout carry, and it's an increasingly large share of what shows up as "alternative-manager earnings." Apollo also originated 74 billion dollars in the quarter and separately struck a 35 billion dollar AI financing facility with Broadcom, to be drawn down over multiple quarters. So what: when you hear alt-manager earnings beat expectations, ask what mix of fees, spreads and carry actually drove it — the buyout business is a shrinking share of that story at the largest platforms, and the annuity re-rating investors are paying for rests more and more on credit and insurance flows than on the exit cycle the prompt is really asking about.

Now to the other side of the ledger: is any of this converting into actual distributions. Here the exit window is genuinely, if narrowly, reopening. Through August 5th, 21 US-listed IPOs involving private-equity-backed companies had already exceeded the entire 2025 total of 20 — the fastest pace since 2021. That's real and it's new. But it's concentrated in a handful of sectors — AI, aerospace and defense, a few trophy names — not a broad-based thaw. We walked the bigger liquidity story here on Friday: 121 billion dollars of global secondaries volume in the first half of the year, with GP-led continuation-vehicle volume growing far faster than traditional LP-led secondaries. The contrast matters. A public IPO is a clean exit to a third party. A continuation vehicle keeps the general partner in control of the same trophy asset while new investors buy in. So what: the industry is still solving most of its liquidity problem through structures it controls, and a narrow public reopening for AI and defense names doesn't change that for the bulk of stuck portfolios.

The same capital that isn't chasing control buyouts is finding other outlets, and Apollo again supplies the clearest example. On August 11th, Yankee Global Enterprises, the holding company for the New York Yankees, announced a 2.6 billion dollar financing agreement with Apollo Sports Capital, structured as a mix of credit and equity, funding both franchise growth and a refinancing of existing debt. The Steinbrenner family keeps full control; Apollo's Al Tylis joins the board. This is not a takeover — it's large-check, non-control structured capital, exactly the kind of deployment big managers reach for when traditional control buyouts are scarce or expensive. So what: capital is still moving at scale, but a growing share of it doesn't show up in the buyout statistics analysts use to gauge the health of the PE cycle, which means headline deal-value tallies increasingly understate real capital deployment by the majors.

Pull the threads together and the fundraising data closes the loop. Fund close rates fell to 57% last year, down from 94% in 2020, and first-time fund timelines have stretched to sixteen to twenty months from twelve to fifteen historically — by reported figures, so treat those as approximate. Yet aggregate dollars raised actually rose 9% in the first half of this year versus a year earlier, because a shrinking number of established managers with proven distributions-to-paid-in capital are capturing a growing share of commitments. So what: the same distribution drought that makes carry hard to verify is quietly redistributing the fundraising machine itself toward the biggest, most liquidity-proven platforms, starving the middle of the market — and that concentration is exactly the fuel behind the GP-stakes and manager-consolidation trade, as capital pools around the handful of platforms that can still prove real cash back.

Where does the cycle actually stand. The fee-related earnings machine is real, growing, and largely decoupled from the deal cycle — that much the quarter confirmed cleanly. What it did not confirm is that carry realization is falling behind it; the only clean comparison available argues the opposite. The binding uncertainty is still the exit and distribution problem: a narrow IPO reopening, a secondaries market growing mostly through GP-controlled structures, and a fundraising base concentrating around firms that can prove real distributions. The catalyst that would settle this read isn't another record fee print — it's the majors finally disclosing clean, comparable, year-over-year realized-carry figures, or a run of IPOs that actually clears lockups into real cash for limited partners.
