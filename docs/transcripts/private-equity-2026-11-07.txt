Good morning. It's November 7th, and this is your private-equity briefing.

Yesterday we said public investors will keep pricing alternative managers on their fees, because fees are the steady leg of the earnings stool while carry swings around. Today's news tests that. In the wealth channel, fees just turned out to be negotiable.

Start with Partners Group and Global Value, its flagship evergreen private-equity fund. The financial news site finews puts the fund's reported net assets at about 6.6 billion euros. It's a semi-liquid vehicle sold to wealthy individuals, the equity-side analog to private credit's retail push. In the second quarter, redemption requests reached an estimated 9.8% of net asset value. In June, the fund responded by limiting redemptions to 5% of net asset value per quarter. That alone could have been a one-off spike. It wasn't. Third-quarter requests again reached the 5% ceiling. Two straight quarters at the gate means the demand to get cash out is persistent, not a blip.

Now the restructuring. The details come from Bloomberg reporting and an investor presentation, and there are three moves. First, the fund splits in two. A distributing sub-fund is built to let investors realize gains through the orderly sale of holdings. A compounding sub-fund targets long-term capital growth. The split runs by the vintage of the underlying assets, not by investor. So an existing holder's units can end up in both sub-funds. Here's the rule that governs the exit. Each sub-fund keeps its own 5% quarterly redemption limit, the same level as the June gate, and requests that go unmet roll forward to later dates.

Existing holders then get three choices. They can stay, which means keeping the exposure they already have across the two sub-funds. They can move units from the distributing fund into the compounding fund. Or they can request redemption. Notice that the only switch on offer runs one way, toward the compounding fund.

Second, management fees come down. Distributing-fund investors get cuts of at least 25 basis points for three years. Compounding-fund investors get cuts of up to 75 basis points, running until January 2030. So the one-way switch points toward the sub-fund with the larger headline cut.

Third, there's one temporary tightening. New share classes start at a 2.5% limit from October 30th, and the compounding fund is meant to return to 5% as soon as practicable. For now, new money gets a narrower door than old money.

Put those together, and the split doesn't widen the exit. It sorts the queue. A holder who chooses to leave is still waiting in line, just inside a vehicle explicitly built to sell.

There's a genuinely two-sided read here. Separating vintages can protect the holders who stay from being diluted by forced sales into a weak exit market. That's sensible structural repair, not just damage control. But the fund is being reorganized around a liquidity constraint, and investors aren't getting better redemption terms. And whatever the intent, the fee cuts work economically as a payment to stay. That's a direct concession on exactly the revenue that was supposed to be the annuity-like part of a manager's earnings. The cuts are time-limited, not a permanent reset. But the lesson stands. In the wealth channel, the fee bends when the exit door jams.

Step back to why any sponsor ends up here. When requests in a semi-liquid vehicle keep running at the cap, the manager's menu narrows to three options, and none is free. It can keep gating, which tests the patience that brought investors into a semi-liquid product in the first place. It can cut fees to hold the money, which costs fee-related earnings directly. Or it can sell assets to raise cash, which ties the vehicle's liquidity back to the same stuck exit market that built the queue. Every lever either taxes the fee line or re-exposes the fund to the exit market.

For comparison, private credit shows the same shape. Blackstone's retail credit vehicle, BCRED, drew third-quarter redemption requests of 10% of net asset value, double its 5% quarterly limit. So flagship retail vehicles in both private equity and private credit have each seen requests run at about twice their caps in a recent quarter.

That sets up the second-order risk for the managers themselves. Two flagship gates in two asset classes give financial advisors a reason to reread redemption terms across every semi-liquid alternative. If that slows retail subscriptions, it hits the inflows that managers' forward fee-growth stories lean on. And if other sponsors copy the fee-concession playbook, retail fee margins compress. So does the premium multiple public markets pay for those fee streams.

So if retail holders want cash, where does it ultimately come from? Realizations. And here the picture gets murkier. Carlyle's global private-equity segment posted distributable earnings of 237 million dollars in the third quarter, up more than 60% from a year earlier. That looks like the realization pickup the exit story needs. But Carlyle's 10-Q says private-equity distributable earnings are expected to be lower in the fourth quarter. The filing gives direction, not size. Yesterday we said one strong Carlyle quarter on top of soft ones was lumpiness, not a run-rate. Carlyle's own filing now points the same way. A step-down after a quarter that strong isn't a re-freeze. It just means the third quarter wasn't the start of a trend.

Set that against Blackstone. Management told its earnings call to expect a significant step-up in net realizations in the fourth quarter. So for the same quarter, two of the largest managers are guiding their realization lines in opposite directions. The metrics aren't like-for-like. Blackstone's is firm-wide net realizations. Carlyle's is private-equity segment distributable earnings, which blends realized carry with fee income and isn't cash returned to limited partners. Still, the divergence is the signal. Carry isn't behaving like a dependable bridge to the cash retail holders are lining up for, and distributions stay lumpy just as those queues form.

Pull the threads together. The exit-and-distribution logjam hasn't broken. It has surfaced in a new place, the redemption queues at the wealth-channel edge of both private equity and private credit. Fees are under pressure at that retail edge, and carry stays lumpy. The binding risk is still realizations, not headline assets under management. And in the reporting we've seen this week, no marquee exit or final fund close changes that read. Where's the market complacent? In treating retail assets and retail fee streams as sticky, when a major sponsor has just cut fees in the middle of a redemption queue. Where might it be over-discounting? In reading one fund's restructuring as a sector-wide run. Keep the scale in view. This is one fund at one sponsor, and nothing yet shows the same stress, or the same fee concessions, spreading to other evergreen vehicles.

Watch three things. Partners Group's shareholder vote is scheduled for the week of November 9th, with the sub-funds due to go operational November 30th. That's the test of whether investors accept the new structure. KKR's results are expected around November 9th as well. Read them for realizations, against Blackstone's promised step-up and Carlyle's guided-down segment. If KKR also points realizations lower, Carlyle's guide starts to look like the sector's. And Blackstone's January print will show whether its significant step-up arrived, or was guidance that didn't hold.
