Cautious Optimism Briefings

Digital Money, Crypto, and Market Structure

Transcript · November 20, 2026

Good morning. It's Friday, November 20th, and this is your digital money briefing.

Today starts with a correction to our own read. On November 15th, this desk said bitcoin ETF redemptions were front-loaded and fading. We concluded that leverage, not the wrapper, was setting the marginal price. Thursday's data says otherwise. The new four-month low came with a heavy redemption day and no sign of a leverage flush. So the marginal seller now looks like the wrapper again.

Start with the tape. US spot bitcoin ETFs recorded $582 million in net outflows on November 19th, according to SoSoValue. That's nearly double the $306 million that left the day before. It's also well above the roughly $430 million on November 5th, the day we called the month's biggest. That single comparison is what breaks our earlier read. Redemptions didn't fade. They came back larger, into a falling price.

The monthly total needs more care. Per crypto.news, November's cumulative net outflow reached $3.47 billion through Thursday. Trackers disagree on the exact total, because their cutoffs and data providers differ. So treat the direction as real and the precise total as approximate.

Price confirms the direction. Bitcoin was at $61,546 when crypto.news published, down 10.8 percent on the week. That's its lowest level in more than four months. Prints vary by source, so treat it as a snapshot, not a settled close. Even so, it sits about $400 below the Coinbase low from the weekend of November 15th. The level that marked last week's flush has given way.

So who sold this time? That's the question that matters, because the answer tells you whether the selling has a natural stopping point. Start with the derivatives. CoinDesk puts bitcoin funding at about 0.0026 percent, open-interest weighted. That's essentially neutral, and it's outright negative on OKX and Bitget. Open interest, per CoinDesk's cited data, rose 1.61 percent to $42.5 billion.

Earlier this week, we set a simple test. If dip-buyers re-levered and funding rebuilt, the weekend reset was a pause, not a clearing. Funding hasn't rebuilt. So this leg doesn't look like the mid-month one, when longs were paying up and got flushed. Two cautions keep that honest. Near-zero funding after a flush is partly mechanical, because the longs who paid it were liquidated. And open interest rising into a selloff can mean new shorts, not longs reloading. Still, funding shows no crowded long book being forced out. The marginal seller looks like it has rotated from the levered book to the ETF wrapper.

That distinction matters for how deep this runs. A leverage flush exhausts itself once positions clear. Wrapper selling works differently, and this is our inference from standard ETF mechanics. When holders redeem on net, the underlying bitcoin has to be delivered or sold. That makes ETF outflows direct spot supply, in a way that liquidations on offshore perpetual venues are not. And a demand-side exit has no mechanical end point. It runs on the rate path and on how allocators size their risk budgets. On Wednesday, we said bitcoin's floor had become a demand problem. Thursday moved that from hypothesis to the evidence-backed read.

There's a competing reading, though, and the per-fund data sharpens it. BlackRock's IBIT alone accounted for $541 million of Thursday's $582 million total. That's roughly 93 percent of the day. Fidelity's FBTC, ARKB, and Grayscale's GBTC each lost well under $50 million. Reporting also credits a couple of smaller funds with inflows, though we couldn't verify those figures. And this isn't new. On Tuesday, IBIT was about nine-tenths of that day's outflow too.

That concentration cuts against a broad institutional exit. One dominant fund driving most of a day's flow also fits one or a few large holders rebalancing. And that reading changes the stopping-point question. A single allocator's rebalancing ends when the rebalancing is done. A broad exit doesn't. One day's data can't settle which story is right. The steadier signal is the trend. Since November 5th, the funds have posted net outflows in every session but one. So the wrapper is persistently a net seller, even if its biggest days are concentrated.

So why would the wrapper be selling? CoinDesk tied the week's move partly to Wednesday's FOMC minutes. It characterized them as the Federal Reserve pushing for higher rates. That's CoinDesk's paraphrase, not Fed language, and we couldn't retrieve the minutes text to check it. The direction does fit the rates tape. A week ago, CME FedWatch priced a December hike at 90 percent. By Wednesday, markets had it at 95 percent. In that environment, a zero-yield asset gets marked down, and so does the wrapper around it.

There's a read-through beyond price, and it's our inference. Sustained redemptions shrink the fee-earning assets of the issuers whose products anchored the institutionalization story. And for allocators, a bitcoin sleeve that sells off on hawkish minutes is a weaker equity diversifier than the pitch promised. So the selling hurts the business case, not just the price.

Now a cross-check on a story that circulated this week. The site crypto.news suggested Anthropic's IPO drained liquidity that would otherwise have supported bitcoin. A standing disclosure: Anthropic makes the model behind this show. The IPO raised $81.34 billion. But per crypto.news itself, the debut briefly lifted bitcoin toward $64,000, before tech stocks reversed and bitcoin went with them. If the deal were draining crypto's liquidity, bitcoin should have fallen while it popped. Instead bitcoin rose with the risk-on move, then fell with tech. That fits an asymmetry we've flagged before. Bitcoin transmits equity selloffs, but joins rallies only briefly. So the evidence points to bitcoin trading as tech beta, not losing a rotation into AI equity.

And tech beta in a falling tape puts the levels in play. Price now sits inside the $59,500 to $62,600 band that Glassnode modeled as its largest liquidation cluster. That's a model of where forced selling could concentrate, not an observed order book. The bottom of the band sits almost exactly on the June cycle low of $59,517. So a break through the band would also be a break of the cycle low.

Here's the strongest bull case the evidence supports, and it's interpretation, not a forecast. Funding is neutral to negative. That means no crowded long book is left paying to stay in. So there's little forced-selling fuel left below. And price is sitting on a zone identified in advance. A flush of whatever stops remain there could be mechanically bounded rather than cascading. What the bull case still lacks is a buyer, and nothing in hand shows one.

Now a contrast worth sitting with. While capital leaves the bitcoin wrapper, traditional managers are still building a reserve wrapper for the on-chain dollar. Invesco announced IROXX on November 17th. It's a tokenized government money-market fund, with shares recorded on Ethereum. Invesco positions it explicitly as reserve infrastructure for payment stablecoins, ahead of the GENIUS Act taking effect in January. No launch-day subscription or assets figure is available. And no stablecoin issuer has said it holds the fund in reserves. So call this capacity, not adoption.

It extends an arc this desk has tracked all month. First came a bank stablecoin consortium. Then a round-the-clock liquidity facility for BlackRock's tokenized Treasury fund, BUIDL. Now a major traditional manager is building reserve-grade rails for issuers.

Here's the second-order point, and it's our inference. If issuers park reserves in tokenized money funds instead of holding bills directly, front-end Treasury demand needn't change. What changes is the intermediary. Stablecoin redemptions would then transmit through government money-fund and repo flows, rather than through direct bill sales. That ties crypto's cash more tightly to the plumbing of the money market.

Back to bitcoin demand for one last name, the largest corporate buyer. Strategy's latest weekly filing showed it spent about six dollars buying back its STRC preferred stock for every dollar it put into bitcoin. That ratio tells you whether the biggest corporate treasury is still adding coin, or defending its capital structure. Separately, Strategy leaves MSCI's indexes at the close on November 30th. That forces passive index trackers to sell MSTR, the common stock that funds both the buybacks and any bitcoin buying. Our inference is that the corporate bid could weaken further, just as the ETF bid has turned seller.

Three things to watch into next week. First, whether ETF redemptions persist at this scale, or Thursday proves a concentrated one-off. Second, whether the $59,517 cycle low holds. Third, whether Strategy's next filing pushes that six-to-one ratio higher as the MSCI deletion lands.

The bottom line: the wrapper, not the levered book, is now setting bitcoin's marginal price. How long that lasts depends on whether one big holder is rebalancing or many are leaving.

That's the briefing. Have a good Friday.
