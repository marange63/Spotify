Good morning. It's Friday, September 4th, and this is Strategic Power. Almost every instrument of American power this week is being judged on one test: the gap between what the state asserts and what can actually be counted. Right now the counting is going badly.

Start at the chokepoint. Central Command confirmed strikes on September 1st against Iranian air-defense sites, radar systems, maritime assets and mine-laying capability. That's the news peg, and it is not the story. A strike list tells you what the United States is doing. It doesn't tell you what six months of war have done to the most important oil chokepoint on earth. For that you need a flow number. There are two of them, and they do not agree.

Here is the arithmetic, in order. Before the war, the Strait of Hormuz carried roughly 20 to 21 million barrels of oil a day. That baseline is not in dispute. Energy Secretary Chris Wright says about 9 million barrels are still moving through daily. Treasury Secretary Bessent has gone further, claiming 15 to 17 million on one recent day. Independent tanker trackers say the real figure is 7 million at best, and more likely closer to 5. Two cabinet secretaries and the data vendors the oil market actually prices off are describing different straits.

The tracker side of that case has real weaknesses, and I want to be straight about them. The freshest official figure is the Energy Information Administration's, at 4.9 million barrels a day, and that is second-quarter data. It is a full quarter stale against claims made this week. The freshest read available isn't a flow rate at all. It's a vessel count: as of August 30th, the day before the strikes, commercial transit through the strait had fallen to 7 percent of its pre-crisis norm. A ship is not a barrel. So that count corroborates the direction and severity of the collapse without settling its size.

Before you call the official numbers dishonest, give the duller explanation its due. The EIA's own chokepoint methodology splits Hormuz into two baskets: crude and condensate on one side, refined petroleum products on the other. In the first half of last year, crude and condensate alone ran about 14.7 million barrels a day, well below the all-liquids total. So if the administration is quoting every liquid that moves and the trackers are quoting crude, part of this gap closes on definitions rather than on honesty. No source ties either camp's figure to a specific basket, so treat that as plausible, not proven. And notice what it can't do. A basket argument narrows the gap. It does not close a gap this wide.

So use the tiebreaker, which is price, because a price is a claim somebody has to fund. West Texas Intermediate settled above 90 dollars a barrel for a third straight session. Brent closed over 95 for the first time since late July. The insurance market is pricing this as a live war rather than a rhetorical dispute. War-risk premiums on a Hormuz transit have been running 3 to 10 percent of hull value, against roughly a quarter of a percent before the war. Freight on the Gulf-to-China route jumped 24 percent in a single day, the steepest move of the year.

Carry the vintage on those last two figures. Both are July readings describing the standing war, not a fresh repricing after Monday's strikes. They still carry the argument, because that pricing regime has not been withdrawn. Insurers do not charge crisis premiums on a chokepoint moving normal traffic. The market is voting for the trackers' number, not the cabinet's. So the practical takeaway is this: the distance between the claim and the count has itself become a tradable input.

A credibility gap that size does not stay in the oil market. It shows up in what people buy when they stop trusting the number. On August 18th, Treasury announced it would double its long-end buyback support, to at least 4 billion dollars per operation, starting September 9th. One market strategist called the dollar size trivial against the overall market, but the signaling effect very powerful. The tape agreed with him.

Gold had capitulated to a two-week low earlier this week, apparently confirming the hawkish repricing this show recorded on Wednesday. That capitulation did not survive two days. Gold is back near 4,428 dollars an ounce, up more than 8 percent on the month. Bitcoin has moved with it. One bitcoin now buys 18.17 ounces of gold, the most since January, and the correlation between the two assets has reached 0.86, the highest reading since mid-2020.

That closes an evidence gap this beat flagged on Tuesday. It said then that a record central-bank gold quarter could say nothing about bitcoin, because there was no comparable fresh figure to test the debasement call against. There is one now, and it points the same way. Read this as one bid, not two. A pure war-risk flight would lift gold far more than it lifts bitcoin. A debasement bid lifts both together, which is exactly what that correlation describes.

Hold two caveats on it. A 90-day correlation is a lagging window, and a rally that coincides with an oil shock may not outlive the oil shock. But the reading still cuts against the crypto industry's own sales pitch. An asset marketed as an uncorrelated diversifier, moving this tightly with the oldest reserve asset on earth, is not diversifying anyone's portfolio. It is confirming the same trade. Allocators sizing it as a diversifier are mis-specified.

And the timing bites. Those expanded buybacks begin operating on September 9th, into a Brent price at the highs. One arm of the state will be leaning on long yields while an energy shock pushes headline inflation the other way. That is the cleanest fiscal-dominance test on the calendar, and it runs in public.

The American answer to a chokepoint shock is supposed to be abundance at home. Half of it is working. On molecules, it is working well. The EIA confirms US liquefied natural gas exports averaged 17.4 billion cubic feet a day in the first half of this year, 23 percent above last year, the fastest growth rate since large-scale exports began in 2016. That is gas already shipped, from terminals already operating. On a beat that has spent a week grading permits, court wins and appropriations, it is the first item to post a delivered number.

On electrons, the story inverts. Data center electricity demand has nearly doubled since 2023, to about 42 gigawatts now, on S&P Global's numbers. The queues to serve it have exploded. ERCOT's large-load interconnection queue quadrupled in a year to over 233 gigawatts, most of it data centers. PJM's reformed process drew 811 new generation projects in its first cycle alone. But a queue application is a developer's demand signal, not committed load. Of the projects that began screening by 2020, only 40 percent in ERCOT and just 24 percent in PJM ever reached an interconnection agreement or went operational.

Be careful with those completion rates. They describe a pre-2020 cohort, which is precisely the vintage PJM's reform was designed to fix, and no reformed cycle has produced a finished cohort to test it against yet. The honest claim is narrower: the historical base rate is low, and nothing has disproved it. Resist the tidy conclusion as well. An export terminal is one permitted coastal site with a foreign buyer. A grid queue is thousands of distributed projects with ratepayer politics attached. Those are different problems, not just different execution.

Still, the practical verdict holds. The abundance case is passing on exports and failing on interconnection, and the money already knows it. That gap is the best available explanation for why hyperscalers keep signing behind-the-meter power deals instead of waiting in line. This is the third independent measurement of that same bottleneck on this beat in five days, and it keeps returning the same verdict.

One lever was supposed to sit entirely within Washington's control: chip export policy. It has the same problem. The Southeast Asia loophole that let China-linked buyers acquire controlled Nvidia chips existed because license triggers were keyed to the shipping address rather than the buyer's ownership. The administration is moving to close it, though the sources don't establish whether that rule is final or still in progress. Meanwhile the Remote Access Security Act, which would extend controls to renting compute rather than buying it, passed the House 369 to 22 in January and has sat in the Senate ever since. That is the mirror image of the Senate's Russia sanctions bill, passed 86 to 11 and still stuck in the House. Two overwhelming bipartisan votes, neither one law.

There's a harder possibility underneath it. The tighter rule may be aimed at a pipe that is barely flowing. This beat reported on Monday that Nvidia's licensed sales into China came in under one percent of its data-center revenue, which suggests the binding constraint sits in Beijing's customs clearance rather than in Commerce's licensing. If that's right, closing the diversion loophole mostly buys Huawei and China's domestic foundries a larger captive market. So an export-control regime tightened by executive action, while its statutory backbone sits unpassed, looks exactly like the chokepoint claim and the interconnection queue. Authority asserted, running well ahead of effect delivered.

Watch two things over the next week. First, whether any independent September transit figure appears to settle the Hormuz dispute. Second, whether those expanded Treasury buybacks can hold the long end with Brent where it is. The August payrolls report also lands this morning, and it feeds straight into the Fed's decision on September 16th. That's Strategic Power for September 4th.
