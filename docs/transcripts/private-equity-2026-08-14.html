Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Equity & the Deal Economy

Transcript · August 14, 2026

Good morning, it's Friday, August 14th, and this is your private equity briefing.

The number to start with is a crossover, and it's the kind that only happens once. Evercore's H1 2026 Secondary Market Review, as reported, puts first-half secondary market volume at roughly 121 billion dollars, up about 19 percent year on year. Buried inside that total is the actual headline: GP-led volume, at roughly 65 billion dollars, has overtaken LP-led volume, at roughly 56 billion dollars, for the first time on record. GP-led grew 35 percent year over year. LP-led grew just 4 percent. Single-asset continuation vehicles alone hit 34 billion dollars, up 88 percent, and now make up 53 percent of all GP-led activity. This is the arc this show has been tracking all week — continuation-vehicle usage tripling, then most top sponsors using them — and today it crosses a structural line. The workaround is now the primary engine.

Here's why that distinction matters and isn't just secondaries trivia. LP-led secondaries are a stranger writing a check for a portfolio someone else built. GP-led is the sponsor selling its own best asset into a vehicle it still manages, still earns fees on, and effectively still controls on both sides of the trade. When the growth in the market is almost entirely concentrated on that side, what's expanding isn't third-party price discovery. It's sponsors transacting with themselves at scale. The buyer and the seller answer to the same investment committee. That's a regime change in how this industry manufactures liquidity, not a cyclical wrinkle.

So naturally the next question is the one that actually settles whether this is fair value or a discount dressed up as a distribution: what price are these continuation vehicles actually clearing at, relative to net asset value? And here the honest answer is that nobody can tell you, at least not for this half. A targeted follow-up dig into Evercore's own report and the secondary write-ups citing it turned up nothing quotable for H1 2026 pricing-to-NAV — for either LP-led portfolios or single-asset continuation vehicles specifically. The last time this market handed over a clean pricing number was last year, when LP portfolio stakes were clearing around 87 cents on the dollar of net asset value — the figure this show cited yesterday. That's exactly the number we cannot refresh for this half. Worse, the sources that do try to put a current number on it contradict each other for the very same period. One says single-asset vehicles are pricing near par, with some deals running above NAV. Another puts the same category at an average discount to NAV. One source flatly admits its commentary was written before real H1 2026 pricing data existed and is extrapolating. That's not a case of a hard number being hard to find. That's the industry's marketing keeping the harder number off the table entirely, and the aggregators trying to fill the gap disagreeing with each other rather than actually reporting it. And remember who is sitting on the other side of these trades — Ardian, Lexington, Coller, Blackstone's own Strategic Partners. That handful of secondaries buyers is quietly becoming the price-setter for great swaths of private-market net asset value, and they never have to publish the price they set. So the record volume headline stands, but the one figure that would tell you whether it represents genuine liquidity or capital being recycled among vehicles the sponsor controls is, again, missing. Bank that, because it's the through-line of the week.

Now, why is this happening at all, when manager earnings this quarter looked strong across the board? Because the underlying cash problem to limited partners hasn't gone away. Distributions ran at roughly 6 percent of buyout fund assets in the year to June 2025, against a ten-year average closer to 14 percent — an 8-point shortfall this show has flagged before, and it hasn't visibly cleared. Allianz Trade's own model, one house's forecast rather than a market consensus, sees a baseline 5-point improvement in 2026, landing distribution yields around 17 to 19 percent — still short of the historical norm even in the optimistic case. DPI, distributions to paid-in capital, has become the metric that actually drives limited partner re-up decisions, precisely because exits collapsed and general partners are sitting on unrealized paper gains they haven't converted to cash. Fee-related earnings keep compounding off assets under management regardless of whether any of that gets sold. The manager gets paid on the mark, not on the exit. So the incentive runs backwards from what a limited partner would want: managers have limited structural reason to force a real, market-clearing exit when they can manufacture a distribution instead. That's the causal link: the drought is still binding, and continuation vehicles are the pressure valve.

Which brings us to the hidden cost sitting underneath all of this. NAV lending — credit facilities collateralized against a fund's own portfolio value — is scaling fast as the other tool for manufacturing distributions without an actual sale. The six largest U.S. banks now carry an estimated 300 to 322 billion dollars in committed lending to private equity and private credit fund sponsors, reported figures rather than a disclosed bank total, up from roughly 10 billion dollars in 2013. That's roughly thirtyfold growth in a little over a decade. To be clear, that is a reported estimate, not a figure the banks themselves disclose — but the direction and the scale are not in dispute. The mechanics matter here: this debt sits senior to limited partners' equity in the underlying portfolio companies. So when a fund draws a NAV loan to send cash back to LPs, those same LPs are now standing behind fresh leverage on assets they used to own outright, unencumbered. It isn't visible in headline assets under management. It isn't visible in a DPI print either — a distribution funded by a NAV loan looks identical on a capital-account statement to one funded by a genuine sale. It's latent leverage stacked into the plumbing, and it would only surface as a problem if portfolio marks correct sharply and that debt gets called against collateral that's worth less than the fund believed.

Put it together and the picture is coherent, even if it isn't comfortable. The exit drought hasn't cleared this year — it's being financialized. Sponsors are selling to themselves through continuation vehicles at prices nobody outside the deal can verify, borrowing against portfolios that haven't sold to fund the distributions that do go out, and reporting all of it as evidence the liquidity machine is working. None of that is necessarily fraud or even mismanagement — fee-related earnings are real cash, and continuation vehicles do let some limited partners cash out who genuinely want to. But it means the health of this cycle can't be read off AUM growth, GP-led volume records, or fee-related earnings prints alone. Every one of those can rise while the cash actually reaching investors stays flat. The catalyst that would actually confirm or refute this read isn't another record continuation-vehicle quarter. It's a disclosed, verifiable H1 2026 pricing-to-NAV figure, or a wave of genuine third-party exits at prices that hold up against public comparables. Until one of those shows up, treat every liquidity headline in this asset class the way you'd treat a paper mark: real on the page, unproven in cash.

That's the private equity briefing for August 14th. Have a good weekend.
