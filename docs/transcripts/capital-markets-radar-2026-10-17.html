Cautious Optimism Briefings

Capital Markets Cross-Asset Radar

Transcript · October 17, 2026

Good morning, it's Saturday, October 17th, and this is your Capital Markets Radar.

Last week ended with the market going all-in on the credit-scare trade. Rates and the dollar both leaned dovish. This briefing asks whether the credit evidence actually justifies that bet. The short answer is not yet.

Start with Friday's Treasury move, because that's the new information. The hot producer-price print itself was yesterday's story. The Fed's own H.15 release shows the ten-year yield fell another 1.2 basis points on Friday, to 5.075 percent. The two-year fell three times as much, down 3.6 basis points to 4.715 percent. That's a bull steepener, and the front end is doing the work.

That answers two questions this desk left open. In Thursday's briefing, we said we lacked clean two-year data. So we couldn't tell whether the front end was staying pinned by hike risk while the long end rallied. It didn't stay pinned. It rallied harder than the long end. Then in Friday's briefing, we asked whether the long end would give back its rally once the market digested that hot wholesale-inflation print. That was the report showing producer prices up 6.3 percent from a year earlier, the biggest annual gain since 2022. Through Friday, the long end hasn't given anything back.

The dollar broke its own pattern too. Earlier in the week, the dollar index set successive eighteen-month highs. Then on Thursday, the day of that hot print, it fell 0.45 percent to 101.99, according to a market note from EBC. Hike bets eased instead of building. The so-what: the market isn't just hedging against a slower economy. It's actively fading the chance of another Fed hike.

There are two other ways to read the same tape, and both deserve airtime. One is a pre-weekend flight-to-quality bid during a global tech selloff. That's fear, not a considered view on the Fed. The other is a plain positioning unwind after a violent week. One session of H.15 data can't rule either out. And a single-day dollar dip ends a streak without making a trend.

But take the dovish read at face value for a moment, and the risk map shifts. If the curve is wrong that credit stress will stay the Fed's hand, the two-year has the most to give back. The repricing risk now sits at the front end, not the long end. There's a gentler implication too, and it's our inference. A softer dollar, if it persists, eases the global dollar-funding squeeze we flagged on Thursday as compounding the AI credit repricing.

That dovish lean runs straight into oil. Brent was priced at about $108.51 a barrel early Friday morning, according to Fortune. In Thursday's briefing, we reported Brent sliding on reports that the US might ease restrictions on some vessels carrying Russian oil. In Friday's briefing, we named an oil fade as the one thing that could let producer-price pressure ease on its own. Oil didn't fade. It's now higher than when we flagged the fade. So the inflation side of this bet just lost its easiest escape route. The so-what: the front end is leaning dovish into rising oil, which makes that lean harder to justify, not easier.

Which means the whole bet only works if the credit scare is as serious as rates are now pricing. So here's what credit itself says. On Tuesday, we couldn't verify a spread for CCC-rated paper, the bottom of the junk stack, where stress surfaces first. Now we have one, straight from the Fed's FRED database. The ICE BofA CCC-and-lower option-adjusted spread jumped from 10.76 percent on September 17th to 12.78 percent on October 15th. That's roughly 200 basis points of widening in four weeks. Seeking Alpha's coverage calls this the widest CCC spread since November 2022.

Now compare the rest of the quality stack over the identical four weeks, on the same FRED data. The broad high-yield index widened about 53 basis points. BBB-rated paper widened about 10. The broad investment-grade corporate index moved just 6. So CCC widened twenty to thirty times as much as investment grade. Strategist Lance Roberts puts it bluntly. Credit markets, he says, are "flashing yellow, not red." He adds a caution that matters here. The CCC market is small and less liquid, so problems at just one large issuer can disproportionately move the whole index.

The so-what: stress is real, but it's concentrated at the very bottom of the credit stack. Headline junk-spread moves this month may say more about a few leveraged names than about the credit cycle. The honest counter is that investment grade is always the last thing to move before contagion. Calm there isn't proof of safety. But if contagion were already underway, you'd expect more than single-digit moves in BBB and investment grade. That's not what the data shows yet.

The clearest single-name version of that stress is one we've followed all week, CoreWeave. On Thursday, we cited a 15 percent intraday yield on its bonds. Reuters now reports that on Wednesday the 14th, the yield on CoreWeave's April-issued bonds due 2031 surged past 16 percent. That's above the level that typically signals distress. It answers the first thing we said to watch in Friday's briefing. Yes, CoreWeave's credit kept widening. The stock then fell nearly 12 percent more during Friday's session, per GuruFocus, as the broader AI-infrastructure selloff continued. Part of that drop is plain sector beta, not company-specific credit news.

D.A. Davidson's Gil Luria laid out the bear case starkly. In a scenario where AI capital-spending growth slows, he said, "The equity value is going to be zero." That's his downside scenario, not his base case. The so-what: CoreWeave is the concrete shape of the stress we just saw in index terms. One heavily levered AI-infrastructure issuer breaking hard fits concentrated, bottom-of-the-stack stress. On its own, it isn't evidence that stress is spreading into higher-rated, hyperscaler-adjacent credit. And we haven't confirmed CoreWeave's rating, so treat its link to the CCC index as a rhyme, not proof.

One market behaved differently this time. Bitcoin broke below $80,000 on Thursday, falling to about $79,800. By CoinDesk's account, that break lasted all of two hours. Bitcoin opened Friday at $82,132, up 0.7 percent on the day, per Yahoo Finance. Gold, meanwhile, sat at $4,168 an ounce Friday morning, barely changed from the morning before. And despite some headlines, gold is nowhere near its January record.

Twice this week, on Tuesday and Thursday, we described bitcoin as a risk asset trading inside the duration trade, selling off alongside tech. A dip below $80,000 on hot-inflation day, followed by a fast recovery while the AI complex kept falling, is a first, tentative sign that pattern might be breaking. It's just as consistent with bitcoin simply riding the front-end rate rally. That would be the opposite of a decoupling story. The so-what: one day doesn't settle it, and our rule holds. It's noise until it repeats.

So pull it together. Rates and the dollar are priced as though a credit scare serious enough to stay the Fed's hand has already arrived. Oil just made the inflation side of that bet harder to defend. And credit itself, outside the CCC tier, isn't confirming the scare. Investment grade has barely moved. That's the mismatch heading into the Fed's October 28th decision. That meeting hasn't happened, and we're not forecasting it. Two things to watch instead. First, do higher-rated spreads start moving? That would validate what the front end is pricing. Second, does oil keep pushing producer prices higher? That would expose it. Watch the gap between those two signals, not the headline of either one.

That's the radar for today. Have a good weekend, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
