Good morning, it's Wednesday, November 4th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

On Tuesday, this desk set up one test, and AMD's third quarter was it. The setup was a memory story. Local Korean media, relayed by the Korea Herald, reported that Samsung had opened talks on HBM4 for 2027 delivery. Its reported asking price per gigabit was more than triple what it gets for current HBM3E. That compares across generations, so it isn't a like-for-like increase. Samsung hasn't confirmed it, and it's an opening bid, not a signed contract. But it sharpened one question: who pays for the memory? Our working thesis has been that the accelerator makers are the next stop for that cost. AMD was the first accelerator vendor to report since that signal surfaced. So we said the number to listen for wasn't gross margin against AMD's guide of about 56 percent. It was whatever AMD said about memory cost and supply. AMD reported last night, and we now have both answers.

Start with the headline, from AMD's earnings release. Total revenue was 13.4 billion dollars, up 45 percent year over year. Data Center segment revenue was 8.5 billion dollars, up 97 percent. That segment blends two different businesses, EPYC server CPUs and Instinct GPUs. AMD does not split them out. So treat 97 percent as evidence of a data-center business running hot, not as a GPU-specific number.

Now place the Helios rack-scale system precisely on the ladder between announcement and volume, because that's the distinction that matters here. On the call, CEO Lisa Su named five lead customers: Anthropic, Meta, Microsoft, OpenAI and Oracle. For transparency, Anthropic makes the model behind this show. In Su's words, those customers brought their first Helios racks online quickly. And they are now running their latest models and workloads on Helios. Customers running their own workloads is a real step past the engineering-sample stage. But AMD gave no unit count and no dollar figure for Helios inside that 8.5 billion. So Helios has moved from qualification into early production. What we can't say yet is that it ships at meaningful volume.

Be equally precise about which competitive dimension this print moves. It is strong evidence on customer relationships. Five of the largest compute buyers in the world were named on an earnings call as running Helios. It is also evidence that a production ramp has started. It is not evidence of architecture leadership, because AMD offered no benchmark against Nvidia's rack systems. It says nothing about software-ecosystem parity with CUDA, since nothing in what we've reviewed addresses ROCm. And it says nothing about pricing power, because no per-system price was disclosed. So AMD's share of the buyer's wallet is moving. Its moat against Nvidia hasn't been tested yet.

Which is where the real claim in this print sits: the forward guide. AMD is guiding fourth-quarter revenue to about 17 billion dollars, plus or minus 3 percent. At the midpoint, that implies roughly 66 percent year-over-year growth and 27 percent sequential growth. Su was explicit about why. She said Q4 is the first quarter where the data-center AI business crosses over server CPUs in size. And she said growth would accelerate further in the fourth quarter as AMD scales Helios. So Q3 proves Helios started. Q4 is where AMD promises the volume shows up in the numbers.

That forward framing comes with one wrinkle, and it cuts against the easy "accelerating" read. Last quarter, AMD's Data Center segment grew 107 percent year over year. This quarter, it grew 97. That's a slowdown in the annual growth rate, not a speed-up. So "accelerate" is management's forward claim about the fourth quarter. And the only number behind it is the total-revenue guide. It isn't a description of the trend so far. That matters if you're trying to separate a structural story from a cyclical one. Year-over-year comparisons get harder as the base grows. The doubling headlines will fade even if the business keeps compounding.

Now, back to the question we started with: who pays for the memory. Q3 non-GAAP gross margin was 56 percent, right on AMD's guide. CFO Jean Hu guided fourth-quarter non-GAAP gross margin to approximately 55 percent, down just one point. On its face, that is not evidence of a memory squeeze.

Did management address memory at all? Yes, but without a number. Asked about costs, Su acknowledged inflationary pressure, in her words, "given where the memory market is." She indicated AMD is working through that with customers. She attached no dollar figure and no margin point. Jean Hu, for her part, tied the 2027 margin trajectory to the mix between the data-center AI and server CPU businesses. She did not name memory as the swing factor.

So here is the honest state of the evidence. The Q3 margin and the Q4 guide show no visible squeeze yet. That partly breaks the memory-transfer thesis, and we should say so. The accelerator makers were supposed to be the next stop, and so far AMD's income statement doesn't show it. But Q3 predates any 2027 HBM4 contract repricing. And management has now named memory inflation as a live pressure. A flat margin today does not settle what happens once next year's memory contracts reset. If margin does soften, keep two explanations apart. One is mix, the explanation Hu's framing points to: AI systems growing faster than server CPUs. The other is HBM cost, the one Su's remark keeps alive.

And the Helios ramp runs straight into the supply side of that memory story. SK hynix said on its Q3 call last week that long-term agreements with fourteen key customers cover more than half of its 2027 and 2028 capacity. Counting demand still under discussion, it described DRAM and HBM volumes as effectively sold out through 2029. The same call said HBM4 should overtake HBM3E in shipments and revenue share in the fourth quarter of this year. That is the same quarter AMD says it scales Helios. Put those together. Buyers diversifying away from a single GPU vendor are not diversifying away from a small pool of memory suppliers. If AMD's 2027 memory is bought on terms like SK hynix's, that is the margin risk embedded in the Q4 acceleration. That's our judgment, not a company statement. Either way, AMD's growth adds to HBM demand. It doesn't relieve the constraint.

One more comparison, and it's context rather than news. Intel reported last Thursday, and it guided fourth-quarter revenue below its own third-quarter actual. AMD guided the other way, with the 27 percent sequential step-up from earlier. So two x86 vendors grew their data-center businesses sharply in the same quarter, then issued opposite forward guides. Resist reading this as AMD taking CPU share from Intel. Intel's Data Center and AI segment grew 78 percent, and it's CPU-centric. AMD's 97 percent blends CPUs and GPUs. So the two aren't like-for-like. Strong growth at both is better read as the whole data-center silicon pool expanding. That's our inference from two segment figures, not a measured share result. What the market is pricing is the forward guide, and these two point in opposite directions.

A short watch list before we close. Qualcomm reports fiscal fourth-quarter results later today. Nothing is known yet, so treat its data-center ambitions and chip roadmap as an open question, not a fact. On China, two pauses are due to expire. Beijing's suspension of its rare-earth export curbs runs out November 10th. Its separate suspension of the gallium, germanium and antimony export ban runs out November 27th. Unless Beijing extends them, those restrictions come back into force. Neither has changed yet, so these are calendar catalysts, not policy moves. And AMD's own fourth-quarter print is now the real Helios volume test, the one this quarter only set up.

So here's where that leaves us. AMD has moved the escape route around Nvidia from demo to deployment, with five major customers running workloads on its first rack-scale systems. Volume, and the dollars that come with it, is what the fourth quarter has to show. But every Helios rack still draws from the same tight pool of memory suppliers as everyone else. The toll on that route gets paid in HBM, not avoided by it.
