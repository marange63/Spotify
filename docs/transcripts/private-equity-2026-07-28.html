Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Equity & the Deal Economy

Transcript · July 28, 2026

Good morning, it's July 28th, and this is your private equity briefing.

For a week we've been inferring that the M&A exit channel was stuck, reading it off proxies like a flat IPO pop and a lone take-private. PitchBook's Q2 2026 US PE Breakdown, out yesterday, finally measures it. Total US private equity exit value fell roughly 46 percent quarter over quarter, to $102.6 billion. That's not a soft patch, that's a cliff.

Look inside that number and the damage is concentrated exactly where you'd expect: the channels that actually return cash to LPs. Asset sales to corporate strategics, historically the largest pool of buyers for sponsor-owned companies, fell 63 percent to $38.5 billion, with deal count down 21 percent to 107. Sponsor-to-sponsor sales fared even worse in relative terms: down 57 percent to $24.5 billion, and the deal count sank 38 percent to just 94 transactions, the lowest quarterly mark in at least a decade. So the two pipes that carry real, third-party cash out of portfolios both narrowed sharply, and the sponsor-to-sponsor pipe narrowed to a floor we haven't seen in ten years. That's the exit logjam, quantified.

Now, the counter-move headlines have been chasing all month: IPOs. PE firms took 12 US portfolio companies public in Q2, double Q1's count, totaling $27.6 billion in post-money valuation, over 40 percent more than the prior quarter. That lifted IPOs to roughly 31 percent of total exit value, nearly triple their Q1 share. Real, but treat that share cautiously. $27.6 billion is post-money valuation, not cash landed in LP accounts, and PitchBook's own framing shows it's still a minority of total exit value. A reopening concentrated in a handful of large, high-quality names doesn't rescue the long tail of 2018-to-2021 vintage buyouts still waiting for a buyer. Those are the funds now past their expected hold, carrying marks the market hasn't tested with a bid. So the practical read is that distributions-to-paid-in pressure and elongated hold periods carry straight into the back half of the year.

When genuine third-party exits contract this hard, the pressure has to go somewhere, and it's going into continuation vehicles. GP-led continuation volume hit a record $108 billion in 2025, and single-asset deals already made up $34 billion in the first half of 2026, more than half of all GP-led activity in the period. That's the pressure valve: GPs manufacturing distributions by selling assets to vehicles they themselves control, rather than to an independent third party. And this is the part worth sitting with. Distributions-to-paid-in doesn't distinguish a clean cash sale to an outside buyer from a GP moving an asset from one of its own pockets to another. So a continuation vehicle can lift the DPI line without closing the actual marks-versus-cash gap at all. It's a liquidity solution on paper more than a realization in fact, and it's worth remembering that every time a headline touts one this year.

Here's the complication that breaks the "uniform freeze" story, though. KKR disclosed, in an intra-quarter update, monetization income in excess of $900 million for the period from March 31 through June 24, roughly 80 percent realized performance income and 20 percent realized investment income. That follows a first quarter where monetization activity hit $878 million, 62 percent above the $542 million quarterly average KKR ran from 2023 through 2025. So while the market-wide aggregate exit value was falling, KKR's own book was accelerating. Be precise about what that figure is: monetization income at the manager level, KKR's realized carry and investment income, not a fund-level check written to limited partners. But even read narrowly, it tells you this isn't a market frozen everywhere. It's realized liquidity concentrating in the strongest-positioned managers. KKR reports full Q2 results this Thursday, July 30th, and that print will show whether the acceleration held into quarter-end.

That dispersion doesn't stay contained on the realization side. It feeds straight back into fundraising, and this is the second-order effect that matters for the shape of the industry. Private equity's rolling twelve-month fundraising has now declined for eight straight quarters, and the capital that is being raised is pooling at the top. Funds under $500 million now account for just 13 percent of total capital raised, down from 17 percent five years ago. Put the two together. The largest managers are the ones still returning cash, so they're the ones LPs re-up with, while the median manager, stuck in a frozen exit market with no distributions to show, struggles to raise at all. The freeze isn't uniform, and neither is the fundraising machine. Both are bifurcating toward the same handful of incumbents, and the gap compounds.

Blackstone's own Q2 print, out last week, corroborates the slowdown from inside the largest book in the industry, even as its headline numbers keep compounding. Its Private Equity segment posted distributable earnings up 31 percent year over year, and fee-related earnings kept climbing. But management guided to a sequential deceleration in net realizations for the third quarter, before an expected pickup later this year and into 2027. That guidance is the same M&A slowdown PitchBook just measured, showing up inside Blackstone's own realizations line. So the question for the whole sector's premium multiples is which kind of multiple you're actually paying for. A fee-related-earnings multiple keeps growing regardless of the exit cycle, because it rides assets under management and management fees. A carry-cycle multiple is still exit-dependent, and it just got a fresh air pocket. Those are not the same bet. AUM at record highs and fees compounding tell you the franchise is durable. They tell you nothing about whether the carry cycle has cleared. This quarter is forcing that distinction, and the market has been lazy about drawing it.

Pull it together and here's where the cycle stands. The traditional exit channel, M&A and sponsor-to-sponsor, contracted to a decade low in the second quarter, and the IPO reopening, while real, is too narrow to offset it. That keeps distribution pressure on LPs and keeps hold periods stretching. At the same time, the pressure isn't hitting every manager equally. KKR's own disclosure shows realized liquidity concentrating in the largest, best-positioned GPs, and the fundraising data shows that same concentration feeding back into who can raise the next fund. The gap between the winners and the median manager is widening on both sides of the ledger at once. The next week is the test of which story dominates. KKR reports Thursday. Apollo and Carlyle follow in the first week of August. If their realizations echo KKR's acceleration, the dispersion thesis holds. If they echo Blackstone's guided air pocket instead, the freeze is broader than this week's data suggests, and it reads across the whole asset class rather than one manager. Either way, the binding question stays the same one it's been all week: not what the marks say, but what cash actually lands with limited partners.

That's the private equity briefing for July 28th. Talk to you tomorrow.
