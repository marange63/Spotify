Bitcoin broke through its overhead supply on Monday and printed an eight-month high. That supply sits between eighty-three and eighty-six thousand dollars, where a dense cluster of long-term holders bought their coins, and this desk flagged that band on Sunday and again on Monday as the level any credible breakout would have to clear. On Monday it cleared. Bitcoin opened at eighty-one thousand, one hundred sixty-two dollars and reached an eight-month high of eighty-five thousand, one hundred thirty-four by mid-morning Eastern time. CoinDesk, reading the same session independently, had it up five point four percent over twenty-four hours. So the wall fell. The question is what knocked it down.

The evidence says leverage did the work, not fresh demand. On Coinglass data reported by CoinDesk, shorts accounted for six hundred forty-eight million dollars of seven hundred forty-seven million dollars in liquidations over twenty-four hours. That is roughly seven of every eight dollars forcibly closed. It is forced short covering. It is not spot buyers stepping in.

Now the sourcing, because on this item it matters. A second read of the same day, from an aggregator called CoinStats, carries the identical six hundred forty-eight million dollar short figure. But CoinStats puts the day's total liquidations at two hundred seventy-three million dollars, a total smaller than the short-side number in its own report. Those two cannot both be right. CoinStats is an AI-generated news digest, not a primary dashboard, and that arithmetic is exactly why we use the Coinglass figures throughout and set the CoinStats totals aside. What the two sources genuinely agree on is the short liquidation itself, and that is the one number the argument rests on.

The funding read splits the same way, and we are not going to paper over it. CoinStats puts annualized perpetual funding at eight point two percent. The Coinglass-sourced read puts it near sixty percent, about seven times higher. We lean toward Coinglass on the same provenance logic, but we cannot close a gap that wide. So take the conclusion in a form that survives either number. Open interest rose seven point six percent to one hundred fifty-six billion dollars through the liquidation, on that same Coinglass read, though that figure is described as market-wide rather than Bitcoin alone. Rising open interest through a forced unwind means traders replaced positions rather than stepping back. Leverage rotated from short to long. Spot did not absorb the overhead supply. And whichever funding number is nearer the truth, the crowded longs who inherited this move are paying a real cost simply to hold it.

This happened through a rate hike, not a cut, and that matters because it removes the easy explanation. The Federal Reserve raised its target range a quarter point to three and three-quarters to four percent on September sixteenth. It was a unanimous twelve-to-zero vote, and the first hike since July of 2023. So the simple story, that crypto rallies because policy is loosening, does not apply here. But that does not hand the win to the debasement trade either. One week built around two unusual catalysts, a squeeze and a hike, does not grade a macro relationship. It just means the market reached for a positioning explanation because the liquidity one was not available.

So if leverage did the work, who actually wrote checks? The ETF wrapper is where you would look, and the answer is: not many people, and not recently. Across the five sessions from September fourteenth through the eighteenth, US spot Bitcoin ETFs netted roughly six million dollars. That is essentially flat. And it was built from a whipsaw. A four hundred fifty million dollar outflow on the fifteenth, the worst since late June, was followed by inflows of one hundred sixty million on the seventeenth and four hundred thirty-three million on the eighteenth. That last session was carried almost entirely by two funds, Fidelity's FBTC and BlackRock's IBIT. Now the important caveat. That window ends before Monday's breakout, and flow data for the days immediately around the squeeze is not available to this briefing yet, so we will not guess at how the wrapper responded to the move itself. What we do know is that the spot vehicle was not providing a structural bid heading into it.

The other marginal buyer to check is corporate treasuries. Here the recent pattern did break. But the break strengthens the deceleration case rather than weakening it. On Sunday this desk said the corporate bid had not just slowed, it had stopped. It has restarted. Strategy, formerly MicroStrategy, added nine hundred fifty Bitcoin for roughly seventy-six million dollars in the week to September twentieth, ending a two-week pause and taking total holdings to about eight hundred forty-six thousand Bitcoin. Strive Asset Management separately bought one thousand, three hundred fifty-five Bitcoin for about one hundred eight million dollars. Both figures are secondary aggregation. No 8-K or investor-relations disclosure sits behind them, and sources differ on the exact purchase date. Now compare the size. Strategy's prior purchase, on August thirty-first, ended a ten-week pause with four thousand, six hundred three Bitcoin for three hundred seventy million dollars. This resumption is roughly a fifth of that, and it follows another multi-week gap. A buyer that returns smaller each time, and pauses longer between purchases even as price rises, looks like financing capacity constraining the bid. It does not look like a price-insensitive structural buyer reasserting itself.

The same substitution of price momentum for fundamentals shows up a layer out, in the listed equities. Coinbase stock jumped eleven and two-thirds percent on September eighteenth as Bitcoin climbed back above eighty thousand dollars. The most recent quarterly number available, from the second quarter of 2026, showed revenue of one point two two billion dollars, down eighteen and a half percent year over year. There is a real bull case here. Coinbase is trying to shift its mix toward payments and subscription revenue and away from volatile trading fees, and if that works it would eventually earn a steadier, less crypto-beta multiple. But that shift is not visible in the only quarterly figure we have. The stock is moving on Bitcoin's tape, not on evidence the mix has actually turned.

Underneath all of this sits the dollar base that every one of today's bids ultimately draws on, and it is not growing. Total stablecoin supply stood at three hundred two point eight billion dollars as of September tenth, having contracted eight tenths of a percent over the prior ninety days. Tether's USDT accounts for about a hundred eighty-three billion of that, roughly sixty percent of the market. Circle's USDC is at about seventy-four billion, roughly twenty-four percent. Together the two issuers hold ninety-four percent of major stablecoin supply. That comes from a single secondary aggregator, and a change of less than one percent is close enough to flat to sit inside measurement noise. So call it not growing rather than shrinking. That still matters for anyone modelling stablecoin adoption as an incremental source of Treasury bill demand. A flat float is a stable buyer of bills. It is not a growing one. And with ninety-four percent of it in two issuers, it is as much a counterparty question as a flow question.

Put it together. An eight-month high built on covered shorts, a fractional corporate purchase, an ETF flow window that ends before the move, and a stablecoin base that has not grown in three months is the most fragile price of the cycle, not a confirmation of it. The real test is whether spot demand actually shows up before Friday's quarterly futures expiry, the one this desk flagged on Monday as sitting directly beneath that supply band.
