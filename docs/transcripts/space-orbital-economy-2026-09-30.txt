Good morning. It's Wednesday, September 30th, and this is Cautious Optimism Briefings.

Yesterday we covered Starship's first orbital flight as a hardware story. Today the story is where the defense money is going, and it is going to the same company.

Start with Golden Dome, the Pentagon's missile-defense program. On September 25th, Bloomberg reported that SpaceX's Golden Dome-related contract awards now exceed eight billion dollars. That is more than SpaceX's entire quarterly revenue of seven point eight billion. One sourcing caveat up front. Bloomberg's article is paywalled, so these figures reach us through a secondary aggregator summarizing its reporting. They are not from Bloomberg's own text or a Defense Department contract notice. Treat them as reported, not confirmed.

The losers are the real story. York Space Systems told investors in its IPO this year that a piece of Golden Dome would fuel growth. According to Bloomberg's reporting, after SpaceX won a two-billion-dollar contract, York and a few other companies were left with deals worth just a few million dollars. York's own annual report points the same way from the other side. It calls York, quote, "a leading candidate to win" Golden Dome contracts. But it discloses no Golden Dome award and no dollar figure at all.

That forces a correction on this show. On September 25th, we said near-term Golden Dome revenue sits with the sensor and bus builders already shipping satellites. We cited York's twenty-one Tranche 1 data-transport satellites as the example. We had the layer right. The money is going to satellites, not to the interceptor prototype pool. But we had the breadth wrong. It isn't spreading across the bus builders. It's pooling at one vendor, and that vendor is also the government's dominant launch provider. Our read on the consequence: for York and the other new entrants, the equity story shifts from winning program share to subcontracting or teaming with the winner.

Now pressure-test the "SpaceX wins everything" read, in three steps. First, what's actually inside that eight billion. Follow-up reporting lets us itemize most of it. Two Space Force awards to SpaceX, announced within days of each other in late May, account for about six and a half billion. One, worth roughly four billion, is a satellite constellation to track missiles and aircraft. The other, two point two nine billion, is what the Space Force calls a Space Data Network Backbone, linking sensors, shooters and command centers. So this concentration is in satellites and the data architecture, not just launch services bundled in. The remaining billion and a half or so isn't itemized in anything we found. We also can't confirm whether Bloomberg's two-billion-dollar award is that data-network contract or a separate one.

Second, scale. Bloomberg's reporting puts the whole program at one hundred eighty-five billion dollars. York's own filing says about one hundred seventy-five billion. On either number, eight billion is under five percent. That's an early slice, not capture of the program.

Third, a competing explanation, and this is our inference, not reporting. Two large awards in one week looks less like permanent lock-in and more like the Pentagon buying speed. It is paying the one vendor with proven cadence during a sprint phase. That fits the familiar sole-source-then-compete pattern. An agency locks in the early mover to hit a schedule, then opens competition once the architecture stabilizes. So read the headline literally. Right now, defense-space revenue is concentrating faster than the vendor base is diversifying. That holds whichever explanation wins.

Concentration also changes SpaceX's own risk profile. For SPCX, government work at this scale is material. Keep in mind that awards are booked over years, so eight billion in contracts is an order book, not a quarter of recognized revenue. Still, it ties more of the business to one customer's budget cycle. That's a large, sticky revenue stream, and it's also concentration risk. There's a design question too. A proliferated architecture gets its resilience from many satellites. Having the money and much of the hardware pool at one vendor sits awkwardly with that logic. We found no on-record response from the Pentagon, the Space Force or Congress on vendor concentration. So treat it as an open question, not as reported pushback.

Why can one company win at this scale? Go back briefly to Monday's flight, which we covered in full yesterday. The number that matters today is the capacity math SpaceX is now making explicit. SpaceX says one full Starship launch of V3 Starlink satellites adds the same network capacity as twenty Falcon 9 flights of the older V2-Mini satellites. That's the company's claim, as relayed by TechCrunch. SpaceX also says each V3 satellite is designed to add about one terabit per second. That is the unit-economics logic behind underbidding on proliferated architectures, defense included. Put twenty launches' worth of capacity on one vehicle, and cost per unit of capacity should fall structurally, at least on paper.

But treat that as a claim about capacity per launch, not yet about cost per bit. Two qualifications matter. First, deployed is not in service. Orbit-raising, checkout and entry into revenue service for Monday's twenty-six satellites are still unconfirmed. Second, the twenty-to-one equivalence says nothing about the cost of a Starship launch. It only becomes a cost advantage once Starship flies often and the vehicle is reused. Monday's flight was expendable, and the ship lost one of its six Raptors after stage separation. So the moat SpaceX is building right now is cadence. The cost half of the equation is still unproven. That matters for Golden Dome as well. The Pentagon is effectively betting on a cadence advantage that hasn't yet become a demonstrated cost advantage.

The obvious check on that moat is still sitting on the ground. Blue Origin's New Glenn, the other U.S. heavy-lift vehicle with a credible path to challenge SpaceX, hasn't flown since a static-fire test exploded on the pad at Cape Canaveral on May 28th. Blue Origin traced the cause to the main oxygen valve on one of its BE-4 engines. The company says it wants New Glenn back in flight before year-end, but that's a company target, not a manifested launch. To be clear, SpaceX was winning most national-security business before that explosion. So the grounding explains part of the concentration, not all of it. Still, every month New Glenn stays down is another month with no heavy-lift alternative for national-security payloads, and that reinforces the concentration Bloomberg describes.

Which leaves the independents choosing between scale and sale. AST SpaceMobile, the direct-to-cell challenger, rose five percent to sixty-three dollars and eighty-five cents on September 29th. The reported trigger was a new change-of-control severance plan, which set off buyout speculation. Be careful with that. Boards adopt these plans routinely, and no approach, bidder or process has been disclosed. The takeover case rests on inference, not news. Separately, the secondary reporting we flagged on Sunday, that AST's forty-five-satellite commercial constellation has slipped into 2027, still has no company statement behind it. Put those together and the pattern is worth naming. If launch and procurement keep concentrating around SpaceX, a standalone challenger faces a choice. It either reaches commercial scale on its own timeline, or it becomes someone else's acquisition. Nothing is disclosed on either front. But a five percent move on a severance plan alone suggests the market is starting to price AST's strategic options, not just its build-out.

So where does that leave things? Genuinely inflecting: procurement dollars and launch capacity are concentrating around one vertically integrated company, which is also the government's fastest-moving vendor. Still a demo, not a business: Starship reuse and the cost-per-bit case behind it, the twenty-six V3 satellites entering revenue service, and any AST transaction. The open question is whether the Pentagon diversifies once real alternatives exist, or whether cadence itself becomes the barrier to entry and the concentration feeds on itself. Four things to watch. Today's FCC vote on expanding satellite spectrum, which we previewed last Friday, and which is scheduled, not yet held. The next Starship flight, and what SpaceX says about engine performance and reuse. New Glenn's actual return to flight against its year-end target. And the Golden Dome space-based interceptor test the Pentagon has targeted before year-end, which remains a target, not a confirmed date.

That's the briefing. Talk tomorrow.
