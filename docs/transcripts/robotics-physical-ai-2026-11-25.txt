Good morning, this is Cautious Optimism Briefings for Wednesday, November 25th, 2026.

This week's hardest number in physical AI came from a warehouse, not a humanoid. On Monday, this desk noted that the listed Chinese robotaxi operators were the ones giving public investors hard numbers to work with. Today Symbotic does the same for warehouse automation. It just reported a full fiscal year of GAAP profit, with an annual filing behind it. Set that against the humanoid makers, who are still quoting factory capacity instead of output. That contrast is today's spine. The hard numbers come from the companies that have to file them.

Start with the headline, from Symbotic's earnings release. Revenue for the fiscal year reached 2.8 billion dollars. On the call, management called it the first full year of GAAP profitability for the company. The swing backs that up. Symbotic went from a net loss of 57 million dollars in fiscal 2025 to a net profit of 133 million dollars this year. So this is a genuine change of sign, not a rounding change.

Now the quality of that profit. By our arithmetic, that is a net margin of under 5 percent, and the fourth quarter ran richer than the year. Gross margin in the quarter was 22.6 percent on a GAAP basis, according to the call. That is the margin profile of a systems integrator installing heavy hardware. It is not a software company. Keep that in mind whenever a humanoid pitch implies that robotics economics will look like software. Any humanoid valuation built on that assumption is pricing in far better economics than a scaled, profitable automation vendor actually earns.

The guidance tells you whether that richer fourth quarter is a step up or a plateau. For the first quarter of fiscal 2027, Symbotic guided revenue to between 805 and 825 million dollars. That's a modest step up from the fourth quarter. Adjusted EBITDA is a company non-GAAP measure. Symbotic guided it roughly flat with the fourth quarter. So revenue is guided up, while that profit measure holds. By our reading, the margin isn't stepping up again next quarter. Symbotic has reached integrator-grade profitability, and the guide doesn't yet promise operating leverage beyond it.

Backlog is the other half of the story, and it tells you where the constraint sits. Symbotic ended the fiscal year with 22.3 billion dollars of backlog. By our arithmetic, that's roughly eight years of this year's revenue. So demand is not the binding constraint. Conversion and deployment pace are. And backlog is contracted work, not revenue. Management was candid that converting the Walmart work will take, in its words, multiple years.

The direction matters as much as the level. At the end of the third quarter, Symbotic's backlog was reported at 22.5 billion dollars, per an August earnings summary. So backlog slipped slightly, even as revenue grew. That means the quarter's new commitments didn't fully replace the work Symbotic burned down. One quarter is not a trend. But for a company whose story rests on its order book, a shrinking order book is the number to watch.

That sets up a puzzle in the Walmart news. On the call, management described an expanded Walmart agreement worth roughly 7 billion dollars. It covers filling out the regional distribution centers the two companies started with. It also includes a small pilot. Symbotic is now installing an in-store e-commerce system, called SymMicro, in two Walmart stores. Here's the tension. A 7 billion dollar expansion did not show up as a jump in year-end backlog. Our inference is that either much of it isn't booked yet, or a good part of it re-papers work already counted. The disclosure doesn't settle which. That's the question to put to management next quarter.

Then there's concentration. Symbotic's chief financial officer called Walmart, quote, our largest customer. Several outside reports go further. They put Walmart at roughly 87 percent of Symbotic's fiscal 2026 revenue. This desk couldn't confirm that figure in Symbotic's own filing, so treat it as reported, not disclosed. If it's close to right, it tilts the two competing readings. The bull reading is a scalable automation model posting its first profitable year. The bear reading is mature, repeat deployments for one enormous retailer. On the evidence so far, the second reading has more support. Symbotic's profitability is real. Whether it carries over to a second large customer is unproven.

So what does that mean for the humanoids waiting to get into the warehouse? Last Friday, this desk covered GXO lining up two humanoid vendors, Agility and Apptronik, with trials gated to 2027. Those robots now face a harder comparison case. It's a purpose-built system that is already profitable at scale, with most of a decade of contracted work behind it. Our inference is that a humanoid won't win that warehouse by beating Symbotic on cost per tote. Its case has to rest on brownfield flexibility. That means redeploying across tasks, in buildings that were never designed around automation. And that raises the bar for the uptime and cycle evidence those 2027 trials will need to show.

Now the other end of the disclosure spectrum: the humanoid makers. On November 17th, Figure's chief executive, Brett Adcock, put the capacity of the company's BotQ manufacturing line at about 40,000 robots a year. He also gave a timeline for putting Figure's robots into homes. Both claims came through an industry aggregator, Humanoids Daily, not a Figure press release or filing. No home product, price, or safety certification has been shown. And the home is the hardest bar a humanoid can set itself, on safety and on liability. Figure's most recent confirmed valuation is 39 billion dollars, post-money, from a Series C of over a billion dollars. That round closed in September 2025, more than a year ago. One competing read is that a capacity claim is aimed at investors and recruits, not at customers. Either way, a factory's rated capacity is a claim about the factory, not about the product.

Tesla gives a second example of the same gap. In its third-quarter update last month, Tesla said Optimus production had started. It called the output, quote, the first production-quality bots that we have manufactured. But Tesla has not disclosed how many units it has built. Elon Musk's target is 20,000 robots a week by the end of 2027. By our arithmetic, that annualizes to roughly a million a year. Musk himself called the ramp slow and, in his words, somewhat bumpy. A 2026 humanoid tracker supplies the history. Tesla's 2025 plan called for 5,000 units, and delivered only hundreds. On Sunday, Musk said Tesla's new AI5 chip goes into Optimus first. That call, too, came without a unit count.

What about the robot brain underneath these bodies? Back to Figure for a moment. Figure's chief executive made his capacity claim after showing an updated version of Helix, the company's own robot model. Reports don't even agree on what to call that version. And none of the coverage this desk saw came with an autonomy rate, intervention data, or a word on how much was teleoperated. Beyond that, this desk found no model or data-layer release this week that changed the picture. So the binding constraint on display this week is not model capability. It is deployment and financing: built units, paying sites, and cash flow. Notice, too, that Symbotic's profit comes from purpose-built, task-specific engineering, not from a general-purpose robot brain. It's the same pattern this desk has tracked all week, from Hyundai's Atlas plans onward: capacity, not output.

That gap has a capital-markets edge. Once automation carries a GAAP profit and a multi-year contracted backlog, investors can underwrite it on cash flow. Humanoid makers are still raising money on capacity claims. Our inference is that this gap in financeability, more than any gap in model capability, is what separates the two segments today.

So here is where things stand. What's genuinely inflecting is structured automation, with a GAAP profit and a contracted backlog measured in years. Its open risk is customer concentration, not capability. What's still a demo, in the sense of unproven at scale, is humanoid output at any volume you could put in a filing. Watch three things. First, Symbotic's next backlog print. Does it resume growing, and how much of the Walmart expansion shows up in it? Second, whether Figure or Tesla ever puts a built-unit number on paper, instead of a capacity target. Third, whether Figure stands behind the BotQ figure in a statement of its own. Until then, the warehouse has the income statement, and the humanoids have the factory claims.
