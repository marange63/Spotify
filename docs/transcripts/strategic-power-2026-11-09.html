Strategic Power, Energy, and Institutional Change

Good morning. It's Monday, November 9th, 2026, and this is Strategic Power. Here's the thesis for today. Washington's levers on the real economy are getting slower and more procedural. Beijing's are working without paper at all. Start with Congress, because the election just changed the odds on the main federal tool for speeding up grid buildout.

Democrats now control both chambers, which means a Democratic Congress facing President Trump's Republican White House. Northeastern University's roundup of reporting puts Democrats at 54 Senate seats against 44 for Republicans, with seven seats flipped. Alaska and Mississippi were still uncalled, so that split isn't final. In the House, the AP had Democrats at 254 seats to the Republicans' 162 as of Sunday.

The number that actually moves this thesis is smaller and more specific. After the election, the investment firm TD Cowen cut its odds that the bipartisan permitting-reform bill succeeds, from about 40 percent to about 25 percent, according to Reuters. That bill is arguably the biggest federal lever for speeding up the transmission and grid buildout that AI data centers need. Divided government just made it less likely to pass.

But gridlock cuts both ways. Analysts expect this Congress to deliver, in their words, a check on further rollbacks rather than new pro-clean-energy law. So the same arithmetic that dims new permitting law also makes the existing clean-energy tax credits harder to unwind. That's relative support for solar, storage, and independent power producers. It's measured against a repeal scenario, though, not against any new tailwind.

The market's first reaction is a warning against over-reading the result. On Wednesday the 4th, gold rose as much as 3.6 percent, its biggest move since mid-August, as the dollar and Treasury yields fell. Bitcoin rallied too. But by Thursday, bitcoin had given back that whole rebound, sliding to roughly 75,650 dollars. So treat the post-election hard-money bid as a one-day read on an uncalled Senate map, not a verdict on the dollar. The durable change is in Congress. New permitting law got less likely, and so did repeal of the existing credits.

If Congress isn't going to rewrite the rules anytime soon, the queue and the regulator end up doing the sorting. Start with the queue. Oncor, the Texas transmission utility, reported about 293 gigawatts of data-center requests in its transmission queue as of September 30th. For scale, that one utility's queue is more than three times the entire Texas grid's record peak demand. Oncor itself calls the figure a planning signal, not a forecast of what will be built.

That fits a pattern. Governor Abbott ordered an audit of ERCOT's statewide queue. PJM's utilities cut their own near-term large-load forecasts. Now a Texas wires company is disclaiming its own number. Announced gigawatts keep getting discounted the moment someone checks them.

The next concrete catalyst is regulatory, not legislative. All six regional transmission organizations owe FERC their responses on load-side interconnection reform by November 16th. The Southwest Power Pool's response is due November 20th. With the legislative path at roughly one in four, that docket is now the live federal lever on how fast new data-center power gets connected.

Keep it in proportion, though. These are responses in a FERC proceeding, not final rules, and FERC still has to act on them. Regulatory change moves faster than a bill, but it's also easier to reverse. Meanwhile, siting keeps routing around Washington, through state deals, queue audits, and behind-the-meter gas. The gigawatts that matter aren't the ones sitting in a queue. They're the ones that survive that docket.

That's how Washington pulls its levers: slowly, on paper, with a comment period attached. Beijing pulls its own differently. China's one-year pause on the expanded rare-earth export controls it announced in October 2025 runs until November 10th, under Announcement No. 70. That's tomorrow, and it's the real test.

Here's what rides on it. If Beijing quietly extends the pause, the urgency behind non-Chinese supply chains eases. Capital flowing into alternatives could slow. If the controls snap back instead, the biggest exposure is magnet supply. The likely result is a renewed spike in magnet-input prices, felt first by the defense supply chains that depend on those magnets. And Beijing has a middle path. It could let the pause lapse and run licensing case by case, keeping the leverage without producing an escalation headline.

A snap-back sounds like a windfall for American producers. Here's the catch. Back on June 22nd, Beijing's Ministry of Commerce put ten US companies on its export-control list. Two of them were MP Materials and USA Rare Earth, the federally backed firms furthest along in building an American mine-to-magnet chain. So the flagship winners of a snap-back are already inside Beijing's targeting. Federal backing hasn't turned into de-risked execution.

Now the newest American move. The Army has offered a company called REalloys a non-binding term sheet for a lease of up to 75 years at Tooele Army Depot in Utah, for a heavy rare-earth facility. The Army keeps the land. REalloys carries the development, the financing, and the operation. No financing package or construction timeline is attached in the reporting.

That structure puts the capital and the execution risk on a private developer, while the state contributes land and a signature. It's a template likely to produce announcements faster than magnets. So whichever way Beijing moves tomorrow, Washington's answer, for now, is still a lease rather than a mine.

Beijing's other undeclared lever this week was at sea. Taiwan's National Security Council reports more than 100 PLA Navy and China Coast Guard vessels operating across the region. Beijing has not announced any large-scale drill. A joint assessment from AEI and the Institute for the Study of War, dated November 6th, reads it sharply. It says the scale of these deployments suggests the PLA is conducting a major exercise that simulates isolating Taiwan. It places forces from the Eastern, Northern, and Southern Theater Commands in the activity. And it judges the exercise greater than the PLA's Justice Mission 2025 drill. Still, AEI and ISW frame this as an assessment, not a confirmed fact.

That qualifies the read we gave yesterday. We called the activity steady background pressure, based on Taiwan's defense ministry counts near the island over twenty-four hours. Today's council figure is region-wide. The two measure different things, so this isn't a jump from ten ships to a hundred. But background pressure now looks too mild a label for the full picture. It needs qualifying, not reversing, until intent is evidenced.

There's a calmer interpretation too. Taiwanese analyst Su Tzu-yun, as paraphrased by the Taipei Times, describes a quiet exercise: sustained gray-zone pressure without a formal announcement. A separate argument cuts across both readings. Experts cited by the Taipei Times note that after Xi Jinping's military purge, the only members left in the PLA's high command are Xi and General Zhang Shengmin. In their view, that has reduced the risk of a planned war against Taiwan. But it has increased the danger of a conflict sparked by miscalculation or accident. Their reasoning is blunt: amateurs and sycophants are far more likely to make missteps that inadvertently lead to war.

For capital allocation, sustained undeclared pressure keeps diversifying chip supply chains away from Taiwan a live theme, whether or not this exercise is ever confirmed. Undeclared pressure is cheap for China to sustain and expensive for everyone else to price.

One last lever, and it's the one actually removing physical capacity. It isn't Washington's paper or Beijing's discretion. It's Kyiv's long-range strike campaign against Russian refineries. Ukrainian reporting puts the drop in refining volumes at the halted Russian plants at 55.4 percent since the start of the year. That's an interested-party figure, with no neutral corroboration. But if the squeeze holds into winter, it can tighten global diesel and distillate markets without a single new sanctions notice.

Watch three things this week. First, whatever Beijing publishes, or doesn't, on the rare-earth pause starting tomorrow, and whether licensing quietly turns case by case. Second, any PLA or Chinese defense ministry statement on the vessel buildup around Taiwan, plus Taiwan's next counts. Third, the RTO filings due at FERC on November 16th and 20th. One calendar note: October's CPI print is due tomorrow morning. We won't guess at the number. But it lands alongside the rare-earth date, and it will shape how much room the Fed has either way.

That's Strategic Power for November 9th. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
