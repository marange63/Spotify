Good morning, it's Friday, August 21st, and this is your private credit briefing.

For four straight days this show has tracked one side of the wealth channel — the redemption side, the gates going up one fund at a time, at Blue Owl, Blackstone, Cliffwater and Golub. Today we finally have the other half of the ledger. It's the sales side, and it's worse than the queues implied.

The second-quarter tally of non-traded BDC fundraising is out — Stanger's data, as relayed by the non-listed BDC trade press — and it shows gross sales collapsing, not just redemptions rising. Publicly registered non-listed BDCs raised two billion dollars in the second quarter. That's an eighty-two percent decline year over year, and the weakest quarterly total since the fourth quarter of 2020 — before Blackstone's BCRED and Blue Owl's OCIC existed as fundraising vehicles at all. For the first half, the figure is seven point one billion dollars, against twenty-three and a half billion over the same stretch last year. PIMCO's Christian Stracke, speaking to Bloomberg this week, put the mechanism plainly: wealth distributors, in his words, do not want to and cannot sell the direct lending private credit retail vehicles any longer. Discount that appropriately. Stracke competes for the same allocator dollar and pitches alternatives to direct lending in the same breath, so attribute the claim to him, not to the market. The fundraising print doesn't need his framing to matter.

Here's why this lands harder than another gate headline. Net flow is what showed up in the redemption stories all week. Gross sales are what fund new origination. A wrapper can be net-negative on flows and still write new loans, as long as fresh money keeps arriving at the front door. An eighty-two percent collapse in that inflow is what constrains new lending capacity from here.

So follow that forward, because neither branch is neutral. If the retail wrapper stays anywhere near a two-billion-dollar quarterly run-rate, direct lenders either slow originations into an approaching maturity wall, or they fund the same pipeline off insurance balance sheets and CLO liabilities instead. Either path should, at the margin, widen spreads on new deals — which would finally restore some of the illiquidity premium this show sized on Tuesday at under a hundred basis points over syndicated loans, down from more than three hundred on the 2017 and 2018 vintages. And there's a second consequence that's easy to miss. The industry's biggest structural prize right now is the 401(k) and defined-contribution channel, and the regulatory door to it has been swinging open. But rulemaking was never the binding constraint. The distributor relationship is, and that's what broke this quarter.

So is the wealth channel wrong to be walking away? The asset side gives a partial answer. Fitch's trailing twelve-month private credit default rate now sits at roughly six point one percent through July. That's a fresh record, and more than double the high-yield bond default rate. It's also up from the six percent through April that this briefing led with on Tuesday, and Fitch counted seventeen unique private-credit defaulters in July alone. Notice the shape of that move, though. Going from six point zero to six point one over a quarter is a grind, not a lurch. The record is real; the rate of change is decelerating. The faster series sits underneath it — the median non-accrual ratio across the twenty largest BDCs went from two percent to two point eight percent in a single quarter, the highest reading since 2017. Golub Capital's co-CEO summarized the state of play about as honestly as anyone on that side of the table has: we are clearly in a credit cycle, it's not a particularly bad one, but there will be winners and losers.

And here is where it gets genuinely strange, because the instruments measuring this book do not agree with each other. Kroll's Direct Lending Index puts the trailing twelve-month default rate at one point five percent, over a broadly comparable window in which Fitch reports six point one. That is a four-and-a-half-point spread between two rating agencies looking at the same asset class. Neither agency's methodology note is in front of me, so I'm not going to reconcile them for you. The gap is definitional, not credit.

Now layer the payment-in-kind data on top, and it cuts against the easy story. Loans that closed with no PIK feature but carry one today — sponsors quietly switching stressed borrowers from cash pay to accrual — run at roughly five point nine percent of all loans. That's about one loan in seventeen, and market participants call it a shadow default rate. It fits the flattered-default-rate thesis this show has run all week. What doesn't fit is the direction. That bad-PIK share was six point four percent in the fourth quarter of last year. It is falling while the headline default print sets records. The aggregate PIK data points the same way: across the largest exchange-traded BDCs, PIK income slipped to eight point two percent of total interest income in the first quarter, from eight point six the quarter before. That figure is a quarter stale and sits on a different income base, so hold it loosely. But the direction is consistent, and it's the opposite of what a pure stress-hiding story would predict. Either amend-and-extend capacity is being used up, or some borrowers genuinely went back to paying cash. The data cannot tell you which, and pretending otherwise would be the exact marketing-versus-data failure this briefing exists to call out. Bank this instead: when the dispersion between two measurements of the same thing is wider than the thing being measured, the binding risk in this asset class isn't the default rate. It's definitional and valuation opacity.

Which brings the real question. If retail is walking away from a book nobody can price consistently, who is left holding it? The Bank of Canada answered part of that on Tuesday, in its Financial Stability Report — the first regulator to publish a hard exposure map for this asset class. Canadian investors and banks carry about five hundred billion Canadian dollars of private credit exposure, roughly three hundred and sixty billion US, most of it to American funds. Take that aggregate as a scale measure, not a risk measure. It bundles direct fund positions, pension allocations, and short-dated lending to the managers themselves, and the Bank does not break out the mix. The figure inside it that actually carries weight is the insurers. Canada's three largest life insurers held just over two hundred billion Canadian dollars of private credit in the first quarter of this year — twenty-two percent of their invested assets. Canadian banks separately had at least forty billion Canadian dollars of loans outstanding to the asset managers running these funds, about one percent of overall bank lending. Those are first-quarter snapshots, not live positions.

That insurer number is the load-bearing one, because annuity-funded, duration-matched insurance capital cannot gate. It has no redemption queue to signal distress the way the wealth channel just did. If losses build in that book, they don't arrive as a proration notice. They arrive later, in marks and reserve assumptions, on balance sheets that don't report daily. So the retail retreat doesn't shrink this asset class. It changes the identity of the lender, shifting weight toward insurance and institutional capital, and toward the handful of managers with an insurance affiliate behind them. That's a durable structural advantage for those firms, and a structural blind spot for the system, because the crude early-warning mechanism retail investors just demonstrated has no equivalent on an insurer's balance sheet.

So where does the cycle actually stand? Not cracked, but not benign either. The default data is record-setting and real, even if it's grinding rather than lurching. The biggest unresolved risk isn't a number. It's that two credible measures of default in this asset class disagree by more than four points, and the capital increasingly absorbing that risk has no mechanism to reveal stress before it is already booked as a loss. Watch three things from here. Cliffwater's third-quarter repurchase window, which closes August 31st. The third-quarter gross-sales prints for the non-traded BDCs, which tell you whether two billion dollars was a shock or a new run-rate. And whether other central banks follow the Bank of Canada into publishing their own exposure maps — because in an asset class this opaque, that is how repricing actually happens. Through disclosure, before default.

That's the briefing. Have a good weekend.
