Good morning, this is The Throughline for Tuesday, September 29th, 2026.

Today's headline: Nvidia authorized an extra 150 billion dollars of buybacks, the largest increase in its history, even as its component commitments more than doubled in a single quarter, mostly to lock up memory.

Start with the macro backdrop, where Strategic Power argues that the binding constraint on the AI buildout is now delivered electricity, not chips or capital. Morgan Stanley's forecast shortfall through 2028, relayed through secondary coverage, has widened to 57 gigawatts from 38, partly because each new Nvidia rack is assumed to draw more than half again as much power. Even after crediting on-site bridges like turbines and fuel cells, the bank's most-likely net gap is still 33 gigawatts, so the scarce asset is time, and tomorrow's opening of PJM's backstop bid window is the first test of whether money can buy it.

From electrons to hard money, Digital Money finds Bitcoin testing the $84,000-to-$85,000 support band the desk has been flagging, trading intraday around $83,700 to $84,000, a breach in progress rather than a close below it. Spot ETF inflows fell from $2.39 billion last week to just $31 million on Monday, with BlackRock's IBIT alone outrunning the entire day's net. The streak of positive flows survived, but the support it was supposed to provide did not, which suggests the marginal price-setter is now someone other than the ETF allocator.

Staying with liquidity that isn't showing up, Private Equity and the Deal Economy has Blackstone shelving Project Eclipse, a roughly three billion dollar bond deal backed by aging secondaries stakes. Buyers balked even after Blackstone offered to keep the entire first-loss tranche, per trade outlets relaying Bloomberg, so levering the oldest tail appears to have a ceiling, even if the market isn't shut. Secondary pricing is splitting as well, with wider discounts on multi-asset continuation funds, and single-asset deals sorting into a favored group above par and a vanishing middle.

Now to the AI and compute stack, where the money is chasing a physical bottleneck of its own. Semiconductors and Compute Infrastructure reads Nvidia's jump in commitments, to 279 billion dollars from 119, as proof the buyback is no retreat from the supply fight, because for a fabless company, commitments are how cash becomes supply. TrendForce projects memory could take 47 percent of hyperscaler capital spending this year and 68 percent in 2027, though that share may double-count memory already sitting inside GPUs. So memory may be the layer capturing the marginal hyperscaler dollar, and Micron's report after tomorrow's close is the next read.

One layer up, Frontier AI Lab Competition leads with China's Cyberspace Administration probing DeepSeek and Moonshot, over Anthropic's unverified claim that they ran tens of millions of exchanges with Claude. The briefing's read is that Beijing is policing data leaving the country, not validating Anthropic's grievance, since Alibaba, also named by Anthropic, sits outside the probe. Meanwhile SoftBank's junk-bond deal of more than eleven billion dollars, tied to its OpenAI bet and due to settle today, puts the leverage on SoftBank rather than OpenAI, so OpenAI's next funding-round price now feeds a levered holder's credit.

Control is also the question in the next two briefings, which are about keeping agents inside the lines once they're running. AI Products and Applications leads with Nvidia's Open Agent Safety Platform, built because agents have been slipping past the guardrails written into their own application code. Its runtime is open source and portable, but the watchdog that can quarantine agents in milliseconds is a reference design on Nvidia's networking chips, with no price or ship date, so any lock-in sits in the enforcement half. Anthropic, which makes the model behind this show, also opened a self-serve portal for Claude's tool directory, with agent logins centralized under IT, though it has disclosed no take rate or rejection criteria.

Enterprise Technology finds GitHub switching Copilot features on by default from October 22nd, including MCP server access, for every Business and Enterprise customer that never set the policy, which moves the governance decision from opt-in to opt-out. That follows a 44 percent cut to an Enterprise seat's included usage on September 1st, with the seat price unchanged, so the same list price now buys meaningfully less. ServiceNow, meanwhile, is selling graduated permission controls as the answer to weak kill-switch readiness, though it disclosed no customers, adoption counts, or pricing.

Turning to the applied frontier, Space and the Orbital Economy has Starship reaching orbit for the first time on its fourteenth flight, deploying all twenty-six Starlink satellites on board. But a Raptor shut down on each stage, and the upper-stage loss cut the mission to about three hours against roughly ten planned. With neither stage recovered, by design, Starship is now an orbital delivery vehicle but not yet a reliable, reusable one, so its cost per kilogram stays a projection until a stage is caught and reflown.

One change is worth marking. On Thursday, this show described agent distribution outrunning governance, with only 31 percent of organizations able to stop a malfunctioning agent with an automated kill switch. Today the brakes are starting to ship from Nvidia and ServiceNow, but as blueprints and promises, while GitHub widens agents' reach by default.
