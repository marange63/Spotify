Good morning, it's Wednesday, August 5th, 2026, and this is your Strategic Power briefing.

We start in Texas, where the state with the friendliest data-center permitting environment in the country just installed a chokepoint. On August 3rd, Governor Greg Abbott directed the Public Utility Commission of Texas and ERCOT to audit every data-center project moving through the grid's interconnection process. The directive itself is precise about the mechanism: the PUCT and ERCOT must complete this audit before any data-center project moves forward, and any project that fails to comply will be denied connection to the grid. So this is not a blanket construction moratorium — it's a verify-before-advance gate, with grid-access denial as the penalty for noncompliance. But it already has teeth. ERCOT responded within a day by pausing its "batch zero" transmission planning study, the very process that was clearing the first tranche of large-load interconnection requests. So whatever the legal label, the practical effect for that first batch is a freeze right now.

The number that prompted this is the real story. ERCOT is tracking roughly 474 gigawatts of large-load connection requests, about 90 percent of it tied to data centers. Compare that to the grid's own record: a preliminary peak of 91,089 megawatts set on July 22nd. The queue, in other words, is running at more than five times actual peak demand. Treat that ratio carefully — a 474-gigawatt queue is tracked requests, not a construction forecast, and interconnection queues everywhere are notoriously padded with redundant, speculative filings. An audit that culls phantom megawatts isn't the same as 474 gigawatts of real capacity being turned away. So Abbott's order reads less as a war on data centers and more as the state forcing a reckoning on how much of its own celebrated pipeline is actually real — the announcement-versus-asset gap this show has been flagging for weeks, now written into a permitting requirement.

Two open questions matter for how far this goes, and neither is resolved in the public record. There's no stated deadline for the audit — reporting from the Texas Tribune says plainly it's unclear how long this will take. And it's unclear whether the gate reaches projects already under construction or energized, or only those still sitting in the queue; even contemporaneous Texas reporting flags that ambiguity as unresolved. Watch for PUCT or ERCOT to clarify scope in the coming weeks, or for an industry legal challenge aimed at exactly that gap.

If Texas — the easiest state to build in — is now gatekeeping, other large data-center hosts, Virginia, Georgia, Arizona, may feel political cover to follow, which raises siting and timeline risk industry-wide. And the escape valve for hyperscalers facing a slower on-grid path is off-grid, behind-the-meter gas generation, a trend this show has already tracked. That doesn't ease aggregate power demand — it just relocates who procures the baseload and how visibly. So the grid fight doesn't disappear; it moves off ERCOT's books.

Now the other end of the energy stack moved too. One day after Iran's foreign ministry flatly denied that any US-Iran talks were even happening, the diplomatic picture on the Strait of Hormuz shifted materially. Secretary of State Marco Rubio said progress had been made in Oman-mediated talks over reopening the strait. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent went further, saying an agreement could materialize as early as today or tomorrow. And Iran's own foreign ministry spokesman, Esmaeil Baghaei, confirmed the Oman talks were positive and ongoing — but he also named what's still unresolved: the mechanism for reopening, maritime service fees, and security arrangements.

That's a real, dated shift — from a contested claim to converging-but-unsigned positions — and markets should price it as a narrowing risk band, not a closed deal. Be careful with the optimism, though: "today or tomorrow" is a US cabinet official's characterization, not a bilaterally agreed deadline, and there's no confirmation of how Tehran itself reacted to that framing. The named sticking points also matter for the medium term — fees and security arrangements suggest that even a finalized corridor could carry higher transit costs than pre-conflict Hormuz passage, a structural tax on Gulf-origin energy that survives the de-escalation headline. One line on the macro read-through: this eases one of the two inflation drivers the Fed named at its July 29th meeting, marginally reinforcing the case against a September hike, but that's a footnote to today's story, not the headline.

From one contested chokepoint to another — the same finite stockpile now being fought over on two different battlefields. An AFP analysis of Ukrainian air force data found Russia fired 376 missiles at Ukraine in July, more than double June's total and a new wartime record. Ballistic missile use is the sharpest edge of that: roughly 126 ballistic missiles in July alone, against an estimated 200 to 300 for the whole of 2024. Ukraine intercepted under a third of what was fired. That's happening exactly as US enthusiasm for backfilling Ukraine's air defenses wavers — President Trump had floated letting Ukraine produce Patriot interceptors domestically, but has since pulled back, saying on August 1st that Washington needs to be very careful about that decision.

The connection analysts are drawing, and it's an analytical read rather than a confirmed Pentagon allocation statement, is that the Iran conflict is competing with Ukraine for the same US-made interceptor supply chain. Treat that as the live hypothesis, not established fact. But even as a hypothesis, it's the sharpest test yet of whether US defense-industrial capacity can actually supply two high-intensity theaters simultaneously, or whether one gets rationed while the other gets priority.

That same missile-saturation threat is exactly why the Pentagon is racing to put more sensors in orbit. The Space Force nearly tripled the contract ceiling for its Lane 1 commercial launch program, from 5.6 billion dollars to 17 billion, on July 17th. Two weeks later, SpaceX won 1.6 billion dollars in task orders for 18 Falcon 9 launches from Vandenberg through the end of 2027 — the largest single order publicly disclosed under that program to date — carrying satellites for missile detection, tracking, and near-real-time targeting data relay. Don't stack that number on top of SpaceX's other 2026 defense awards; several of those may overlap with adjacent Starshield contracts, so the clean figures to hold onto are the 1.6 billion order and the tripled ceiling. The structural signal is what matters: commercial launch procurement is now the Pentagon's primary vehicle for fielding military space infrastructure fast, and SpaceX's early, largest capture of that flow underscores a genuine trade-off the Pentagon is accepting — speed against single-vendor dependency.

Pull the thread across all four stories and the pattern is the same: power is being rationed at its chokepoints, and in every case the pledged pipeline is running ahead of the delivered asset — a 474-gigawatt grid queue five times real demand, a Gulf corridor that's talked-about but unsigned, and an interceptor stockpile being asked to cover two wars at once. Watch three things in the coming days: the verbatim scope of Abbott's directive once PUCT clarifies it, whether Hormuz produces a signed text or just another optimistic statement, and whether the White House actually greenlights Ukrainian Patriot production.
