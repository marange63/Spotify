Good morning. It's Thursday, October 29th, 2026.

Here's the thesis for today. Washington just started financing the scarcest input in the AI economy, firm power. It did so in the same week that private capital for that power got meaningfully more expensive. Watch how those two facts collide.

Start with the lead. On Tuesday, the Department of Energy's Office of Energy Dominance Financing offered Constellation Energy a conditional loan commitment of up to five billion dollars. The money would fund uprates, meaning incremental output added to existing reactors. There are eight plants in all: six in Illinois, one in Maryland, and one in Pennsylvania. DOE puts the added capacity at nearly 1.1 gigawatts.

This deal fits one pattern and complicates another. DOE already closed a one-point-nine-billion-dollar loan in September to restart Iowa's Duane Arnold plant. That is the template for federally financed megawatts. Meanwhile, the big hyperscaler nuclear deals have mostly bought output from reactors that already exist, rather than building anything new. Uprates are a third category, sitting between existing iron and new build. They are real incremental megawatts, bolted onto old reactors, and quicker to add than a new plant. But these particular megawatts are financed by Washington and largely pre-sold before the loan even closes.

Here's the catch. According to American Nuclear Society trade coverage, five of those eight plants were also named in Google's October 6th power-purchase agreement with Constellation. The Maryland plant, Calvert Cliffs, is the focal point of a separate Amazon deal. Then there's Constellation's own release. It says the Google and Amazon deals "incentivized Constellation to invest more than 7 billion dollars to add approximately 1,080 megawatts." That is nearly identical to DOE's "nearly 1.1 gigawatts." Nobody has published a megawatt-by-megawatt reconciliation. But the plant overlap and the matching totals point the same way. This is very likely the same capacity the hyperscalers already claimed, not a separate pool of new power. TechCrunch puts it plainly: most or all of the added power from seven of the eight facilities has already been bought by Google, Meta, or Amazon.

So who carries the risk? The federal balance sheet takes on construction and financing risk underneath a conditional loan. The incremental output goes to the hyperscalers. Socialized risk, privatized offtake. The counter-read is legitimate too. A federal loan that lowers Constellation's cost of capital gets grid capacity built faster, whoever ends up buying it. Both things are true. But don't mistake this for energy abundance arriving. It is public capital de-risking power that was already sold.

There's a quieter implication, and this is inference. DOE's recovery on these loans ultimately leans on hyperscaler offtake contracts. That ties public credit risk to the durability of AI capex. The winners are the incumbents. Owners of existing fleets with uprate headroom, Constellation first among them, now get a federal financing channel stacked on contracted revenue. Greenfield developers get neither. Whoever already holds the interconnection holds the scarce asset.

And keep the stage straight. Constellation itself says the commitment "is not a final investment decision on any individual uprate." Neither DOE nor Constellation has published an in-service date. This is a conditional loan, not a plant under construction.

Now scale it against the actual hole. PJM, the country's largest grid operator, ran its auction for the 2028-29 delivery year back in July. It cleared at the regulatory price ceiling and still came up 6,831 megawatts short of its reliability target. That's the third straight year it has missed. Without the cap, the clearing price would have been seventy percent higher.

Two qualifiers apply before you net anything against that gap. DOE frames these uprates as serving "the PJM and MISO regions," so not all of the 1.1 gigawatts necessarily counts toward PJM's shortfall. And with no in-service date, nobody can say the power arrives in time for 2028. Zoom out further. DOE's broader uprate program has identified about 4.7 gigawatts of potential additions nationally, toward a 5 gigawatt target. Even that entire national pipeline is smaller than PJM's single-year gap. The federal government is financing real, incremental power. It is not, on its own, closing the gap that AI load is opening.

Now the handoff. Federal loans matter more when private capital gets expensive, and on Wednesday it did. The Fed decision came in hawkish. The Fed held its target range at three-seventy-five to four percent. But two members, Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack, dissented in favor of a quarter-point hike. That's a hawkish dissent, not a dovish one. The committee's own words: "Inflation remains elevated."

The bond market heard it. The ten-year Treasury yield rose to about five-oh-four percent intraday. Yet the dollar index slipped about two-tenths of a percent. My inference is that long-end yields are rising on term premium and supply, not on dollar strength.

Here's why that matters for the build. Every long-dated dollar that nuclear, grid, and data-center projects need just got pricier to raise in private markets. DOE hasn't disclosed the rate or tenor on the Constellation loan. But any financing channel insulated from that move is now relatively more valuable than it was a week ago. Projects that can tap a federal or hyperscaler balance sheet pull further ahead of projects that depend on capital markets. The state isn't just subsidizing megawatts anymore. It's subsidizing the capital stack itself.

Stay on rates for the hard-money angle. On Tuesday, the Treasury fully filled a six-billion-dollar buyback of twenty-to-thirty-year bonds. After August's buyback, bitcoin rallied. This time, bitcoin fell below eighty thousand dollars instead. One episode isn't proof of a regime change. But the fair inference is that the debasement trade tracks the direction of real rates, not the mechanics of Treasury operations. If that holds, the hard-money narrative needs a better story than "Treasury buys bonds, bitcoin rallies."

Fuel prices are one reason inflation stays elevated, and that leads straight to Russia. Washington has made two separate moves here, not one. The first is a Treasury license easing sanctions on Russian diesel, running through April 7th. It's part of an arrangement with Moscow meant to bring down fuel prices ahead of the midterms. The second is an executive order Trump signed on October 16th. It waived new Russia sanctions and tariffs under a new sanctions law before they took effect, while leaving existing sanctions in place. The length of that second waiver is disputed. CNN reports it has no fixed expiry, while other reporting describes a sixty-day review. We called it a sixty-day waiver earlier this week, and that should be treated as unsettled.

Europe went the opposite direction. The EU reportedly adopted roughly a thousand new sanctions listings against Russia's military-industrial complex. One source cited by Al Jazeera calls it the largest expansion of the list since the war began. And according to reporting this week, Ukraine resumed strikes on Russian oil refineries after a brief pause. So the two sides of the Atlantic are now running opposite Russia policies at once. Washington is easing for pump-price relief, while Brussels is tightening. My read is that every waiver bought for pump prices erodes the credibility of the joint sanctions toolkit, including against other adversaries. It's a live test of whether sanctions survive domestic politics in the country enforcing them.

Power and capital aren't the only strategic inputs with a clock on them. Rare earths have one too. Beijing's one-year pause on its broader export-control package is due to lapse around November 10th. That's twelve days from today, and Beijing has published no extension. The trade data doesn't settle which way it goes. China's rare-earth exports fell nine-point-six percent over January through September. Yet September alone rose six-point-seven percent year on year. One month of growth isn't a signal of loosening. It could just as easily be buyers front-loading orders ahead of the deadline. The nine-month decline is the structural number to hold. And licensing on seven medium and heavy rare earths, including dysprosium and terbium, has stayed in force since April 2025 regardless of the pause.

So who's exposed if November 10th passes in silence? The magnet-dependent chain: EV makers, wind turbines, robotics, and defense primes building precision munitions. For those buyers, a lapse is a sharper near-term risk than another round of chip export rules. It's the same lesson as the power story. Whoever controls the scarce input sets the terms, whatever the announcements say.

Pull it together. The federal government is now underwriting the capital for the power the AI buildout needs, just as the Fed made that capital more expensive everywhere else. The megawatts it's financing are largely spoken for already. And ownership of existing, interconnected generation remains the scarce asset worth having. Watch three things. Constellation's financial close, or a first in-service date, on these uprates. Whether the ten-year holds near five percent, because that sets the price of every privately financed megawatt. And whether November 10th passes with or without a word from Beijing.
