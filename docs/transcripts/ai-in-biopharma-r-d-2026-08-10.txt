Good morning, it's Monday, August 10th, and this is your briefing on artificial intelligence in biopharma R&D.

We've argued three days running on this beat that the defensible AI value in drug development is migrating away from originating the molecule and toward the data and operations layer underneath it. Today a name finally proves it with a profit-and-loss statement instead of a pitch deck. Tempus AI reported second-quarter results on July 30th, and the headline is not just growth, it's a business that actually turned the corner. Total revenue came in at $382.5 million, up 22% year over year. The segment that matters most for this thesis, Data and Applications — Tempus's real-world-data and modeling business — generated $93.2 million, up 28% year over year, so the data layer is growing faster than the company as a whole. And on profitability, Tempus swung from an Adjusted EBITDA loss of $5.6 million a year ago to a profit of $8.0 million this quarter. That is a genuine inflection, not a rounding error, but it's also thin: $8.0 million of Adjusted EBITDA on $382.5 million of revenue is a low-single-digit margin. And the reported net income of $5.6 million — against a $42.8 million loss a year ago — is barely worth reading as earnings, because it swings on large non-cash items. It includes a $98.5 million unrealized gain on marketable securities that inflates it, set against $55.6 million of stock-compensation expense, so the bottom line tells you very little about cash earnings power. Call this the start of an earnings story, not proof of durable earnings power.

The more interesting data point for this beat is what Tempus shipped, not just what it earned. The company said it delivered its first oncology foundation model to AstraZeneca this quarter — a bespoke, real-world-data-trained model handed over as a contractual deliverable, not a data license or a dashboard. That's new: pharma paying for modeling capability as a product. But be precise about what that claim supports. The filing language is exactly one sentence — "successfully delivered our first oncology foundation model to AstraZeneca" — with nothing describing what the model predicts, what it was validated against, or what decision it's meant to inform. Delivery is a milestone, not evidence of downstream clinical or commercial impact, and the two shouldn't be conflated. Tempus also announced it's acquiring genomics and diagnostics peer Personalis for roughly $1.5 billion in enterprise value to add minimal-residual-disease testing — a useful bolt-on, but a diagnostics acquisition, not itself an AI capability, so keep it separate from the Data and Applications growth story when you're pricing the platform.

Zoom out and this is the closest thing the prompt's real-world-data question has gotten to a monetizable answer this year: can AI actually turn the balkanized, post-HITECH patchwork of EHR and claims data into something pharma will pay real money for. Tempus's answer today is yes, on scale and now on profit too. The caveat is that Tempus's own earnings materials say nothing about how it clears the HIPAA and cross-format linkage hurdles that make that data balkanized in the first place — the industry's practical answer has generally been privacy-preserving tokenization layers rather than raw data pooling, but Tempus didn't address that mechanism in this release, so treat the interoperability question as open rather than solved. The so-what: the money in AI biopharma right now sits with businesses that sell data and models as a service, not with platforms still trying to get a de novo molecule through the clinic.

Which brings us to why the data layer, not the discovery layer, is where the auditable value is landing: the clinic remains the bottleneck, and this week the regulator is the one trying to move it. The FDA held an educational webinar on August 6th outlining its proposed Expedited IND Pilot Program. The mechanism: sponsors would partner with a network of what the agency calls Qualified Research Institutions — academic medical centers, health networks, CROs, and regulatory advisors — to develop and pre-review first-in-human trial protocols before the IND is even submitted, with FDA aiming to select up to ten programs by year-end. Public comments are due by August 24th. This is a process change, not a capability change, but it lands exactly on the bottleneck this beat keeps naming: the years of toxicology, manufacturing scale-up, and IND filing that no amount of model capability shortcuts. If it's finalized, a faster path to first-in-human trials would disproportionately help capital-constrained AI-native platforms with large candidate pipelines, since large pharma is less bottlenecked by first-in-human timing to begin with. The caveat here matters: this is still a comment-period proposal, nothing is selected yet, and FDA has drawn no explicit line connecting the pilot to AI-discovered candidates specifically — that link is inference, not agency language.

One more thread, briefly, because it's a third face of the same migration. Chai Discovery, the OpenAI-backed molecular design startup, closed a $400 million Series C in mid-July that tripled its valuation to $3.8 billion in about seven months, on the back of licensing its Chai-3 structure and design model to Pfizer and a new antibody-discovery collaboration with Novartis. The pattern is sell the model, not the molecule — pharma paying for design capability as a subscription rather than betting on a pipeline asset, which rhymes directly with Tempus shipping a foundation model as a deliverable rather than a therapeutic. The risk cuts the other way too: if structure-prediction access keeps getting licensed out this broadly, it can commoditize the way AlphaFold's open release once did, and today's premium pricing power erodes.

So where does that leave the honest scorecard. The discovery side hasn't moved this week. Peer-reviewed work in Nature Reviews Drug Discovery has already made the case that despite a wide variety of AI methods benchmarked in drug discovery, evidence of clinically relevant impact remains, in the journal's own words, disappointingly limited. Isomorphic Labs, sitting on nearly three billion dollars in pharma milestones from Lilly and Novartis, still hasn't dosed a single patient with an AI-designed compound, and its first-trial guidance has already slipped once. Insilico's rentosertib remains the standing Phase III test case nobody has read out yet. None of that changed today. What changed is that one name in the data-and-operations layer just showed you a profit-and-loss statement instead of a roadmap, and a regulator just signaled it's willing to try to widen the clinical bottleneck that model capability alone has never been able to shortcut. The realistic read for R&D spend and clinical success rates: the money is following auditable earnings in data and operations well before it follows any proof that AI raises the odds a molecule actually works in patients.
