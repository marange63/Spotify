Good morning, it's Thursday, November 12th, and here's the Capital Markets Radar.

After Tuesday's hot core inflation print, Governor Waller floated a half-point December hike instead of the quarter point markets had priced. The two-year note jumped to 4.736 percent. The question since then is where that repricing actually lands. The surface of the market says nowhere much. The tails say otherwise, and that's today's story.

Start with credit, because it's the clearest evidence yet of where the stress sits. Vanguard published a note on Tuesday titled "Beyond CCCs, high-yield appears resilient." It says spreads on CCC-rated bonds have widened by more than 500 basis points from their lowest levels this year. They now sit at roughly 1,000 basis points over Treasuries. Higher-quality BB and single-B bonds, in Vanguard's words, "have remained relatively contained." Vanguard calls the distress acute, but largely contained. It describes a sharp divide between the weakest borrowers and the rest of the credit market.

Be clear on timing, because this is easy to over-read. Vanguard measures the move from this year's lows. So it's a deterioration built across 2026, not a reaction to Tuesday's CPI print. And it isn't contagion, because the rungs above CCC are holding. The under-read is the opposite mistake. A calm aggregate spread can hide a bottom rung that has already cracked. What Tuesday's repricing does is raise the refinancing cost on a tail that was already breaking.

That answers a question this desk has carried for a week. On Sunday, and again yesterday, we said broad high yield was lagging, not confirming, the AI-linked equity stress. The latest data sharpens that. Per FRED, the ICE BofA High Yield option-adjusted spread sat at 3.34 percent on Monday, November 9th. That's tighter than the 3.42 percent we reported for the previous Friday. So the broad index isn't just lagging the tail. It's moving the other way. One limit worth stating. That reading predates Tuesday's CPI release, so it says nothing yet about credit's reaction to the hike talk.

One more gauge, and keep it separate. ICE BofA's CCC-and-Lower index read 13.11 percent that same Monday. That index also sweeps in CC, C and D-rated paper, which Vanguard's CCC figure doesn't. So the two numbers measure different populations, and they aren't interchangeable. What they share is direction. However you slice the bottom of the market, it sits far wider than an index that keeps tightening.

Now the paradox sitting next to that. Credit Benchmark reviewed a sample of US private-credit funds. It finds the median loan spread over the base rate sits in a 4 to 5 percent range. A year ago, it was 5 to 6 percent. So public CCC paper pays roughly double what direct lenders typically charge, and those lenders are charging less than they were. The benchmarks differ, so treat that comparison as rough. Two readings compete, and neither is settled. Competition for deals may be underpricing risk. Or private borrowers simply aren't comparable to the public CCC pool.

There's also a timing gap in that private-credit data. Credit Benchmark's sample draws on March filings, so it predates this month's repricing. And the margin may already be turning. Our Private Credit desk reported Tuesday that Blue Owl's listed business development company priced new loans 40 basis points wider last quarter. That's one manager, not an industry series, but it cuts against the compression story. So here's the December stake. If the Fed goes 50 basis points rather than 25, refinancing pain concentrates on the floating-rate, lowest-rated tail. That's exactly where direct lenders have thinned their own spread cushion over the past year.

The same split between a calm index and a stressed tail shows up in equities. Per CNBC's Monday piece, the VanEck Semiconductor ETF fell 22 percent over the past month. The Roundhill Memory ETF fell about 30 percent. Those are pre-CPI numbers. Now set them against the index on CPI day itself. As we reported yesterday, the S&P 500 fell just 0.38 percent on Tuesday, despite the front-end shock. Chips and memory are carrying a drawdown the benchmark isn't showing.

Morgan Stanley's Mike Wilson offers the bullish counter. In his words, "A final retest of last week's lows likely sets up a year-end rally." That's a forecast, not evidence. It also concedes that the lows haven't been retested yet. And on our read, it needs the rates repricing to stop at a quarter point. A half-point December would land hardest on the long-duration chip names that are already bleeding. The open question is whether chips and memory are seeing a de-rating of multiples or a cut to earnings. Multiple compression can reverse quickly if rates calm down. An earnings cut can't. Nothing in hand today tells us which one this is.

And the gauge that's supposed to price that tension has gone quiet. The VIX closed at 28.18 on November 5th, during the AI selloff, per FRED. By November 10th, CPI day, Cboe had it closing at 19.92. So most of the spike had unwound on the same day the two-year jumped more than 18 basis points. There are two ways to read that. Either the shock got absorbed, and the market believes the credit and chip stress stays contained. Or volatility sellers have re-levered into a fragile surface. We have no fresh positioning data to tell those apart.

That matters because the next test is dated and compressed. Nvidia reports earnings on November 17th. VIX options expire the next day. Equity options expire on November 20th. That's three major events in four sessions. ING's bids on its data-center loan auction, which our Private Credit desk flagged, are also due November 19th, right in the middle. With the VIX back near 20, options and hedging flows around those dates can move the index more than fundamentals do, in either direction. So the risk going into next week sits in the mechanics and the tails, not the headline index.

The fourth tail is the on-chain dollar. Glassnode data, reported by The Block, shows major stablecoin supply fell 14.2 billion dollars over the past 30 days. That's the biggest drop since the 2022 crypto bear market. It advances an arc we've tracked all week. On Sunday we flagged the steepest weekly drop of the year as a leak. Yesterday, CoinDesk data reframed it as a flat float with faster-moving money inside. The two reads reconcile on timing. CoinDesk's data ran through September. This contraction covers mid-October into November. So the leak is now a multi-week trend, not one bad week.

Set against that, the banks are building. Per Bloomberg, Goldman Sachs, Citigroup, Santander and Deutsche Bank have pledged roughly 270 million dollars toward a joint dollar stablecoin venture. It plans to launch a stablecoin in the first half of 2027. That's startup capital for a future product, not live issuance. So don't read it as banks taking over the market. But the direction is notable. Crypto-native dollar tokens are shrinking while bank-controlled rails get built through the drawdown. If that divergence holds, the next cycle's issuance may be more bank-controlled than the last.

Here's our inference on the rates link. Large stablecoin issuers hold short-dated Treasury bills in reserve. A shrinking float trims that marginal bid, right as the front end reprices higher. It's small next to the bill market, but it points the same way as everything else today. For a price anchor, Bitcoin is down roughly 28 percent year over year, and Ethereum about 40 percent. The speculative edge is retreating while the institutional core keeps building.

Three things to watch. First, October producer prices land Friday morning. They haven't printed, so there's no number to anticipate. Second, whether broad high-yield spreads finally follow the CCC tail wider once post-CPI readings come in. Third, the November 17th-to-20th cluster, led by Nvidia's print, where a quiet VIX meets a lot of mechanical risk.

That's the radar for November 12th. Trade carefully out there, and I'll see you tomorrow.
