Cautious Optimism Briefings

Strategic Power, Energy, and Institutional Change

Transcript · August 28, 2026

Good morning. It's Friday, August 28th, 2026, and this week's beat has kept circling one theme: a state that buys positions instead of building capacity. Today the instrument that does the buying gets bigger, and the first real structural objection to it shows up.

Start with the Pentagon. On August 10th, the Department's Office of Strategic Capital signed a batch of critical-minerals agreements the White House valued at just over two billion dollars, and the fact sheet gives the breakdown by name. The largest by far is one point four billion dollars into Sila Nanotechnologies, to expand silicon-carbon battery anode production and build a lithium-ion cell plant. Next is four hundred million into Sunrise Energy Metals, an Australia-based company, to develop what the fact sheet calls the world's first primary scandium mine. Then a hundred and fifty million into Niron Magnetics, a Minnesota firm making permanent magnets with no rare earths in them at all. And over eighty-five million into Strategic Bauxite, to lock down refractory-grade bauxite supply. Four names, one week, roughly two billion dollars aimed at the inputs a defense and battery supply chain actually runs on.

Now watch the verbs, because they carry the whole story. Every one of these except the bauxite stake, which is an equity investment, is described by the Department's own program as a conditional loan commitment. A commitment is not a disbursement. A conditional commitment, a signed equity stake, a closed transaction, and an operating mine or magnet line are four different assets with four different odds of ever existing, and press coverage collapses all four into a single impression of momentum. Nothing available in the public record confirms that a single one of these commitments has reached financial close or actually funded. There isn't even a stable number for how much the program has committed in total: the figures in circulation range from roughly five billion to over eight billion dollars depending on which source you read, and that instability is itself the tell. So the honest sentence is that two billion dollars was announced, not that two billion dollars moved.

What was announced, though, is structurally new, and you can see it in the template being copied. The Pentagon's earlier position in MP Materials combined four things: an equity investment, a loan, a guaranteed minimum price for the company's main products, and a promise that all the magnets produced at the facility would be bought by defense or commercial customers. Read that list slowly. That is not a subsidy. A subsidy lowers a cost. This structure takes price risk and demand risk off a private balance sheet and moves both onto the government's. The company is left holding execution risk and essentially nothing else. Replicating that shape across anodes, scandium, magnets and bauxite in a single week is a different act than a tariff or a grant. It is the state becoming the buyer and the price-setter of last resort across an entire tier of the minerals chain.

Which produces a second-order effect that will show up in capital markets long before it shows up in tonnage. If a floor price plus a full offtake becomes the standard federal template, then every private developer in lithium, cobalt, gallium or magnets is now bidding against a competitor with a federally guaranteed revenue line. That competitor's cost of capital is not set by its own geology or its own management. It's set by the Treasury curve. Underwriting a challenger in these sectors stops being a question about resource quality and becomes a question about whether Washington has already picked the counterparty. That is a durable distortion, and it is the mechanism by which industrial policy crowds out the private capital it claims to be catalyzing.

Here's the turn. Reporting published on August 21st found that the outside counsel who structured the MP Materials transaction for the Department, a lawyer named Alan Waldenberg, was simultaneously the personal trustee and attorney of Deputy Defense Secretary Stephen Feinberg, and outside counsel to Cerberus Capital, the firm Feinberg founded. Three parties, one lawyer, on the deal that became the template. Asked about the conflict directly, Waldenberg's on-record answer was, quote, "you've got to be kidding me." To be clear about what that is and isn't: there is no inspector general finding here, no adjudication, and nothing improper established. His response is evidence of posture, not of wrongdoing. What exists is an unresolved conflict-of-interest question attached to the exact deal architecture now being replicated four times over.

Treat that as a durability problem rather than a personnel scandal, because that's where the exposure actually sits. The risk isn't that any single deal was mispriced. The risk is that a governance defect at the template level invites congressional scrutiny and inspector general process precisely at the moment the program is scaling. And the failure mode for this program was never a bad investment. It was conditional commitments that quietly never close. Anything that slows closings converts announced billions into nothing at all.

The same shape shows up where Washington built its other big instrument this year, export controls on advanced chips. The setup you already know: Nvidia has been licensing H200 chips into China in batches under a case-by-case framework, with ByteDance and Tencent among the recipients. The volumes aren't the story. The hole in the wall around them is. Commerce is now reviewing how Chinese AI firms are getting access to advanced Nvidia compute sitting in offshore data centers, in places like Thailand, without ever owning the hardware. The problem, stated plainly by the analysts studying it, is that the US regime "controls physical AI chips" and "does not cover remote access to those chips."

Follow that to its conclusion. If compute can be rented across a border, then restricting the sale of the physical box was never the right unit of control, and the strategic value of the hardware restriction decays toward zero as the rental channel matures. Washington's two options are both expensive. It can start regulating compute as a service, which is a vastly larger regulatory reach than anything currently on the table and one nobody in this administration has shown appetite for. Or it can accept the leak. That review has not concluded, and I'd caution against assuming an open process produces a rule at all. Two things follow in the meantime. Nvidia's China revenue carries a rising political risk premium rather than a regulatory one. And Southeast Asian data-center hubs stop being neutral capacity and become contested infrastructure.

One deal this week did aim squarely at a real chokepoint, and notably no federal check is attached to it. Centrus Energy signed a definitive contract with X-energy to supply enrichment services, including high-assay low-enriched uranium, for X-energy's small modular reactor and fuel programs. On Monday this beat argued that separative work unit capacity, not gigawatts announced, is the number that actually gates the nuclear-for-AI trade, given that the US share of global enrichment capacity has fallen under one percent. This is the first binding commercial commitment against that constraint since.

Don't overstate it. No dollar figure or volume is public, so this is a claim on future capacity, not delivered fuel, and the reactors it serves don't exist yet. Centrus also remains the only near-term domestic route for this material, which makes the contract progress and a single point of failure in the same breath. Every utility, independent power producer and uranium equity that has been re-rated on the advanced-reactor pipeline is now exposed to one supplier's execution schedule. But hold the contrast with everything above it, because that's the day's real signal. State capital is concentrating where positions can be bought quickly. The bottleneck that actually gates deployable reactors got its customer from a private counterparty instead.

Last, the number that prices all of it. Gold reached forty-six ninety-six an ounce earlier this month, its highest since mid-May, and it's on pace for its biggest monthly gain since 1999. Don't draw a straight line from industrial policy to the gold bid; the cited drivers are debt and a soft dollar. The narrower claim is the defensible one. Every deal in this briefing is financed on a curve where net interest costs approached a trillion dollars in 2025 alone. A sovereign that is simultaneously expanding its lending book, its equity stakes, and the debt used to fund both is a different credit than one that only taxes and spends, and gold is the market visibly pricing that.

Watch two things from here: whether a single one of these Pentagon commitments actually reaches financial close, and whether the Commerce review produces a rule that reaches remote compute. Until one of those happens, positions are still being bought faster than capacity is being built.

That's the briefing. Have a good weekend.
