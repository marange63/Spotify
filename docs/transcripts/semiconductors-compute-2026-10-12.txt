Good morning. It's Monday, October 12th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

The thesis today: scarcity pricing keeps spreading through the chip stack. And the industry is splitting into two camps. There are companies that can hold their price, and companies that need someone else's balance sheet to survive long enough to compete. A word of warning up front. It's a light news week. There's one genuinely fresh development, and we'll test the thesis against a couple of older stories that this week puts in a new light.

First, the scale of the backdrop. The Semiconductor Industry Association says global chip sales hit $159.7 billion in August. That's up 8% from July's $147.9 billion, and it pushed year-to-date sales past $1 trillion. The year-over-year comparison looks far bigger, but it's measured against a weak August 2025, so lean on the sequential number instead. The point isn't that demand is in question. It isn't. The point is who inside this stack captures the margin, and who needs financing to stay in the game.

We'll begin with the fresh story, and the company that needed a government backstop. Wolfspeed has landed a conditional loan commitment of up to $1.5 billion from the Department of War's Office of Strategic Capital. All week we've tracked whose balance sheet pays for AI compute. Last Tuesday it was Broadcom's, financing Anthropic. Since then we've seen SpaceX's debt for xAI, and SoftBank's junk bond for its OpenAI commitments. Each of those was private capital backing compute demand. This one is different. It's the Pentagon's balance sheet, and it's backing the supply chain itself.

Wolfspeed's own release, filed with its 8-K, spells out the structure. It's a senior secured, delayed-draw term loan facility, described as a 30-year commitment. In exchange, the government gets warrants for up to 7.5% of Wolfspeed's fully diluted equity, issued as each tranche actually funds. The proceeds are earmarked for gallium nitride. That means upgrading GaN epitaxy for communications infrastructure, onshoring GaN power-device production, and advancing GaN-on-silicon-carbide RF wafers. All of it sits across Wolfspeed's existing North Carolina, New York, and Arkansas footprint. That's a defense and communications rationale, not simply an electric-vehicle one.

Now, why the channel matters. At last confirmed reporting, in mid-2025, Wolfspeed was still waiting on the administration to decide whether to release a separate $750 million CHIPS grant from the Commerce Department. Nothing in the filing ties this loan to that grant, so don't read it as a replacement. But the money that actually moved this time came through a defense financing vehicle, not the Commerce subsidy program. If that pattern holds, reshoring for strategically important but strained chipmakers becomes partly a function of the defense budget and its conditions, rather than of commercial returns.

Two caveats matter more than the headline number. First, the release says plainly there's no assurance this closes. It's subject to due diligence, definitive agreements, government appropriations, and waivers from existing lenders. The $1.5 billion is a ceiling, not drawn debt. Second, Reuters reports Wolfspeed shares jumped about 27% in extended trading. Read that move as the market repricing financing and survival risk, not silicon-carbide end demand. A conditional loan doesn't change how many EVs or grid projects get built. And the warrants cut the other way for existing holders, with up to 7.5% dilution if the facility is fully drawn. So what it tells us: the state is now a conditional lender of last resort to a strategic chipmaker, and it wants equity upside for the risk. The thing to watch is whether a definitive agreement actually closes.

Wolfspeed needed a backer because it lacks pricing power. Samsung Foundry is betting that it has some.

This one is last month's report. Reporting relayed through TrendForce, originally from the Korean outlet The Bell, says Samsung's 2 nanometer gate-all-around yield has climbed above 70%. That's up sharply from below 50% at the start of this year. If accurate, it addresses Samsung's historical weak point at the leading edge. But the same reporting says Qualcomm's 2 nanometer chip production at Samsung could be pushed back into 2027. The reason given is price, not technology. Qualcomm wants a lower price, and Samsung has been reluctant to agree.

Separate that into three dimensions. On manufacturing capability, Samsung has reportedly improved. On the customer relationship, Qualcomm may be delayed. On pricing power, Samsung isn't discounting to close the deal. Better yield hasn't converted into a marquee win, because Samsung won't cut its price to buy one.

If the number-two foundry won't discount either, leading-edge buyers may face scarcity pricing across every qualified supplier, not just TSMC. Our inference from there: if Qualcomm stays on TSMC, TSMC's leverage over leading-edge mobile chips grows further. Second-sourcing becomes a hedge on capacity, not on cost. The competing interpretation is that Samsung's stance is negotiating posture, and it could collapse the moment Samsung needs the volume. And remember the sourcing. This is a single secondary outlet, a month old, and neither company has confirmed it. So, provisionally: the scarcity premium may be reaching even the backup foundry.

If owning scarce capacity is what earns pricing power, the companies without it sit on the other side of that trade. That's Cerebras.

Cerebras shares fell below their IPO price on September 30th. CNBC reports the drop coincided with the end of its post-IPO lockup and insider filings to sell more than $84 million of stock. CNBC also attributed part of the slide to competitive pressure from Nvidia. The specific claim circulating is that OpenAI moved a fast inference tier onto Nvidia hardware. That traces to a single social-media post, not a disclosure from OpenAI or Cerebras. Cerebras disputes it, and the two sides may be describing different model versions. Treat it as unconfirmed, not as a verdict on the technology.

What is confirmed is simpler. A lockup ended, insiders are selling, and the stock is down. That's a supply-of-shares story as much as a competitive one, and right now you can't cleanly separate the two. It's also a reminder of the gap between benchmark leadership and production leadership. Wafer-scale speed claims win demos. Durable revenue depends on which hardware the big labs actually run in production, and that is exactly what nobody has documented here.

Set Cerebras next to Wolfspeed and the pattern holds. A differentiated chip company without scarce capacity of its own depends, for now, on someone else's capital. For Wolfspeed, it's a government balance sheet. For Cerebras, it's public-market buyers absorbing a lockup unwind. That has a valuation consequence. Equity holders in this group are underwriting financing risk, not just technology risk. So what it tells us: the scarcity rents in this cycle are not yet landing on the alternative accelerator makers trying to unseat Nvidia.

So where are those rents landing? Memory is the clearest case, and we've covered it at length this month, so just a quick reminder of the evidence. TrendForce projects the average selling price of HBM will rise 121% next year, more than doubling. That's a blended projection, and part of it is the shift to pricier HBM4, not pure price inflation. And Micron has said it has already contracted the bulk of its 2027 HBM bit supply. In memory, sellers are setting terms a year out.

One forward look before we close. Fab-equipment stocks, including Applied Materials, Lam Research and ASML, sold off in mid-September on worry that AI capital spending could decelerate faster than guided. That happened even though Applied had grown revenue 25% year over year and guided the next quarter higher still. Equipment is the one layer where the market is already pricing a slowdown the fundamentals haven't shown. ASML and TSMC both report later this week, and those prints are the next real test of whether tool demand is cooling or September was just nerves. One inference worth holding: a genuine slowdown in tool orders would likely tighten advanced-node capacity a year or two out, not ease it. That would reinforce foundry pricing power, not undercut it.

So, briefly: pricing power sits with the memory makers and, provisionally, with the foundries. Capital dependence sits with Wolfspeed and, for now, Cerebras. That's the split to watch.

That's your briefing for October 12th. Stay cautiously optimistic.
