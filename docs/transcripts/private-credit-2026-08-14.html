Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Credit & the Corporate Credit Cycle

Transcript · August 14, 2026

Good morning. It's Friday, August 14th, and this is your private credit briefing.

This week's arc has run from eroding marks, to sponsor intervention, to a broader lending pullback. Today it culminates in a capital-formation announcement at a scale that dwarfs all of it.

Nvidia has assembled a six-firm coalition — BlackRock, Goldman Sachs, Apollo, Blackstone, Brookfield, and KKR — to mobilize, in Nvidia's own words, more than $500 billion of third-party capital for the AI buildout. Notice four of those six names are the same alternative managers whose listed credit vehicles have been showing strain all week. This is the AI-capex rotation we've tracked bottom-up — Blue Owl's data-center fund, Apollo's shifting origination mix — now crystallized at maximum scale. And it is a live test of a question this asset class has never actually had to answer: whether non-banks can absorb concentrated, single-sector, single-counterparty risk without banks or public bondholders ending up with the tail.

Read the fine print and the picture gets more cautious. Nvidia's own press release calls this a target to mobilize capital "over time," not a funded pool, and it says the partnerships remain subject to execution of final agreements — meaning the MOUs aren't even signed contracts yet. Jensen Huang himself told reporters the $500 billion is an aggregate target spread over years, not a single fund and not a commitment to any one customer. So this is intent, not deployed capital. Nobody has disclosed how much of it will actually be private-credit direct loans versus equity co-investment or public bonds, and no per-firm allocation exists anywhere. Treat the headline number as a ceiling on ambition, not a floor under exposure.

The more interesting credit detail is what Nvidia is keeping on its own book. Under a residual-value mechanism, Nvidia will cover up to 25 percent of the gap, reviewed project by project, if the resale or reuse value of installed GPUs used as financing collateral falls short at the end of a loan term. That's a vendor-financing tell. Nvidia isn't walking away clean — it's underwriting the depreciation risk on its own hardware to get lenders comfortable. And because that guarantee is assessed deal by deal rather than applied uniformly, Nvidia keeps discretion over which projects get the backstop. That discretion cuts against the reassuring reading: it lets Nvidia steer downside support toward its most strategically important partners and leave everyone else more exposed, which means the risk transfer here is selective, not systemic. The market's initial relief showed up in Nvidia's five-year credit-default-swap spread, which had blown out to roughly 82 basis points of implied protection cost in late July on circular-financing worries, then retraced to about 73 basis points after this announcement. That's a partial de-risking, not a resolved one.

The wave is broader than this one headline. Brookfield led a roughly $605 million financing for AI data-center developer 5C Group, and JPMorgan led a $441 million debt facility for Global AI, a company citing $6.2 billion of contracted revenue and a target of one gigawatt of capacity by 2029. Both banks and alternative managers are chasing this trade. But look closer at that contracted-revenue figure before you treat it as collateral. Only about a billion dollars of the $6.2 billion has actually been built and delivered to customers — the rest is a forward book, a promise of revenue not yet earned. And Global AI is a two-year-old company raising a nine-figure debt facility against it. That is exactly the profile where covenant and collateral protection matter most, and exactly where an unproven operating history gives lenders the least to hold onto.

Now turn to the book these same managers are already marking. Fitch's trailing twelve-month private credit default rate hit a record 6 percent as of the end of the second quarter, up from 5.7 percent the prior quarter, and stayed there into the following month. Moody's adds the texture that matters more than the headline: it estimates distressed exchanges — debt swaps and maturity extensions agreed under duress — made up roughly 65 percent of all 2025 private credit defaults. Depending on whether you count those as defaults, Moody's own range for the 2025 rate spans from 1.6 to 4.7 percent. Two rating agencies, same asset class, wildly different numbers, because the industry keeps choosing amend-and-extend over clean payment default. And there's a subtler cost to that habit. If distressed exchanges keep defaults out of the formal statistics, the recovery and mark assumptions baked into BDC and CLO valuations can drift too high — eroding slowly and invisibly rather than repricing in public view. That's precisely the kind of slow leak a retail interval-fund holder has no way to see. So the same managers now raising capital to finance AI infrastructure are sitting on a legacy book whose calm is manufactured, not tested.

At the deal level, spread compensation is genuinely mixed. Ares is leading $2.2 billion of second-lien direct lending behind MedImpact's acquisition of Medical Card System, pricing at least 8 percentage points over the benchmark rate — a real, wide spread for subordinated healthcare risk, even as investors pull money from private credit funds at a record pace over worries about loose underwriting and AI-exposed software credits. That spread tells you lenders are still pricing risk honestly in some corners of the market, even while AI-infrastructure spreads compress as capital stampedes toward that trade. But note what the wide spread and the structure have in common: if MedImpact underperforms, that 800-basis-point loss lands inside Ares-managed vehicles, including retail-facing BDCs, not syndicated across a dozen banks. The dispersion itself is the signal: this isn't a market uniformly asleep, and it isn't uniformly awake either.

Who actually bears the losses if any of this turns? Investors sought to withdraw more than $20.8 billion from the largest semi-liquid private credit funds in the first quarter alone, and managers met barely half of those requests under contractual gates. Blackstone's BCRED saw redemption requests hit a record 7.9 percent of shares, roughly $3.7 billion. That's the binding constraint here — not credit quality in isolation, but liquidity mismatch in the retail-facing vehicles that increasingly fund this machine.

And it isn't only retail money on the line. The same alternative managers increasingly fund this lending through insurance balance sheets — annuity-backed capital that has quietly become a major LP to these platforms. So a demand shortfall at the AI-cloud tenants behind all this financing wouldn't stay neatly contained inside private credit. It would transmit from the funds into insurance balance sheets, and from there, eventually, toward public markets. That is the channel regulators keep flagging when they talk about non-bank financial intermediation, and it's why the concentration in this week's headline is not an abstract worry.

So here's where the cycle actually stands. Record capital is being committed to concentrate risk in a single sector and a handful of counterparties, at the same moment the existing private-credit book is posting record defaults flattered by restructuring rather than repayment, and retail investors in the funds behind both are already being gated. That's concentration and opacity compounding, not diversifying. Watch three things from here: whether any of Nvidia's $500 billion actually gets drawn against a signed agreement, the next default or distressed-exchange print from Fitch or Moody's, and whether redemption pressure on BCRED and its peers keeps building into year-end.

That's the briefing. Have a good weekend.
