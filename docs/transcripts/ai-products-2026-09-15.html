Good morning. This is Cautious Optimism Briefings for September 15th, 2026.

Here's the pattern this week. OpenAI is taking roughly one per-seat software category every week, and the tool doing the taking is not a better model. It's a connector.

Two days ago this beat read ChatGPT for Financial Services as an attack on the dataset subscription rather than on the model. OpenAI licensed Daloopa, PitchBook, LSEG and Crunchbase straight into the assistant and aimed the result at the research-and-modeling seat. The interesting thing about that move was never the finance vertical. It was the repeatability. The input OpenAI needs to enter the next white-collar category is not a training run. It's a partnership plus a permissioned pipe into somebody else's data. A frontier training run takes quarters. A data partnership takes weeks. That difference is the whole explanation for why the cadence is weekly rather than annual.

Start with the newest one. On September 10th, OpenAI introduced a Data agent inside ChatGPT Work. You ask it a plain-language question about your business, and it connects to your company's own data warehouses to answer it. It plugs into Redshift, BigQuery, Snowflake, Databricks, MongoDB, Datadog, ClickHouse, and flat files, and on the output side it builds and interacts with dashboards inside Power BI, Tableau, Sigma, Omni, Oracle BI, and ThoughtSpot. No query language required.

Notice what that output list tells you. The agent builds dashboards inside Tableau and Power BI. It doesn't replace them. That's the tell. OpenAI isn't attacking the BI platform, and it isn't touching the data-engineering team that owns the underlying models. It's attacking the layer in between: the query-writing and dashboard-assembly work that today justifies a self-serve analyst seat. That's a narrower claim than "OpenAI kills your BI tool," and it's the more honest one.

It also tells the incumbents where to dig in. The part of a BI platform an assistant cannot synthesise is the semantic layer — the agreed definitions of what counts as revenue, which customers count as active, how a fiscal period gets cut. Those definitions are negotiated inside a company, not derivable from the raw tables. If Tableau, Power BI and Sigma own that layer, every agent question has to come through them to be answerable correctly. So the entry-level seat is contestable. The definitions underneath it are not, at least not yet.

Now the skeptical turn, because this is where the lead story runs thin. There is no published price for the Data agent, and no stated tier — whether it's bundled into an existing ChatGPT Work plan or metered separately is genuinely unknown, not just unannounced. There's no named customer and no usage figure. And there's no accuracy or evaluation number for the harder part of the job: investigating why a metric moved inside a live production warehouse. That's a much tougher correctness bar than generating a dashboard from a prompt. One thing did get confirmed this week. OpenAI's own announcement post states the agent runs under a company's existing table, row, and column permissions, with admins controlling which connections it can touch. That's a real answer on governance, stated by OpenAI itself, and it's worth taking at face value as a claim while remembering it's still OpenAI grading OpenAI. Strip away the framing and what you're left with, checkably, is a connector list and a date. No price, no customer, no accuracy number. That's an unusually thin evidence base for a launch this beat is treating as significant.

If an assistant is going to read your warehouse, something has to carry your permissions into it safely. That's the connector layer, and this week it standardized. Box's integration with ChatGPT went generally available on September 10th, for every Box customer, rolling out across ChatGPT's paid tiers over the following days. Box content now shows up directly inside the ChatGPT library — you can browse folders, preview files, and edit Box Notes without leaving the chat window. The plumbing underneath is Box's own MCP server, exposing tools to reference, extract, update, and create content, all under a company's existing Box permissions.

The significant word there is "generally available," not "beta" or "preview." Five days earlier, this beat covered Perplexity wiring MCP tool calls into its developer-facing Agent API — a protocol for people building things. Now MCP is shipping as procurement plumbing inside a live enterprise product. That's the bar Dropbox, SharePoint, OneDrive, and Google Drive now have to clear, or risk losing default-assistant mindshare inside companies already running ChatGPT Work. It's also a warning for connector and RAG vendors whose entire product used to be the integration itself — if the protocol does that for free, the integration stops being the product.

There's a less comfortable read on Box's own side of that trade. Box is renting out its permissioned content graph to the assistant most likely to become the place the work actually happens. That is the same trade this beat flagged two days ago for Daloopa, PitchBook and LSEG, one category over — attractive licensing revenue in year one, a wholesale-supplier relationship by year four. And be precise about today's footprint. General availability is days old, no adoption or usage figure exists anywhere, and mobile support was still listed as coming at announcement. What shipped is a capability, not evidence of use.

None of this works if the underlying model takes an hour to finish a task. OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra, shipped September 3rd, is built around computer and browser use rather than chat quality, and on OpenAI's own OSWorld benchmark it cut average time per task from about 75 minutes to about 40, while scoring higher than its predecessor. Treat that figure carefully — it's self-graded, unreproduced independently, and the primary announcement page wasn't directly verifiable. But directionally, it's the mechanism that makes agents like the Data agent and the Box integration commercially plausible rather than novelty demos. If it holds outside the benchmark, it also changes the unit buyers should be comparing. Not price per million tokens, but cost and time per completed task. Those two rankings do not have to agree, and the second one is the one that shows up on an invoice.

There's a second, incompatible way to sell an agent, and Anthropic took it. On September 2nd it published Claude Commerce Agents as Apache 2.0 reference code, giving developers a working shopping agent and a merchant-facing agent, deployable through the Claude API, Amazon Bedrock, Microsoft Foundry, or Google Cloud Vertex AI. That's cloud-agnostic, open reference code — the opposite go-to-market from a bundled assistant sold inside a seat contract. It reopens the build-versus-buy question for mid-market retailers specifically. Though reference code is a starting point, not a managed product: the integration and the maintenance bill land on the retailer.

Anthropic backs it with the only hard commercial-outcome numbers anyone published this week: retailers running the shopping agent have seen carts up to 35 percent larger, and shoppers 60 percent more likely to complete a purchase. Take those for what they are — Anthropic's own aggregate claim, across unnamed retailers, with no sample size and no stated methodology. A bigger cart at checkout isn't the same as a completed, unreturned order. This is now a running theme on this beat: the only performance figures available come from whoever is selling the product. Today's lead story escalates that pattern, because it ships with no number at all.

Close on the counterexample, because absorb-everything isn't the whole story. OpenAI's Sora-2 API goes dark on September 24th — nine days from this broadcast — returning an error code to anyone still calling it. That decision was announced back in March, so the news is the deadline arriving, not the choice. While OpenAI is entering enterprise content, business intelligence, and commerce this month, it's exiting text-to-video outright. Independent trackers now put Kuaishou's Kling and ByteDance's Seedance at the top of the video-generation leaderboards, with Google's Veo as the main Western alternative. Any developer still building on Sora has nine days to move.

So watch for two things that would actually settle this week's argument: a published price or tier for the Data agent, and one customer outside OpenAI willing to say, on the record, that it's running against a real production warehouse. Until then, this is a connector story, not yet a results story.

That's the briefing for September 15th. Talk to you tomorrow.
