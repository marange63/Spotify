Good morning. It's Thursday, August 20th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

We start with the rout. On Tuesday, the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index dropped five percent — its largest one-day decline since late July. The selloff didn't stay in the US. By Wednesday it had spread to Seoul, where Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix both fell more than seven percent. Nvidia alone lost as much as one hundred fifty-three billion dollars of market capitalization in a single session. That's larger than the company's entire projected fiscal 2026 profit. This is the payoff of an arc we've been tracking all week: Applied Materials' margin selloff, Intel's twenty-billion-dollar raise, Nvidia's five-hundred-billion-dollar financing memoranda, and AMD's record bond sale. For the first time, the market's unease about how the AI buildout gets financed showed up as an actual price break, not just a footnote in a filing. The trigger cited by markets was rising long-end bond yields, which hit capex-heavy, long-duration names hardest, alongside oil above ninety-one dollars a barrel on renewed Iran tensions. Treat the geopolitical piece as a contributing narrative rather than a confirmed cause — no specific triggering event was cited.

The proximate spark for the semiconductor-specific selling was a revival of circular-financing fears — the worry that a chip supplier is also bankrolling its own customer's spending. Here we can now be precise, because Nvidia itself filed the underlying structure. In an SEC filing dated August 17th, Nvidia disclosed that its guarantee tied to OpenAI's Ohio data-center buildout is capped at one hundred five billion dollars. That's not an open credit line. It's a residual-value guarantee covering an initial four-and-a-quarter gigawatts of capacity at SB Energy's Pike County campus, with an option — not an obligation — to extend to another three-and-three-quarter gigawatts. Nvidia only pays if SB Energy defaults or goes insolvent and can't recover the guaranteed minimum lease value. And critically, OpenAI has agreed to indemnify Nvidia for anything it actually pays out. That's a materially narrower and more contingent risk than the number that first spooked the market. Reporting had described a discussed two-hundred-fifty-billion-dollar backstop scaled back to under one hundred twenty billion after investor pushback. The two figures are close but not identical, likely differing on scope rather than substance, and Nvidia's own filed number is now the one to anchor on.

So what does this tell us? The market is pricing the financing architecture of the AI buildout, not a collapse in demand for the compute itself. And the structure matters more than the headline. Because this is a conditional guarantee with an indemnification behind it, rather than an unconditional obligation, the realized downside to Nvidia's balance sheet in a default is contractually far narrower than the two-hundred-fifty-billion-dollar framing implied. That argues against reading Tuesday's break as a mispriced tail risk that suddenly got repriced. One thing is still missing — the financing rate, the depreciation schedule, and the guaranteed minimum values behind that cap. Nvidia says those terms land as exhibits in its 10-Q for the quarter ended July 26th, not yet filed. Until that document appears, anyone quoting a precise downside number is speculating.

Now the feedback loop, because this is where a sentiment shift turns into something real. The market is no longer taking vendor-financing structures on faith — and Nvidia's own filing is evidence the scrutiny already bites. It scaled the Ohio backstop down after investors pushed back, before the deal was even fully papered. Expect the same discipline to travel across the other vendor-financing arrangements this buildout now runs on — Broadcom's custom-silicon commitments to OpenAI, AMD's own OpenAI ties. The mechanism is straightforward: tighter scrutiny slows the announcement cadence of new mega-deals and forces more conservative disclosure on the ones that do get struck. That's how a re-rating in the tape feeds back into the actual pace of financing, and it's the channel worth watching over the next few weeks.

There's a narrower channel too, and it cuts against the smaller players. A sustained drawdown in the semiconductor index tightens capital-markets access for the AI-silicon challengers — Cerebras, Groq, Tenstorrent — that fund themselves through strategic rounds and equity access rather than operating cash flow. Contrast that with the incumbents. AMD, as we covered Wednesday, just termed out debt at blue-chip spreads despite sitting on more than thirteen billion dollars in cash. Nvidia and Broadcom can tap that same receptive credit market whenever they want. The challengers can't. So a valuation event that looks like a sentiment wobble at the index level becomes a genuine competitive constraint further down the stack — the well-capitalized names ride it out, and the ones dependent on the next round feel it first.

If this is a financing story rather than a demand story, we'd expect the underlying compute business to keep printing strong numbers elsewhere. It did. Analog Devices reported record fiscal third-quarter revenue of four-point-oh-two billion dollars on Wednesday, up forty percent year over year, led by its data-center and industrial segments. ADI is a bellwether for power management and signal-chain content across the data center, not a pure AI-logic name, so treat this as corroboration rather than proof. But a record quarter, printed the same week pure-play AI chip stocks sold off hardest, is a useful cross-check. The demand underneath the buildout doesn't look broken — the selloff is in how it gets paid for, not in whether the boxes get bought.

The second piece of counter-evidence complicates a story we've told twice this week — that China's high-end AI chip market has gone almost entirely domestic, and that Nvidia's China revenue should be modeled as structurally impaired. The Financial Times reported Wednesday that ByteDance and Tencent have each received roughly ten thousand Nvidia H200 processors over the past several weeks. That's the first confirmed physical movement of frontier-adjacent Nvidia silicon into China since Washington shifted export licensing there to case-by-case review earlier this year. Each buyer is reportedly capped at one hundred thousand units against Nvidia's roughly five-hundred-thousand-chip available inventory. The practical route runs through Hong Kong, which sits outside mainland customs. But Hong Kong's limited data-center power and capacity genuinely constrain how much can actually be deployed at scale. And Beijing's own regulator, the National Development and Reform Commission, still has to sign off on purchases even where a US license exists. So the domestic-only framing was real, but it wasn't absolute. Access is loosening at the margin, even if this reopening proves more symbolic than commercial in the near term. The whole account traces to FT sourcing relayed through secondary reporting, with no primary Nvidia or Chinese-government statement yet, so hold it as directional, not confirmed volume — and remember the same case-by-case regime has stalled at zero shipped units before.

Put it all together. What broke on Tuesday was confidence in how the AI buildout gets financed, not evidence that the compute isn't wanted. Nvidia's own filing narrows the circular-financing risk to something conditional and indemnified rather than open-ended. ADI's data-center strength and the H200 flow into China both argue demand is intact. But the scrutiny is now live, and it's already reshaping how these deals get struck and who can afford to keep building. The piece the market actually needs — the depreciation and residual-value assumptions behind these guarantees — won't arrive until Nvidia's 10-Q lands. The next real test is Nvidia's fiscal 2027 second-quarter print on August 26th. Everything before that is analysts talking past incomplete disclosure.

That's the briefing. Have a good day.
