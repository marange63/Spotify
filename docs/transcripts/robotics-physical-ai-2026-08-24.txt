Good morning. It's Monday, August 24th, and this is your robotics and physical AI briefing.

For the better part of a week, this show has covered Unitree's Shanghai listing as a stock story: the pop, the offer price, the valuation. Today the disclosure underneath that tape finally arrives, and it isn't a valuation number. It's a mix number.

Trade-press analysis of Unitree's own offering materials shows that around seventy percent of its robots went to universities and research institutions, and fewer than ten percent of its sales are for commercial deployments. That is analysis of the offering, not an audited segment disclosure. But it is the first hard answer to the question this show closed with on the eighteenth: a paying-deployment count, or anything harder than a demo, from any humanoid maker. The answer arrived, and it runs the wrong way. Unitree is profitable on a full-year basis, with 2025 revenue of 1.7 billion yuan against 278 million yuan of net profit, so this is not a solvency problem. It's a demand-quality problem. The bull rebuttal is that research sales seed a developer base, and that's fair as far as it goes. Here's why it doesn't go far enough. A university buying a research platform is spending a grant line. It is not clearing a payback period against a loaded labor rate. So the humanoid order book that's been marked up all week carries almost no information about the thing this capex cycle is supposedly underwritten on: cost per unit, uptime, and displaced labor cost. And if the most mature humanoid maker in the world, one of the few that is actually profitable, is still overwhelmingly a research-equipment vendor, then the private comparable set trading off its tape, Figure and Apptronik and AgiBot, inherits a demand base exposed to grant cycles and science-funding budgets rather than to industrial payback math. That is a different risk than the one those valuations are being priced for. And one discipline before we move on: that mix figure describes Unitree. It is not yet a fact about the category.

So is it the category? For the first time, we have a denominator. A private research firm, Smart Analytics Global, puts global humanoid shipments at roughly 19,100 units in the first half of 2026, up 272 percent year over year. Treat that as a private estimate, not an audited industry figure. Real growth, tiny base. Inside it, the pecking order flipped. AgiBot shipped about 8,400 units for 44 percent share, ahead of Unitree's roughly 5,900 units and 31 percent share. And hold onto the word shipped. Shipped, manufactured and permitted are three different numbers, and none of them is fielded commercial work. That is the test for the rest of this briefing.

So what does a shipment look like on the ground? Reuters-sourced reporting from China has Robotera running more than one hundred parcel-sorting robots across fifteen warehouses, deployed at China Post logistics sites. That count comes from a company sales representative, not an independent audit, and the autonomy level is not specified. DexForce has robots on a phone-assembly line at a Lens Technology factory, a supplier to Apple and Huawei, since early this year. X Square Robot is testing in hundreds of homes ahead of a planned commercialization next year, and its longest single deployment so far has lasted one month. That one-month figure is the sharpest number in the story. It's an endurance ceiling, not a product. And notice what the fielded work actually is: parcel sorting and phone assembly. That's task-specific engineering in a humanoid form factor, not evidence that a general-purpose robot brain is doing the work.

Capital is moving faster than any of that. AgiBot, the new shipment leader, is reported to be targeting a Hong Kong listing at a valuation between 40 and 50 billion Hong Kong dollars, against the 15-billion-yuan mark disclosed last year. That report comes from people familiar with the matter rather than a filing, so treat both the number and the timing as unconfirmed. It also resolves a catalyst this show named on the nineteenth, when we asked whether AgiBot, UBTech or Fourier would pull an IPO timeline forward to catch this window. One of them is trying. So the listings are arriving faster than the deployments. And within weeks, the two public tapes this whole category gets marked against will both be Chinese, with nobody having yet shown that AgiBot's sales mix looks any different from Unitree's.

Now put that half-year global shipment number next to what a single American county authorized in one week. On August 20th, Nevada's Transportation Authority approved up to five thousand Tesla robotaxis, one thousand Waymo vehicles, and one thousand more run by Uber's autonomous partners, all in Clark County, over the next twelve months. That's roughly seven thousand authorized slots in Las Vegas alone, more than a third of the humanoids the entire world shipped in six months. And it lands less than four weeks after Nevada capped Tesla at ten vehicles, forty-five miles an hour, on an approved stretch of the Strip. Capital and narrative sit with the humanoids. Permitted, fielded, wheeled machines sit with the robotaxis. That's the pattern this show named on the twenty-second, and it escalated rather than broke.

Discipline that number immediately, because a permit ceiling is not a fleet. Tesla's own Cybercab chief engineer said the five-thousand figure has always been a ceiling for the company, and that Tesla would be extremely happy and satisfied to reach something like twenty-five hundred vehicles within the year. And no source tells us how many robotaxis from any operator are actually running in Clark County today, as against merely licensed to. So read Nevada as a test, not a fleet. Zoox is already charging fares in Las Vegas under a federal exemption capped at twenty-five hundred vehicles a year, which we covered yesterday. Add state authority for Waymo, Tesla and Uber's partners on top, and Las Vegas becomes the first city where four operators work under comparable authority in the same twelve months. It is the first place a permit-to-deployed conversion rate can be measured at all.

The same week, Waymo disclosed that its newest vehicle, the Ojai, runs on a custom chip it designed in-house: a five-nanometer part delivering more than a thousand TOPS, described as roughly in the same range as Nvidia's Drive AGX Thor. That's a rough comparison, not a benchmarked result. Now be precise about what the chip does, because the easy version of this story is wrong. It is dedicated to processing raw data from the vehicle's thirteen cameras before that data reaches the core driving system. That's a sensor and machine-learning front end, not the full driving-compute stack. Waymo has relied on Nvidia and AMD silicon up to now, and nothing in the disclosure says that reliance ends for planning, orchestration, and the parts that actually drive the car. So this is real vertical integration at the sensor layer. It is not Waymo exiting merchant AV silicon, and it would overstate the story to say the addressable market for AV compute just shrank. And on the number that would actually matter, per-vehicle cost, cost per mile, any cost-reduction figure at all, Waymo has published nothing. On Saturday it was the Ojai's build cost; today it's the chip inside it. Every account uses qualitative language about bringing costs down. There's no figure to attach, so we won't invent one.

One more reading from underneath, and this one comes from a filed quarterly report rather than a trade-show claim, though the company labels the figures unaudited. Hesai, the lidar maker, reported second-quarter shipments of just over 628,000 units, up 78 percent year over year, and the growth is lopsided. Robotics lidar shipments rose 193 percent, more than three times the 60 percent growth in lidar for driver assistance in cars. Sensor demand from robots is now the faster-growing business. But Hesai's gross margin compressed to 40.1 percent from 42.5 percent a year earlier. Volume up, margin down is the same signature this show flagged in Unitree's own quarter last week, now visible one layer down the chain, and against rare-earth magnet costs we've been tracking since the eighteenth. One caution: Hesai's robotics segment mixes warehouse mobile robots in with humanoids, so 193 percent is not a humanoid demand proxy. Much of it is the same wheeled equipment this briefing has been describing.

So where does embodied AI actually stand today? The fielded, permitted, instrumented work is wheeled and narrow. The humanoid category is real and growing quickly, and by its own leading company's disclosure it is still mostly selling to laboratories. Watch three things next: whether any humanoid maker publishes a commercial-deployment count instead of a shipment count, whether Nevada's permit ceilings convert into operating vehicles inside the year, and how AgiBot's Hong Kong listing prices against a sales mix nobody has yet shown to be any different from Unitree's.

That's the briefing. Talk tomorrow.
