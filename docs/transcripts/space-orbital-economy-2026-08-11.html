Cautious Optimism Briefings

Space & the Orbital Economy

Transcript · August 11, 2026

Good morning. It's August 11th, and this is your space and orbital economy briefing.

On Saturday we flagged Rocket Lab's August 10th earnings as the catalyst worth watching. The results are in, and the verdict was split.

Rocket Lab posted record quarterly revenue of 234 million dollars, up 62 percent year over year. Backlog rose to a record 2.36 billion dollars, built on more than a billion dollars of new contracts signed in the quarter alone. On paper, that is a company executing. The stock did not agree. Shares fell 3.4 percent in the regular session to 80.04 dollars, then dropped another 6.8 percent after hours to 74.60, as investors zeroed in on a wider-than-expected adjusted loss and continued cash burn. So the market rewarded neither the revenue record nor the billion-plus in new bookings. It punished the burn. That's the same "good quarter, sold off" pattern we've tracked across chips and software all week, and it has now reached space. Capital discipline, not top-line growth, is the bar this earnings season.

Here's why the burn matters more than usual right now. Rocket Lab's Neutron rocket — its answer to Falcon 9, and the vehicle underwriting most of that backlog — has never flown. Management says it's still on track for a debut this year, with first-stage tank production aimed at a fourth-quarter delivery to the pad. But prediction markets are pricing just a 14 percent probability that Neutron launches before 2027. That's not a rumor. It's real money betting against the company's own guidance. Spaceflight Now reported the same day that the 2026 window "is narrowing." And Rocket Lab booked yet another Neutron mission this quarter — a deal with Kepler Communications — that doesn't launch until 2028 at the earliest. So the backlog keeps growing while the flight record stays at zero. That's a demand proxy stacking up against a vehicle that still hasn't proven it can reach orbit. Record backlog is not vindication. It's a promise the market is now discounting.

That cash-burn thesis has a hard number behind it. Rocket Lab held 2.13 billion dollars in cash and equivalents as of June 30th, but it used 134 million dollars in operating cash over the first six months of the year. That is the burn, and it is real. Now layer on the ambition. Rocket Lab is taking on an acquisition of Iridium Communications at an enterprise value of roughly eight billion dollars — structured as 27 dollars in cash plus Rocket Lab stock for each Iridium share, and expected to close in mid-2027. That's on top of two smaller deals already in flight, Mynaric and Motiv. Put it together, and this is Rocket Lab betting its balance sheet on becoming a vertically integrated space-systems operator — a company that builds, launches, and now owns and runs an operating satellite fleet — rather than just a launch provider.

The strategic logic isn't crazy. Iridium brings an operating, revenue-generating constellation — recurring cash flow to set against the cash Neutron is consuming. That's the whole vertically integrated thesis: own the launch, own the manufacturing, own the constellation, and capture margin at every layer the primes charge for. The problem is sequencing. Rocket Lab is trying to buy its way up the value chain before it has proven the launch vehicle at the base of it. If Neutron works, this looks visionary. If Neutron slips into 2027, the company will have layered an eight-billion-dollar integration on top of an unproven core, and the market will keep charging it more for every dollar it needs to raise. Higher ambition, higher cost of capital, worse timing.

There's a subtle twist worth naming in how that deal is priced. The stock portion of the Iridium purchase rides on a collar — an exchange ratio banded between 67.50 and 112.50 dollars a share of Rocket Lab stock — with a notional value of 54 dollars per Iridium share. Which means the effective cost of the deal to Rocket Lab shareholders moves with Rocket Lab's own share price between now and closing. So the very selloff punishing the stock this week modestly cheapens the stock half of the acquisition. That's a small consolation against a much larger problem: the cash component and the integration risk still have to be funded while the core rocket remains grounded.

The second-order read is straightforward. Every quarter Neutron doesn't fly is another quarter SpaceX's Falcon 9 stays the only reliable Western reusable medium-lift vehicle. That's not a hypothetical. It's the default outcome if the 14 percent odds are closer to right than management's guidance. The marginal launch demand — including the defense payloads Rocket Lab has been winning — keeps flowing back onto Falcon 9. Rocket Lab's own order book, ironically, is a bet against exactly that outcome.

Worth a real contrast here, because it sharpens the point. While Rocket Lab burns cash waiting on flight one, China's state-linked constellations keep compounding launch by launch. Qianfan is up to roughly 218 satellites now, Guowang around 190, both climbing toward year-end targets on a steady, near-monthly cadence. That's real hardware in orbit, not a paper filing. Qianfan's operator is reportedly in talks with, or has signed agreements with, something like 30 countries — Brazil, Malaysia, Thailand, Kazakhstan, South Africa — with Brazil expected to be its first commercial market this year. Treat those country deals as hedged reporting, not booked revenue. And treat "megaconstellation" itself with skepticism: the combined fleet is still only a low-single-digit percentage of the tens of thousands of satellites eventually planned. But the lesson cuts the same way on both sides of the Pacific. In this sector, flown hardware is the only currency that counts, and China keeps minting it while the Western medium-lift entrant waits.

Two catalysts to watch from here. First, Rocket Lab's fourth-quarter tank-to-pad milestone for Neutron — the next real test of whether guidance or the prediction market has this right. Second, SpaceX's Starship Flight 14, targeted for late August. Flight 13, back on July 24th, put the booster into a hard but on-target splashdown and sent the upper stage on a successful sub-orbital hop. Flight 14 is supposed to go further — the first attempt to deploy an operational payload into orbit, and possibly the first try at catching the ship stage with the tower, after thirteen flights doing neither. Both are stated intentions, not executed milestones, and Starship has a long history of slipping. But the logic is symmetric. If Flight 14 succeeds, SpaceX's lead widens. If Neutron slips again, it widens anyway.

So here's where it nets out. The space sector's value has stopped accruing to the biggest order book and started accruing to whoever can actually fly. Rocket Lab has the backlog, the ambition, and now an eight-billion-dollar acquisition — and a stock the market just marked down for spending against a rocket that hasn't left the ground. The next few months resolve the bet.
