Good morning. It's Tuesday, October 20th, and this is your Cautious Optimism briefing on the space and orbital economy. Today's thread is that commercial satellites are becoming contested assets in three senses at once: physically, commercially, and financially.

Start with the physical one. A Russian satellite just spent more than two weeks running down a specific commercial radar satellite. The Russian craft is Cosmos 2612. The target was ICEYE-X36, a synthetic-aperture radar satellite from the Finnish company ICEYE. According to The War Zone, Cosmos 2612 began a fresh sequence of maneuvers on September 30th. The independent tracking outfit KeepTrack puts its closest approach in the early hours of October 18th. Then came a small departure burn, about 1.3 meters per second. By the morning of October 19th, Cosmos 2612 sat 292 kilometers behind the ICEYE satellite. So this was a deliberate approach, a close pass, and a clean exit.

Now to how close it actually got, because this is where the headlines and the data part ways. One widely shared headline put the approach within 100 feet. The War Zone's own estimate is in that neighborhood, at about 26 meters. KeepTrack says public catalog data supports only a range of 150 to 650 meters. LeoLabs, a separate commercial tracker, put it at roughly 500 meters. None of these numbers is confirmed by the operator. The low and high estimates differ by about twenty-five times, on the same event, from credentialed trackers.

The real story isn't which number is right. It's that the public space-situational-awareness data available today cannot settle a proximity claim to within an order of magnitude. That is a capability gap in its own right. It matters whatever Russia's satellite was actually doing, because you cannot deter or attribute what you cannot measure.

On intent, the on-record voices are analysts and one allied officer, not governments. Orbital analyst Marco Langbroek called the approach "completely non-consensual." Germany's space commander, Major General Michael Traut, described the pattern as "chasing" and said it "concerns me quite a lot." No government has stated what Cosmos 2612 was for. Readings range from intelligence collection, to signaling, to a rehearsal of something more aggressive. ICEYE itself did comment, contrary to some early reporting. A company spokesperson said ICEYE is "aware of the situation and tracking it closely," and that its constellation is "designed for continuity and resilience." That statement doesn't address Russian intent. It also doesn't confirm the reports, flagged by The War Zone as unverified, that ICEYE-X36 has operated more directly under Ukrainian government control. So the honest summary is this. The target acknowledged the event without confirming the stakes, and no government has characterized the intent.

This also isn't a first. Russia has run this playbook against American government satellites for years, under what trackers call the Nivelir inspector program. In 2020, Kosmos 2542 shadowed a National Reconnaissance Office satellite, though from a far wider standoff of 150 to 300 kilometers. In 2022, Russia put Kosmos 2558 into the exact orbital plane of another NRO satellite, USA 326. US Space Command's commander at the time called that "really irresponsible." KeepTrack's tracking shows the shadowing carried on regardless. So what's new this month isn't the tactic. It's the target. A pattern aimed at government reconnaissance satellites since at least 2020 has now been pointed at a named commercial one.

Connect that to something this show flagged last Thursday. On October 15th, our Throughline carried the report that Ukraine's military Starlink terminals had lost service, with a dispute over who controls that switch. Put the two together, and commercial space is now in the Ukraine war from two directions. It is a lever over service, and it is a physical target.

Here is our inference on what follows, not a reported finding. For operators selling imagery to defense customers, maneuverability and tracking resilience shift from an engineering nice-to-have to both a selling point and a cost line. We found no quoted insurance or contracting response yet, so treat that as a direction, not a priced fact. The episode also strengthens the case for proliferated, many-satellite architectures, the kind the Space Development Agency has been putting into orbit, over a handful of high-value assets. A single satellite that can be shadowed is a single point of failure. What was demonstrated here is proximity, not an attack. But an adversary just rehearsed it against a commercial, allied-aligned satellite with no stated consequence, and that is the part worth sitting with.

If the physical threat is to whoever owns the satellite, the commercial contest right now is over who owns the spectrum. On Monday, AST SpaceMobile confirmed that four of Europe's largest carriers signed a letter of intent. They are Deutsche Telekom, Orange, Telefónica, and Vodafone. The letter commits them to explore a joint bid for European satellite spectrum, built around AST's BlueBird fleet.

That matters because it's the first organized counter-move to an arc we've tracked all week. On Thursday and Friday, we described SpaceX accumulating two scarce inputs its rivals need. One is launch. The other is the 800 megahertz spectrum it agreed to buy from Grain Management, which The Wall Street Journal priced at about eight billion dollars, still pending FCC approval. On Friday we also said carriers wanting a non-SpaceX satellite partner faced a thin alternative. Now four major European telcos have said, in writing, that they want that alternative to exist.

But be precise about what this is. It's a letter of intent to explore a bid, three steps removed from revenue. Spectrum still has to be awarded. A binding commercial contract still has to be signed. And AST still has to field enough satellites for continuous coverage, which is the deployment-pace doubt we raised Friday. The carriers also have a reason to sign something this cheap regardless of outcome. It is a bargaining chip against SpaceX's own direct-to-cell model, whether or not AST ever earns a dollar from it.

So there are two reads. If the letter converts into an actual spectrum position, AST's leverage improves, and European carriers negotiate with Starlink from strength. If it stalls, AST's thin-alternative problem persists, and the letter was mostly a negotiating prop. The market isn't crediting the bull case yet. AST stock remains down roughly 65 percent from its May all-time high, after six consecutive quarterly misses. The thing to watch is whether exploratory language becomes an award, not whether more carriers sign on.

The company those carriers are hedging against reports tonight. SpaceX, public on the Nasdaq since its June IPO, is scheduled to release third-quarter results after the close today. This is its first real test as a public company, and we flagged the date on Saturday. No third-quarter numbers exist as of this recording, so we won't offer any, or any consensus estimates either.

What we do have is the baseline. In the second quarter, SpaceX posted 7.8 billion dollars of revenue. That's up 92 percent from 4.1 billion a year earlier. Against that, it booked a net loss of 541 million dollars. It also ended the quarter with a backlog of 47.5 billion dollars, roughly six quarters of revenue at that run rate. Tonight is the first filed answer to two questions. Does the loss turn? And does that backlog convert into recognized revenue, or stay an order book? One caution in advance. A first profitable quarter would not by itself settle whether Starlink justifies the valuation, because mixed segment reporting can flatter a consolidated number. A backlog is a promise, not revenue, which is exactly the distinction the AST letter is wrestling with too.

Pulling the threads together. What's genuinely inflecting this week is the contest over commercial space assets, both orbital and spectral. Russia has shown it can stalk a commercial satellite, and public tracking can't even agree on the distance. What's still a demo, or a letter, is the European direct-to-cell coalition and SpaceX's claim to sustained profitability. The near-term catalysts are SpaceX's print tonight, and any Russian, ICEYE, or allied response to the Cosmos 2612 approach. After that comes Thursday's FAA hearing on Blue Origin's requested New Glenn cadence cap, and the FCC's review of SpaceX's spectrum purchase from Grain.
