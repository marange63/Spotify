Good morning. It's Monday, August 24th, and this is your private equity briefing.

Start with a concession, because it changes an argument this show has been running all week. Across four sessions, we've treated general-partner marks as private equity's soft spot. On Wednesday it was continuation-vehicle prices set by the sponsor itself. On Thursday it was single-asset vehicles clearing essentially at par. On Friday it was a pension's headline private equity return being a mark rather than cash. Bain and Company's 2026 midyear report cuts against that read. Drawing on a proprietary MSCI analysis, Bain finds that roughly seventy-five percent of buyout assets are still exiting above their next-to-last quarterly mark. That's the valuation a general partner sets immediately before the final mark ahead of a sale. Bain calls it a cleaner measure of valuation accuracy, because it lands before serious price discovery begins. On that test, sponsors are not systematically overstating what they eventually sell.

Qualify that immediately, because the sample is doing a lot of work. It covers only assets that actually exited. The industry's roughly seven-year average holding period exists precisely because a large share of the book has never been tested against a market price at all. So the honest read is narrower than the headline. General partners aren't overstating the assets they choose to sell. That tells you nothing about the assets they're choosing not to. And note where the statistic comes from. Bain attributes it to a proprietary MSCI analysis, not to its own primary research. The release discloses no measurement window, no exit-sample size, and no indication of whether seventy-five percent is a mean or a median. Treat it as suggestive, not as an audit of the industry's books.

So where did the real markdown happen? One sector. Software. Bain's data, current through March thirty-first, shows private-equity-held software valuations down roughly eight percent overall. American software marks fell nearly nine percent, against just over four percent in Europe. And tech deal value slumped seventy percent from the fourth quarter of last year into the first quarter of this one. Bain ties those two together in a single sentence: as anxieties over AI's impact clouded valuations for the tech industry, and particularly the software sector, tech deal value slumped, as fewer large software transactions cleared. That's Bain's framing, not this show's inference. Now date it honestly. That's a June eighth report describing March-quarter marks. We went looking for a June-quarter update and could not find one. So this is the freshest available diagnostic, and it is five months old.

Here's the turn. On Friday, and again yesterday, this show argued that AI-linked assets are private equity's one working channel — the place sponsors can still deploy capital and still find a buyer on the other side. Hold that beside the software markdown. The same AI cycle driving that enthusiasm is repricing the software portfolios sponsors most need to sell. It is one bet, pointed in two directions at once. The chairman of Bain's global private equity practice puts the environment bluntly: private equity has entered a much more difficult and competitive era. So the recovery trade is more fragile than the deal-count headlines suggest, because the entry thesis and the exit risk share a single driver. And because the shock is sector-specific rather than industry-wide, the exposure is manager-specific too. A sponsor whose book skews toward enterprise software carries a repricing risk a diversified generalist simply doesn't. That gap will surface first in how easily each of them raises its next fund. It shows up in realized losses much later, if at all.

That collides with the underwriting math. Bain puts seventy-one percent of value creation now coming from organic revenue and EBITDA growth rather than from leverage or multiple expansion. With two of the three planks of the old return bridge gone, the growth bar rises. Bain models it this way: a deal that needed only five percent annual EBITDA growth to deliver a strong return a decade ago now needs closer to ten to twelve percent for the same outcome. Treat that as a modelled requirement from a consultancy, not an observed distribution of results. Its content is directional. And the direction matters, because software is exactly where sponsors were underwriting that growth. The sector taking the valuation hit is the sector carrying the heaviest growth assumption in the portfolio. There's a mechanical consequence as well. When returns depend on operating outcomes that vary fund by fund, rather than on financing conditions every sponsor shares equally, performance spreads widen. Bain's own numbers already show the gap between top- and bottom-quartile buyout funds stretched to nearly fourteen percentage points. A harder growth hurdle separates winners from losers mathematically, not just competitively.

Against that backdrop, deployment is still shrinking. Sponsors put four hundred and sixty-one billion dollars to work in the first half of this year, on a report published August twenty-first, down ten point six percent year over year. Set that beside the roughly four point three trillion dollars of global dry powder Bloomberg reporting puts sponsors sitting on. That is unspent capital under pressure to move, not evidence that dealmaking is accelerating. Two cautions on those figures. This show cited a much smaller American dry-powder number on Thursday, from a different provider, and these estimates simply aren't standardized across sources. The size composition behind the aggregate is disputed too — Thursday's data had first-half deal count falling sharply on a much larger average check, and I won't assert a direction the sources don't agree on. Take the scale, not the decimal. There is a great deal of capital waiting, and it is waiting longer than sponsors would like.

So if institutional fundraising keeps concentrating into a handful of brand-name platforms, where does the next marginal dollar come from? Increasingly, the wealth channel. Assets in evergreen and semi-liquid private-markets vehicles topped roughly five hundred and thirty billion dollars in 2025, about double where they sat three years earlier. A record hundred and twenty-three of these vehicles launched last year, with another thirty in just the first two months of this one. Wealth investors now make up about a fifth of the assets in these structures. The obvious critique is the liquidity mismatch — quarterly access wrapped around genuinely illiquid holdings. Push past that to the sharper point. An evergreen vehicle's redemption price is the general partner's own quarterly mark, not a realized sale price. That turns a valuation-lag question into a first-mover question. If marks trail a sector repricing — say, in software — early redeemers cash out at the stale, higher value. The cost of the eventual write-down lands on whoever is still holding units when the mark finally catches up. Nothing like that has happened yet in an equity evergreen vehicle, and I'm not claiming it has. But notice the asymmetry with where we started. Bain's comfort about marks comes entirely from realized exits. An evergreen redemption never has to pass that test.

One more piece, on the limited-partner side. This show has covered continuation vehicles twice in the past week as the industry's answer to the exit logjam, both times from the sponsor's side of the table. Here's the missing half. The trade publication Buyouts has reported that CalSTRS' private equity book runs roughly two hundred basis points above its fourteen percent long-term target, and that staff there have been opting to take liquidity rather than roll existing stakes into new GP-led continuation vehicles. That is reported staff practice at one large pension, as of that reporting. It is not board policy, and it is not proof of a broad limited-partner revolt. It's also over-determined, because an over-target allocator has portfolio-construction reasons to want cash regardless of what it thinks of the terms. But it is a real constraint. The continuation-vehicle mechanism only works at scale if enough existing limited partners agree to roll rather than cash out. Every over-target allocator that declines is one less seller-turned-holder on the other side of the trade. If more of them follow, the next round of GP-led pricing becomes a genuine market test rather than a sponsor-set number with thin competition behind it.

Pull it together. The mark-staleness story isn't wrong so much as mislocated. It isn't an industry-wide opacity problem. It's a software-specific repricing, tied to the same AI cycle sponsors are counting on for their next round of deals and exits. Three things would confirm or break that read. First, September quarter-end marks, which reach the tape in manager earnings calls in late October. Second, whether a marquee GP-led continuation vehicle still prices near par once more large limited partners follow CalSTRS in declining to roll. Third, whether tech deal value recovers off its first-quarter base, or whether the software rout turns out to be the new normal rather than a one-quarter shock.

That's the briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
