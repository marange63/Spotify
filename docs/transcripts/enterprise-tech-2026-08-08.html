Good morning. It's Saturday, August 8th, 2026, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

Here's the thesis for today. Enterprise AI demand is no longer the open question. Palantir just settled it. So the market has moved on to a harder question — is this revenue durable, high-quality, and profitable — and this week it punished two respected vendors for failing different halves of that test, even while both beat on the top line.

Start with the settling event. Palantir reported second-quarter revenue of one point nine three five billion dollars, up ninety-three percent year over year and nineteen percent sequentially. That's not a rounding-error beat — that's acceleration at scale. U.S. commercial revenue, the part of the business that isn't government contracting, grew a hundred and forty-nine percent year over year to seven hundred sixty-four million dollars. U.S. commercial remaining deal value — essentially contracted revenue not yet recognized — hit six point two three eight billion dollars, up a hundred and twenty-four percent. The company closed two hundred twenty deals worth at least a million dollars apiece, for total contract value of three point three seven three billion dollars, up forty-nine percent. And Palantir didn't just beat — it raised full-year revenue guidance to between eight point one five and eight point one six billion dollars, up from a prior range of seven point six five to seven point six six billion, along with free cash flow guidance of four point five to four point seven billion. CEO Alex Karp called demand for what the company brands "AI sovereignty" now "unleashed" — that's color from the top, not a metric, but the underlying numbers back the mood. The stock jumped roughly twenty-nine percent the next trading day, its best day in over two years.

The so-what here is straightforward: this is the clearest evidence yet that enterprise AI spending is converting into recognized, accelerating revenue rather than sitting in pilots and proofs of concept. But don't over-generalize it. Palantir is one company, with a concentrated base of government and a relatively small number of very large commercial accounts, riding an idiosyncratic sovereignty and defense narrative. What it proves is that demand exists at the top of the market — not that every AI-exposed software vendor gets to print like this. And notably, Palantir's earnings triggered a broad sympathy rally across enterprise software — Snowflake, MongoDB, Cloudflare, Okta, Atlassian and others all moved up on one company's print, most of them before reporting their own numbers. That's a data point about how thin and reflexive current positioning is — one earnings call moved dozens of unrelated tickers.

Demand is real — but watch what happened two days later. Datadog reported the same week, and it also beat and raised. Revenue was one point one two billion dollars, up thirty-six percent year over year, above its own guidance. Non-GAAP operating income came in at two hundred fifty-seven million dollars, a twenty-three percent margin. Customers paying at least a hundred thousand dollars a year grew to roughly four thousand seven hundred twenty, up twenty-three percent. Full-year guidance was raised to between four point four five and four point four seven billion dollars. And yet the stock fell somewhere between fifteen and nineteen percent, depending on which trading session you measure. Why? Because management disclosed that its largest customer — described only as a leading AI company on a nine-figure contract using seventeen different Datadog products — is cutting its usage starting in the third quarter. That reduction is baked into third-quarter guidance of one point one three five to one point one four five billion dollars, which implies growth decelerating to roughly twenty-eight to twenty-nine percent, down sharply from the second quarter's thirty-six.

This is the first concrete evidence this earnings season that an AI-native company is shrinking its footprint with an infrastructure vendor rather than expanding it. That directly tests an assumption the market has been running on all year — that AI labs and AI-native companies are unambiguously net-additive demand for enterprise infrastructure. Here's one that isn't, at least for now. The so-what isn't that Datadog's business is broken — underlying growth and customer expansion both look strong. The so-what is how the market reacted: punishing a beat-and-raise this severely over a single customer's concentration risk signals a much lower tolerance for revenue-quality questions than existed even a quarter or two ago. That's a read-through worth watching for other vendors with heavy exposure to frontier AI labs as customers — names like New Relic, Dynatrace, and Grafana Labs sit in the same position.

Datadog was a demand-quality story. Now the cost axis. Figma reported the same week, and it also beat — revenue of roughly three hundred seventy million dollars, up forty-eight percent year over year, with adjusted earnings of eight cents a share, double the consensus estimate. But cost of revenue jumped a hundred seventeen percent year over year, and the company posted a GAAP operating loss of a hundred seventeen point three million dollars, on a GAAP operating margin of negative thirty-two percent, confirmed in its own SEC filing. Gross margin compressed to eighty-four percent, down from roughly eighty-nine percent a year earlier — that compression is attributable mainly to AI infrastructure and hosting costs.

Here's where the story needs a correction from the initial read. It's tempting to say AI infrastructure cost is what tipped Figma into a loss — but the arithmetic in its own filing doesn't support that. Total operating expenses rose by two hundred seven million dollars, from two hundred nineteen point seven million to four hundred twenty-six point nine million. Cost of revenue — where AI hosting costs actually live — rose by only thirty-two point six million dollars, from twenty-seven point nine to sixty point five million. So the operating-expense growth was more than six times the size of the AI-driven cost-of-revenue increase. Coverage of the filing attributes much of that operating-expense swing to stock-based compensation, though that specific dollar figure isn't confirmed in the primary release, so treat it as reported, not verified. The accurate read is this: AI infrastructure cost is real, and it's compressing gross margin by about five points — genuine, but a narrower effect than "AI costs caused the loss." The loss itself is mostly an operating-expense story that sits elsewhere in the P&L.

There is a real monetization story underneath this, and it's a more mixed one. Figma says the second quarter was its first full quarter of AI credit monetization, with more than eighty percent of paid customers above ten thousand dollars in annual revenue using AI credits weekly — that's a genuine adoption signal, and it's straight from the filing. Separately, earnings-call commentary — not independently verified against a transcript, so treat it as attributed rather than confirmed — suggests some AI features still in beta aren't yet drawing paid usage, meaning Figma is absorbing inference cost ahead of matching revenue on those. So the fair version of the Figma story is: modest, real gross-margin pressure from AI infrastructure, a genuine monetization ramp already underway, and a GAAP loss driven mostly by non-AI costs. Two footnotes. The company lost its chief marketing officer and chief product officer around the same print — a leadership-continuity flag, though separate from the P&L. And the widely floated idea that Anthropic's Claude Design is actively taking Figma's customers remains market narrative, not evidence — no customer-loss data supports it yet.

So is demand actually rolling over? The backdrop says no. Microsoft's fiscal fourth-quarter print, from about ten days ago, is the context worth holding onto here. Azure grew forty-three percent at constant currency, accelerating from forty percent the prior quarter, and its annual revenue run rate crossed one hundred billion dollars for the first time. Microsoft 365 Copilot reached more than thirty million paid seats. CFO Amy Hood guided Azure to roughly forty-five percent growth next quarter, on unchanged capex. The largest single distribution channel for enterprise AI is still accelerating, not decelerating — which is the important context for reading this week's selloffs correctly. They're not a demand rollover. They're the market applying a sharper bar to revenue quality and cost discipline.

Pull it together. Demand for enterprise AI is settled — Palantir proved that emphatically. The bar has now moved to durability, customer concentration, and margin cost, and this week showed the market will punish a beat-and-raise on either axis. Datadog failed the concentration test. Figma showed real, if narrower-than-advertised, cost pressure alongside a genuine — if early — monetization story. Microsoft's numbers confirm the underlying demand is still there to be won or lost on exactly those terms.
