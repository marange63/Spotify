Semiconductors & Compute Infrastructure

Good morning. It's September 7th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

All week this beat has been moving the binding constraint down the stack. Logic yields, then memory allocation, then packaging slots, then construction crews. On September 1st we argued that a credible second source to Nvidia would show up first in the price a buyer can negotiate, not in how many accelerators get finished. This week that second source actually shipped, and it led with a price claim. Today's story is what that claim is worth, and what it costs somewhere else.

AMD launched Helios, its first rack-scale AI system, and says it is now in full production. One rack connects 72 MI455X accelerators with 31 terabytes of unified HBM4 memory and 2.9 exaflops of FP4 compute. AMD says shipments start at the end of this quarter and ramp through 2027, with demand for the MI450 series running above the company's own expectations. Microsoft Azure and Oracle are named as early customers, and OpenAI and Meta together have committed to 12 gigawatts of AMD accelerator capacity. This is the first time AMD has competed at the rack level, which is the unit hyperscalers actually buy, not the chip.

Apply one discount to that gigawatt figure before it does any work in your head. A gigawatt is a measure of power capacity, not of chips shipped or dollars booked. This show applied exactly that caveat to Broadcom's customer schedule on September 3rd, and it applies with the same force to AMD's own commitments. A commitment to power is a statement about intent and about data-center siting. It is not a purchase order with a delivery date on it.

AMD's headline claim is up to 30 percent more tokens per dollar than Nvidia's Rubin NVL72. Notice what kind of claim that is. Not a peak-performance claim, a cost claim. That is exactly the currency this show said a second source would need to compete in. But independent analysis from the Futurum Group has already checked the math, and it is worth separating from the marketing. The 30 percent figure comes from AMD's own modeling on a single workload, run on AMD's own performance labs data, not an independent benchmark. Futurum also flags a datatype problem in the adjacent peak-throughput comparison. AMD measures against Nvidia's published dense NVFP4 specification, while Nvidia markets Rubin NVL72 on sparse NVFP4 arithmetic. Those two figures describe different arithmetic, so the percentage claims flying between the two vendors are not directly comparable at all. Futurum's own conclusion is blunt: customers should not expect to see AMD's precise claimed advantage in practice. Nvidia, for its part, has not rebutted AMD's number directly. Its public Rubin claims compare the new chip to its own prior generation, not to AMD. So the 30 percent figure is genuinely unrebutted and genuinely unverified at the same time.

Separate this into four scoreboards, because netting them together overstates what has happened. Architecture: real, a 72-GPU rack with that spec sheet exists. Customer relationships: real, Azure, Oracle, OpenAI and Meta are named. Production: asserted but unquantified. "Full production" and "above expectations" are AMD's own words, with no shipment volume and no revenue figure behind them yet. Pricing power: claimed, not verified. AMD has plausibly gained ground on the first two. It has shown nothing measurable yet on the last two. And note the asymmetry in how those scoreboards resolve. Architecture is settled by a spec sheet, which we have. Production and pricing power are settled by an earnings print and a customer disclosure, neither of which exists.

Now the number in AMD's own spec sheet that matters more than the benchmark fight: 31 terabytes of HBM4 in a single rack. That is not a performance figure. It is a claim on the scarcest input in this industry, and there is now a sourced link between AMD's ramp and that scarcity. Samsung was named as the primary HBM4 supplier for the MI455X under an agreement signed in March. Samsung began commercial HBM4 shipments generally back in February, producing the logic layer beneath the memory stack on its own four-nanometer foundry process. That primary designation is not exclusive. Counterpoint Research puts SK hynix at 58 percent of first-quarter HBM revenue, with Samsung and Micron at 21 percent each, so AMD's volume almost certainly draws on all three. What the agreement does confirm is that Samsung now has a second gigawatt-scale AI customer beyond Nvidia.

Which brings us to the bill for all of this. Nvidia will ship zero new gaming GPU architectures in all of 2026, breaking a release streak that runs back to the GeForce 256 in October of 1999. The reason is not chip design. It is memory. A global DRAM and HBM shortage is forcing Nvidia to funnel scarce supply toward its far more profitable AI accelerators. Reporting from The Information, via secondary aggregation and not yet confirmed by Nvidia itself, says the company quietly shelved a completed-design 24-gigabyte RTX 5080 Super refresh in December of last year. The same reporting pushes the next-generation RTX 60-series out to 2028. On the supply side, Micron says its entire 2026 HBM output is already committed under long-term contracts, and the company has laid out roughly 200 billion dollars in planned capacity expansion to address what it calls a historic memory supply crunch.

This breaks a frame this show has run for a week. We have been treating the memory squeeze as a price story: bill-of-materials inflation landing on PC, phone and console makers with no AI revenue to offset it. That assumed the product still ships. Nvidia skipping an entire model year is the harder version of the same logic. Not a price effect, a product-existence effect, and this time it lands on Nvidia's own consumer franchise. A company willing to write off a decades-old product line rather than divert memory away from AI accelerators is telling you something no benchmark could. It is telling you how wide the margin gap between those two businesses has become.

Follow that down to who absorbs it. If the reported timeline holds, the PC gaming upgrade cycle stalls for two years, and a revenue line that Nvidia's board partners and PC OEMs treated as stable and non-cyclical goes to zero for a full year. Those same partners are paying rising DRAM prices on everything else they build. So the memory shortage hits that layer twice, once on input cost and once on the product that was supposed to carry it, and neither hit shows up in the numbers everyone watches at Nvidia.

Put the two stories together and the second-order effect cuts against the optimism in AMD's launch. A credible second accelerator vendor does not add memory bits to the world. It adds another gigawatt-scale bidder for the same sold-out HBM4. The immediate effect of competition upstream is more pricing power for Samsung, SK hynix and Micron, not more finished or cheaper accelerators for buyers. That also tells you where to look for damage to Nvidia. Not in its revenue line, which can keep rising for several quarters while it loses share of each incremental memory and packaging slot, but in supplier allocation commentary. And Micron's 200 billion dollar expansion is the classic supply response to a shortage. Capacity commissioned now typically arrives after the allocation fight it was meant to win, which is the risk sitting inside the bull case for the memory makers.

So what would settle where this actually lands? Four things, and none of them has happened. A named Helios customer disclosing a delivered deployment. An AMD data-center revenue figure with the MI450 series broken out. A memory maker naming a specific AMD HBM4 allocation. Or Nvidia confirming or denying the gaming-lineup timeline in its own voice. Until then, rank what you heard today honestly. AMD's hardware and customer list are real, its production and pricing claims are not yet confirmed, and Nvidia's memory allocation decision is the most solid data point in the whole story, because it is a decision, not a claim.

That's the briefing for September 7th. Have a good day.
