Good morning, it's August 4th. Today's freshest story in digital assets is about custody, not price -- the biggest failure in Bitcoin self-custody history is still spreading, and where the money actually went afterward complicates the tidy narrative you'd expect.

Start with Coldcard, the popular hardware wallet from Coinkite. A firmware flaw traced to a March 2021 integration error routed seed generation to a software pseudorandom number generator instead of the chip's hardware random number generator. That let attackers reconstruct wallet recovery phrases for anyone who ever generated a seed on an affected device. The drain has kept growing day over day. As of August 3rd, Fortune reported the running total at roughly 1,816 bitcoin, worth nearly 116 million dollars, pulled from more than 5,200 addresses across four separate waves. That's up from about 89 million dollars just a day earlier. Treat 116 million as the latest confirmed figure, not a final one -- this is still an open wound.

Here's the detail that matters for anyone who owns one of these devices. Coinkite's own security advisory confirms the flaw was not confined to older Mk2 and Mk3 units. Newer Mk4, Mk5, and Q models are affected too, for any seed generated before specific patched firmware versions. The newer models carry higher effective entropy, around 72 bits versus roughly 40 bits on the worst-hit Mk3 units, which is why the real damage has concentrated on the older hardware. But broader exposure than initially assumed is itself the story. This wasn't a legacy-only problem, and the patch doesn't retroactively secure a single seed ever generated on one of these devices. The exposure is historical, baked into keys that already exist.

So does a hit like this push capital out of self-custody and into regulated wrappers -- the ETFs and qualified custodians that make up the institutionalization thesis? The data says no, at least not yet. On July 31st, net inflows to exchanges -- Binance, River, Kraken, OKX -- topped 11,000 bitcoin, the same day spot bitcoin ETFs recorded 265 million dollars in net outflows. The money that fled self-custody didn't graduate into a fund. It moved sideways into another custodian. That's a meaningfully different outcome, because exchange custody carries its own counterparty risk, and CoinDesk was quick to draw the FTX comparison. The institutionalization story assumes that when self-custody scares people, they climb the ladder into regulated wrappers. What actually happened is they reached for the fastest available custody, and right now that's an exchange, not an ETF. So the honest read is that operational risk in self-custody is not yet confirmed to be feeding the wrapper-migration trade. When bitcoiners get scared, the reflex still runs toward a hot wallet at an exchange.

Which brings us to what the wrapper flows are doing on their own terms, independent of the hack. Spot bitcoin ETFs are having their weakest month since launch. On July 31st alone they shed 265 million dollars, a day after taking in 233 million -- choppy, and net-negative. Zoom out and it's structural. Through the first half of 2026, bitcoin ETF flows turned negative for the first time since these products launched, with 5.4 billion dollars in net redemptions. Meanwhile spot ether ETFs just did something they've never done before. In the July-to-August window, ether products pulled in roughly 3.87 billion dollars while bitcoin ETFs saw about 750 million in net outflows -- the first month ether ETF inflows have overtaken bitcoin's outright. Combined ether ETF inflows across July and August reached 9.3 billion dollars, reversing an eight-week outflow streak. That's a real, measurable rotation in institutional risk-taking, not narrative. It's also one month, so don't extrapolate it into a permanent regime -- but the direction is unambiguous.

Step back and ask what's actually driving the rotation, because it isn't crypto-native. Bitcoin has been trading around 64,000 dollars, going nowhere, while the macro backdrop turned against it. The Fed under new Chair Kevin Warsh held rates at three-and-a-half to three-and-three-quarters percent last week and told markets there is, quote, no soft inflation target. That's a higher-for-longer signal, and higher-for-longer raises the opportunity cost of holding a zero-yield asset. Bitcoin pays you nothing to wait. Ether, through staking, pays a yield -- and the ether products drawing the flows are increasingly the staking-enabled ones, with BlackRock's staked ether product specifically taking money in. So the marginal institutional dollar isn't abandoning crypto. It's repricing crypto against a risk-free rate that just got more stubborn, and rotating toward the wrapper that throws off a coupon. That's the caution on calling this durable, too. If it's really a yield trade, it can reverse the moment the rate math shifts. And it hands bitcoin ETF issuers a genuine problem: when the competing product yields and yours doesn't, you have a design problem, not just a flow problem.

Here's the counterweight, and it's the strongest piece of the bull case. While the ETF tape bleeds, the on-chain data says the bitcoin bid didn't disappear. Long-term holder supply -- coins that haven't moved in more than 155 days -- sits near an all-time high, and those holders have shifted from distributing back to accumulating even as price sagged. Read the two together and the story isn't capitulation. It's a change of hands. The marginal seller is the ETF allocator watching a hawkish Fed. The marginal buyer is a conviction holder taking coins off the visible, liquid tape and into cold storage. That matters for what comes next, because every coin that moves into long-term-holder hands thins the liquid float. A thinner float means the next liquidity shock, in either direction, hits a smaller pool and moves price harder.

Not all the selling pressure here is a market view. Strategy, the company formerly known as MicroStrategy and still the dominant corporate bitcoin buyer, sold 3,588 bitcoin in July. Executive Chairman Michael Saylor said the sale raised about 216 million dollars specifically to fund dividends on the company's Digital Credit preferred securities. That's balance-sheet plumbing, not a change of conviction. But it introduces a mechanical, recurring source of bitcoin sell pressure tied to preferred-dividend obligations, independent of whatever Saylor actually thinks the price should do. From the single largest corporate holder, that's a new kind of supply the market has to absorb on a schedule, not a sentiment.

All of this stays stuck in a patchwork regulatory regime, because the rulebook keeps slipping. The Senate shelved the Digital Asset Market Clarity Act ahead of the August 8th recess, prioritizing other business, with an unresolved ethics provision -- barring senior officials, including the president, from crypto business ties -- still the sticking point. Galaxy Research now puts the odds of the bill becoming law in 2026 at just 30 percent, down from 50 percent a month ago. That's a research-shop estimate, not a legislative certainty, and no confirmed floor schedule exists for when it resurfaces. Until it does, custody and exchange decisions stay fragmented across state and agency regimes -- the exact ambiguity that keeps the next wave of bank and TradFi entry on the sidelines.

Put it together and the honest read is de-risking, not collapse, but the direction of that de-risking is less settled than it looked a few days ago. Capital is leaving bitcoin ETFs for ether ETFs, chasing yield against a stubborn rate. Capital is leaving self-custody, but for exchanges, not for the regulated wrappers the institutionalization story wants. And the rulebook that would clarify where all of this is supposed to land just slipped another quarter.
