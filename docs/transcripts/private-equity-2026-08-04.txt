Good morning, it's Tuesday, August 4th, 2026, and this is your private equity briefing.

The largest leveraged buyout in history is set to close today. Electronic Arts' take-private, led by Silver Lake alongside Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund and Affinity Partners, is expected to complete on or about August 4th at $210 a share, all cash. As one report put it, at $210 per share the deal tops the $45 billion TXU Energy Future Holdings buyout that had stood as the biggest LBO since 2007, with a final structure of $36 billion in equity paired with $20 billion in debt arranged by JPMorgan. That's roughly two-thirds equity. We tracked this deal as an anticipated closing catalyst across the last few days; today it actually happens, and that distinction matters. This is not proof that cheap LBO leverage has returned to the market. It's proof that sovereign-anchored, equity-heavy consortiums can now underwrite deals at unprecedented scale when the debt piece stays disciplined. Watch how smoothly that $20 billion syndicates in the coming weeks — if it clears bank books cleanly, it reopens the financing window for other shelved mega take-privates; if it hangs, the opposite signal. Either way, here's the so-what: this is capital going out the door to buy a company, not capital coming back to any existing limited partner. The entry side of the machine is working. The question the rest of this briefing has to answer is whether the exit side is too.

That question was supposed to get answered today. Apollo was scheduled to report full second-quarter results this morning, and prior briefings flagged this date explicitly as the test of whether realized carried interest — actual cash performance fees, not paper marks — is thawing. It isn't resolved yet. As of this recording, Apollo's own investor-relations site had not posted the release, and no realized-performance-fee actuals were retrievable. What we do have, from an Apollo regulatory filing ahead of the print, is a preliminary estimate: the company estimates that alternative net investment income will be approximately $350 million pre-tax for the quarter, equating to a 9% annualized return on alternative net investments, with Athene's pooled vehicle at 10% and other alternatives at 6%. Be precise about what that is. It's a balance-sheet spread and return metric tied to Apollo's insurance-linked alternatives book, not the fee-related-earnings line and not the realized-carry figure the exit thesis actually turns on. So today's live test of whether carry is thawing at Apollo remains open — flag it as unresolved rather than assume an answer either way.

Blackstone already told us what it thinks the honest answer looks like, and it reported last week. Private equity was Blackstone's standout segment, with segment distributable earnings of $981 million, up 31% year over year, and net realizations rising 27% to $414 million. Those are real numbers, not just fee growth. But the same release included guidance that realizations are expected to slow sequentially in the third quarter before improving later in 2026 and into 2027, citing a stronger IPO market and active M&A activity. That's the largest alternative manager in the world telling you directly that the exit thaw is lumpy, not clean. Separate the two engines here. Fee-related earnings, which climbed 22% to $1.8 billion, is management-fee-driven and grows with assets under management regardless of whether anyone sells anything. Realized carry is the frozen part, and Blackstone is now admitting in its own guidance that the next quarter gets worse before 2027 gets better, contingent on an IPO market that hasn't actually reopened yet. So the fee engine is durable; the carry engine is still a bet on the future.

If cash realizations are lumpy, the industry has found another way to manufacture liquidity, and it's worth being skeptical about what that liquidity actually represents. Secondaries volume is tracking toward $250 to 260 billion for full-year 2026, building on a record $240 billion in 2025 that was itself up 48% year over year. Within that market, GP-led continuation vehicles have shifted decisively toward single-asset deals: they now represent 62% of H1 2026 GP-led volume, and they're pricing at an average discount to net asset value of just 2.9%. That's a striking change. A prior read on this market pointed to widening discounts later this year as dedicated dry powder fell; near-par pricing on single-asset deals breaks that prediction, at least so far. Here's the skeptical read. A continuation vehicle is a transaction where the general partner selects which trophy asset goes in, hires the advisor, sets the timeline, and negotiates price with a small handful of repeat secondaries buyers — Ardian, Lexington, Coller, Blackstone's own Strategic Partners unit among them. Pricing at 2.9% off NAV in that kind of structure isn't obviously third-party price discovery. It can just as easily be the sponsor marking its own homework and finding a counterparty willing to transact near that mark because both sides have a repeat relationship to protect. So what: as continuation vehicles keep growing as a share of what the industry counts as "exits," headline distribution statistics increasingly capture capital rotating from one sponsor-controlled vehicle to another, not cash actually leaving the private equity system for a limited partner's pocket.

That same rotation logic shows up in a live deal this week. Carlyle and Bain Capital are the final two bidders for Wealth Enhancement Group, a wealth-management platform with nearly $160 billion in client assets currently owned by TA Associates and Onex, in a process that could value the business at roughly $7 billion including debt. That would rank among the largest disclosed U.S. private equity acquisitions of a wealth manager, and it's equal to nearly a quarter of this year's total $30.3 billion of global wealth-management deal value. No transaction is signed yet — this is a final-round auction, not a done deal. But notice the shape of it: this is one financial sponsor selling to another financial sponsor, not to a strategic acquirer and not through an IPO. It's the cleanest illustration going of what an "exit" increasingly means in this cycle — capital changing sponsor hands rather than truly leaving the private markets ecosystem. And the same reporting notes that some PE executives now worry the decade-long wealth-management roll-up trade itself is overinvested at these valuations, which is worth banking as a complacency flag rather than dismissing.

Pull the threads together. Every liquidity signal on offer this week looks like a thaw and behaves like something more engineered. Entry capital is flowing at record scale through EA, but it's sovereign-anchored and equity-heavy, not a return of cheap leverage. Continuation vehicles are pricing near NAV, but the GP largely authors that valuation. Sponsor-to-sponsor deals like Wealth Enhancement improve headline exit activity without a genuine third-party cash-out. And the one manager that's been most candid about the difference — Blackstone — is guiding its own realized carry lower next quarter before betting on a 2027 recovery that depends on an IPO market that hasn't shown up yet. Distribution optics are improving faster than distribution quality. Watch Apollo's actual realized-carry figure once it lands, and watch Carlyle's results tomorrow, August 5th, for whether that pattern holds or breaks.
