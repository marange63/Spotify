Good morning, it's Thursday, November 12th, 2026, and this is your private credit briefing.

Two ends of the private-credit balance sheet moved this week. Money wants out on one side. New risk is being pushed in on the other.

Start with the funding side. Last week this desk read the redemption queue one fund at a time, starting with BCRED. Now there's a full third-quarter tally across nineteen non-traded, tender-offer BDCs and interval funds. The data is Robert A. Stanger and Company's, compiled by a secondary aggregator rather than drawn from any filing. It's survey-based, and the method behind its estimate of net asset value isn't disclosed. So hold the precision loosely and read the direction.

The headline is that investors asked to redeem 13.8 billion dollars in the third quarter. That's 11.5 percent of estimated tender-offer net asset value. It's down from 12.7 percent in the second quarter.

The backlog moved the same way. Unmet requests fell to about 8.2 billion dollars, from 9.8 billion the quarter before. Sponsors filled roughly 40 percent of what was asked, versus 37 percent in the second quarter. That fits the modest easing we read last month from earlier Stanger tender data. Every one of those figures moved the right way.

Now set it against the structure. BCRED, whose cross-quarter backlog this desk flagged last week, took in about 4.3 billion dollars of requests this quarter. That's roughly 10 percent of its shares. It held the line at its 5 percent quarterly cap.

Scale that up. Across the nineteen funds, requests at 11.5 percent of NAV run at more than double a 5 percent cap like BCRED's. So even after the easing, demand to exit is roughly twice what a structure like that is built to pay out in a quarter.

Here's why the gross number overstates fresh demand. Blue Owl's Technology Income Corp received redemption requests equal to 39 percent of its shares outstanding. The vast majority were resubmissions. These are investors who asked to leave in an earlier quarter, got cut back by the cap, and filed again. That mechanism is what makes the backlog structural rather than a one-quarter shock. It also means aggregate requests count the same investors' carried-over demand more than once. So the honest gauge isn't the 13.8 billion dollar gross ask. It's the 8.2 billion dollars left unmet, and the fill rate.

Two caveats before moving on. First, the easing reads two ways. It could be calmer investors. Or it could be fatigue, with fewer people refiling requests that don't clear. Second, the better fill rate is itself liquidity leaving these funds. That cash is going out just as the asset class is being pitched harder into 401k and wealth-channel accounts. That's the push behind the Labor Department proposal this desk flagged last Saturday. And one quarter of modest improvement is not a reversal.

So bank this. Redemption pressure stopped escalating in the third quarter, but it did not clear. The exit is still rationed, and resubmissions keep carrying the queue forward.

Now the other side of that balance sheet, the risk coming in. Start with L Catterton's buyout of Fullscript. Per Private Equity Wire, relaying Bloomberg, the debt package is about 1.3 billion dollars. A Morgan Stanley-led bank group underwrote it at a maximum spread of four percentage points over the benchmark rate. Preliminary talks with leveraged-loan investors reportedly failed to generate enough interest. So private lenders are now in talks to provide the financing instead. Apollo and Blue Owl are reportedly among Fullscript's existing private-credit lenders. Final pricing, structure, and covenants haven't been disclosed.

Be precise about that 400 basis points. It's the ceiling the banks underwrote, not a clearing price. The signal is that loan investors showed too little appetite inside that cap.

There are two readings worth holding side by side. One is the bank-to-private substitution this desk keeps tracking. A public market passed at a known ceiling, and a private market is stepping up at an undisclosed price. The other, more cautious reading starts with incumbency. If Apollo and Blue Owl end up providing the new debt, they bring an existing lender's information edge. Then this may be a refinancing in place, not a market-wide verdict on credit appetite. And one failed syndication can't tell you whether loan investors are pulling back broadly or just passing on this credit. Today's reporting carries no broad loan-demand data to separate the two.

It also connects to an earlier thread. On Tuesday this desk covered Blue Owl's listed BDC pricing its new loans wider. My inference is that when the syndicated route closes, pricing power shifts to whichever private lender holds the pen. But Fullscript's own terms remain unknown, so that stays a hypothesis.

Here's what we can say plainly. The public no is observable. The private yes that replaces it won't trade with a visible price. Each time that substitution happens, risk moves from a market that prices it into a book that models it.

Morgan Stanley shows up again, this time in data centers. The bank that led the Fullscript underwriting also authored an estimate on AI infrastructure finance. Morgan Stanley thinks private credit could supply over half of the roughly 1.5 trillion dollars needed for the data-center build-out through 2028. Take that number for what it is. It comes from a bank whose business includes arranging and distributing this kind of debt. That makes it an interested party's sizing of demand, not a neutral outside estimate.

The live test of what that paper is worth comes next week. ING is reportedly auctioning about 500 million dollars of data-center loans, a sale this desk has followed since last week. ING has reportedly declined to comment, and bids are due November 19th. One slice in that book is tied to the Stargate campus in New Mexico.

That matters because of an older data point. Back in September, the Financial Times reported on Oracle's Project Jupiter construction loans for that same campus. Banks were quoting the debt at 89 to 91 cents on the dollar after distribution stalled. That was a bank indication, not a trade, and it's two months stale. But it shows the discount question isn't hypothetical. The ING bids will show whether competitive buyers land near par, or closer to the discount those September quotes implied.

That brings the two halves of today together. Vehicles sold to wealth clients filled only about 40 percent of redemption requests last quarter. Meanwhile, Morgan Stanley's own estimate has private credit funding more than half of a multi-year data-center build. Here's my inference, stated plainly. Long-dated, illiquid construction risk is being aimed at a funding base that already rations exits. Nobody in today's reporting says which vehicles will end up holding the data-center paper, so don't overstate the link. But the shape of the mismatch is visible whoever ends up holding it.

So where does the cycle stand? Stress in private credit is still being deferred inside books that mark to model. This desk has made that case repeatedly and won't re-argue it today. What's new is that fresh risk keeps arriving from two directions. A bank is reportedly auctioning AI data-center loans. And the syndicated loan market just passed on an LBO financing inside a 400 basis point cap. The biggest unresolved risk is that both streams land, at least partly, on a funding base that already rations exits. Its retail vehicles still pay out only about four dollars of every ten their investors ask to redeem. And neither stream carries a public price once it arrives.

Watch three things. First, November 19th, when ING's bids are due, for the first competitive clearing price on data-center loan risk. Second, whether Fullscript's final terms and pricing ever surface. Third, the fourth-quarter tender results. They'll show whether this quarter's easing holds, or whether the resubmission backlog simply rebuilds.
