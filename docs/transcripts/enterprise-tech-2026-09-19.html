Good morning, and welcome to Cautious Optimism Briefings for Saturday, September 19th, 2026. This is the enterprise technology edition.

The most useful number in enterprise AI this month did not come from a keynote. It came from an invoice. On September 1st, GitHub's promotional Copilot credits expired. The included usage per seat dropped, and the seat price stayed exactly where it was.

Here are the mechanics. GitHub's own blog and documentation say a Copilot Business seat is nineteen dollars a month and includes nineteen dollars of AI credits. Enterprise is thirty-nine dollars a month and includes thirty-nine dollars of credits. One credit is one cent. During June, July and August, existing customers got more than that. GitHub's launch blog puts the promotional amounts at thirty dollars for Business and seventy dollars for Enterprise.

Do the arithmetic on those dollar figures and the drop is about thirty-seven percent for Business and about forty-four percent for Enterprise. Those percentages are our math on GitHub's numbers, not a GitHub statement. CloudZero, a cost-management vendor, published the same two percentages. The cut only hits customers who were on the promotion, and the credits pool across the organization.

So what does that tell us? Included usage now equals the seat price exactly. Every dollar of agent activity beyond that is metered overage. The seat becomes a floor, and the variable bill becomes the growth line. Teams that ran on the richer promotional allotment all summer have a usage baseline that overstates what the standard allotment covers. September and October invoices are their first honest read of overage exposure.

That is one vendor. The pattern is wider, and what matters is that it is arriving with published prices. On September 14th, this briefing noted that Salesforce had named seven agent roles and published no rate card. Three days later, at Investor Day, it published a tier at five hundred fifty dollars per user per month, alongside per-seat, usage, hybrid and outcome-based options. ServiceNow got there in May. Its Action Fabric charges outside AI agents by how many operations an agent completes. JPMorgan's Mark Murphy called that effectively a tax on customers using outside AI agents.

Notice what those two vendors have in common. ServiceNow and Salesforce own the system of record. Metering agent access to their data defends the base. The agent can come from anywhere. It simply has to pass a meter on the way in.

Now the opposite attack on the same seat. Google added pay-as-you-go Gemini Enterprise with no base subscription. It also added spending caps and Flexible Savings Plans, ten percent off for a one-year commitment and twenty percent for three years. Google says these are available to select customers and will roll out more broadly, so there is no general availability date. And the news dates to late August, so treat it as a trend input rather than this week's headline.

The so what is a pincer. Vendors with the data are defending the seat by adding a toll. Hyperscalers are removing the base fee entirely. Those are opposite strategies, and both end at metering.

I would offer a skeptical read of the consensus here. The consensus says metering means a bigger revenue pool. The alternative is that metering is what you adopt when you cannot defend the seat. GitHub cut included usage rather than raise the price. Google dropped the subscription altogether. Neither looks like confidence in the seat number. And a price change is not revenue. Metering only helps the incumbents if metered revenue grows faster than the seat revenue it displaces. Salesforce reported Agentforce annual recurring revenue above one and a half billion dollars in late August. That is a total-agent figure, not a metering figure, and it sits against a company guiding to roughly forty-six billion dollars of revenue this year. That gap is the size of the bet.

Next, move down the stack to the freshest item in the file. Yesterday, AWS made the next-generation AgentCore Runtime generally available. Per the announcement, it uses elastic memory management that reclaims unused memory throughout the session, so customers pay for actual usage rather than the peak.

That is the same thesis one layer lower. The application vendor meters access to the data. The hyperscaler meters the agent's own runtime. So one agent action can now be billed by two different parties.

Two cautions. First, billing by actual rather than peak usage is a concession, because peak-based billing is more lucrative. That suggests pressure from Google and Microsoft more than generosity. Second, general availability is not adoption. No customers and no dollars were disclosed.

The buyer-side problem is total cost. CIO's reporting on Google's launch put it well. One user request can trigger an opaque chain of model calls, so consumption can grow much faster than headcount, with the possibility of surprise bills. Stack a runtime meter on a data-access meter and the cost of an agent workflow becomes hard to forecast at procurement time. That pushes buyers toward single-vendor bundles, which is the opposite of the interoperability everyone is announcing. It also makes AI FinOps a purchased category. And the fourth-quarter renewal is where that negotiation happens.

Now the supply side of the same shift. Oracle reported on September 10th, and I will skip the backlog headline, which we worked through yesterday. The interesting part is how Oracle pays for growth. Management said supplier financing, customer prepayments and bring-your-own-hardware structures can support growth. That is commitment converted into cash up front, the same instinct as metering.

Look at the cost. According to ERP Today, free cash flow in the quarter was negative five point four billion dollars, against record operating cash flow of twenty-three billion. Total debt stands at one hundred twenty-five billion dollars. Interest expense rose fifty-five percent, to one point four billion dollars in the quarter.

The bull case says prepayments and customer-supplied GPUs make Oracle's growth capital-light. Test that the other way. Capital-light growth bought with customer money is customer-concentration risk by another name. Prepayments carry a financing component, which means Oracle is effectively borrowing from its own customers at a rate it does not disclose. I am arguing that as a structural feature of prepaid contracts generally, not as a sourced Oracle disclosure. Reporting puts the share drop at around seven percent on an earnings beat. That suggests investors are pricing funding risk, not growth.

The so what: the buyers of AI capacity are being asked to fund it, and the buyers of AI software are being asked to meter it. Either way, the customer carries more of the risk.

Now to the demand side, where metering already shows up as reported revenue. Snowflake reported on September 2nd, so this is a two-week-old print used as evidence, not as news. Product revenue was one point four nine billion dollars, up thirty-seven percent. Management raised full-year product revenue guidance to six point oh seven billion dollars. Consumption pricing is converting into actual revenue there, and that is the strongest evidence we have that metered spend converts.

The caveat is that Snowflake's AI metrics are still counts, not dollars. Its AI product CoCo passed nine thousand one hundred accounts, adding more than two thousand in the quarter. Accounts are not usage, and usage is not revenue. Commentary attributes the acceleration to AI adoption, but no AI revenue line was disclosed. And the same query volume that lifts Snowflake is what triggers the FinOps scrutiny that eventually caps it.

Last, why enterprises tolerate metering at all. At Dreamforce, one customer said the majority of agentic outcomes are not driven by frontier capabilities. They are driven by last year's AI. Docusign's chief executive, Allan Thygesen, described routing each request to whichever model delivers the best value for that job.

That is buyers purchasing inference on a cost curve, not a capability curve. If it holds, cost deflation flows to the application vendors' gross margin instead of to the frontier labs. It also makes a five-hundred-fifty-dollar tier justified by advanced reasoning harder to defend.

Pull the week together. This beat has spent the past week asking when a vendor would attach a checkable number to an agent claim. Today the prices are published, and GitHub's is already on invoices. What we did not find in the evidence we hold is any realized revenue from these metered schemes. We are not saying none exists. So the question moves from whether vendors will charge for agents to whether anyone is paying. That is next quarter's test, and the leading indicators are current remaining performance obligations and net revenue retention at the system-of-record vendors.
