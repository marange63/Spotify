For three sessions this show has asked the same question. Can the industry sell what it already owns. This week it answered by going shopping instead.

Start with KKR's approach for UGI. That's the Pennsylvania-based regulated gas and electric utility that also owns the AmeriGas propane business. On reporting from Bloomberg and the private equity trade press dated August eighteenth and nineteenth, KKR has offered forty-two dollars and fifty cents a share, against a prior close of thirty-five dollars and nine cents. Do that arithmetic and it's a premium of roughly twenty-one percent, valuing the company at about nine billion dollars. Now the part the headline leaves out. This is an unsolicited, preliminary approach. UGI has not accepted it. No board response has surfaced in the five days since, and no financing structure or equity partner has been disclosed. Treat it as exactly what it is right now. A letter, not a deal.

The frame in the press is that this is private equity chasing AI power demand. Be skeptical of that frame, because it belongs to the reporting, not to any disclosed KKR thesis. UGI is a mixed business. It's a regulated utility bolted onto a propane distributor, and propane has nothing to do with a GPU cluster's power draw.

So we went looking for the two numbers that would settle it. What share of UGI's earnings actually sits in the regulated, AI-adjacent utility segment versus propane and midstream. And what valuation multiple the offer implies. Neither could be pinned to a source solid enough to put on air. The segment tables sit in UGI's own filings, which we could not verify directly, and no confirmed multiple for this bid has been reported anywhere we could stand behind. That absence is the finding. The AI-power story is running well ahead of any disclosed financial substantiation. And twenty-one percent over a stock the market had already marked down is not obviously a rich price. A premium to a low reference is not the same thing as a full price. So this may be a thematic bid. It may equally be a value bid wearing a thematic label, and the disclosed evidence does not yet let you choose between them.

What the approach tells you is narrower than the headline, but it's clean. The marginal thing private equity is buying right now is an infrastructure cash flow, not a corporate leveraged buyout. And there's a cost embedded in that trade the deal headlines don't price. A private-equity-owned regulated utility needs state utility-commission approval, and approval-laden deals are slow deals. So the offense here lengthens duration for an industry whose binding problem is already that it holds things too long. The buying problem and the selling problem turn out to be the same problem, viewed from opposite ends.

Because buying is the easy half of this business. Selling is where the strain shows. Zombie funds are vehicles well past their intended life, still collecting management fees while returning little or nothing to their limited partners. Analysts working off PitchBook and Preqin data, in figures that ran in Institutional Investor and in Bloomberg's August nineteenth piece, put those funds at roughly one point two trillion dollars. That's about twelve percent of global private equity assets under management, up thirty-eight percent year over year and nearly tripled since 2019. Two caveats before you bank that. These are analyst-compiled estimates, not disclosed fund data. And the definition varies by source on both the age threshold and the distribution threshold, so part of that growth rate is simply a category becoming fashionable to measure. The harder question the number doesn't answer is how much of that trillion is genuinely impaired versus merely slow, and who is still earning fees on it either way.

The corroborating figure is cleaner, because it measures assets rather than a category. At the end of 2025, roughly forty percent of the net asset value of American private-equity-backed companies, more than eight hundred and sixty billion dollars, had been held for more than seven years. On Friday this briefing named the falsifier to watch: whether realization strength was broadening beyond the same five or six largest managers. This is an early answer, and the answer is no. The back book is aging, not clearing. There's a second-order effect for anyone buying that back book. A growing population of stuck funds means more motivated sellers arriving into limited-partner-led secondaries, which should pressure pricing as a percentage of net asset value even while headline secondaries volume keeps setting records. Rising volume there isn't the same thing as a healthy market.

And where an exit does happen, look hard at the price it clears at. On August thirteenth, Thoma Bravo agreed to take the insurance-exchange platform Accelerant private for twenty dollars and twenty-five cents a share, at an enterprise value above four billion dollars. Reuters and the trade coverage put that at a forty-nine percent premium to where the stock had fallen the day before the offer. It is also below Accelerant's own twenty-one dollar initial public offering price from roughly a year earlier, after the shares had dropped about fifty-two percent from their debut. Those figures come from secondary reporting, not from the merger filing itself. And one company is not a dataset. Thoma Bravo may simply believe the public market got this asset wrong.

The honest reading isn't that initial public offerings are broken. It's that the clearing price for a recent-vintage IPO exit is now being set by a sponsor buying it back, not by the public market that originally priced it. For anyone counting proceeds, that's a worse fact than a shut IPO window, because here the exit happened and still landed below the issue price. It also creates a new supply of take-private targets that has nothing to do with the aging buyout backlog. Sponsors are now re-underwriting other sponsors' failed exits. That's real deployment, and it quietly caps how aggressively the next wave of sponsor-backed listings can be priced.

That price feeds straight back into how the largest managers carry their own books. Blackstone points to eight initial public offerings currently on file as the basis for stronger realizations ahead. But a pipeline count and realized proceeds are two different variables, and the market is treating them as one. Accelerant is what the last cycle of that kind of pipeline turned out to be worth once it reached the public market. Now hold that beside a second disclosure from Blackstone's second-quarter call. A third of the firm's accrued performance revenue balance now sits in positions that are publicly marked. That share of the carry balance is no longer a private estimate at all. It's beta to the same public market that carried Accelerant fifty-two percent below its debut. The old worry about this industry was that private marks lag reality. For the largest managers, that worry has partly inverted. The marks now move with the public tape in real time, for better and for worse.

Now put a number on the distribution side, and be clear that this one is our arithmetic rather than a disclosed ratio. Carlyle's second-quarter release says it returned nearly thirty-seven billion dollars to clients over the trailing twelve months, against roughly four hundred and eighty-five billion dollars of assets under management as of June thirtieth. Divide one by the other and you get about seven and a half percent. The ten-year buyout distribution benchmark this show cited on Friday runs near fourteen percent. Now the caveat, in the same breath. That comparison isn't apples to apples. Carlyle's thirty-seven billion is firm-wide, spanning credit and secondaries alongside buyouts, and those strategies distribute faster than buyout funds do. But notice the direction of the bias. A firm-wide number should flatter the buyout comparison, not depress it. So the point survives the caveat. A manager having a genuinely strong year is still returning cash at roughly half the historical clip.

So where does the cycle actually stand. Private equity is answering its own liquidity problem by switching sides of the trade. It is deploying fresh capital into AI-linked power infrastructure and into cheap post-IPO wreckage, rather than clearing the aging inventory it already holds. That converts a liquidity problem into a duration problem. Worse, it ties the industry's entry point and its one genuinely working exit channel to the same underlying bet on AI-driven demand. A recovery concentrated in a single theme is more fragile than the deal-volume headlines suggest. It's one trade being repriced twice, not a broadening cycle. Watch three things from here. Whether UGI's board engages with KKR or rejects the approach outright. Whether the next sponsor-backed listing prices above or below its own last private mark. And whether third-quarter results show distributions running at anything better than roughly half their historical rate.
