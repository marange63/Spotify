Good morning, it's August 13th, 2026, and this is your private equity briefing.

For five straight days this show has argued one thing: cash is not coming back to limited partners fast enough, and the industry's reported marks are running ahead of what's actually being realized. On Saturday we closed on the mark side of that argument, with CalPERS marking its private equity book up 17 percent. Today that argument gets its cleanest piece of market evidence yet, and it comes from the one corner of private equity where marks actually get tested against real money. That's the secondary market. So let's start there, and then come back to whether the CalPERS number survives it.

Jefferies' review of the global secondary market found that 2025 volume hit a record 240 billion dollars, up 48 percent year over year. Nearly half of that, 115 billion dollars, was GP-led activity, up 53 percent, where sponsors run continuation vehicles rather than sell an asset to a third party. Dedicated secondary capital also hit a record, 327 billion dollars of dry powder aimed squarely at buying these stakes. But the number that matters most for our thesis is on the LP side. Portfolio pricing for stakes sold by limited partners came in at 87 percent of net asset value, down 200 basis points from the year before. So when an LP actually needs liquidity and goes to sell a fund stake in a real transaction, the buyer is paying 13 cents on the dollar less than the reported mark. That is a market-clearing price, set by two sides with money on the line. And it is telling you the reported net asset value is optimistic relative to what a buyer will actually pay today. Here's the so-what: the secondary market is the industry's own price check on its own marks, and right now it is marking them down.

Now hold that 87 percent against the number we flagged Saturday. CalPERS, the largest public pension fund in the United States, reported a preliminary net investment return of 14.8 percent for the twelve months ended June 30th, with private equity the standout asset class at 17 percent. CalPERS credits the gain to a 2022 strategy overhaul led by Anton Orlich, who was just promoted from head of private equity to deputy chief investment officer for private markets. The overhaul refocused the portfolio on manager selection, lower-cost structures, and diversification into venture, growth, and middle-market buyout, away from concentrated mega-fund exposure. Taken at face value, one sophisticated allocator marks its private equity book up 17 percent, while the market where those same stakes actually trade hands is clearing below net asset value. Those two facts are not contradictory. They are the same tension seen from two different vantage points.

Here is the hard qualifier, and here is what is genuinely new since Saturday. That 17 percent is a mark, not cash. CalPERS's release carries no breakdown of realized versus unrealized performance, no distributions-to-paid-in figure, no cash-yield disclosure for the private equity book at all. We've now confirmed that absence runs through the entire release, so state it flatly rather than hedge it. What we can add today is a second data point sitting on the same disclosure. CalPERS's board voted in 2024 to raise its private equity target allocation from 13 percent of the fund to 17 percent. Reporting on this year's numbers now puts the fund's actual private equity weight at 19.3 percent of its 637 billion dollar portfolio, meaning CalPERS is currently running above its own newly-raised target. That is the denominator effect showing up in the open. If distributions were flowing normally, strong marks would periodically get monetized and rebalanced back down toward target, rather than drifting past it. Instead the weight keeps climbing. The overallocation looks less like conviction and more like marks piling up faster than cash is coming out. Read closely, the CalPERS print doesn't break the drought thesis. It's another symptom of it.

So put the two numbers together properly. The CalPERS result is real evidence of manager-selection dispersion. It is not proof the exit and distribution problem has cleared. Top-decile managers may genuinely be pulling away from the pack, and that's the whole design of Orlich's overhaul, betting on selection over scale. If that thesis is right, the lesson for allocators is that manager selection, not brand-name size, is what now separates the winners from the asset-class average. But a strong result at one large, sophisticated fund sits inside an industry-wide drought. It does not refute it. It's a data point living within it. And that points to a second-order effect worth naming. If large pensions keep rewarding manager selection over mega-fund beta, capital keeps concentrating at the margin toward middle-market and specialist managers who can actually demonstrate dispersion. So the drought is quietly redistributing capital toward the GPs who can show realized results, and away from those trading on scale alone. That's a fundraising story worth watching over the next few quarters.

Now notice which of these two numbers is the more informative one. A pension's mark is arrived at by the general partners themselves, on assets that have not been sold. The 87 percent secondary print is arrived at by a buyer writing an actual check. When those two diverge, the check is the harder data. That is also why the record 327 billion dollars of secondary dry powder cuts both ways. It means there is enormous capital standing ready to give LPs liquidity, but only at a discount to the marks. The liquidity is there. The full valuation is not. A continuation vehicle run by the sponsor can paper over that gap by pricing an asset closer to net asset value, but that's the general partner setting the price on both sides of the trade, not a stranger. The cleaner the price discovery, the wider the discount to the mark tends to run.

And there's a further wrinkle inside that record secondary volume. Nearly half of it, the 115 billion of GP-led deals, is not limited partners cashing out at all. It's sponsors moving assets into new vehicles they themselves run and continue to collect fees on. So even the headline number that looks like liquidity is, in large part, the industry recycling assets among structures the same managers control. The genuine LP cash-out, the part where an outside investor truly exits, is the smaller half, and it's the half clearing at that 87-cent discount. Strip out the engineered liquidity and the picture the secondary market paints of the marks only gets less flattering.

So where does the cycle actually stand. The distribution logjam is still the binding constraint, and neither of today's numbers changes that verdict. Together they sharpen the question sitting underneath it: how much of the industry's reported performance is genuinely realizable at anything close to the marked price. The 87 percent secondary print is the closest thing to an answer we've had all week, and the answer is a discount. What would actually confirm the marks isn't another marked-up quarter from a pension fund. It's a disclosed cash or distributions figure from a major limited partner, or a wave of exits priced by a genuine third-party buyer rather than a continuation vehicle the sponsor is running itself. Watch for those next.
