Private Credit & the Corporate Credit Cycle

Transcript · November 7, 2026

Good morning, it's Saturday, November 7th, and this is your private credit briefing.

Yesterday the stress we've been tracking all week sat in loan books and ratings data. Today it shows up in a net asset value. And a proposed federal rule that would open these same vehicles to your 401(k) just reached the White House.

Start with Goldman Sachs BDC's third-quarter print, the sharpest we've seen this earnings season. Non-accruals jumped to 4.2 percent of the portfolio at fair value, from 2.9 percent last quarter. At amortized cost, the same book went to 7.3 percent, from 5.0 percent. That's a cost-to-fair-value gap of roughly three points. It's also the widest reading in an arc this desk has tracked all week. On Wednesday we flagged about a point at Blackstone's BCRED. Yesterday it was about two points at Blue Owl's listed BDC. Now it's three at Goldman's vehicle. So far, the gap is widening print by print, not mean-reverting.

There are two honest ways to read a gap that wide. The generous reading is that Goldman is marking its known problem loans down hard. That's conservative, and it's a point in the manager's favor. The less comfortable reading is that the loss embedded in the book is larger than the fair-value headline suggests. The release doesn't give enough to choose between them, so hold both.

Now the detail that should give you pause. The number of portfolio companies on non-accrual rose by just one, from ten to eleven. CFS Management and MerchantWise Solutions went on non-accrual this quarter. Additional first-lien debt at two existing names, ATX Networks and Streamland Media, joined them. One net new name, and the cost-basis ratio still rose about two and a third points. That isn't a wave of credits turning bad. It's a few large positions going non-accrual at once. Loss recognition in these vehicles is lumpy, and it's driven by position size, not breadth. So quarter-to-quarter BDC credit metrics can swing on a handful of loans. That's why manager selection is doing more work than the asset-class label. Yesterday, Blue Owl's BDC ran the other way. And it's why one BDC is not a read on the whole industry. There's no industry-wide non-accrual figure yet to test it against.

Next, the NAV. Goldman's net asset value fell 3 percent to 11 dollars and 70 cents a share, from 12 dollars and 6 cents. Management put the drop down to 36 cents a share of net realized and unrealized losses. They described the source only as "a small number of older vintage positions," without naming them. The quarter's GAAP result was a loss of 4 cents a share. Net investment income, meanwhile, came in at 32 cents a share. Management called that "in line with our base dividend," so the base dividend was covered this quarter. Behind it sits 87 cents a share of spillover income as a cushion.

So here's the tension worth naming. For a year, the skeptical case on private credit has been that marks don't move. Model-based valuations, the argument goes, sit untested until a real downturn forces price discovery. Goldman's marks just moved, and GAAP earnings went negative. That's a point against the "nothing ever marks" critique. But look at when they moved. They moved in the same quarter the loans went non-accrual, not ahead of it. Recognition arrived with the non-payment, not before it. My read, and it is inference, is that NAV in these vehicles is a lagging signal of credit quality, not a leading one. If that's right, a listed BDC's discount to NAV carries the forward-looking information, not the reported NAV itself. That's one data point, so hold it loosely. But it's the shape of the problem.

So where is that stress actually coming from? Not where the market has been trading it. Fitch's sector breakdown dates from late October, but it's a detail we haven't drawn out yet. And it speaks to a question this desk left open yesterday, when we noted that KBRA doesn't break out software. Fitch does. Technology software has the lowest default rate among Fitch's largest private-credit sectors, at 0.6 percent, down from 1.2 percent. That's under a definition that counts PIK introductions and stressed maturity extensions as defaults, not just missed payments. At the other end, industrials and manufacturing, and consumer products and services, sit at 9.7 percent each. That fits KBRA's data from yesterday, where commercial and professional services ranked among the two most stressed sectors. So the next non-accrual wave looks to be building in old-economy middle-market borrowers. It is not showing up in the software book the market keeps worrying about.

One caveat on that. A trailing default rate lags valuation stress. If AI disruption hurts software borrowers, it shows first in valuations and refinancing terms, not in a default count. So 0.6 percent weakens the software-default story. It doesn't clear software credit.

Now the public side of the same credit pool. The Morningstar LSTA leveraged loan index's trailing twelve-month default rate eased to 2.97 percent as of September 30th. That's down from 3.30 percent at the end of March. The source is an Oxford Lane Capital filing with the SEC this week. That measure counts distressed exchanges and sub-par buybacks, not just hard defaults. So syndicated loan defaults are easing, while Fitch's private credit default rate sits at a record. The definitions differ, so don't read a precise spread between the two numbers. The direction is the story. It's consistent with the mechanism we described on Tuesday. Stronger credits refinance out of direct-lending books and into the syndicated market. The weaker tier stays behind in private hands. Two limits on that read. This is a September snapshot. And the October loan distress ratio we cited yesterday jumped, so the syndicated picture isn't uniformly benign. Still, on defaults, the public market is healing and the private one isn't.

And that private tier is what Washington now wants to open to retirement savers. On Thursday, the Department of Labor filed a proposed rule on alternative assets in 401(k) plans. The Block reports it went to OIRA, the White House unit that reviews federal regulations before publication. That came two days after Democrats swept both chambers in the midterms. So be clear on the stage. This is still a proposal, not a final rule. It hasn't been published, and there's no publication date. In the filing, the department said it was still weighing the costs of the proposal. Finalizing it is the next step, and that's now a race against the new Congress.

One adviser, Wolfe, quoted by Alternative Credit Investor, expects the department to "push hard to finalise the rule before the end of the year." He argues that a Congressional Review Act disapproval would face a presidential veto. In his words, "the midterms changed the referees, not the rulebook." That's one practitioner's view, and it's a framing that suits the industry. It protects a finalized rule only while this president holds the veto pen. Durability past 2028 isn't settled. And even a final rule doesn't move money by itself. At most, it lowers litigation risk for plan sponsors who want to offer these funds. My inference is that sponsor uptake, not the rule, ends up the binding constraint on flows.

Tie it back to the lead. The product being readied for plan menus carries NAV that, on this week's evidence, recognizes loss when a loan stops paying, not before. It sits in semi-liquid wrappers. On Wednesday we measured a redemption backlog carried across quarters at BCRED. If the rule is finalized before January, new savers would buy in at NAV that may not yet reflect deteriorating credits. That's inference, but it raises the political stakes of the next real markdown under a more hostile Congress. The binding risk in the retail channel isn't the headline yield savers get quoted. It's valuation opacity layered onto liquidity mismatch.

So pull the threads together. On defaults, the syndicated market isn't turning. The deterioration is concentrated in private middle-market credit. And it's now reaching marks, NAV, and GAAP earnings at managers like Goldman's BDC. Recognition is lagging the non-payment rather than anticipating it. The biggest unresolved risk is retail capital lining up to enter that recognition-lag system just as the regulatory door starts to open. Watch three things from here. First, KKR's results on Monday. Second, the rest of the BDC prints, including FS KKR and Golub. And third, whether the Labor Department's proposal clears White House review and gets finalized before a more hostile Congress is seated.

That's the briefing. Have a good weekend.
