Good morning. It's September 2nd, and this is your briefing on robotics and physical AI.

Today's briefing takes one cut through this sector, and it is deliberately narrow: how to read the numbers everyone is quoting. Start with the best-disclosed company in physical AI. That is Symbotic — an audited public issuer running warehouse automation at commercial scale, not a private humanoid maker counting its own robots. Its filings are the closest thing embodied AI has to a gold standard. And its most recent filing shows why that gold standard is not the reassurance it sounds like.

Take the quarterly picture cleanly first, because it matters that we get it right. The release Symbotic issued on August 5th covers its fiscal third quarter of 2026, the period that ended June 27th. Revenue was 721 million dollars, up 22 percent year over year. Net income was 55 million dollars, against a 21 million dollar net loss in the same quarter a year earlier. Those are the figures from the company's own release.

Now the line inside the filing that changes the reading. Symbotic's 10-Q for that same quarter reports its largest customer, Walmart, at 82.8 percent of revenue, up from 75.8 percent in the prior-year period. For the full prior fiscal year, more than 84 percent of total revenue came from Walmart. Nothing about that disclosure is hidden and nothing about it is new. The filing is nearly a month old. What is new is that nobody had put the concentration line next to the backlog headline and done the arithmetic. So this is not a legacy artifact from an old filing that diversification has since eroded. Concentration is sitting in the low-to-mid eighties through the most recent quarter, and in that quarter it went up rather than down.

What does that mean? Not, automatically, that the business is fragile. Eighty-plus percent of revenue running through a single master automation agreement, one reported to extend out to 2037, is also a description of a long, contracted installation program. That is closer to a utility relationship than to a wobbly customer account. Revenue concentration and revenue durability are not opposites here, and treating a concentration number as automatically bearish is lazy analysis.

The honest read is narrower than either the bull case or the bear case. Symbotic has a joint venture with SoftBank called GreenBox, plus newer contracts with Target and Albertsons, and those are supposed to broaden the base. But there is no quantified figure anywhere — not in the filing, not on the call — that isolates what GreenBox, Target or Albertsons individually contribute. The company's own language is that Walmart and GreenBox together make up the vast majority of the backlog, which tells you nothing about GreenBox standing alone. So the diversification story stays a claim rather than a number. And that reframes the headline everybody quotes. The 22.5 billion dollar backlog, and the count of revenue-generating systems that gets held up as this sector's cleanest operating metric, are to a large degree one retailer's rollout schedule. They describe a program timeline, not proof of a durable multi-customer automation category.

That has a direct consequence for private marks. Right now, humanoid valuations are routinely benchmarked against Symbotic, because Symbotic is the one profitable operator in the same end market. All of those marks have been comparing themselves to a narrower business than the headline suggested.

The bigger lesson generalizes, and it is the point of today's briefing. Compelled disclosure bounds how much a company can misstate. It does not bound how much a metric can conceal. Symbotic's numbers are audited and real, and they still hid a single-customer structure until someone went back through the filings and did the arithmetic. So the verification problem in this sector is not only the gap between regulated issuers and self-reporting labs. It shows up inside a single audited issuer. The fix is not more regulated companies. It is better-specified metrics. If the best-disclosed number in physical AI still conceals its own structure, the obvious next question is what the next-best-disclosed number is hiding — and that takes us to autonomous vehicles, where the disclosure is thinner still.

Tesla's unsupervised robotaxi footprint kept widening through late August. By August 31st the fleet had logged more than 380,000 miles of unsupervised driving across six metro areas — Austin, Dallas, Houston, Miami, Orlando and Tampa — with autonomous mileage growing at a double-digit weekly rate. Take that seriously for what it is. It is an operating number with a real denominator: miles actually driven with nobody in the driver's seat, not a benchmark run once in a lab. That shape of figure is exactly what this sector normally refuses to give you, and it is the closest anything in autonomy has come recently.

Then put it in proportion. The fleet behind that mileage is roughly 85 active vehicles, and that count comes from community trackers, not from Tesla. Divide one by the other and you get something under 5,000 miles per car — a few months of ordinary driving, spread across six cities. Double-digit weekly growth off a base that small is arithmetically easy and tells you very little about the long tail of edge cases. And what is absent matters more than what is present: no intervention rate, no incident count, no miles per disengagement, from Tesla or from any regulator. So the pattern repeats. A company crosses a genuine threshold — unsupervised, multi-metro operation — and the one number that would let you judge how good the system actually is stays unpublished.

Waymo and Zoox crossed a different threshold in the same window — the commercial one. Amazon's Zoox began charging fares in Las Vegas on August 10th, its first paid commercial launch, ending a free-ride pilot. Zoox has declined to say what it charges. Waymo opened its next-generation Ojai vehicle, co-developed with China's Zeekr, to paying riders in San Francisco, Los Angeles and Phoenix on August 19th. Waymo says separately that it is running roughly 500,000 paid rides a week nationally, and that is Waymo's own statement rather than a filing. Permits are not the story here. The new point is this: a company can cross the line from pilot to paying customer and still tell you nothing about the economics of the crossing. Paid is not the same as priced. Cost per mile, depot cost, utilization, insurance load — none of it is public for Zoox, for Waymo or for Tesla. The intensity of the race is evidence about capital availability, not evidence that anyone has solved unit economics.

There is a real strategic disagreement buried in that contrast, and it is worth naming. Tesla is accumulating validation miles inside a tight geofence with a small fleet. Waymo and Zoox are buying cities and fare-paying customers as fast as regulators will let them. Those are opposite bets about where the binding constraint sits — software maturity on one side, permitting and fleet build on the other. They also imply very different capital requirements, because one path spends on data and iteration while the other spends on vehicles, depots and operations before the economics are proven. Whoever is right about the constraint determines which of those balance sheets was correctly sized, and nothing published in August settles it.

So pull the three threads together. Symbotic gives you a revenue-generating system count that turns out to describe one customer's rollout. Tesla gives you a cumulative unsupervised-mile total with no intervention numerator behind it. Zoox and Waymo give you a commercial launch with no published fare. Those are three different units of account, and none of them converts into the others — which is why ranking this sector by who discloses the most is the wrong test. And note where that leaves the humanoid and robot-foundation-model layer, which is not today's cut but is where the test bites hardest: unit counts there are self-reported by the makers, and model results are published by the labs that trained them, with no fielded reliability figure attached at all. Name what would settle each case instead. Symbotic disclosing GreenBox's actual dollar contribution. Tesla or a regulator publishing an intervention rate against those miles. Zoox or Waymo publishing a fare and a cost per mile. Until one of those lands, treat every headline number in this space as partial by construction, not by accident.
