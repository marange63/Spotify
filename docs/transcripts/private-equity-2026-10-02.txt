Good morning, it's Friday, October 2nd, 2026, and this is your private equity briefing.

Today's story is simple to state and uncomfortable to sit with. The strongest buyout managers are getting cash out the door again, but only from certain kinds of companies.

Start with Warburg Pincus. Speaking at SuperReturn Asia this week, chief executive Jeffrey Perlman said the firm has realized around twelve billion dollars from exits so far in 2026. That already matches the record amount Warburg returned across the whole of 2025, and he expects to beat it before year-end. Yesterday this show said no industry-wide distribution data had moved. This doesn't overturn that read, because it is one firm, not the industry. But it is the first on-record realization upturn we've seen from a top-tier manager in this stretch.

Now look at how that twelve billion was built. The two largest exits were very different animals. The first was Consolidated Precision Products, an aerospace-parts supplier. Warburg and co-owner Berkshire Partners agreed to sell it to GE Aerospace for eleven and three-quarter billion dollars. That's the headline deal value, and no split between the two sponsors has been disclosed. The second was Ensemble Health Partners, a healthcare-services business, which was only partially sold, to investment firm Thoreau. One is a clean, full handoff to a strategic buyer. The other is a slice sold while Warburg's funds keep exposure to the rest. Partial sales return some cash, but they also re-mark the retained stake, so part of the headline is a new valuation rather than money in hand. It's the same liquidity-in-slices shape we saw yesterday in Bain Capital's partial sale of MRO Holdings to AAR. That shape is becoming the default way this cycle's assets get monetized, not the exception.

Then Perlman said the line that matters more than the total. In his words, "you can't sell the software businesses so easily." That tells you where the twelve billion did not come from. Perlman's quoted line doesn't give the reason, so treat the cause as our read rather than his. And there is independent data pointing the same way. Forvis Mazars and PitchBook report that median EV to EBITDA multiples on private-equity SaaS deals fell to eleven point seven times in the first half of this year. The prior comparison was twenty point four times. That's a drop of more than forty percent in what software assets command.

So the exit recovery isn't broad. It's sorted by sector. The clean exits this show has logged lately, aircraft maintenance going to AAR and now aerospace parts going to GE Aerospace, both went to corporate buyers. Those strategics are bidding for physical, scarce, cash-generative businesses. Software platforms bought at the last cycle's peak don't appear to have an equivalent strategic bid. Treat the twelve billion as real progress at one firm, and treat the software line as the honest asterisk attached to it.

Keep three caveats in view before anyone extrapolates. First, this is a chief executive's conference remark, not an audited fund disclosure. Second, twelve billion is gross realization value. There's no net-of-fees figure and no fund-size or NAV denominator, so it can't be turned into a distributions-to-paid-in ratio for any limited partner. Third, there's selection at work. Managers tend to talk numbers on a fundraising-adjacent stage when the numbers flatter them. One firm's record is a sample of one, not a read on the industry median. For allocators, the gap between a manager's realization headline and fund-level DPI is exactly where complacency sits. The real signal is dispersion between managers, not the best manager's headline.

Now, if software is the door that won't open, watch where the new money is walking in instead. Bloomberg reported on Tuesday that Bain Capital is among the bidders for Edged. Edged is a data-center developer founded by Koch Inc. Koch's sale process could value the business at more than fifteen billion dollars. That number is the valuation the process is seeking, not Bain's offer price. And the process itself has been reported since late July. The fresh fact is that Bain has stepped into it. There's no signed deal, no disclosed equity check, and no named financing or co-investors.

What makes Bain's entry notable is its own earlier posture. Bain managing partner David Gross warned late last year that the surge of investment into AI infrastructure could be creating, in his words, "a massive arms race." Now his firm is bidding to own a piece of that buildout.

There are two honest readings. One is plain inconsistency. Warn about an arms race, then chase the asset anyway, because the fear of missing out beats the fear of overpaying. The other reading is more interesting. Owning the data centers and the power access underneath the AI buildout is a bet on selling into the arms race, not on winning it. It's the difference between owning the landlord and owning the tenant's business.

Either way, the risk profile is new territory for a generalist buyout fund, and this part is our analysis. Classic leveraged buyouts are underwritten on compounding cash flow from an operating business. A data-center platform's value rests on long-dated leases to a few hyperscale tenants and on securing power. That's a duration-and-concentration risk, not an operating-margin risk. It also puts buyout funds in direct competition with infrastructure-dedicated capital, which has underwritten those risks for longer.

There's a financing angle too, again as our inference. Yesterday we relayed, secondhand, a KKR credit report describing an unusually concentrated AI-infrastructure borrowing cycle. An equity buyer at Edged's scale would need that same credit market for its debt leg. So the question isn't only whether Bain wants this asset. It's whether lenders already crowded into AI want more of it.

And notice the Bain Capital through-line across two days. Yesterday it was taking partial liquidity out of MRO, an aircraft-maintenance business that a strategic wanted. This week it's bidding to put capital into AI physical infrastructure. That's capital recycling in real time. So buyout capital isn't solving its exit problem here. It's swapping a stuck-exit risk for a duration and capex-cycle bet on an asset class nobody in buyouts has owned through a full cycle.

That migration doesn't fix the backlog sitting behind both stories. Treo Asset Management estimates that so-called zombie funds hold about one point two trillion dollars, or twelve percent of global private-equity assets under management. These are vehicles more than ten years old that no longer invest but still hold unsold companies. Two flags on that number. It's from July, so treat it as standing context rather than this week's news. And Treo sells liquidity solutions, so it benefits commercially from a zombie narrative.

The more useful part is the explanation a Treo partner gave. General partners have been reluctant to sell below peak NAV, he said, "because they've gotten accustomed to the marks that they saw four years ago." Four years before that July report puts you near the tail of the 2021 and 2022 peak. Here's our inference connecting the two stories. The vintages anchored to the stalest marks are likely the same ones that paid peak software multiples. Those owners are now facing a market where the median SaaS deal clears near twelve times. That makes the logjam as much a pricing problem as a liquidity problem.

So where does the cycle actually stand? Realizations are genuinely improving at the strongest managers, but only where strategic buyers want what's being sold, in aerospace and healthcare services. Software and the aged inventory bought at the peak remain the logjam. Meanwhile new sponsor capital is rotating toward AI infrastructure rather than toward clearing that backlog. The biggest unresolved risk is still the exit-and-distribution logjam, now visibly concentrated in software-era vintages rather than spread evenly across the asset class.

Watch three things. First, whether the Edged process produces a signed deal, and at what price. Second, whether other managers echo Perlman's software comment. Third, Blackstone's third-quarter results on October 22nd, the next hard read on realizations.
