Good morning, it's Wednesday, July 29th, and this is your private equity briefing.

We spent the past several days reading the exit-and-distribution logjam off proxies — a weak quarter of exit value here, a soft IPO print there, Blackstone guiding to an air pocket in realizations. Bain & Company's own 2026 Global Private Equity Report now puts an authoritative number on what those proxies were hinting at. Distributions to limited partners as a share of net asset value have stayed below 15 percent for four consecutive years, which Bain calls an industry record. In 2025 specifically, that figure was 14 percent. This is not a rounding-error soft patch. Across the industry, roughly 32,000 portfolio companies worth an estimated $3.8 trillion sit unsold on general partners' books, and average holding periods at exit have stretched to about seven years, up from five to six years for most of 2010 through 2021. So the mechanism that's supposed to turn paper marks into cash for LPs has been running at historically depressed throughput for four straight years running — this is structural, not cyclical.

Now, the headline the industry will use to argue otherwise is the 2025 rebound in deal activity: buyout deal value climbed 44 percent to $904 billion, and exit value rose 47 percent to $717 billion. Those look like thaw numbers. But look at where that deal-value growth actually came from. Bain's own release states that just 13 megadeals above $10 billion combined for $274 billion, fully 30 percent of the entire year's global deal value. That's an extraordinary concentration in a handful of trophy transactions — and overall deal count still fell 6 percent year over year. Meanwhile buyout fundraising itself dropped 16 percent, to $395 billion, the fourth consecutive annual decline. So the recovery general partners will show limited partners in fundraising decks this year is a story about a dozen or so giant deals, not a broad-based clearing of the backlog. The median portfolio company, and the median mid-market vintage, is not living inside that 44 percent headline. The honest read is that the exit machine is still stuck, and the deal-value bounce is concentrated exactly where you'd expect capital to chase scarce, financeable, brand-name assets.

And that 16 percent fundraising decline is not a separate story from the distribution drought — it's the same story one step downstream. When distributions stall, limited partners don't get cash back to recycle into the next vintage, and their existing private holdings swell as a share of the total portfolio even without new commitments. That denominator effect crimps re-ups. Starve LPs of cash for four years and the fundraising machine that the whole model depends on starts to grind. It also pushes LPs to lean harder on the secondaries market to manufacture their own liquidity, selling fund stakes rather than waiting for GPs to sell companies. So the real risk hiding behind record assets under management is this: the flywheel that turns realized cash into fresh commitments is losing speed, and no single strong IPO quarter reverses four years of it.

Here's the tension that matters most right now: while the exit side stays frozen and cash to LPs stays starved, the entry side of the cycle looks genuinely hot. Sponsors are competing hard, and paying up, to deploy capital into public-to-private processes. The clearest live example is easyJet. Castlelake reached an agreement in principle with the airline's board on July 5th at 690 pence a share, a 73 percent premium to easyJet's late-May closing price. Five days later, Apollo tabled a richer counter-offer at 715 pence a share, prompting easyJet's board to say it was no longer minded to recommend the Castlelake proposal. Both approaches remain non-binding agreements in principle under UK takeover rules, and both face hard deadlines within days of this briefing — Castlelake must announce a firm offer or walk away by August 3rd, Apollo by August 7th. Either bid could still lapse, and a completed deal would face additional scrutiny under EU airline foreign-ownership rules, since Castlelake had proposed holding only a 49 percent stake alongside two EU-national co-investors. But set the regulatory uncertainty aside for a moment: the underlying signal is that two large sponsors are bidding each other up for a capital-intensive, low-multiple-expansion operating asset in a supposedly disciplined capital environment. That's the opposite of the caution the industry claims to be exercising.

You see the same pattern in Australia, where EQT has now raised its offer for asset manager Perpetual three times this month — from 21.64 Australian dollars a share on July 1st, to 22.07 mid-month, to 22.50 on July 26th, a premium of almost 19 percent to the prior close. That offer stays conditional on Perpetual's separate wealth-management carve-out to Bain Capital closing first, and Perpetual's board hasn't recommended it yet. But the sequential-bid pattern is the point: sponsors are escalating, not standing on the sidelines. Put easyJet and Perpetual together and the picture is consistent — entry-side competition for public targets has thawed considerably faster than the exit side that actually returns cash to investors.

So where is all that dry powder that isn't chasing classic leveraged buyouts actually landing? Increasingly, in long-duration, sovereign-backed infrastructure rather than traditional corporate control deals. Blackstone, Brookfield and KKR each just took an equal share of a 49 percent joint-venture stake in Kuwait Oil Company's crude pipeline network, a $16 billion lease-and-leaseback structured over 20 and a half years with a volume-based tariff, generating $7.85 billion of upfront proceeds for Kuwait. Kuwait Oil Company keeps 51 percent and full operational control, so this is a minority economic interest, not ownership, and it's coming out of these firms' infrastructure platforms rather than their flagship buyout funds. But the logic still tracks the broader theme: when corporate exits are scarce and leverage-and-multiple-expansion economics have faded, the largest multi-strategy managers are rotating toward contracted, yield-like cash flows with sovereign counterparties. That diversifies fee-related earnings away from exit-dependent carry. It also deepens these firms' capital ties to a narrow set of Gulf sovereign wealth funds — a concentration that's becoming a recurring feature of where the biggest checks in this industry now get written.

Pulling the threads together: the private equity cycle currently has two speeds. The exit and distribution side is stuck at what Bain calls a record low — four straight years of sub-15 percent distributions, with nearly $4 trillion of inventory waiting for a buyer and holding periods still lengthening. The entry and deployment side is comparatively hot, with sponsors bidding up premiums for scarce public targets and routing capital into sovereign infrastructure deals that don't depend on a buyout exit at all. The near-term catalysts to watch: KKR reports earnings tomorrow, July 30th, followed by Apollo on August 4th and Carlyle on August 5th, which will show whether Blackstone's guided realizations air pocket and modestly thawing IPO pipeline are firm-specific or industry-wide. And the easyJet deadlines on August 3rd and August 7th will tell us whether that bidding war converts into a binding offer or lapses. Until distributions actually accelerate, the deal-value headlines are a marketing story more than a liquidity story.

That's the state of the private equity machine. Thanks for listening, and I'll see you tomorrow.
