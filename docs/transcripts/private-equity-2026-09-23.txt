Good morning. It's September 23rd, and here's where the private-equity cycle stands.

Start with the deal that closed this week. On September 18th, the FedEx and Advent consortium completed its tender for InPost, the Polish parcel-locker operator. Shareholders tendered 89.81 percent of shares, clearing the 80 percent minimum. The offer was all-cash at 15.60 euros a share, valuing InPost at 7.8 billion euros. Per the original deal announcement, that price is a 50 percent premium to the undisturbed share price on January 2nd. Against the three-month volume-weighted average before that date, it's 53 percent. Those two numbers sit close together, so the premium isn't an artifact of one bad trading day. The deal was agreed back in February. What's new this week is the close and the acceptance level, not the price.

The ownership split is the real story. After settlement, FedEx and Advent International each hold 37 percent of the consortium. A&R Investments, the vehicle of founder and chief executive Rafał Brzoska, holds 16 percent. The Czech group PPF holds the remaining 10 percent. On financing, the four parties committed 5.918 billion euros of equity, according to the announcement. Banks committed up to 4.95 billion euros of debt on a certain-funds basis. So the equity commitments exceed the debt ceiling. Whatever this deal is underwriting, it isn't leverage.

That means Advent, one of the world's largest buyout firms, wrote a minority of the equity in its own take-private. That's the pattern worth naming. Large entries are clearing because a strategic, a rolling founder, or another financial partner shares the cheque, not because one drawdown fund underwrites the whole thing. On Monday, this show argued that the GFL contest showed no single sponsor could underwrite that asset alone. The Baldwin deal made a related point, with permanent capital setting the price. InPost is what that pattern looks like once it actually closes.

There's a cost to Advent in that structure, and it lands on the manager's own economics. A 37 percent stake means fees and carry on a smaller slice of the equity than a sole-sponsor deal of the same size would give. The eventual exit is also shared with FedEx, a strategic owner whose logistics rationale doesn't run on a fund clock. When Advent wants to sell, it needs a partner who may prefer to keep holding. So the shared cheque that got the deal done also dilutes the sponsor's control over how and when cash comes back.

Keep one distinction clean. This is capital going in, not cash coming back. Completing a take-private deploys Advent's fund capital, and it returns nothing to Advent's limited partners. So don't read the close as evidence the exit window has thawed. It's a deployment story wearing a transaction headline. One question stays open. No source in hand establishes whether Advent carried an earlier InPost stake into this consortium, so I won't call any part of this a realization for Advent.

Two mechanical points also remain unsettled. At 89.81 percent tendered, the consortium is short of the 95 percent that would trigger a statutory squeeze-out of the remaining holders. And the decision on declaring the offer unconditional was due by today, and it hadn't been announced as of this recording. Treat both as open.

The same sponsor is on the other side of the ledger this year, though. Advent is reportedly nearing a close on its latest flagship buyout fund. Per sources speaking to Buyouts, it has generated more than 12 billion dollars of realization proceeds so far in 2026. Bound that carefully. It's an unnamed-source figure from a paywalled trade report. It's gross proceeds across funds, with no paid-in denominator, so it isn't a DPI number. And no fund target or close size is on the record. Directionally, though, it says something real. Cash returned, not marks on a spreadsheet, is what's carrying a flagship fundraise right now. On Sunday this show saw the same thing with Carlyle and KKR. Advent is a second named case.

Now the denominator Advent's number doesn't have. Foley & Lardner's September analysis puts 2017-through-2021 vintage funds below 1.0 times distributions-to-paid-in capital. The 2019-through-2021 vintages sit below 0.35 times. Some of that is simple immaturity. Those funds are five to seven years old, and DPI builds slowly. The worry isn't the level itself. It's whether the gap to historical pace is closing, and nothing in this data says it is. Behind those vintages sits the inventory. CBIZ, cited by Foley, estimates more than 13,500 U.S. companies in sponsor portfolios, nearly a third of them older than six years. That's a domestic count. It differs in scope from the global inventory figures this show cited earlier in the week, so treat it as a different lens, not a competing estimate.

Then the fundraising split. In Foley's tally for the year, experienced managers took 139.3 billion dollars, against 20.3 billion for emerging managers. That's roughly seven to one. Foley calls it concentration, not collapse, and that's the right frame. Now put Advent's number next to Foley's. An LP sitting on 2019-to-2021 funds below 0.35 times has less cash to recycle. So it sizes its re-ups toward the managers who can show distributions. That pushes more of the next fundraise toward the handful of brands with a realizations haul, which compounds the concentration. The distribution gap is becoming a fundraising gap for everyone else.

So where does that stranded inventory go, if not through a sale or an IPO? Increasingly, into continuation vehicles, and the fastest-growing kind is single-asset. Evercore's first-half review, published in July, is the freshest full read of this channel. Single-asset continuation-vehicle volume hit 34 billion dollars, up 88 percent year over year. That's now 53 percent of all GP-led secondary volume. Continuation-vehicle exits made up 14 percent of global sponsor-backed exit volume, roughly one exit in seven. And 82 of the world's 100 largest sponsors have now done at least one. One clause of caution: the advisers who publish these tallies earn fees on the volume they count.

This show has covered the governance fight all week, the regulatory scrutiny and the conflicts questions, so I'll leave it there. Today's increment is sizing. Single-asset growth means sponsors aren't just clearing diversified tail portfolios. They're keeping their best individual assets, at a price set by a buyer group they helped assemble, and counting that as an exit. The LP who rolls into the new vehicle hasn't been paid a dollar. There's no pricing data in hand on whether these deals clear at, above, or below the prior mark, so I won't claim either way.

There is a second-order problem for the marks, though. If the best assets move into continuation vehicles, what's left for true third-party sales is the rest of the inventory. That's the part of those 13,500 U.S. portfolio companies the continuation channel isn't clearing. It's also where marks are least tested by a real buyer. The so-what is plain. A meaningful and growing share of reported exit volume is a related-party transfer, not a market-clearing sale.

Pull it together. The cycle is clearing in two specific places. At entry, it clears through shared cheques like InPost's. At the top of the fundraising table, it clears through managers who can show cash. Everywhere else, the logjam hasn't moved. That means the 2019-to-2021 vintages below 0.35 times, and the aging portfolio companies with no strategic buyer and no IPO window. That's still the binding risk: an exit-and-distribution problem that engineered liquidity can dress up but hasn't solved. Three things would confirm or refute this read. First, third-quarter earnings from the public alternative managers, starting in late October. Second, whether Advent's flagship actually closes, and at what size. Third, whether anyone publishes real pricing on what continuation vehicles pay relative to the prior mark.

That's the state of the machine. Talk to you next time.
