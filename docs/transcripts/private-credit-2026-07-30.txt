Good morning, it's July 30th, 2026, and this is your private credit briefing.

We've been telling you for two days running to watch one number: whether Ares Capital's non-accrual rate, sitting at 2.1% of cost after the first quarter, would move up or down when the second-quarter print landed. It landed yesterday, and it moved up. Ares Capital, the largest listed business development company and the bellwether for the entire direct-lending industry, reported that loans on non-accrual status at amortized cost rose to $708 million, or 2.4% of the total portfolio, up from $546 million, or 2.0%, a year earlier. At fair value the increase is smaller but points the same direction: 1.4% versus 1.2%. That's the test this show flagged twice, and the answer is now in primary numbers, not speculation.

Non-accruals didn't move alone. Net asset value per share slipped to $19.35, down from $19.59 last quarter and $19.90 a year ago. And portfolio-company leverage, the debt multiple borrowers are carrying against their own earnings, climbed to 5.8 times. Three metrics, all moving the wrong way, in the same quarter, at the single most-watched vehicle in the asset class. That's the headline: the first hard, primary-source confirmation that the private credit cycle is normalizing upward in stress, not just in the media narrative that's been building around it.

Now weigh that skeptically, because Ares wants you to read it a different way. Core earnings per share held flat at 47 cents, and the board maintained the 48-cent quarterly dividend. Ares's own framing stresses that non-accruals remain below its long-term historical average. Both of those things are true, and neither of them settles the question. A rise off an unusually low base could be simple normalization, or it could be the early edge of real deterioration — the honest answer is we don't know yet, and anyone telling you definitively either way is selling something. What we do know is that private BDC valuations are model-based and struck once a quarter. So a reported NAV that looks merely soft can be lagging real deterioration underneath, because nobody is marking these loans to a live market every day the way a public bond trades. The dividend held and the marks held. That is not the same as the book being tested.

Here's where the leverage number actually bites. Ares also disclosed, in its own release, that the weighted average yield on debt and other income-producing securities it funded this quarter was 9.4%, and 9.1% across total investments funded, both at amortized cost. We don't have a prior-quarter comparison for that specific yield, so I won't claim a direction on pricing — but take the level as given: call it roughly 9% compensation for taking on borrowers whose leverage just climbed to 5.8 times. If that multiple keeps climbing while the yield you're paid for it doesn't move up to compensate, the recovery cushion under each loan gets thinner. Covenant protections were already loosened industry-wide when spreads compressed over the last two years. So rising leverage into a period where yields aren't visibly widening is exactly the setup that turns a routine credit cycle into a painful one when it turns — not a crisis today, but the kind of quiet math that shows up in recoveries two years from now.

And the supply side of the lending market is pushing in the same direction. Recall the deployment picture we've laid out this week: US direct-lending volume fell about 55% quarter on quarter in the second quarter, to $33.6 billion, even as fundraising into the strategy ran at its fastest pace in two years. That gap is the mechanism. Committed capital keeps piling up while the number of financeable deals shrinks, and a growing pile of unspent money chasing fewer deals pressures managers to compete on price and on terms, not to hold the line on them. So the same quarter that gave us rising borrower leverage at the bellwether is a quarter where the structural incentive is to loosen underwriting, not tighten it. That's the wrong reflex at this point in the cycle, and it's the reflex the dry-powder overhang is quietly rewarding.

Now, where does that risk actually sit inside the book? Software and services remained Ares's single largest sector exposure, at 22% of its $29.3 billion fair-value portfolio. That's the specific transmission channel worth naming. There's been reporting that a chunk of that software book carries elevated risk from AI-driven revenue disruption — the idea that AI coding and automation tools erode the pricing power of legacy enterprise software borrowers. I can't put a verified number on how much of that exposure sits at elevated risk; the underlying disclosure hasn't been confirmed in a form I can quote directly, so I'll leave it exactly there — a real, named concentration risk, not a quantified one. What matters for you as an allocator is simpler: nearly a quarter of the largest BDC's book sits in the one sector most exposed to a technology shock that's still unfolding in real time, and the marks on that quarter won't update until the next print. That's the mark-to-model gap and the AI-disruption gap sitting on the same 22% of the portfolio at once.

That concentration has a mirror image on the real-asset side of private credit, and it's worth the hand-off because it's the same underlying bet wearing a different structure. Blue Owl and Pimco recently structured a $27.3 billion bond offering with Meta to finance a two-gigawatt data-center campus in Louisiana — part of more than $40 billion in combined 144A private placements from data-center issuers since November. Morgan Stanley has projected private credit will supply an additional $800 billion in data-center financing over the next two years; treat that as a forecast, not booked flow. Put the two pieces together. Ares's software concentration is the corporate-loan side of the AI capex story, and these data-center placements are the real-asset side of the same story. The industry markets these as diversified exposures. They're closer to one correlated bet on hyperscalers continuing to spend at the current pace. A digestion phase in AI capital expenditure wouldn't hit one pocket of private credit — it would hit software loans in BDC books and data-center loans in structured placements at the same time, because they're both downstream of the same spending decision at a handful of companies. Correlation dressed up as diversification is exactly what you don't want to discover the day the capex cycle cools.

So where does the cycle actually stand? The arc we've been tracking resolved this week, and it resolved in the direction of caution: non-accruals up, NAV down, leverage up, all at the bellwether lender, alongside a book still concentrated in the sector most exposed to AI disruption, and a deployment market that rewards loosening terms just as borrower leverage rises. That's deterioration at the margin, not a broken model — the earnings and the dividend both held. The biggest unresolved risk is the mark-to-model gap: private valuations struck quarterly, on a book that's leaning into a fast-moving technology risk nobody has fully priced yet. The near-term catalysts to watch are the rest of Q2 BDC earnings season, particularly from Blue Owl and Apollo where we still lack verified primary numbers, and whether the non-accrual trend Ares just confirmed shows up industry-wide or turns out to be idiosyncratic to one lender's book.
