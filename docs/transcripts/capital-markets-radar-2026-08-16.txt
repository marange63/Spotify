Good morning, it's Sunday, August 16th, and this is Capital Markets Radar.

We're leading with the first concrete catalyst to come out of a week-long theme we've been calling "priced for perfection." Reuters reported on August 13th that Silver Lake is in talks to take Workday private, in a deal that could rank among the largest software buyouts ever. Workday's standalone market value was cited at about 43 billion dollars going into the report. The stock surged on the news, up roughly 18 to 26 percent depending on the source, pushing its market cap above 50 billion dollars. Nothing is signed. This is a reported-talks story sourced to people familiar with the matter, with no company confirmation and no disclosed financing structure. Treat the stock pop as pricing in a deal that isn't assured, not as pricing in a done one.

Here's why this matters beyond one ticker. High-yield spreads have sat all week in the richest decile of their history, an option-adjusted spread in the high 270s to low 280s basis points against a long-run median near 450. That's the cheapest high-yield financing in roughly two decades. We've been calling that complacency. Workday is the first sign it's turning into activity. Silver Lake ran this exact playbook last year, teaming with Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund and Affinity Partners on a roughly 55 billion dollar take-private of Electronic Arts. A 43 to 50 billion dollar software target now, financed into a market where credit risk has almost never been priced this cheap, is the same sponsor running the same pattern because the leverage is there to run it. The open question, which we can't answer yet because none of it is public, is how much of this gets financed through banks versus direct lenders, and whether it adds another large single-name concentration to private-credit portfolios that are still working through stress elsewhere. There's also a read-through past Workday itself: every high-multiple, cash-generative software name trading below its old highs now screens as a potential take-private target on the same cheap-leverage logic. So watch the financing structure once it's disclosed. That's the real test of how much risk appetite is actually behind this rally in deal-making, not just the headline multiple.

Now the other side of that same coin. While cheap credit is funding risk-taking, real money spent this week hedging against a scenario that same credit market isn't pricing at all. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said on August 14th that the US will apply new economic measures on Iran "like the world has never seen," with the administration maintaining a naval blockade of Iranian ports to force Tehran back to the negotiating table on the Strait of Hormuz. Oil responded. Brent settled above 88 dollars a barrel, a roughly 6 percent weekly gain, with WTI up at 82.40. Oil is now up more than 40 percent year to date on the Gulf disruption. On the ground, the disruption is real but partial. Lloyd's List Intelligence's most recent Hormuz brief counted 78 total vessel transits in the week of August 3rd through 9th, down from 95 the week before. That's a meaningful decline, on the order of 15 to 20 percent, not a closure. The firm itself flags that GPS jamming in the Strait means even those figures are conservative floors. So the right framing is a serious, worsening supply disruption, not a full blockade. And note the mechanism has shifted: this is escalating financial warfare rather than kinetic escalation, which raises its own second-order risk. Aggressive new sanctions on Iranian trade tighten dollar funding and correspondent-banking channels, and that friction tends to leak into emerging-market and energy-trade finance well beyond the oil price itself.

Gold is where we need to correct the record slightly. Earlier this week gold futures briefly touched 4,500 dollars an ounce, a two-month high, and spot pushed above 4,400. But the most recent confirmed settlement we have, from Friday August 14th, has gold at 4,387.62 dollars, up about 26 dollars, or 0.61 percent, on the day. That's below the 4,400 level and well below that 4,500 futures print from August 11th. So this is a partial pullback from an early-week high, not an ongoing push into record territory. That's worth being precise about, because the two reads imply different things about momentum. What hasn't pulled back is the structural case underneath it. China's central bank added 20 tonnes of gold in July, its 21st consecutive month of purchases, the longest buying streak on record, taking official holdings to 2,366 tonnes, or 8 percent of its foreign-exchange reserves, according to the World Gold Council. That's a genuine reserve-diversification bid, not a bet on the Fed cutting. It means gold doesn't necessarily unwind even if the Fed's hawks win the September argument. And notably, this hedging is happening under a still-low VIX. So this is selective hard-asset positioning beneath a calm tape, not a broad risk-off move. Hold onto that distinction rather than reading it as fear spreading everywhere at once.

That same hedge-versus-risk split shows up in crypto this week. Spot Bitcoin ETFs logged 389.7 million dollars in net outflows for the week of August 10th through 14th, their largest weekly withdrawal in six weeks, ending a five-day inflow streak. BlackRock's IBIT alone shed 55.5 million dollars on August 14th. Bitcoin traded around 62,000 dollars during the stretch, down from breaking above 65,000 in early August, and the ETF complex is still roughly 4.5 billion dollars net negative on the year. Ethereum funds were also net negative for the week, about 2.25 million dollars out. But Solana ETFs took in 10.26 million dollars, their best week since May. That's a small number in absolute terms, but the direction matters. This isn't a blanket crypto selloff. It's real money rotating out of the "digital gold" trade and toward actual gold, while speculative flows persist within crypto itself. If Bitcoin is losing that macro-hedge bid to the metal during an actual geopolitical and inflation scare, that complicates the institutional case for holding it as a hedge rather than as straightforward liquidity beta.

Tie it together with where the Fed sits. The July FOMC held rates at a 9-to-3 vote, with three hawkish dissents, the first time since 2016 that three officials have pushed the same direction, arguing inflation risks getting embedded even as the labor market has clearly slowed. That split hasn't resolved. It's the backdrop against which everything above is happening, with the 10-year Treasury near 4.63 percent, the 2-year at 4.15, and the effective funds rate at 3.63 as of mid-week. Right now the tape is doing two things at once. It's spending the cheapest high-yield financing in twenty years on mega-deals like Workday, while a narrower slice of real money quietly hedges the inflation and supply risk that same tape isn't pricing at all. Spreads this tight leave almost no cushion. If the hawks are right about oil-driven inflation, or growth disappoints further, the repricing in credit won't be gradual.

That's Capital Markets Radar for August 16th. Stay sharp out there.
