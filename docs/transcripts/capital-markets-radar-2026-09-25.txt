Good morning, it's Friday, September 25th, and here's the capital markets radar.

On Wednesday this desk said risk assets were pricing peace and rates were pricing persistence. On Thursday we said rates had won that argument. This morning the peace leg itself has broken too, and that's where we start.

Brent crude closed Thursday at 106.39 dollars a barrel, up 3.21 percent on the day, according to Trading Economics. WTI closed at 94.76 dollars. The proximate cause was Houthi missile fire aimed at Saudi cities including Yanbu and Taif. Per The National's live coverage, the Houthis claimed they were targeting an Aramco facility in Yanbu specifically.

Yanbu matters because it's the Red Sea end of the East-West pipeline. That's the Hormuz bypass this desk has spent the week calling unconfirmed. Now the bypass itself is the target.

I want to be precise about what we know and what we don't. The Houthi claim is a statement of intent. No source we have confirms that export infrastructure was hit, that loadings stopped, or that anything was disrupted on the ground. Coverage of whether the missiles were intercepted conflicts, and we couldn't verify either version. So treat this as an attack, not yet a supply loss.

There's also a less dramatic read of the price move. Part of Thursday's jump may simply be shorts covering after Tuesday's relief sell-off. Without loading or transit data, the two can't be separated.

The same news cycle cut the other way as well. Reports say US and Iranian negotiators are exploring a deal to lift the naval blockade on tankers crossing the Strait of Hormuz, in exchange for eased sanctions. That reporting rests on unnamed sources. No official has confirmed it on the record. So headline risk runs both ways, and it now runs from a much higher price base.

The practical read is that Tuesday's Hormuz-relief trade is fully unwound. And the monthly numbers show where the premium sits. Brent is up 21.9 percent over the past month, against 15.06 percent for WTI. On Thursday's prints, that leaves the two benchmarks roughly 11.6 dollars apart. That gap is my own calculation, not a quoted spread. But it tells you the premium is in seaborne, Middle-East-exposed crude, not the US domestic barrel. The burden falls hardest on countries that import their oil and pay for it in dollars. We'll come back to that.

So why does this land harder now than it would have a month ago? Because the energy shock was already showing up in the survey data before Thursday. September's flash composite PMI from S&P Global was the strongest since July 2021. Inside that survey, input costs rose at the fastest pace since October 2022, mainly due to higher fuel and transport costs.

That's a diffusion index, not the CPI print. September CPI doesn't land until October 14th, so don't mistake one for the other. But it's the freshest read we have, and it says the energy channel was already pushing prices up.

Now layer on the September dot plot. The median projection for fed funds at the end of both 2026 and 2027 sits at 4.125 percent, per MUFG's recap. That's well above the committee's long-run neutral estimate of 3.25 percent. Against the current range of 3.75 to 4 percent, the median implies one more quarter-point hike this year, and no cuts penciled in for 2027. Chair Warsh has been explicit about the priority. In his words, "our predominant focus is on the price stability side of our mandate."

On Monday this desk called the Fed's 2027 disinflation forecast a leveraged bet on oil. Thursday is that bet moving against the committee. The so-what: oil doesn't just confirm the one-more-hike baseline. It raises the odds the Fed has to go further than that median dot shows.

But the hawkish read has a real hole in it. A supply-driven oil shock doesn't just lift prices. It taxes growth, because it hits real incomes and corporate margins at the same time. And if the Hormuz blockade talks actually produce a deal, the input Warsh is pointing to fades fast. That makes front-end rate pricing one of the most exposed positions on the board. It can move sharply in either direction, depending on which headline lands next.

The margin point deserves its own line. The PMI's input-cost jump was driven by fuel and transport. That's a direct squeeze on freight, airlines, chemicals and any business that moves physical goods. Heading into third-quarter earnings season, that cost pressure is a risk the equity market has not yet had to confront.

Now to where the market has already made up its mind, and the cleanest expression of that is the dollar. The DXY index held above 101 on Thursday, its highest level in almost two months. Trading Economics attributes the move to stronger-than-expected data reinforcing expectations of further tightening. Track the escalation. This desk had the index at 100.6 on Tuesday, and back above 100 on Wednesday.

That dollar strength is a real tightening of funding conditions for the same oil-importing economies now facing a bigger crude bill. They pay more barrels' worth of dollars, just as each dollar costs more. That double hit is getting far less attention than the US equity range.

Gold is the mirror image, and it's breaking a pattern. Gold fell to 4,250.85 dollars an ounce on Thursday, down 8.74 percent over the past month. It fell on a day when Brent rallied more than 3 percent on a war-escalation headline. That's exactly the kind of day gold is supposed to catch a bid, and it didn't. Last Sunday this desk said gold's carry case was a reason to keep it, not a reason to add. It has kept sliding since.

One down day doesn't prove the hedge is broken. Gold is still up 13.36 percent year over year, so this reads as a drawdown inside an uptrend, not a regime call. But the monthly comparison is hard to ignore. Over the same stretch, oil is up roughly 22 percent and gold is down roughly 9, while geopolitical risk was rising. Plainly, the real-yield and strong-dollar channel is beating the safe-haven channel.

Which leaves the market that has refused to move at all, and that's equities. The S&P 500 closed Wednesday at about 7,706. Per Bloomberg, it's still inside the 7,600 to 7,800 range it has held since August, even as crude and yields advanced. The VIX sat at 15.67 on Thursday, against 30-day realized volatility of 10.71.

That gap is worth sitting with. Last Sunday we said a VIX near 15 wasn't charging for the October meeting. The realized number changes that framing. With implied vol running roughly five points over realized, the market is charging a real premium for a range-bound tape. That's not pure complacency. The range holding is also consistent with strong-PMI earnings offsetting a higher discount rate.

The honest question is whether that premium covers a combined scenario: a second hike landing on top of a genuine oil shock. We have no dealer-positioning or options-flow data to answer that cleanly. So here's the narrower read. Equity vol looks fairly priced for more of the same. The under-read, if there is one, is the case where both risks arrive together.

A few dates will resolve pieces of this. First, the US-Iran blockade talks, which could move oil sharply in either direction on the next headline. Then the September employment report on October 2nd, CPI on October 14th, PPI on October 15th, and the next FOMC decision on October 28th. None of those numbers exist yet, so don't let anyone tell you otherwise between now and then.

That's the radar for today. Have a good weekend, and I'll see you back here next week.
