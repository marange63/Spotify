Cautious Optimism Briefings

Strategic Power, Energy, and Institutional Change

Transcript · September 7, 2026

Good morning. It's Monday, September 7th, 2026.

Start in Texas. ERCOT, the operator that runs most of the Texas grid, has become the effective gatekeeper of the American AI buildout — not because it has told anyone no, but because of how it now decides who gets in line and on what terms. Today's story is what that gate actually is, and how little of what is queued behind it is real.

Reporting late last week said the grid operator had stopped processing new data-center connection requests entirely, with the queue standing at 474 gigawatts. The primary document describes something different. The ERCOT market notice governing this cycle, filed as M-B062326-01, is not a halt. It replaces the old approach of evaluating large-load requests one project at a time with a study of all of them together, on a systemwide basis. ERCOT calls it Batch Zero. It took effect July 11th, seven weeks before the coverage that described it as a freeze, and the notice itself carries the header "action required: none." That is not the language of an enforcement action. It is the language of a methodology change.

One honest limit. We located the July notice. We could not rule out that some separate, later action by ERCOT or the Public Utility Commission of Texas describes an actual pause on new submissions. If one exists, none of the coverage cited it. What is documented is a change in how the queue is studied, not a decision to stop taking names.

The second gate is a political one, and it is real. Governor Abbott's August 3rd order said ERCOT was considering roughly 474 gigawatts of connection requests, about ninety percent of it from data centers. He directed developers to prove their projects are legitimate — identified funding, named customers, control of the land — before they proceed. That is a state government inserting a capital test between an announcement and a grid connection. It is the most consequential thing that has happened to data-center siting in the country's most permissive market.

Here is the number that tells you why a test like that was coming. Trade-press tracking of the same queue found only about 5.9 gigawatts of large load actually energized. Hold that against a queue reported at 474. That figure is not an ERCOT statistic, and the source does not label it a ghost-demand share, so treat it as the closest available proxy rather than an official count. But it is the sharpest quantified gap we have between what is queued and what is drawing power. Texas has never recorded grid peak demand above 100 gigawatts. Against that benchmark, even a deeply discounted queue looks enormous. Against under six gigawatts energized, the queue looks less like a demand forecast and more like a waiting list nobody has had to pay to join.

Which is also why the headline gigawatt number should not be traded as a series. The figures circulating this year — 410 gigawatts in April, 438 in June, 474 in August — are point-in-time snapshots with different scope and different verification behind each one. That is not a growth curve. Analysts who track the queue call it queue pressure, not a validated demand forecast, and they warn specifically against reading the sequence as demand rising. Friday on this show we cited the same queue at over 233 gigawatts; that was a different vintage and a different measure, not a smaller world.

Now separate three things this story keeps merging: the queue shrinking, contracted load shrinking, and delivered megawatts shrinking. Nothing established today supports any of the three. What Batch Zero establishes is a change in how the queue gets studied, and a systemwide batch study has no stated end date. That cuts against the bullish read as much as the bearish one, because a batch process holds genuine projects in the same line as speculative ones. Screening is not accelerating.

And apply the same standard to the audit that we apply to the projects. An ordered audit is an announcement too. Abbott ordered it five weeks ago. There is still no completed audit, no published approval criteria, and no operator figure for how much of that queue is duplicative. Neither ERCOT nor the Public Utility Commission has put a number on it.

So what shifts. The audit standard — funding, customers, land control — is a capital filter, and it favors well-funded hyperscalers over speculative site aggregators. That concentrates the buildout rather than halting it. It also raises the option value of skipping the grid entirely, which is the behind-the-meter contracting behavior this beat identified on Friday, now with a harder cause behind it. And if other grid operators adopt Batch Zero-style systemwide study, then an executed interconnection agreement, not a queue position, becomes the asset worth pricing into utility and independent power producer valuations. That is a discriminating call inside the sector, not a sector-wide short: own the developers who can show a signed agreement, not the ones quoting pipeline.

Turn now from a gate somebody controls to a negotiation where nobody has conceded anything. Trump envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner spent three hours with Vladimir Putin in Moscow on Saturday, then flew to Kyiv on Sunday for what both sides called substantive talks with Volodymyr Zelensky. That was the first Kyiv leg of this diplomatic push, and it aimed at reviving negotiations frozen for nearly eight months. It happened, and it produced no disclosed terms.

Both capitals held off striking each other over the visit window, which is a real if narrow deliverable — and note what it is evidence of. Two belligerents can lower the cost of talking without lowering the cost of fighting. There is no broader ceasefire. A Kremlin aide's description of "a whole series of ideas for a peace settlement" is adjectives, not content. Process has moved. Substance has not.

Notice what the absence of terms holds shut. Every serious settlement question sits downstream of terms nobody has published: the sequencing of sanctions relief, the fate of the frozen Russian central bank assets held in Europe, and whether European defense budgets keep their active-war justification. Those are the actual transfers of power in this story, and none of them can begin from a travel itinerary. Until terms exist, the settlement trade in European gas and reconstruction industrials has nothing to anchor to. The catalyst stays live; it is not yet priceable.

What a settlement would be worth to oil runs through how much supply OPEC-plus is still holding back — and that reserve is larger than the headlines suggest. Eight OPEC-plus producers approved a 188,000 barrel-a-day increase for September. That completes the unwind of a 1.65 million barrel-a-day voluntary cut package structured back in 2023, which reads like the end of supply discipline. It is not. A separate and larger cut of roughly 2 million barrels a day stays in place through the end of this year. A little over half of the combined package is still withheld. We sized that cushion as spent yesterday; it is not. That matters because it is the shock absorber any Russian re-entry to the market would land in, and it makes a Ukraine settlement materially less bearish for crude than the spent-cushion framing implies — which in turn makes the reconstruction and European gas trade less of a one-way bet on falling oil.

The caveat cuts both ways. A quota is not a barrel. As we noted yesterday, several OPEC-plus members reportedly cannot physically lift to their new allotments, so the deliverable cushion is thinner than either headline number implies. The Gulf's real leverage sits somewhere between the paper cut and the pumped barrel, and nobody publishes that number either.

Three gates, then: a grid operator changing how it studies who gets power, a war where no terms have changed hands, and a cartel still sitting on roughly two million barrels a day. Watch whether ERCOT or the Public Utility Commission of Texas ever publishes a duplicative share or an executed-agreement gigawatt figure, whether other grid operators adopt batch-style screening, and whether the Witkoff-Kushner track produces a summit or any disclosed terms this week.
