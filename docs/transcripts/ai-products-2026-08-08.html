This week's product moves aren't really about smarter models. They're two opposite bets on the same underlying variable: distribution and trust. One company is tearing friction out of its product to grab more users. Another is being forced to build friction in just to keep the legal right to exist. Let's take them in that order.

OpenAI's move is the lead. Starting this week, GPT-5.6 Luna becomes the default model for everyone on ChatGPT's Free and Go tiers. And starting next week, OpenAI is removing the rate limit on text-based chats for those same free users entirely. That's the headline: no more caps on how much you can talk to ChatGPT in plain text, so long as you're not uploading files, generating images, or invoking tools — those stay metered. And it's not fully unmetered even for text; usage still remains subject to what OpenAI calls abuse protections, so heavy automated scraping or obvious misuse can still get throttled. But for a normal user, the cap that used to push long sessions elsewhere is gone.

Read that as a funnel move, not a capability leap. Free users have historically hit a wall mid-conversation and either paid up or wandered over to Gemini or Perplexity to finish a long research thread. Removing that wall keeps them inside ChatGPT and inside OpenAI's account graph. It's the same logic behind two other moves OpenAI made just days earlier this week — rolling out "Sign in with ChatGPT" as a login layer for third-party apps, and folding the standalone Atlas browser back into ChatGPT. Three separate moves, one week, all aimed at owning the surface where users spend time, not at making the underlying model smarter. The capability story has largely commoditized across labs; the fight has moved to who owns the interface and the account.

Now, OpenAI did pair this with a real product change on the paid side. GPT-5.6 Sol, the default model for Plus and Pro subscribers, was retuned for everyday use — search, planning, writing, coding, decision-making — and OpenAI is claiming a meaningful cut in factual-error rates. Specifically, OpenAI's own account states that in what it calls a high-stakes evaluation spanning finance, medicine, and law, Sol produced 68% fewer factually erroneous responses than the prior GPT-5.5 Instant model, with Luna showing a 62% reduction on the same evaluation. Two independent outlets quoted those same two numbers from OpenAI's internal testing, so the figures are corroborated as OpenAI's claim — but they're still OpenAI grading its own homework, an internal eval, not an independently audited benchmark. Treat 68% and 62% as OpenAI's self-reported number, not a verified industry result.

Plus and Pro users also get something free users don't: a Reasoning Slider, five settings running from Instant up to High, that lets you dial in how much reasoning effort the model spends before answering — Instant responds almost immediately, High can take several minutes. Free users get a simpler version of that idea, a Think button that gives Luna extra time on harder questions, but not the graduated slider. So the segmentation is deliberate: free users get unlimited access to a decent free model with a blunt reasoning toggle, paying users get the better-tuned model with fine-grained control. That's a coherent monetization ladder, and it's worth watching whether it actually converts casual free users into subscribers or just makes OpenAI's free tier a more expensive line item — nobody has disclosed usage or engagement numbers alongside this change, so the funnel effect so far is inference, not measurement. It's also worth remembering this pressures Google to keep expanding its own free Gemini limits and Meta to do the same with Meta AI — the free-tier arms race just escalated, and it raises OpenAI's own compute exposure if usage actually scales the way the unlimited offer implies.

So that's one company stripping friction out to grow reach. Now the other side of that trade: a company being forced to build friction in, just to keep distributing at all.

Suno, the AI music generator, announced this week that it will start watermarking and fingerprinting every song it generates, specifically so that streaming platforms can identify Suno-made tracks and tell them apart from human recordings. This isn't a feature launch in the normal sense — it's a legal survival move. Suno is currently facing lawsuits from Universal Music Group, Sony Music Group, and the German rights body GEMA, plus a Massachusetts class action tied to a data breach. Under that pressure, Suno says the watermark is being engineered to be durable and resistant to tampering, without degrading the actual listening experience. It also signed a deal with the lyrics platform Musixmatch to use its Sentinel system for copyright detection, and it updated its community guidelines to explicitly bar deceptive audio presented as real and any unauthorized use of a real person's voice or likeness. On top of that, Suno says it plans a new download policy specifically designed to stop mass distribution of AI-generated songs onto streaming platforms — though it hasn't published the specifics of how that policy will work yet.

A caveat worth stating plainly: none of this is live yet. Suno "says it will" build these systems; there's no confirmed shipping date beyond vague "coming weeks" language in secondary coverage. And there are no public figures on how large the streaming-fraud problem actually is — no count of fraudulent uploads, no royalty numbers — so don't mistake this for a quantified crisis; it's a commitment made under active legal and reputational pressure, part olive branch to rights holders, part settlement posture. The timing tells you which. Watermarking and licensed detection give Universal, Sony, and GEMA something concrete to point to at the negotiating table, which is a large part of why this surfaces now, under active litigation, rather than a year ago when the same tools were just as buildable.

But here's why it matters beyond Suno specifically: this is provenance infrastructure being bolted onto a consumer creative tool for the first time on this show, after we've tracked the same trust-and-verification layer show up in enterprise AI agents and in protocol-level security for tool-calling systems earlier this week. Watermarking, fingerprinting, and licensed detection partnerships are becoming the price of admission for AI-generated content to legally coexist with rights holders. Expect Udio, Suno's closest AI-music rival, to face the same demand soon, either from its own litigation or simply to match the new baseline. And expect the same pattern to eventually reach AI-video generation, where the copyright exposure is arguably even larger. And if that download friction actually ships, there's a nearer-term payoff for the streaming platforms themselves: less of the spam, AI-generated filler that has been flooding Spotify and the other services, and less dilution of the royalty pool that human artists draw from — so the platforms have their own quiet incentive to push every AI-music vendor toward this same baseline. The so-what here: trust infrastructure is turning into a competitive requirement, not a nice-to-have, and it's a new product surface in its own right — music-identification vendors and streaming platforms' trust-and-safety teams now have a fresh mandate to build against.

Put the two stories side by side and the thesis writes itself. OpenAI is removing friction to buy reach. Suno is adding friction to buy the right to keep operating. Neither is a smarter-model story — both are about who controls distribution and who gets to be trusted with it, and this week both fights got sharper.
