Good morning, and happy Sunday, September 13th. Here's your capital markets radar.

On Friday this desk named the outcome that would hurt the most positions at once. A retracement in crude, we said, paired with an inflation print that wasn't hot enough to justify the pricing. The framing behind that call has been simple all week. The hawkish repricing that has dominated this market since late August rests almost entirely on one input, and that input is the oil shock out of the Middle East. Take the barrel away, and the inflation leg underneath the whole trade goes with it. Friday delivered the first pieces of exactly that.

Start with the price. Crude fell back to a hundred dollars a barrel on Friday. It still finished nine percent higher for the week, so this is a pause in a rally, not a reversal of one. What moved it was diplomacy. Gulf Cooperation Council diplomats are expected to meet their Iranian counterpart on Monday to discuss a temporary arrangement for managing shipping through the Strait of Hormuz. That meeting is scheduled, not concluded, and fighting across the region has continued alongside the talks. But this is the first time in this episode that the Hormuz risk premium has had a date on the calendar working against it.

Then the demand side, which is the piece nobody is pricing. The International Energy Agency now forecasts that global oil demand contracts by two and a half million barrels a day in 2026. That would be the largest annual decline since Covid, and the agency attributes it to high prices and tighter supplies. Hold both halves of that in your head, because they point in opposite directions. Demand destruction on that scale undercuts the inflation case for hiking. It also describes a world economy consuming a lot less energy, which is a worse backdrop for earnings than the inflation story alone. A cheaper barrel that arrives because nobody can afford the expensive one is not the good version of this trade.

And one callback that carries more weight now than when we made it. We noted Friday that Goldman Sachs had raised its December Brent and WTI forecasts by five dollars, to eighty-five and eighty dollars a barrel. Both of those revised targets still sit below where crude actually trades. When the bank raising its price target lands below spot, consensus is not calling for more escalation. It is calling for less. That was an oddity on Friday. With talks scheduled for Monday, it looks more like the base case.

And the Fed is priced off exactly that variable. August consumer prices, released Friday, rose four tenths of a percent on the month and three point four percent over the year. The headline was in line. The composition is what did the damage. Gasoline alone jumped three point nine percent, and that single line item accounted for more than a third of the entire monthly increase.

Layer that onto a hot producer price report the day before, and the pricing moved violently. CME FedWatch odds of a quarter-point hike at Wednesday's meeting went to roughly ninety percent, up from about seventy the day before. Take that as direction rather than precision, because other venues have read lower all week. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh primed the move at Jackson Hole in late August. He put the Fed's preferred inflation gauge at three point seven percent over twelve months, and four point one percent on a six-month annualized basis. Then he set a condition. The Fed must be confident underlying inflation is moving to target, and in his words, otherwise, we have work to do.

Sit with that phrasing, because it is a threat rather than a commitment. Ninety percent implied probability is near-certainty, and it is built on a headline that came in line and a core rate that is decelerating. Core consumer prices ran two point four percent over the year in August, down from two point five percent in July. The sticky leg of inflation is cooling. The hawkish case rests on the energy leg. And the energy leg is the one that started cracking in the Gulf, not in Washington. Wednesday's meeting has not happened, and every probability here is pre-meeting pricing, not an outcome and not a forecast. But a Fed that tightens into a shock whose cause is fading is running a credibility operation, not a demand operation.

One asset already traded that arithmetic, and it went against us. On September 10th this desk argued that a meaningful share of gold's bid was a hedge on fiscal and inflation credibility, and concluded that a hawkish Fed would not break gold the way the standard model says it should. Here is what happened instead. Gold fell to around four thousand three hundred and fifty dollars an ounce on Thursday, specifically as traders raised hike bets on the hot producer price data. It opened Friday at its lowest level in more than a month. And when the consumer price print pushed hike odds to ninety percent, gold merely held near four thousand three hundred and eighty-five dollars rather than catching a safe-haven bid. War risk rising, gold not rallying. That is the real-yield channel beating the safe-haven channel, and it is the opposite of the call we made.

Two honest limits on that. These are daily headline levels, not a tick-by-tick read of the print itself, so gold did not rally is better supported than gold collapsed on the news. And gold traded above four thousand four hundred dollars earlier in the same week, so the last ten sessions look like whipsaw between the two channels rather than a decisive win for either. The clean test is still ahead of us. If Hormuz de-escalates and the Fed hikes anyway, gold takes the rate penalty with no war bid to offset it. That exact configuration has not been tested in this cycle.

Don't lump copper into that story. Comex copper is up about seventeen percent this year, with the London contract up about fifteen. Bloomberg's reporting is explicit that the rally is fueled by supply disruptions and tariff concerns rather than demand alone. Gold is a rates trade right now. Copper is a scarcity trade. Treating hard assets as one basket into this week would have cost you on both sides.

Now the part of the war premium that does not unwind on Monday even if the talks go well. The Mortgage Bankers Association put the thirty-year fixed rate up six basis points, to six point eight five percent, for the week ended September 4th. That is the highest since June of last year. The mechanism is direct. Hostilities in the Middle East pushed up oil, which fed inflation concerns, which pushed up the Treasury yields that underpin residential borrowing costs. Rate transmission lags. The barrel does not. Even a clean diplomatic outcome this week leaves housing finance carrying a cost that was locked in before the diplomacy started.

One caveat on that, so you don't over-trade it. This is a single weekly print at a fourteen-month high. It is a level, not yet a housing slowdown. The evidence that would make it one shows up in mortgage applications and homebuilder numbers over the coming weeks, and none of that is in hand today.

Which closes the loop, in credit. Investment-grade spreads stood at eighty-one basis points at the start of September. That is roughly forty basis points tighter than three years ago. High-yield sits near two hundred and sixty-six basis points, the bottom decile of its historical range, which is minimal compensation for taking credit risk. There is a refinancing wall out there too. Roughly one point two trillion dollars of leveraged loans and high-yield bonds mature between 2027 and 2029. That is a forward problem, not a today one.

And here I owe you the record. This desk called spreads complacent in five straight briefings and was wrong in all five, said exactly that on the tape yesterday, and then made the call a sixth time anyway. Six is where I stop repeating it. There is a more useful reading available now. Credit may not be mispricing the hawkish path at all. It may already be positioned for the de-escalation path that Friday just made more likely. If that is right, credit has been the best-informed asset class on this tape for two weeks, and the asymmetry into Wednesday is a hike delivered into a shock that has already begun to fade.
