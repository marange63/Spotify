Good morning. It's November 21st, 2026, and this is your briefing on AI in biopharma R&D.

This week the buyers and the labs published their own scorecards for AI in drug development. Pharma put numbers on discovery speed. OpenAI put a number on chemistry reasoning. AstraZeneca put a number on data access. Almost every one of those scorecards measures speed and access at the front of the pipeline. None of them measures the clinical success rate that actually sets R&D returns. That gap is the thread for today.

Start with pharma's own numbers. In Reuters coverage dated November 20th, several top-ten sponsors put company-specific figures on AI's effect in the same news cycle. These are the buyers talking, not vendor marketing. The useful move is to sort the claims by where they sit in the value chain.

Begin with selection. Novartis says AI tools helped it prioritize five targets for kidney disease. That's a count of targets, not a claim about their quality.

Next, design. Novo Nordisk says it cut its design-manufacture-test cycles from around eight to three, while halving the length of each cycle. By our arithmetic, that's better than a fivefold cut in total design-phase time.

Then time-to-candidate. Fiona Marshall, Novartis's president of biomedical research, says the average span from project start to a drug candidate has gone from three years to about eighteen months to two years. Note two gaps in that claim. Her own quoted explanation credits having a lot of data, not AI explicitly. And no source states a baseline period or a portfolio scope behind the figure.

Last comes projection rather than measurement. Sanofi expects AI to cut early-discovery timelines by 20 to 25 percent within two years, and perhaps by half within five. That's a forecast, and it should be heard as one.

Sanofi also offered the one claim that reaches past discovery. It says an AI-enabled approach to translating early biology into clinical decisions let it skip a dose-selection study for its asthma candidate, lunsekimig. Per the coverage, that saved at least a year. No source says a regulator formally accepted or waived that study. So treat it as Sanofi's own account, not a confirmed regulatory sign-off. And notice what kind of win it is. It's still time saved, not a better chance that the drug works.

Here's the skeptical read. Every one of these figures is self-reported in press interviews. None comes with a published methodology, a disclosed baseline, or an audit. Companies volunteer their best programs. Nothing quoted separates AI from concurrent changes like automation or portfolio pruning. And time-to-candidate and design-cycle counts are inputs, not outcomes.

That matters because of what this show argued yesterday. Our view was that target selection, not design speed, is the layer most plausibly connected to clinical success. Today's numbers are mostly about design speed and time-to-candidate. Shaving a year or so off discovery, even if real, leaves the clinical timeline and Phase 2 attrition untouched. Faster candidate generation without better selection may just mean failing faster and cheaper. That's real money. It is not a step change.

Two second-order reads follow, and both are our inference. First, Novartis and Novo Nordisk are reporting this compression from their own programs. So part of what the market pays AI-native design platforms for is being replicated in-house by the buyers. That cuts against the design-as-a-service model this show flagged yesterday in the Merck-Isomorphic deal. Second, suppose time-to-candidate keeps compressing while success rates don't move. Then more candidates reach the clinic on the same discovery budget. The binding constraint shifts downstream, to clinical capacity and enrollment. That favors CRO clinical-operations volume over preclinical services.

The same reporting carries a governance twist. First, the standing disclosure: Anthropic makes the model behind this show. Anthropic's board includes Novartis's chief executive, Vas Narasimhan. That appointment dates to April of this year, per Anthropic's own announcement, so it isn't this week's news. What's fresh is how a rival frames the relationship. GSK's chief scientific officer, Tony Wood, says he views Anthropic as, quote, "another competitor." So a sitting pharma CEO is on the board of a lab that a peer's science chief calls a competitor. That's a live governance and information-flow question. It's our inference, not a reported conflict. And it puts a buyer's voice on the vendor-or-competitor tension this show has tracked since the 18th.

If frontier labs want to be pharma's reasoning layer, partner or competitor, the next question is how good they actually are at chemistry. OpenAI just published MedChemBench, its first medicinal-chemistry-specific benchmark. It's ninety-nine problems drawn from published papers and patents. They test structure-activity reasoning, ADME and toxicity prediction, lead-optimization decisions, and retrosynthesis.

The results are not flattering. GPT-Rosalind-5.5 scored 27.5 percent on the released benchmark. That's about two points above GPT-5.5. OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra model page lists a higher score, 49.3 percent. But that score is on what OpenAI calls an internal variant. The two numbers aren't on the same test, so don't read them as one trend line. Either way, the best listed score sits under half.

Two more cautions. The GPT-6 Astra page says Claude models were excluded because they refuse the majority of MedChemBench questions. That's OpenAI's claim about a competitor's model, under the same Anthropic disclosure as before. If it holds, our inference is that dual-use safety refusals carry a commercial cost in chemistry, just as Anthropic courts pharma. The second caution is contamination. These problems come from published literature, so training-data overlap is a live risk. Scoring well on known chemistry is not the same as prospective discovery.

The pattern itself is becoming familiar. On the 19th, this show covered NVIDIA shipping its own chemistry dataset, LitmusBench, alongside Nemotron for Science. Now OpenAI has done the same. Each vendor is grading on a test it wrote. The practical read for this audience is simple. Large language models remain the workhorse for literature synthesis and lab operations. They are not yet a substitute for specialized design models or the wet lab.

Where models are weakest, data is the moat. On November 16th, Tempus AI and AstraZeneca amended their master services agreement. AstraZeneca committed two hundred million dollars over five years, running through the end of 2031. The details rest on secondary summaries of the underlying filing, not the primary text, so hold them a little loosely. This renewal is also separate from the companies' earlier oncology foundation-model deal with Pathos AI. Don't conflate the two.

Be clear about what the Tempus deal is not. It's a binding spend commitment on licensed clinical data access. It is not evidence that AI on real-world data has changed a trial design, an eligibility criterion, or an indication choice. That outcome still isn't in the record. On the 18th, and again yesterday, this show reported that real-world data had produced no new evidence. That silence is now broken, but on the spending side only. As a capital signal, it still counts. A top sponsor is choosing to license proprietary clinical data for five more years rather than build it. Our inference is that this supports revenue visibility for data and diagnostics names, relative to pure discovery-stage AI platforms.

Pulling the threads together, nothing this week measured a clinical success rate. The realistic near-term gain is faster, cheaper attrition at the front end. Meanwhile the bottleneck shifts downstream, to clinical capacity, enrollment, and data access. On R&D spend, expect reallocation toward clinical operations and data licensing, not an overall cut.

Three things to watch. First, whether any of pharma's timeline claims gets an independent methodology behind it. Second, whether MedChemBench, or a rival's benchmark, gets scored by someone other than the vendor. Third, whether any AI-on-real-world-data deal, Tempus's included, produces a demonstrated trial outcome rather than a line item.

That's the briefing. Talk tomorrow.
