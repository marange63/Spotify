Good morning. It's Monday, October 19th, and this is your digital money briefing, picking up from Saturday since the desk was off Sunday.

On Saturday we left Bitcoin back near $82,000 after its break below $80,000, bouncing into a ceiling it hadn't cleared. Today we can draw a wider and more useful frame, because the market now sits in a measurable box. Above it is a ceiling with on-chain evidence behind it. Below it is a floor this desk has been watching all month. The question that matters is not which way price breaks. It's who is holding the price inside the box.

Start with the ceiling. Glassnode's weekly on-chain report, as relayed by Crypto Briefing rather than read in the original, says Bitcoin must reclaim $88,000 to overcome selling pressure from investors looking to exit. That level is not a chart line. It is the average cost basis of holders who bought between six and twelve months ago. Glassnode's argument is that those holders, once back at breakeven, are the ones looking for the door. Bitcoin approached that level in January and then fell more than 33 percent.

There is a second wall above the first. Holders who bought one to two years ago have a cost basis around $100,000. Bitcoin tested that level in May and then declined roughly 25 percent. One caveat applies, the same one we put on the ETF cost basis on Thursday. A cost-basis wall is an average, not an order book, and nobody at breakeven is obliged to sell. Still, these walls have turned price back twice this year, so they deserve respect.

Now the floor. It is the ETF cohort's average cost basis, about $81,500, also from Glassnode. Price has already slipped through it more than once. It dipped below briefly twice in the past fortnight, and then Thursday's slide took it to about $79,800. Each time, it climbed back. So the floor bends, but it hasn't broken for good.

That brings us to the strongest evidence-based bull case, and it deserves a fair hearing. Long-term holder realized losses have fallen 85 percent from their August peak, the counterweight we flagged on Thursday. What's new in this read is a second signal. Unlike the 2018 and 2022 bear markets, Bitcoin has avoided closing below its Realized Price this cycle. Glassnode frames that combination as bottom-building, not confirmation of a new uptrend. A range held above Realized Price, with the strongest-handed sellers largely done, is genuinely how bottoms form. The bear rejoinder is simple. Exhausted sellers tell you nothing about new buyers. A bottom only forms if someone actually owns the inside of the range.

So who's holding it? Start with leverage, because this is where our own read has changed. On Saturday we reported that funding was negative going into the inflation print, slightly below zero on an annualized basis. We read the bounce as fresh shorts. That read is now out of date. As of Saturday, a single tracker relayed by TokenPost shows Bitcoin funding positive, at the equivalent of 10.9 percent annualized. Open interest on that series is essentially unchanged from the level we cited Saturday. And long accounts now make up 62.5 percent of the total. That's account share, not size, so a few large shorts could still sit against many small longs.

The timing is what makes this matter. This is the same cohort that was flushed on the long side early this month. In that episode, more than $485 million of crypto positions liquidated in a single hour, most of them longs. That was the first of several long-heavy washouts we've covered since. Leverage has now rebuilt a fourth time, and it rebuilt long, within days. That answers the question this desk has carried since last Tuesday. The inside of this range is being rented by traders paying nearly 11 percent a year to sit there. It is not owned by buyers who don't care about price.

Meanwhile, the owners have been stepping back. Glassnode says spot Bitcoin ETFs drew nearly $3 billion of net inflows from late September into early October. Many read that run as fresh institutional accumulation. Then the flows flipped negative. On our arithmetic from TFTC's daily flow table, the funds were net sellers of roughly $939 million over the following eight trading days. That gives back about a third of the prior inflow.

The shape inside that stretch matters too. The only meaningful up-day reversed the very next session. That's not capitulation. Saturday's aggregator figure put the complex at roughly $106 billion in assets, so the outflow is small against the pool. Part of it may also be basis-trade unwinding rather than directional selling. But the ETF wrapper has been a net seller for the entire life of this box. The bid that was supposed to be stable has been chasing flow, in both directions.

Strategy, the treasury company formerly known as MicroStrategy, tells the same story from a different angle. Secondary aggregators summarizing its disclosure last Monday say it bought 250 Bitcoin for about $21.5 million that week. That was its smallest weekly purchase of the year, and the second straight week of shrinking buys. On Thursday we called the slowing pace a choice, not a constraint, and nothing here contradicts that.

The sharper detail came in the same disclosure. Strategy also repurchased about $192 million of its own STRC preferred stock. On our arithmetic, that's roughly nine times what it spent on Bitcoin that week. The company hasn't said why. One fair reading is support for a preferred trading below par. Another is trimming dividend obligations. A third is simply putting cash where it was most useful. None of those is necessarily bearish for Bitcoin demand. But the flagship corporate accumulator spent far more managing its own capital stack than buying the asset it's famous for.

That matters beyond one company. Strategy is the template for the whole cohort of Bitcoin treasury companies. If the archetype is steering cash toward liability management rather than accumulation, the flywheel story behind its imitators weakens. That's an implication, not a measured outcome, but it's the one to test in the next few filings.

Put those three pieces together and you get a leverage-held range. The ceiling is the holder wall overhead. The ETF floor has already bent more than once. And the marginal bid inside has shifted from price-insensitive owners to leverage paying positive carry. If price drifts back toward the floor, the long-heavy liquidation mechanics we've documented all month are the obvious risk. The Fed's October 28th decision, which has not happened yet, is the nearest scheduled trigger. That's a risk to flag, not a forecast.

The dollar layer underneath runs on a different clock. Today is the deadline for public comments on Treasury's proposed rules implementing the GENIUS Act's stablecoin issuance provisions. The comment period simply closes today. Nothing has followed from it yet, and the next step is regulators working toward final rules.

The float those rules will govern has gone nowhere. Total dollar stablecoin supply sits near $303 billion, below a May peak of about $322 billion. The one fresh datum comes from a single tracker, Stablecoin Beat, which shows USDC shedding about $946 million over the past week. Two explanations compete for the stall. One says issuers and allocators are waiting on the rules. The other is Standard Chartered's house view, which we covered Thursday. It says the stall is cyclical and tracks weak crypto activity. The evidence doesn't yet separate them. Either way, a flat float means stablecoins are not a growing source of Treasury bill demand right now.

The companion market-structure bill, the CLARITY Act, was still unenacted as of a mid-September tracker update. So exchanges and brokers still lack a federal market-structure framework.

One correction belongs here. On Saturday we said Circle's USYC led tokenized Treasury funds and BlackRock's BUIDL had slipped to third. A Cryptonomist report published Saturday says BUIDL regained the top spot back in late August. The two sources likely use different inclusion criteria, so treat the ranking as disputed rather than reversed. The same report puts the category at about $15.4 billion. That makes tokenized Treasuries a real growth pocket, even though the category is only about a twentieth the size of the stablecoin float.

The exchange equities sit downstream of all this. Coinbase reports third-quarter results after the close on October 29th. Its filed second quarter already showed revenue down 18.5 percent year over year, before this month's volatility. A range-bound market, with ETF outflows and a flat stablecoin float, is a weak backdrop for the durable-institutional-demand multiple these stocks carry.

So here's the watch-list, and none of it has happened yet. Does the $88,000 wall or the $81,500 floor break first? Does funding stay positive? And then three dates: the SEC's comment deadline on its crypto-assets proposal tomorrow, the Fed decision next week, and Coinbase the day after.

That's digital money for October 19th. Stay cautious, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
