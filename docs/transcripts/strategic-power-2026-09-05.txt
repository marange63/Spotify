Good morning. It's Saturday, September 5th, and this is your strategic power briefing.

All week this beat has graded the AI buildout on delivery, and found it wanting on the power side. This week the same test got run one layer upstream, on the machines that make the chips themselves. The answer came back the other way. Demand is revising upward faster than anyone can plan against it.

Start with TSMC. At SEMICON Taiwan on Wednesday, Deputy Co-Chief Operating Officer Cliff Hou said the company has raised its own estimate of quarterly equipment purchases to about one point nine times what it projected back in December. That same estimate stood at roughly one point five times as of the first quarter. So in about six months, TSMC's read on its own tool needs has nearly doubled.

That is not one executive's offhand remark. TSMC's chief financial officer told a version of the same story on the second-quarter earnings call, saying the company had decided to raise its full-year 2026 capital budget to between sixty and sixty-four billion dollars. Second-quarter capital spending alone was fifteen point seven billion. And the physical footprint matches the money. Thirteen fabs are under construction in Taiwan, plus five or six more overseas — an expansion program approaching the equivalent of twenty simultaneous fabs.

Now hold that number at arm's length, for three reasons. First, a raised purchase estimate is procurement intent, not installed and qualified capacity. That is the same distinction this beat applies to permits and queue positions. Second, a company that has revised its own forecast upward twice in seven months is also a company whose forecasting was wrong twice in seven months. That cuts against the new figure as much as the old one. Third, an executive at an industry conference has an interest in signalling scarcity to the suppliers sitting in the room. So the direction is credible. The magnitude is a planning assumption.

The supply chain does appear to be answering back. An industry report relayed by TrendForce last month puts lead times for imported radio-frequency power-supply equipment at as long as twenty-four months, and says lead times for conventional etch and thin-film deposition tools have doubled, to about twelve months. Attribute that carefully. It traces to a Chinese trade publication's reporting, not to a toolmaker speaking in its own voice. But if it is even directionally right, the marginal constraint on AI compute has moved off Nvidia's allocation list and onto the delivery schedules of the capital-equipment vendors — ASML, Applied Materials, Tokyo Electron. Their order books, not chip design and not capital, would then set the pace.

There is a second-order effect here that runs against the consensus story. Thirteen fabs building in Taiwan against five or six abroad means the reshoring programs are a shrinking share of a much larger base. Arizona, Japan and Germany are real, and they are also being outgrown. So Taiwan concentration is deepening in absolute terms even while the diversification narrative gets told. The so-what is uncomfortable. Taiwan risk is not being retired by this capex cycle. It is being enlarged by it.

Now, a tool order is a promise too. The other half of the gap is what happens to capacity that has already been scheduled to come online. This is the fourth independent read this beat has taken on that question in five days, and the verdict hasn't changed once.

A Congressional Research Service report dated September 1st finds that only about fifty to sixty percent of US data-center capacity scheduled for the next one to two years is expected to energize on time, amid delays and cancellations. Set that against NERC's projection that 2026 US peak electricity demand runs eleven gigawatts higher than 2025, driven largely by data centers. Two caveats. That fifty-to-sixty range is an expectation cited by a research body, not an audited outcome. And the more skeptical reading is the more interesting one. Trimming a speculative pipeline is what a healthy screening process is supposed to look like. If a large share of that scheduled capacity was phantom load, the shortfall measures forecast quality, not build capacity.

The procedural hook is harder. In June, FERC issued Section 206 show-cause orders to all six major grid operators — PJM, MISO, SPP, CAISO, ISO New England and New York's ISO — demanding they justify why their large-load and co-location rules should not be found unjust and unreasonable. The sixty-day deadline to answer passed on August 17th with no published outcome. Stakeholder comments on those filings are due September 16th. So what it means is this. An executed interconnection agreement is now a scarce asset in a way a queue position simply isn't. That is a stock-picking distinction inside the utilities and the independent power producers, not a sector-wide call.

And there is a slower consequence that won't show up where most people are looking. If forty to fifty percent of scheduled energization keeps slipping, AI compute availability gets pushed out on a timeline that no improvement in chip supply can fix. That drag is invisible in chip-order data, which is exactly the data everyone is extrapolating 2027 earnings from. The commitment curve and the delivery curve are now measurable on both ends, and they are diverging.

Both of those stories assume the material chain underneath stays open. There is one input-side deadline sitting on the calendar with nothing new attached to it.

China suspended a set of October 2025 export controls on rare-earth production equipment and materials for one year, running to November 10th, 2026. Say plainly what that is. It's a standing calendar item, not news. Nobody moved this week. But the licensing architecture behind those controls was never dismantled, and enforcement has been getting more active rather than less. Beijing formalized a public reporting mechanism for strategic-mineral export-control violations this year, and Japanese nationals were detained in Dalian in May over alleged smuggling of restricted goods. A suspension expiring is not the same thing as controls being reimposed, and it could equally be renewed. So the honest claim is narrower than the scare version. The option to re-escalate sits with Beijing, on a known date, just over two months from now.

All of this — the tool orders, the fabs, the interconnection fights — is being underwritten at a price the Federal Reserve sets in eleven days.

Which brings us to Friday's jobs report, the print this beat flagged yesterday as the thing to watch. August nonfarm payrolls rose 162,000, and unemployment held at 4.1 percent. The more telling line is in the revisions. July's previously reported loss of 23,000 jobs was revised into a gain of 21,000, and June was revised up as well. Average hourly earnings were up 3.1 percent year over year — firm, but not a wage spiral.

Don't read that as a clean hawkish verdict. A series that printed minus 23,000 and then plus 21,000 for the same month is not a precision instrument for a rate decision. Two months of same-direction revisions say as much about survey noise as about labor-market strength. But it does mean a straightforwardly dovish September 16th is no longer the easy call it looked like a week ago, and the capital cost of this entire buildout gets set at that meeting.

That date is worth sitting with as we close. September 16th is the FOMC decision that prices the buildout. It is also the day FERC's comment window closes on whether the interconnection rules gating that same buildout get rewritten. Two separate tests of one thesis, converging on one date, eleven days out.

That's the briefing. Have a good weekend.
