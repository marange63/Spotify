Good morning, and welcome to Cautious Optimism Briefings for Monday, October 5, 2026.

All week this beat has counted humanoid robots. Now the industry has started putting prices and payback periods on them. Neither one is a delivery, and that distinction is the story this morning.

The lead comes from China. Galbot's ET1 humanoid is reportedly priced at 79,000 yuan for the Standard version. The Pro and Flagship tiers go up to 179,000 yuan. The word "reportedly" matters. The prices come from secondary reporting that cites QbitAI and Pandaily. I found no Galbot announcement and no wire-service confirmation, and no source gives a ship date.

What the robot is for matters as much as the price. Galbot positions the ET1 for interaction and performance work. The examples given are store greetings, theme parks and stage shows. That is not factory work.

The spec sheet is also less settled than the price. Humanoids Daily describes the ET1 as a small machine, 1.25 metres tall and 32 kilograms with its battery. A second, unverified summary describes a much larger and pricier robot under the same name. I cannot reconcile the two, so treat the size as open. The point that survives either version is simple. On the reported pricing, 79,000 yuan buys an interaction robot. It is not a benchmark for industrial humanoids, and nobody should read it as "Chinese humanoids cost this much."

UBTech gives a useful contrast on what a full-size machine earns. Its interim results, as reported by Kr Asia, show 921 full-size humanoid units sold in the first half of 2026. I could not open the Hong Kong filing itself, so that figure rests on the summary.

On my own arithmetic, not the company's, that category's revenue works out to roughly 640,000 yuan per unit sold. The line includes services, so it overstates the hardware price. Even so, it sits about eight times above the ET1's entry tier. The cheap robot and the revenue-earning robot are different classes of machine.

Capacity is the harder side to read. UBTech's chief executive says monthly output can reach around 1,500 units. The factory tour behind that claim does not disclose actual monthly production, utilization or customer deliveries. So 1,500 is potential throughput, not output.

Here is the gap, again on my arithmetic. The first-half sales pace annualizes to roughly 1,800 units. The 1,500-a-month claim implies about 18,000 a year, or roughly ten times that pace. Kr Asia also reports a production target of 10,000 full-size units for 2026. Hitting it would take a very steep second-half ramp.

There are two ways to read that gap. One is idle capacity built ahead of demand. The other is deliberate pre-build for a demand pulse, perhaps state-linked, that has not yet shown up in the numbers. Today's evidence cannot tell those apart. Either way, utilization becomes the number to watch in Chinese humanoid filings. It also bears on Beijing's reported listing test, which asks for sustainable revenue.

So the China story is real on price, and real on sales growth. The capacity claim still has to be proven.

One implication follows, and it is desk inference rather than a finding. If small-format prices keep falling, the first contested market may be research, education and light service. That is not the industrial work Western payback cases target. Western makers could then hold industrial pricing for longer than the headline price war suggests.

Now the Western answer to a price list is a payback period. Boston Dynamics says it is building robots that pay back their cost in under two years. The evidence it points to is Spot. According to Humanoids Daily's summary of the company's film, Spot has more than 500 customers in 46 countries. But the film does not present an Atlas customer case that demonstrates the result.

That gap is the whole argument. Spot is a quadruped inspection robot. A two-year payback on a legged inspection platform says little about a bipedal machine doing variable factory work. Duty cycle, uptime, labor displaced and price all differ between the two. The bull case is fair, and I would state it plainly. Spot gives Boston Dynamics a fielded service record that no new entrant can match on day one. But a service record on one product is not a payback record on another.

This connects to our October 2nd coverage. We reported then, citing Euronews, that Boston Dynamics was unlikely to list next year, because Atlas had not been deployed at scale. The payback pitch arrives before the deployment evidence a listing would need. Those two facts point the same way.

Boston Dynamics also cites a new US facility capable of producing up to 30,000 robots a year. That sits against a plan for 25,000 Atlas deployments. Both are plans. The plant is the more concrete number, and it is still a building, not output.

Tesla belongs in the same conversation about capacity. Its Optimus plant in Texas is under construction, with the steel structure estimated at 40 percent complete. That figure comes from a single secondary source, TechTarget. It measures construction, not production, and says nothing about when the line runs or at what rate. You will remember the October 2nd story, when Musk tied trimmed chip memory to Optimus volume. Memory was the constraint then. The building is a constraint now. I found no official Tesla Optimus production target to set against either one.

So capacity is now the headline currency on both sides. UBTech's monthly claim, Boston Dynamics' plant and Tesla's Texas build all sell future throughput. Discount them the way this desk has discounted order books that have not converted to revenue.

A payback only holds if the robot does the work without a person. That is the bridge to Nucleus. The company says its factory demo was 60 percent autonomous. That is a self-reported share, and I have not verified it. The figure does not say what counted as autonomous. It gives no interventions per hour and no uptime. Without those, a buyer cannot price the labor the robot displaces. An autonomy percentage is a marketing metric until an intervention rate sits next to it.

The same problem runs through the whole beat. Today's new numbers are a price list, a capacity claim, a payback target and an autonomy share. None is a delivered, paying deployment at scale. None puts cost per unit, uptime and labor displaced on one comparable line.

I also found no fresh release this window from the robot-brain labs, including Google DeepMind, Nvidia, Physical Intelligence and Skild AI. The model layer is a gap in today's evidence, not a verdict on the models. I would not read the silence as progress or as failure.

So where is embodied AI inflecting? It is inflecting in the price of small-format Chinese humanoids, and in some makers' willingness to publish unit prices at all. It is also inflecting in UBTech's full-size sales, which are growing from a small base. Where is it still a demo? It is in the payback claims for full-size machines, and in every capacity number that lacks a utilization figure beside it.

The open questions are direct. What does an ET1 cost to deliver, and who actually buys it? What does UBTech ship in the second half against its 10,000-unit target? When will Boston Dynamics show an Atlas customer, rather than a Spot one? And will any Western maker publish an intervention rate next to its payback?

Three catalysts are close. First, Agility Robotics holds its analyst and investor day tomorrow, October 6th, ahead of its SPAC merger. That has not happened yet, and it is the next test of whether a Western humanoid maker discloses unit economics anyone can check. Second, Tesla's October earnings call, the next chance for a company-sourced Optimus output number. Third, UBTech's next disclosure of actual deliveries. Until one of those lands, treat the prices and the capacity as claims.

That's Cautious Optimism Briefings for October 5th. Thanks for listening.
