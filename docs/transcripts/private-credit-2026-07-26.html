Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Credit & the Corporate Credit Cycle

Transcript · July 26, 2026

Good morning, it's Sunday, July 26th, 2026, and this is your private credit briefing.

Here's the setup. The wealth channel has been gating redemptions for two straight quarters now, so insurance balance sheets have become the marginal buyer keeping private credit's growth story alive. The freshest data this week tells you two things about that buyer at once: it's still leaning in, and it's now willing to name the risks out loud.

Start with Marsh's 2026 Global Insurance Investments Survey, fielded in March and April and released earlier this month, covering 123 insurers across 24 countries with more than four trillion dollars in investment assets. Fifty-seven percent plan to increase their private credit exposure over the next twelve to twenty-four months. Among large insurers, those with twenty-five billion dollars or more in assets, that number jumps to eighty-one percent. So the appetite is real, and it's concentrated at the top of the market.

But the same insurers surveyed are naming the exact risks a skeptic would raise. Sixty-six percent cite a shrinking illiquidity premium and tighter spreads. Fifty-four percent point to deteriorating underwriting standards or looser covenants. Fifty-one percent flag rising defaults, spread compression, or payment-in-kind structures as top concerns. That's not a hostile outside critique. That's the buyer's own trade association survey. The marginal source of private credit funding is telling you it knows the premium is being competed away, and increasing its allocation anyway. That's the classic late-cycle signature: risk awareness rising in lockstep with risk-taking, not instead of it.

Now, is this just survey noise, stated intent with no capital behind it? The realized-flow data says no. AM Best's numbers show US life and annuity insurers grew their private placement bond holdings 6.3% in 2024, to nearly one point eight trillion dollars, now over forty-five percent of the industry's entire bond portfolio. Private credit holdings broadly were up 6% that year. So the Marsh survey's forward-looking 57% isn't aspirational noise sitting on top of a flat base. It's a continuation of an already-real, already-large growth trend. The caveat: that's 2024 data, a year old relative to the survey, so treat it as evidence the trend was real recently, not proof it's still accelerating at the same pace today.

Given that, you'd expect regulators to be leaning on this concentration hard. They're not, at least not yet, and that matters. The consensus assumption has been that NAIC scrutiny would be intensifying. It isn't. The NAIC's Spring 2026 meetings adopted no changes to risk-based capital charges for CLOs or collateral loans. Proposals were merely re-exposed for further public comment, and the collateral-loans framework's implementation was pushed back a full year, to 2027. There is real deliberative machinery running underneath, a task force on capital-model governance and an industry-proposed look-through charge structure among them, but none of it has become binding. So the honest read is that regulatory attention is active but deferred, not tightening. The capital-treatment questions that would actually change the economics of insurer-funded private credit remain open into next year, which means insurers keep deploying into today's spread environment without a settled answer on how it will be capitalized tomorrow.

That shrinking illiquidity premium the insurers are worried about isn't just a survey answer, either. The securitization machine that funds leveraged loans is showing it in hard numbers. US CLO managers priced just thirty point two billion dollars across sixty-five deals in the second quarter, the slowest new-issue pace since the third quarter of 2023. Year to date issuance sits at seventy-seven point two billion, running about twenty-three percent behind last year's ninety-nine point eight billion pace at this point in the calendar. The reason isn't caution, it's arithmetic. Loan spreads are too tight relative to CLO liabilities for the equity arbitrage to clear, so managers are refinancing and resetting existing deals instead of printing new ones. That's a structural spread-compression signal, independent of any single rate move, and it corroborates exactly what the insurers just told Marsh: the premium for taking this risk has compressed to the point where it's straining the machinery on both the buy side and the securitization side.

So where does all this capital go if core direct lending and the CLO arb are both too tight to satisfy return targets? It migrates. Fitch Ratings described infrastructure finance this week as undergoing what it called a major expansion, a convergence of corporate debt, project finance, and structured finance techniques, with Ares and Blackstone specifically named as pivoting into it. Insurers, pension funds, sovereign wealth funds, and family offices are the capital base deepening that shift, and much of the underlying asset base is power and data-center adjacent, tying it directly to the AI capex buildout that's been financing itself through vehicles like the Broadcom-Anthropic chip-backed structure earlier this month. The so-what here is important: this isn't the asset class shrinking under pressure, it's the asset class extending its duration and complexity, blending covenant-based corporate underwriting with contracted-cash-flow project underwriting, potentially diluting the discipline that built direct lending's reputation in the first place.

The other migration channel is liquidity itself. Ares closed its debut private-credit secondaries fund at seven point one billion dollars, evidence that direct lending is maturing into having its own secondary market rather than remaining a buy-and-hold-to-maturity asset class. That could genuinely give limited partners an exit they've lacked. Or it could simply be the first place NAV-lagging private marks get priced at a real discount that hasn't been publicly acknowledged yet. Either reading fits the same underlying story: capital that can't clear its return target in core direct lending or CLOs is finding new structures rather than sitting on the sidelines.

Pull it together. The credit cycle right now looks like this: the marginal funding base is institutionalizing through insurance balance sheets even as those same balance sheets say the compensation for the risk is shrinking, the CLO market is independently confirming that compression through failed arbitrage economics, and capital is responding not by retreating but by migrating into longer-duration infrastructure exposure and new secondary-market liquidity structures. The biggest unresolved risk is that none of this has been tested by an actual downturn, and the regulatory backstop that might force better capital discipline on insurers has just been pushed back to 2027.

Watch two things from here. First, the alternative managers' second-quarter earnings, Apollo, Ares, Blackstone, KKR, and Blue Owl, landing late July into early August, for whether non-accrual and payment-in-kind trends actually deteriorated the way this week's data implies they might. The baseline to beat is Ares Capital's last print: non-accruals at cost of 2.1% in the first quarter, up thirty basis points from the quarter before, but still below its roughly 3% historical average. If that figure pushes toward or past 3% across the group in the coming weeks, the benign-default story starts cracking from the inside. Second, watch whether the NAIC's re-exposed proposals harden into an actual capital charge before 2027, because until they do, the insurance channel keeps growing into a risk premium that its own surveyed members admit is shrinking.

That's the briefing. Have a good day.
