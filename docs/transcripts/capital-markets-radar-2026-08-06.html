Good morning, it's Thursday, August 6th, 2026, and this is Capital Markets Radar.

For a third straight day now, this show has been tracking one arc: the tape is separating what companies deliver from what they merely announce. On Tuesday we flagged the capex-heavy names taking the biggest earnings hits. On Wednesday, Palantir got rewarded for turning AI demand into real, guided-up revenue, not just capex promises. Today that same arc shows up inside the hyperscalers' own earnings, and once you check the numbers, the tidy story the market told itself doesn't hold.

Here's the setup. Alphabet opened hyperscaler season on July 22nd by raising full-year 2026 capex guidance to $195 to $205 billion, up from $180 to $190 billion, backed by cloud revenue up 82% year over year to $24.8 billion and a $514 billion cloud backlog. That backlog matters — it says the spend is being pulled by contracted demand, not speculation. Then Meta reported on July 29th: capex guidance narrowed to $130 to $145 billion, quarterly capital expenditure of $31 billion, and free cash flow of just $784 million. Meta stock fell nearly 10% after hours and closed the week down six and a half percent. Microsoft, by contrast, rose 22% on the week, and Amazon rose 17%. The reflexive read — the one this show flagged going into today — was that Microsoft and Amazon had shown more free-cash-flow discipline than Meta, and the market was finally pricing that discipline.

Their own numbers say otherwise. Microsoft's fiscal fourth quarter, the period ended June 30th, showed free cash flow of roughly $19.6 billion — down on the order of 23% year over year, per reporting on that release. Amazon's number is more striking still: trailing-twelve-month free cash flow turned negative for the first time in recent history, an outflow of $7.6 billion, against a positive $18.2 billion a year ago. Amazon's quarterly capex hit $54 billion, more than 60% above a year earlier, and the company raised full-year guidance again, to roughly $220 billion from $200 billion. Microsoft, meanwhile, guided fiscal 2027 capex toward $175 billion. So the two stocks the market rewarded both saw their own free cash flow deteriorate over the same window Meta's did — Amazon's arguably worse, since it went from positive to outright negative.

That kills the "FCF discipline" story as stated. What's left is a more contingent read: investors are pricing cloud revenue growth and backlog credibility company by company, not screening on a free-cash-flow line. Microsoft and Amazon get rewarded because their spend still looks outrun by demand in the market's eyes; Meta gets punished less for the capex itself and more for what came with it — a profit miss and a monetization lag on the ad side. And note what this is not: it's rotation within the Mag 7, not a broad de-rating of the group. The money that came out of Meta didn't leave big tech; it moved to the names whose cloud growth still looks like it's outrunning the spend. So what this actually tells you: the "capex vigilante" narrative is real in the sense that the market is now discriminating, but it isn't discriminating on the metric everyone assumed. Don't extend the Microsoft-Amazon-good, Meta-bad framing to the next earnings cycle as if it were a settled screen — watch revenue and backlog trajectories instead.

Now the same buildout, viewed from the other end. Copper hit a fresh all-time high this week, with the September Comex contract touching $6.7045 a pound — just over $14,780 a tonne — above the prior intraday record of $6.69 set in mid-May. More than 200,000 tonnes of copper arrived at US ports in July alone, the largest monthly inflow on record going back to 2014. That's tariff hoarding, not demand. While US inventories swell toward a million tonnes, the London Metal Exchange's visible stockpile has drained to a near three-month low of 352,100 tonnes, flipping that market into backwardation with a cash premium over $100 a tonne — the widest since January. So the record price is a distortion story, not a growth story: US buyers are front-running an expected Trump tariff decision, and the rest of the world's physical market is quietly tightening underneath it. The read-through for the AI buildout is direct — copper is a core input to data-center and grid construction, so this raises the capex bill at exactly the moment equity investors have turned sensitive to spend. And when the tariff decision actually lands, whichever way it goes, expect a violent unwind of the Comex-LME spread, with margin calls for anyone caught long the arbitrage.

One layer of this story hasn't turned selective at all: credit. High-yield spreads sit near 284 basis points over Treasurys, in the richest decile of their history against a long-run median close to 450. Investment grade trades near 81 basis points, BBBs near 100, and the ratio between high-yield and investment-grade spreads is close to its long-run average — so this tightness is market-wide, not a quality-tier quirk. In plain terms, the bond market is still funding the AI buildout almost as cheaply as it ever has, even as equity investors get pickier about who's spending well. That's a genuine tension: the Fed just delivered a hawkish triple dissent, yet credit is priced for a soft landing with easing on the way. Spreads this tight leave almost no cushion. If Friday's jobs report disappoints, or the Middle East oil story reverses again, credit is the layer most exposed to an asymmetric widening — worth watching hyperscaler-linked data-center debt and CDS specifically as the next tell.

Briefly, on risk appetite: US spot Bitcoin ETFs posted a seventh straight day of net inflows on August 3rd, $170 million, led by IBIT at $111 million and FBTC at $33 million, bringing the streak to roughly $981 million — about $140 million a day — and reversing June's record $4.5 billion in outflows. Don't read that as a macro all-clear. It's happening alongside elevated real yields and a hawkish Fed, which argues this is idiosyncratic crypto positioning finding its way back in, not broad liquidity easing lifting all risk assets together. That the flows are running through IBIT and FBTC also reinforces BlackRock and Fidelity as the marginal price-setters in Bitcoin now — a market-structure shift worth banking even on a quiet week. And in thin summer volumes, a streak that size can reverse just as fast as it built.

And a line of context before we close: oil's war premium has quietly faded rather than resolved. WTI has slid three straight sessions to around $75 a barrel, well off the war-premium peak that carried Brent above $100 in late July, on reports — still unconfirmed by any government source — that Washington, Tehran, and Muscat are close to a deal reopening the Strait of Hormuz. That fade is one reason gold and the front end of the rate curve have stayed calm this week even with the Fed's hawks still on record.

Friday's July jobs report is the catalyst that tests every leg of this: the capex bifurcation, the copper arbitrage, tight credit, and the crypto flow reversal all assume the labor market holds up enough to keep the soft-landing story intact. June's print was just 57,000, badly missing consensus. If July disappoints again, the layer with the least cushion — credit — is where you'll see it first.
