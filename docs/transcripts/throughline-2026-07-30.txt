Good morning, this is The Throughline for Thursday, July 30th, 2026. The single most important development across today's briefings: a hawkish Fed hold and a reignited Iran oil shock hit together this week, and on the same night Microsoft and Meta's earnings gave the AI-capex question this show has tracked all week its first real dividing line, not a clean pass or a clean fail.

Start with the macro backdrop. Strategic Power leads with the physical constraint underneath all of it: the Energy Department forced PJM's data centers onto backup generators during a record-threatening heatwave in June, and PJM's board just proposed a formal curtailment framework for new large loads. Layer on TSMC's incremental hundred-billion-dollar US buildout and yesterday's hawkish Fed dissent, and the read is that grid capacity, chip cost, and rate policy are now three reinforcing constraints on the same buildout, not three separate stories.

That Fed decision is Capital Markets Radar's lead. The FOMC voted nine to three to hold rates at three-and-a-half to three-and-three-quarters percent, but all three dissenters wanted a hike, the first unified hawkish dissent bloc since 2016. Add Iran's missile strikes reigniting an oil shock, and the ten-year yield jumped while equities sold off hard into the close. Then, on the same night, Microsoft beat on Azure's forty-three percent growth while Meta's free cash flow collapsed to under a billion dollars against comparable capex, meaning markets are now discriminating on whether the spending converts, not on how much gets spent.

Digital Money picks up that same hawkish hold from crypto's side. Bitcoin barely moved on the decision, slipping about one and a half percent, because a heavily deleveraged derivatives book had already priced the outcome in. The more durable point is structural: on-chain Treasury products now pay a competitive yield for doing nothing risky, which raises the opportunity cost of holding a non-yielding token exactly as the Fed leans higher-for-longer.

Private Credit and the Corporate Credit Cycle shows where that same rate pressure lands on borrowers. Ares Capital, the bellwether direct lender, just confirmed rising non-accruals, falling net asset value, and climbing portfolio leverage all in one quarter, with nearly a quarter of its book concentrated in software exposed to AI-driven disruption. Private Equity and the Deal Economy shows managers themselves bending under the same distribution drought: KKR's accounting reclassification will make its realized-earnings growth look cleaner than it is, Ares is reportedly in talks to buy buyout shop Leonard Green outright, and secondaries volume is on pace for a record year because it manufactures liquidity rather than reopening real exits.

Turning to the AI and compute stack. Semiconductors and Compute Infrastructure has China notching two real, if narrow, wins: a first domestic immersion lithography tool now in mass production for mature nodes, and CXMT's record IPO putting its DRAM output within reach of Micron's, though neither touches leading-edge or HBM output. Meanwhile Nvidia's five-year credit-default-swap spread hit a record high, a sign bond investors, not just equity traders, are now pricing risk in its vendor-financed deal wave.

Frontier AI Lab Competition shows the same divergence playing out inside the three big US labs. OpenAI's July revenue run-rate reportedly exceeded all of last quarter, but its own CFO says compute is sold out through 2027 at fifty billion dollars a gigawatt. Google shipped three new Gemini models but conspicuously not the flagship update it owes. Anthropic is shipping fastest, posting a real benchmark lead, and quietly building the enterprise distribution, through Cognizant and MCP support, that may matter more than any leaderboard score.

Enterprise Technology puts a number on that same monetization split. Microsoft's Azure growth is accelerating with a backlog up eighty-four percent, while Meta's AI spending shows almost no comparable revenue line to point to, and Google Cloud's five-hundred-fourteen-billion-dollar backlog sits as the disclosed middle case neither rival matches. Underneath the infrastructure fight, ServiceNow's AI governance product just passed five hundred customers in six months, evidence that overseeing agents is becoming its own budget line.

Finally, the applied frontier. Robotics and Physical AI has Anduril reportedly in talks at a hundred-billion-dollar valuation, up forty billion in two months on an unsigned deal, while Symbotic posted its first profitable quarter but missed earnings estimates by an order of magnitude. Both are capital pricing autonomy on narrative faster than the operating numbers back it up, even as Nvidia lines up real Japanese industrial partners behind its on-device robotics model.

Here's the thread worth naming, because it's the moment four straight days of AI-capex skepticism actually resolved into an answer. This show has spent the week asking whether the industry's spending was outrunning its returns. Last night it stopped being a question and became a discriminator: Microsoft's monetizing Azure backlog got rewarded, Meta's uncompensated capex got punished, and that exact same split, cash generated versus cash promised, shows up today in Ares Capital's rising non-accruals against its flat dividend, in KKR's flattering accounting against its real monetization number, and in Anduril's narrative valuation against Symbotic's earnings miss. The capex debate didn't end today. It just found its dividing line, and every domain this show covers is now being sorted by it.
