Good morning, it's Friday, September 4th, and this is your briefing on AI in biopharma R&D.

For a week this beat has tracked one story: the model layer in AI biology closing up. Benchmark scores published without methodology. Weights promised and not shipped. Data released, when it's released at all, under licenses that bar you from building anything commercial on it. Yesterday I closed by naming the one thing that would break that read. A released dataset with no strings attached. That same day, in Seattle, a candidate answer launched.

It's called AI BioDesign. Ninety-four point six million dollars over five years, announced yesterday, September 3rd. The partners are the Allen Institute, the University of Washington's Institute for Protein Design and Brotman Baty Institute, and Fred Hutch Cancer Center. The money comes from the Paul Allen estate. It's led by David Baker, the UW biochemist who won the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for computational protein design, alongside genome scientist Jay Shendure. Launch coverage states the aim plainly: "the public release of models, datasets, assays, reagents, and benchmarks." Take it, provisionally, as the direct rebuttal to a week of closure. A Nobel-anchored consortium is betting that the field's bottleneck isn't proprietary model scale at all.

What's actually new here isn't the money. It's the premise. Baker frames it this way: "The speed of AI is beginning to match the experimental power of synthetic biology. That changes the question from 'what has nature already made?' to 'what else is possible, and how can we test it?'" That answers a question this beat raised yesterday and couldn't cost out. The question was whether the binding constraint on AI biology is model capability or wet-lab throughput. Baker's answer is throughput. He's saying the scarce thing is purpose-generated design-build-test-measure data, not parameters. AI BioDesign is built to fund that loop rather than to train another foundation model. If he's right, the whole open-versus-closed argument has been running at the wrong layer.

Now the skeptical turn, and there are two pressure points here, not one. First, this is a five-year infrastructure promise with zero drug programs and zero near-term commercial output attached. Note also that ninety-four point six million is the five-year total, not an annual budget. No per-year or per-project allocation has been disclosed. That puts it squarely inside the pattern this show named across every beat in Wednesday's Throughline: measuring a promise rather than a delivery.

Second, and sharper. The release commitment carries no named license and no timeline. No coverage reviewed names MIT, Apache, or Creative Commons terms, and nobody has said when anything actually ships. So treat "released openly" as a stated intention, not a shipped release. And David Baker is not a neutral party to open versus closed. He co-founded Xaira Therapeutics. Xaira is a private, very much operating AI drug-discovery company, and it launched, according to its launch coverage, with more than one billion dollars in funding. That is roughly ten times AI BioDesign's entire five-year commitment. This beat covered Xaira yesterday for the opposite reason. It keeps its science closed by delaying publication and by restrictive licensing, rather than by saying nothing at all. And no source addresses whether AI BioDesign's open outputs and Xaira's proprietary pipeline are walled off from one another. The honest read is that the same person sits on both sides of this ledger. My lean is that the open consortium ends up subsidizing the closed platform rather than checking it. Pre-competitive open data is exactly the base layer a well-capitalized private player fine-tunes on top of. And exclusive fine-tuning data gets more valuable, not less, when the base layer is free.

That capital gap sets up the closed side directly. If one founder's private venture outspends the open camp ten to one, look at what the incumbents are actually selling. Isomorphic Labs' Drug Design Engine claims 50 percent accuracy on the hardest protein-ligand cases. AlphaFold 3 manages 23.3 percent on the same set. That figure is Isomorphic's own, and outside researchers have not replicated it.

That's the design layer, where specialized architectures still own the work. Now the workhorse layer, where general-purpose language models are actually earning their keep today. Literature synthesis, protocol drafting, coding, and operational agents. OpenAI's GPT-Rosalind launched on April 16th, not recently, as its first model built specifically for life sciences, on top of GPT-5.5. It's a research preview under a trusted-access program, not general availability. Launch partners include Amgen, Moderna, the Allen Institute, Thermo Fisher Scientific, and Novo Nordisk. OpenAI says GPT-Rosalind "leads GPT-5.5, Grok 4.3, and Gemini 3.1 Pro on the overall score" of its own LifeSciBench evaluation. Note the epistemic posture, because it is identical to Isomorphic's. The vendor publishes the ranking that sells access, and withholds the numbers that would let a buyer audit it. No independent leaderboard exists. And trusted-access gating means even the customers can't fully stress-test the claim. Buyers at both layers are being asked to price a capability they cannot verify.

One note on what isn't in this briefing. Nothing dated moved on the real-world-data side this week. No fresh event anchored the federated electronic-health-record thread, so the standing read there stands unchanged, and I'd rather say that than restate it for a fourth time.

Watch what Novo Nordisk is doing about the verification problem. It's a GPT-Rosalind launch partner, and it also named AWS its preferred cloud and strategic AI partner back in August. One company, hedging across at least two frontier ecosystems at once. That isn't indecision. Large pharma is deliberately refusing to underwrite a single model vendor. The consequence is pricing power. Multi-sourcing caps what any one closed platform can extract at the workhorse layer, which is where the tasks commoditize fastest. Expect that pattern to spread, with sponsors multi-sourcing the operating layer even while they single-source the harder design-layer bets.

Now the economics, brief and hard, because this is what design capability actually sells for today. Insilico Medicine's CNS collaboration with SK Biopharmaceuticals, signed at BIO in late June, carries a headline value exceeding two and a half billion dollars in potential milestones. Insilico's own disclosure puts near-term cash at up to eighteen million dollars, plus single-digit royalties on net sales. Quote those two numbers together or not at all. Under one percent of the headline is anything like committed capital. That is the market price of AI-designed candidates absent clinical proof, and it tells you the bulk of every billion-dollar AI biotech headline is options value. For the AI-discovery equities, price the eighteen million and treat the rest as a lottery ticket. And notice what the AI BioDesign launch does to that ratio. Milestone-heavy pricing depends on design capability being scarce, and an open consortium giving away models and benchmarks attacks precisely that scarcity.

One group gets paid on a much shorter clock than any of this. If the winning approach to AI biology is data generation rather than model scale, then the spend lands first as assays, reagents, and instruments. Tools and consumables vendors, and the cloud providers hosting the compute, book that revenue years before any pipeline event. AI BioDesign's own stated deliverables include assays and reagents, which makes it a purchase-order roadmap as much as a research plan. The platform companies, by contrast, have to wait for a clinical readout before anyone can score them at all.

So pull the threads. The open-versus-closed fight in this field was never really about model weights. It's about who pays to generate the experimental data underneath them. The AI BioDesign launch is credible on method, and roughly an order of magnitude short on capital against Baker's own private venture, Xaira. So the closed platforms most likely keep setting the terms on which their own capability gets scored. The concrete test now is narrower and checkable. It's whether AI BioDesign actually ships weights, data, and benchmarks under a named license with no strings, and on what clock. Watch for the license text, not the next funding headline. And if it does ship open, and the data proves competitive, the interesting question isn't whether it breaks the closed platforms. It's whether they quietly build on top of it.

That's the briefing for today. Have a good Friday.
