Good morning, it's Friday, October 2nd, and this is your digital money briefing.

Bitcoin first, briefly, because today's real story sits one layer down. As we reported yesterday, the spot ETF inflow streak broke on Wednesday with a $148.7 million net outflow. That ended a nine-day run worth $3.1 billion. One outflow day after nine days of inflows is a thin sample, not a trend. Price is still pinned around $83,500. That's just under the $84,000 to $85,000 band where Glassnode puts the heaviest cluster of long-term-holder supply. No newer flow print is in, so we won't re-run those numbers.

What we can add is a positioning wrinkle. Bitfinex has called perpetual positioning close to neutral. A separate derivatives read complicates that. It shows Bitcoin futures open interest down 3.8 percent since August 21st, to about 318,600 coins. Over the same stretch, the cost for longs to hold their positions was rising. Falling open interest with rising long funding means fewer positions paying more to stay long. That's a warning sign for a crowded long book, not a confirmed squeeze setup. So the neutral read needs qualifying. Total positioning is shrinking, but the longs that remain are paying up, so the book is getting more one-sided even as it gets smaller. If that persists, the next move could be amplified by forced unwinds rather than driven by fresh conviction. The September jobs report lands after this airs, so treat it as the next test, not a known result.

While Bitcoin waits, the more durable change is in who gets paid to move dollars on-chain.

Last week, Binance agreed to buy $100 million of Circle stock. The story is less about the money than about what Circle gave up to get it. Binance is paying $80.84 a share, which Circle's own filing calls a discount to the market price. The shares carry a lockup of up to two years, with some exceptions. Alongside the stake, the two companies signed a commercial arrangement with a five-year term.

The reach numbers are real. CoinDesk reports that Binance now has 329 USDC-quoted spot markets, up from 140 when the partnership began. Monthly USDC trading volume on the exchange has roughly doubled. It ran in a $20 to $40 billion range before the partnership. It now runs consistently above $80 billion. Binance had previously sidelined USDC in favor of its own house stablecoin. So this is a genuine reversal, and a real distribution win for Circle against Tether.

Here's the part that matters more than the volume chart. Circle's 8-K spells out how it pays for that reach. Circle pays Binance a monthly incentive fee. The fee is a percentage of the USDC held through Binance's Modular Smart Contract Wallet infrastructure. That's not a flat sponsorship payment. It's a rent that scales with balances in one specific distribution channel. The filing does not disclose the percentage or the dollar size. So we can't quantify it, and you should be skeptical of anyone who does.

One wrinkle in that design deserves attention. The fee tracks balances in that wallet channel, not total USDC on Binance. If more balances migrate into the channel over five years, Circle's distribution cost could grow faster than its float on the exchange. So the cost of this deal is not fixed. It can ratchet up even if Binance's total USDC holdings stand still.

This extends an arc we've been tracking. On September 25th, we showed Coinbase's stablecoin revenue for the quarter ended June 30th falling to $292 million, down $17 million from a year earlier. Over the same period, average USDC held in Coinbase products rose 44 percent. The lesson then was to ask who holds the contract, not just who holds the balance. Binance now shows that lesson from Circle's side of the ledger. Two of the biggest venues in crypto sit between USDC's float and Circle's income line, each under its own contract. So USDC's float can grow while Circle's net interest income lags behind it.

Now be skeptical of the volume number specifically. Exchange volume is not float, and it is not end-user demand. A doubling of USDC trading on Binance can come from market makers and fee-incentivized pair routing. None of that requires new dollars entering USDC. Circle is, in effect, paying to be a default dollar pair on the venue. That is a different claim from "USDC adoption is accelerating." The stake itself also needs perspective. A hundred million dollars is small next to either company's scale, and the lockup has carve-outs. Read it as a commercial alignment signal, not a capital commitment.

Now the bull case, stated plainly, because the evidence supports a real one. Circle's revenue comes from reserve interest on Treasury bills, not from trading activity. Distribution gains that don't depend on a crypto bull market are exactly what that revenue base needs. And locked-up equity aligns the largest offshore exchange with USDC's growth, rather than with a house token. That alignment persists even if Bitcoin goes nowhere.

Set that against Tether. USDT's circulating supply is reported anywhere from roughly $140 billion to $184 billion, depending on the source and the date. That's a real discrepancy, and we won't paper over it. Either way, USDC remains the clear number two. On September 29th we framed USDC as doing more of the settlement work, while Tether carries the bigger float. This deal is Circle paying to compete for venue-level float as well.

The second-order effect is that exchange-stablecoin relationships are turning into fee-for-distribution contracts, rather than loyalty arrangements. In what we've reviewed, Tether has no equivalent disclosed deal. Our read is that Tether now faces a choice. It can match these distribution payments, or cede venue share to USDC. Meanwhile, anyone modeling Circle stock as USDC float times the bill yield will overstate earnings if distribution rents keep rising. The better framework is float times yield, minus whatever Circle pays venues to hold that float. Circle is buying share, and the size of that bill decides whether float growth reaches the income line.

The rulebook may tilt that race too, but only at the margin for now. The Federal Reserve's September 24th GENIUS Act proposal would set standardized capital requirements for the issuers it supervises. Press coverage has circulated a sliding scale that would ease as issuers grow. The Fed's own release states no percentages or thresholds, so we won't air any. If a scale like that survives, it would raise the bar for new bank entrants and favor incumbents. But this is still a proposal, and it reaches Fed-supervised issuers, not Circle or Tether directly. For now, its bite falls mostly on banks weighing whether to issue their own tokens.

So here's where that leaves us. Bitcoin's marginal bid is paused. It's caught between a long-term-holder supply wall overhead and a smaller but more one-sided long book underneath. The more consequential competition is in the stablecoin layer. Circle now pays for distribution on the largest offshore exchange, on top of the contract that shapes Coinbase's USDC economics. That rent will decide whether USDC's share gains reach Circle's bottom line.

Three things to watch. First, the next ETF flow print against that $84,000 to $85,000 band. Second, any Circle disclosure that actually sizes the Binance fee. Third, Treasury's GENIUS Act comment deadline on October 19th, which we flagged on Tuesday.
