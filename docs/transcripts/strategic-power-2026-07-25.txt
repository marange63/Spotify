Good morning. It's Saturday, July 25th, 2026, and this is your briefing on strategic power.

Three of the world's most important oil chokepoints are under attack at once, and yet oil got cheaper this week. Start with the geography. Before this war began in late February, the Strait of Hormuz carried roughly twenty million barrels of crude a day. Treat that as a pre-war baseline, not today's flow, because at the height of the earlier fighting traffic through the strait collapsed to as few as two tankers a day. Since Iran's Revolutionary Guard fired at least two missiles at commercial ships crossing the strait on the night of July 6th, something like a dozen vessels have been hit there. That count varies by source, so hold it loosely. This week the Houthis opened a second front, striking Saudi tankers in the Red Sea after declaring a maritime embargo on Riyadh. And on the Black Sea, Ukrainian drones hit two more tankers at the Caspian Pipeline Consortium's Novorossiysk terminal. That was the fifth strike on CPC facilities.

The Black Sea leg is the genuinely new piece, and it matters because of what that pipeline carries. CPC moves about eighty percent of Kazakhstan's oil exports and close to two percent of global oil supply. So this is not a peripheral route. Its throughput was already softening before the strikes, down seven percent month on month to about one point seven million barrels a day in June. After the July 20th attack, CPC suspended loadings again, reversing a brief resumption from the night before, as its storage tanks neared capacity. Kazakhstan's energy ministry confirmed a controlled cut to production to keep those tanks from overflowing.

Now the honest part. Nobody has produced a verified figure for how many barrels a day that suspension actually took off the market. Secondary press has circulated large numbers for the production cut at Tengiz, Kazakhstan's biggest field, but I could not stand those up against a dated source, so I am not going to put a barrel count in your ear. The freshest confirmed status I have is from July 23rd: loadings suspended, tanks full. Whether CPC is still down this morning, partially running, or restored is genuinely unresolved. And that opacity is itself the story. A major export artery is throttled, the operator is not publishing a number, and the market is trading on inference.

So: three actors, three unrelated strategic aims, one shared effect. Iran is trying to raise the cost of the war on Washington and Riyadh. The Houthis are pressuring Saudi Arabia directly. Ukraine is attacking Russian export infrastructure and catching Kazakhstan, a non-combatant, in the blast radius. This is no longer a single-point outage you can model as a temporary closure. It is a structural test of maritime energy security running on three fronts at the same time.

Here is the paradox. Brent crude fell four percent on Friday, its biggest one-day drop since late June, settling near ninety-seven dollars a barrel. That is still roughly twenty-seven fifty a barrel above where it traded a year ago, so the war premium is very much intact — it just got smaller on a day when the attack surface got wider. The reason is diplomacy. Reports that Pakistan and China are trying to revive US-Iran talks pulled risk premium out of the tape faster than the drone strikes could put it back in. The lesson is not that supply risk went away. It is that the tape is pricing the off-ramp rather than the barrels, and a bet on negotiation can unwind in a single session if the channel goes quiet.

There is a second-order read worth holding onto. When Hormuz, the Red Sea, and the Black Sea are all contested simultaneously, the scarce commodity stops being crude and becomes uncontested logistics. That structurally advantages the barrel and the molecule that never transits a chokepoint anyone is shooting at: US shale, US Gulf Coast LNG, Guyana. It also argues that tanker war-risk insurance and freight are re-rating on a structural basis rather than a cyclical one. And for landlocked Kazakhstan, it is a hard lesson in routing eighty percent of your exports through infrastructure controlled by, and adjacent to, someone else's war.

The same attritional logic is showing up on the refining side. Russia is now weighing whether to extend its diesel export ban another month past its July 31st expiry, and separately discussing a further six months on its gasoline ban. Both bans exist because Ukrainian strikes on refineries and terminals keep degrading Russian output. To be clear, this is a deliberation reported through unnamed sources, not a signed decree. But it resolves the question this briefing left open on Friday, and it resolves it in the direction of attrition. Moscow needs more time, not less. That tells you the refinery damage is compounding rather than healing. Layer it onto an already thin global middle-distillate market and you get upward pressure on diesel that is independent of where crude is heading. The so-what: energy infrastructure warfare is becoming a durable substitute for battlefield gains, and its price effects are now separable from the headline oil number everyone watches.

Now pivot from the contested barrel abroad to the contested megawatt at home. The White House this week expanded its Ratepayer Protection Pledge to cover, by its own account, eighty percent of all power delivered to US homes and businesses. The commitment is simple: large data-center operators, not ratepayers, fund the electricity generation and infrastructure their projects require. The release points to state-level deals as proof — Indiana's NiSource projecting one point four billion dollars in customer savings over fifteen years, Georgia's Southern Company freezing base rates through 2029. Those savings figures are the administration's own, unverified against the underlying utility filings, so treat them as the claim rather than the audit.

The pledge exists because of a problem this feed has tracked all week. PJM's latest capacity auction, covering sixty-seven million people across thirteen states, fell short of its own reliability requirement for the first time in the grid operator's history. It cleared at the price cap for a sixteen point four billion dollar payout to generators — and without that cap, the same auction would have cost twenty-nine point seven billion. That gap between the capped and uncapped price is the cleanest measure of how tight the region actually is. Data-center load is what ratepayer advocates blame for the squeeze.

Here is the skeptical note, and it is the heart of the item. Those same advocates told federal regulators on July 17th that the underlying cost-allocation problem is untouched. Their language was blunt. Each network upgrade added to a transmission owner's revenue requirement, they wrote, embeds another cost shift. And states have, in their words, limited ability to allocate those transmission costs when PJM's zones cross state borders. Read the pledge against that complaint and you find two different problems. The pledge covers local, project-level generation and infrastructure. The advocates are describing regional transmission costs that spill across state lines by design. Closing the first does not close the second. So Washington now has a genuine political answer to the loudest objection, which is rising household bills, while the structural allocation question sits underneath it unresolved. A voluntary, state-by-state, bilateral pledge is not the instrument that fixes a regional cost-allocation gap. Watch whether that eighty percent represents binding, filed rate agreements or signatures in principle.

Last, a short coda that loops back to where we started. Nvidia has shipped only what a senior Commerce Department official described in testimony this month as a trivial number of H200 chips to China since approval — that characterization comes via press reporting of his remarks, not an official transcript. Meanwhile Beijing is expected to cap its own import approvals below two hundred thousand chips, less than half what Chinese buyers asked for, according to a TrendForce estimate rather than a published approval list. Blackwell, Nvidia's top-tier part, stays banned outright. So both governments reopened the chip trade on paper ahead of Xi Jinping's planned September 24th visit, and both are quietly throttling the actual volume. Neither wants to concede real ground before the summit. And China is among the largest buyers of crude moving through Hormuz, which gives Beijing its own reason to want the shooting to stop there — tying the chip track back to the chokepoint story we opened with.

Three things to watch. Whether CPC's Black Sea status clarifies one way or the other, and whether anyone publishes a credible barrel figure for the loss. Whether Moscow actually signs the diesel extension rather than merely discussing it. And whether that eighty percent ratepayer number turns out to be binding commitments or a press release.

That's the briefing. Have a good weekend.
