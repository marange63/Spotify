Good morning, it's Thursday, October 22nd, and this is your digital money briefing.

Yesterday this desk called Monday's bitcoin ETF inflow a one-fund story. It was a big print, but it said little about broad demand. A second data source now backs that read. Per SoSoValue data reported by Yahoo Finance, BlackRock's IBIT alone took in $665.16 million on Monday, October 19th. The whole US spot bitcoin complex netted only $526.6 million. So every other fund, taken together, was a net seller that day.

What's new is what happened next. Yesterday we asked whether Monday would follow through, or reverse the way an earlier October up-day did. It followed through. Tuesday held positive at $23.16 million. Then Wednesday, October 21st, brought $528.40 million, a 22.8-fold jump, according to TokenPost's tally. A second outlet, Chaincatcher, independently confirms that print and calls it a three-day net-inflow streak. With two sources behind each day, the streak no longer needs a hedge. One caveat on the tallies themselves: daily trackers disagree at the margin, so treat these as tracker figures, not official ones.

The composition is the real story. On Wednesday, IBIT took $234.99 million. Fidelity's FBTC took $212.71 million, close to a dead heat. Even Grayscale's GBTC, the high-fee legacy fund that has ceded share to cheaper rivals for years, pulled in $32.60 million. That breaks the pattern this desk has tracked all week, where big days in both directions were mostly one fund's doing. On Wednesday, demand came through at least three issuers in a single session. So the concentration objection we raised yesterday now has its answer.

Now the counterweight, because two big days don't erase a bad month. Bitcoin's all-time high is $126,080, set October 6th of last year. CoinDesk had the spot price at about $82,761 early this morning. One historical data table shows bitcoin pushing above $84,000 on Wednesday before fading back. Either way, price sits far below the peak. Inflows of this size against a price that barely moved read as dip-buying inside a range, not a breakout chase.

One line on the level this desk has watched all month. Bitcoin is still above Glassnode's estimate of roughly $81,500 for the average US spot ETF holder's cost basis, so the typical ETF buyer is in the money for now. Glassnode's warning stands: a daily close below that line would put the average holder back at a loss and remove the nearest on-chain support. What we still can't tell you is whether October as a whole has flipped to net inflow. No source states a month-to-date total we can quote, so we won't stitch one together ourselves.

Here's the skeptical case, and it's the one we can't resolve today. Broadening across funds tells you demand is less concentrated. It does not tell you who the buyer is. The bullish read is that allocators are stepping back in and spreading orders across issuers. The bearish read is a basis trade re-levering. That means buying the spot ETF while shorting CME futures to harvest the spread. It would look identical in flow data. But it would unwind the moment funding moves against it, while a patient allocator sits through that move. We went looking for evidence that separates the two, meaning CME open interest or an annualized basis print, and came up empty. The open-interest numbers that do exist are undated or stale. They also conflict by a factor of five, depending on whether you count contracts or underlying bitcoin. So here's the honest scorecard. The objection about one fund carrying the weight is answered. The objection about who is behind the demand is not, and won't be until basis data cooperates.

If the ETF bid is widening, the buyer that defined the last cycle is moving the other way. Strategy, the company formerly known as MicroStrategy, filed an 8-K on October 19th. It shows Strategy bought just 267 bitcoin that week, for about $22.1 million. Nearly all of that money came from selling MSTR stock, with only a token amount from cash. That extends the run of small weeks this desk flagged on Monday, when Strategy's bitcoin spend was dwarfed by a buyback of its own STRC preferred. Strategy's stack now stands at 848,517 bitcoin, at an average cost of $75,446 a coin.

Run that average cost against this morning's spot price near $82,761. On our arithmetic, Strategy's entire stack sits only about ten percent above water. That's a thin cushion for a company whose playbook is selling stock or convertible debt at a premium to buy bitcoin at spot. And notice the funding mix. There were no converts and no preferred, just common stock, the most dilutive tool in the kit. That could mean common is the cheapest window open right now. Or it could mean it's the only one left. Nothing in the filing tells us which, and without a premium-to-NAV figure we won't pretend otherwise. The mechanism is still worth naming. If Strategy's equity premium compresses, the sell-stock-to-buy-bitcoin flywheel loses its edge, and a source of structural demand outside the ETFs goes quiet. To be clear, small buys are not selling. Nothing in the filing shows Strategy selling bitcoin.

Here's the comparison that lands it. Again on our arithmetic, Strategy's $22.1 million week is under a tenth of IBIT's Wednesday inflow alone. The price-insensitive corporate buyer behind the 2024 and 2025 treasury-company narrative is now smaller than one fund's daily flow.

Both of those bids, ETF and corporate, ultimately settle in dollars on-chain. And the rulebook for those dollars just stopped being a proposal. All week this desk has narrated comment deadlines and advance notices. On October 21st, an agency actually finalized something. The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency issued its final rule implementing the GENIUS Act's framework for payment stablecoins. Per Cointelegraph's account, the rule and the underlying Act take effect January 18th, 2027. Issuers must hold reserves worth at least as much as their outstanding stablecoins. They must generally process redemptions within two business days. The rule covers national banks, federal savings associations and their subsidiaries, plus state-qualified issuers where the OCC has authority. Note what it leaves out. Anti-money-laundering and sanctions requirements are reserved for a separate rulemaking with no date attached, so a core compliance cost is still unpriced.

The detail that matters economically is yield. The ban on issuers paying interest or yield solely for holding a stablecoin survives. But the OCC dropped a proposed presumption that affiliate and related-party arrangements automatically violate that ban. That leaves exchange-paid stablecoin rewards, yield by another name, in a grey zone. They'll be policed case by case under the OCC's anti-evasion authority, not ruled out up front. That's arguably a loosening, not a tightening, because it moves the fight from the rulebook to future enforcement. And it's the question that decides whether stablecoins compete with bank deposits and money-market funds on return, not just on payments. If rewards survive, that deposit channel stays open. If the OCC later calls them evasion, retail stablecoin demand loses its main yield hook. Meanwhile, the January effective date gives issuers under three months to amend reserve and redemption arrangements. Expect a wave of those disclosures before year-end.

The bigger pattern is worth one line. A binding agency rule arriving before any market-structure statute keeps the US build-out administrative, which also makes it reversible. The first public read on exchange economics inside this grey zone comes when Coinbase reports third-quarter results after the close on October 29th. That print hasn't happened, so nothing about it should be assumed today.

So here's where that leaves us. The ETF bid is broadening, though we can't yet say whether allocators or a basis trade sit behind it. The corporate bid that built the last cycle has shrunk below a single fund's daily flow, and it now runs on common-stock issuance. And the stablecoin rulebook is final on backing and redemption, with yield left as an open enforcement question. Watch three things into month-end, all still pending. First, whether ETF inflows keep their breadth across issuers. Second, the Fed's decision on October 28th. Third, Coinbase's print the next day.
