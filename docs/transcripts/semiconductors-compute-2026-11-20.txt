Good morning. It's Friday, November 20, 2026, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

Three actors moved this week against the same two chokepoints in the chip stack: high-bandwidth memory and advanced packaging. Washington wrote them into export law. Memory makers are repricing around them. And Intel just landed its first public outside customer for one of them.

Start with the rule. New Commerce Department rules took effect on Thursday. They remove TSMC's ability to check for itself whether a customer's design falls outside US export controls. That self-certification route had let some Chinese designers get advanced manufacturing without individual licenses. Now, orders using sixteen-nanometer-or-smaller processes must be halted unless the customer holds a US export license. Affected Chinese customers are generally limited to older nodes, twenty-eight and forty nanometers.

Exactly who counts as covered isn't cleanly defined in anything public. One secondary account ties the removed route to Country Groups D-1, D-4 and D-5, which include China. Another frames it around designers missing from the Commerce Department's approved-designer lists. It adds that no mainland Chinese company is on that list today. Neither account is the rule text itself, so hold both loosely.

The operational effect is reported, not confirmed. Per FT and Reuters reporting from unnamed sources, relayed through secondary outlets, TSMC and Samsung have paused some shipments to Chinese customers. Neither company has issued a statement or a filing. The same reporting sketches the customer base at stake. TSMC makes smartphone chips for Oppo, Vivo and Xiaomi. Samsung's large Chinese customers include Baidu and ByteDance. Those are relationships, not confirmed halts. Industry sources told the FT that licenses for consumer smartphone chips would likely be approved, but that the process could delay manufacturing. So for phones, the near-term hit looks like delay and friction, not a permanent cutoff.

On Tuesday, this desk covered a companion rule on who may use advanced compute remotely, with a ninety-day license window for data centers in five named countries. That rule was scheduled for Federal Register publication this same Thursday, taking effect on publication. That speaks to the effective-date question we flagged on Wednesday. Tuesday's rule moved the control point to who uses the compute. Thursday's rule reaches one layer further back, into the fab itself.

Here's the hinge for the whole briefing. Even the twenty-eight and forty nanometer allowance excludes any design that uses high-bandwidth memory or advanced packaging. So Washington isn't just drawing a line at process node. It's defining AI-capable silicon by its memory and its packaging. For a covered Chinese customer without a license, a mature-node accelerator with HBM isn't a workaround. It's out of bounds.

That forces a correction to our own running read. For three straight briefings, this desk said China's domestic accelerator makers gain from tightening controls. Yesterday we said they gained by default. That needs revising. Demand for domestic chips still rises. But for covered designers without licenses, the route to build those chips closes too: leading-edge foundry capacity, HBM, and advanced packaging. On our inference, the gain now depends on China's own manufacturing catching up, and nothing in the record quantifies that. A second inference follows. Designs pushed down to older nodes, or into domestic fabs, raise the payoff to China's own mature-node and packaging capacity. Over time, that could feed the legacy-node overcapacity story. And chip-making equipment, which this desk expected to be the next layer, still isn't touched by anything reported.

Now the sizing, and there isn't one. No source gives a quoted figure for TSMC's or Samsung's China revenue at risk. Treat that exposure as unsized. The only clean market verdict is TSMC's stock. It fell three-point-six-eight percent on Tuesday, on volume about eighty percent above its thirty-day average. That drop came before the shipment-halt reports. So it priced the leak, not measured exposure. The so-what: the policy has moved the contest for AI silicon from the logic node to the two scarcest inputs behind it.

If HBM is where Washington drew the line, it's also where the market is charging the most. TrendForce said on November 18 that HBM4 prices could reach three to five-and-a-half dollars per gigabit next year. That's roughly one-and-a-half to two-point-seven times this year's level of about two dollars and five cents. On Monday, this desk cited a press projection of HBM4 prices roughly doubling. TrendForce's top end now sits well above that.

Three cautions. First, these are projections, not signed contract prices. Second, the range is very wide, and that width tells you 2027 negotiations are still unsettled. Third, the comparison may not be like-for-like. It isn't clear whether this year's baseline is HBM4 alone or a blend of generations. HBM4 is a newer, costlier part, so on our read, some of that multiple may be product premium rather than pure scarcity.

The mechanism is clearer than the price. TrendForce projects HBM's share of total DRAM wafer input at Samsung, SK hynix and Micron to approach thirty percent next year. That's up from twenty-three percent this year. It puts a number on the ordinary-DRAM squeeze this desk has been tracking. Earlier this month, Qualcomm's chief executive said the handset problem was supply, not demand. Every wafer moved to HBM is a wafer that phones and PCs don't get.

Read the pricing two ways. One is structural scarcity rent, because memory capacity simply can't keep up with accelerator demand. The other is a cyclical peak. Multi-year lock-in contracts cut memory makers' spot flexibility. They also raise the odds of a sharper correction if accelerator demand slows.

Who absorbs the higher cost is still unresolved. It lands either on accelerator vendors' margins or on hyperscalers' capital budgets. On Nvidia's call, the only margin explanation management offered was the cost of supply. Add one legal wrinkle. On November 14, a federal judge rejected a motion to dismiss a DRAM price-fixing suit against Samsung, SK hynix and Micron. That's a ruling on a motion, not a finding of liability. But it's a standing tail risk for all three memory majors, just as they reprice their best product higher. The so-what: the fastest-rising cost line in AI infrastructure right now isn't the GPU. It's the memory sitting next to it.

Memory is one scarce layer this week's rule named. Packaging is the other, and Intel just got its first outside validation there. Microsoft has become the first publicly announced customer to adopt Intel Foundry's Embedded Multi-die Interconnect Bridge, or EMIB, its advanced-packaging technology. At Intel's TomorrowX summit, chief executive Lip-Bu Tan also said eighteen-A yields have reportedly climbed to seventy percent.

Track Intel's customer ladder this week. On Monday, this desk said Intel Foundry had no confirmed external leading-edge customer. On Tuesday, we corrected that with Tesla on fourteen-A. Yesterday came an unnamed signing on eighteen-A-P. Today Microsoft becomes the first public customer for Intel's packaging, not its wafers.

Now discount it. All of this comes from conference remarks relayed by trade press. There's no Intel or Microsoft press release or filing behind it. No volume, product, or start date has been disclosed for the EMIB deal. The seventy-percent yield has no stated definition, so it can't be compared cleanly with any other foundry's numbers. It also sits below the single analyst estimate this desk cited on Monday, and neither figure states its method.

So score it on the right dimension. This is packaging capability and a customer relationship, not production leadership. On our read, one Microsoft program doesn't change TSMC's 2027 CoWoS allocation math. But packaging outside TSMC now gains value on two fronts at once. Commercially, it's a second source. Politically, advanced packaging is now written into export rules. The so-what: Intel now has a named outside customer in packaging. But it's a toehold, not proof that its logic foundry has turned the corner.

So here's the scorecard, dimension by dimension. On manufacturing access, TSMC and Samsung lose some China volume and take on compliance friction, both unsized. On pricing power, memory makers gain real leverage into 2027, but now carry a live price-fixing suit. On packaging and customer relationships, Intel gains a named customer, provisionally, pending any confirming filing. Chinese fabless designers that relied on self-certification lose leading-edge access unless they win licenses. And on supply-chain resilience, China's own manufacturing capability, not its designers' ambition, becomes the binding constraint on its AI silicon.

What to watch next. First, whether TSMC or Samsung confirm the halts or put a number on the exposure. Second, how quickly licenses for consumer chips actually come through. Third, whether the control perimeter reaches semiconductor equipment, which it still hasn't. Fourth, how 2027 HBM4 contracts settle against TrendForce's range. And fifth, whether Intel or Microsoft puts a volume, product, or date on the EMIB deal in a filing.

That's the briefing for November 20th. Have a good weekend.
