Good morning. It's Saturday, November 28th. This week the prices in private credit are coming from sellers, not buyers, and that thread runs through everything below.

Start with the lowest price this desk has seen all week, and it's on AI credit. HSBC reportedly sold its roughly $300 million slice of the construction loan behind Oracle's Project Jupiter data center. It got about $255 million, or roughly 85 cents on the dollar. HSBC hasn't confirmed the figures. The sale also closed several weeks before it was reported, so treat this as a recent price, not this week's price.

The sequence is what matters here. The sale followed an Oracle force-majeure notice to the developer, a Blue Owl Capital unit. That notice would let Oracle defer payments if the project is delayed. Be precise about what that means. Nothing reported says the project has slipped, or that any payment has been deferred. What the notice changes is the credit question. It's no longer "is Oracle good for it." It's "is Oracle obligated to pay on time."

Lease-backed AI debt is only as good as the tenant's unconditional obligation to pay. This desk made that point on Monday about the SpaceX GPU notes. Project Jupiter is the case where that obligation isn't unconditional. The tenant has an escape hatch, and the loan clears in the mid-80s.

Put that on this week's price ladder. Goldman's non-traded BDC is reportedly selling a private credit loan book at 94.75, a deal this desk covered yesterday. BC Partners' BDC has agreed to buy Investcorp's listed BDC at roughly 90 cents on its net asset value. These aren't like-for-like. The Jupiter 85 is a loan price against face value, while the Investcorp 90 is a takeout price against a fund's own marks. But they point the same way, with sellers taking less than face or book to get out. And the steepest discount landed on AI construction debt, the paper most often sold as quasi-investment-grade.

Don't over-read that discount, though. The reporting supports sequence, not cause. Other things could explain part of the fifteen-point haircut. This is construction-stage paper, which trades thinly. The seller wanted out. And HSBC held one slice of an $18 billion syndicate, so one trade is not a mark on the whole loan. An investment-grade tenant doesn't make a construction loan investment-grade if the tenant can defer. The desk's inference is that construction-stage tranches could start to price wider than stabilized assets wherever a tenant holds that kind of option.

And the banks aren't only selling outright. Per Semafor's reporting on November 26th, Citi and JPMorgan are marketing parts of their AI loan books. Some of those deals reportedly use synthetic risk transfers. The loans and the borrower relationships stay with the banks, but outside investors agree to absorb some of the potential losses. Neither bank has confirmed the report, and no sizes have been disclosed, so treat it as directional.

There are two ways to read that. The benign reading is ordinary year-end capital management, trimming risk-weighted assets. The less benign reading sits next to the HSBC sale. On that view, banks are quietly moving the loss risk on AI credit off their own books, into vehicles the public can't see into. Either way, the risk doesn't disappear. It migrates, and the link between banks and non-banks gets more opaque, not less.

That migration also blunts the test on marks. An outright sale like HSBC's at least prints a price. A risk transfer prints nothing public, so the loss gets carried at whatever value its new holders assign.

There's a knock-on for the next wave of projects, and this is the desk's inference. If banks are shedding AI construction exposure, the marginal lender to new data centers shifts toward non-bank balance sheets. Those may be the same balance sheets absorbing this discounted paper.

That leads to the open question over the whole AI financing stack. Nobody has reported who bought HSBC's slice. If another bank bought it, the risk stayed inside the regulated system. If a private fund or insurer bought it, the risk moved somewhere its mark is much harder to see. And if any private vehicle carries Project Jupiter paper anywhere near par, its mark already disagrees with an 85-cent trade. That's not yet a finding. It's the test that fourth-quarter marks have to answer.

Now to the listed BDCs, where seller-driven price discovery has been running for months. On Thursday this desk called BC Partners' purchase of Investcorp's BDC a data point, not yet a wave. That deal still needs Investcorp's shareholders to approve it. And the filings show the pattern was already under way before it. In early August, BlackRock's TCP Capital agreed to sell a $523 million loan portfolio, spread across 78 companies, to a continuation vehicle sponsored by Pantheon.

TCP's own filing puts the price at 95 percent of the portfolio's gross fair value as of last December. That's not a fire sale, though the benchmark is an old valuation, on loans a seller typically gets to choose. The stress signal is elsewhere. The same release disclosed that TCP's board had hired KBW to run a strategic review. WhiteHorse Finance followed on September 15th, forming a special committee to explore alternatives that could include a sale.

None of this is new this week. The TCP and WhiteHorse processes are two to three months old. The desk's read is that subscale BDCs are running out of road. So the wave this desk said to watch for was already moving. Every deal adds another reference price for year-end marks.

Small books are being sold, and the biggest one is conceding on income. Blackstone's largest listed BDC, BXSL, is resetting its quarterly dividend from 77 cents to 60 cents. That's about a 22 percent cut, starting with the first-quarter 2027 payment. It was disclosed with third-quarter results weeks ago, and this desk is covering it for the first time.

The cut follows the earnings. According to reporting on BXSL's third-quarter call, net investment income fell to 68 cents a share, from 75 cents the prior quarter. That covered only about 88 percent of the old payout.

Don't read this as a default signal. Net asset value slipped only about 1 percent, to $25.25, which points away from a pure credit story. The reporting also doesn't separate the drivers. It could be lower base rates on floating-rate assets, higher funding costs, tighter spreads on new loans, or credit losses. Read it as an income signal instead.

By this desk's own arithmetic, 60 cents a quarter works out to roughly 9.5 percent annualized on that NAV. That's still a rich headline yield, but well below what the old payout advertised. The coverage numbers had already shown the payout outrunning income. The news is that the largest, highest-pedigree vehicle in the space just conceded it publicly. That undercuts the wealth-channel pitch that private credit delivers stable, bond-like income.

One more inference from the desk. A reset at the flagship gives cover for resets across the sector. Lower headline yields could then widen the NAV discounts that make listed BDCs takeover targets. That loops straight back to the consolidation story.

Briefly, the way these numbers get managed is now showing up in court. A Mayer Brown litigation update earlier this month describes an amended complaint against a BDC's advisers. It alleges that restructurings removed loans from non-accrual status. The company then cited that as evidence of improving credit quality. In the plaintiffs' telling, it was a scheme to conceal deterioration and protect advisory fees. A separate derivative suit accuses Blue Owl Credit Advisors, acting as valuation designee, of systematically overstating portfolio values to inflate fees.

And Jay Clayton has weighed in. The former SEC chair, now U.S. Attorney for the Southern District of New York, has warned that "sketchy marks" have drawn prosecutorial attention. These are allegations, not rulings, and defendants dispute them. But they turn a running analyst suspicion into a legal theory. An improving non-accrual number can mean a loan was restructured, not cured. The so-what is that the reported credit metrics are now themselves contested evidence.

Pull it together. The credit cycle is turning in transaction prices and income statements before it turns in default rates. The biggest unresolved question is where the AI construction exposure that banks are shedding ends up, and at what mark it gets carried. Watch four things. First, fourth-quarter BDC marks, read against this week's price ladder: 85 on Jupiter, 90 on Investcorp, and 94.75 on the Goldman sale. Second, whether Project Jupiter actually slips its schedule. Third, whether more listed BDC boards follow TCP and WhiteHorse into strategic reviews or sales. Fourth, the Investcorp shareholder vote on the BC Partners deal.

That's the lending machine for November 28th. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
