Good morning, it's September 6th, and this is your robotics and physical AI briefing.

For the past week this show has argued that the only trustworthy numbers in physical AI are the ones an outside authority grants, not the ones a company publishes about itself. Driver-out permission for a robotaxi, we said on Wednesday, is a signal a regulator hands out rather than a claim a vendor makes. This week that test broke, and it broke in Austin.

Here's the setup. On September 4th, Tesla opened Cybercab rides to the paying public in Austin. It moved the launch up three hours to two p.m. Central because demand was running ahead of plan. The vehicle itself is the point. There is no steering wheel and there are no pedals. This is the first time a major automaker has put a car with no manual driving controls at all into paid public service. Within a day, the Robotaxi app was number one on the App Store's travel chart. That is the launch, and the launch is not the story.

The story is what Tesla told the federal government, and what the federal government did about it. Tesla told NHTSA it had self-certified the Cybercab as compliant with every Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard. Those standards were written assuming a driver's seat and manual controls. NHTSA opened an investigation the same day. In the agency's own words, the probe will, quote, examine the process and technical data on which Tesla relied when certifying the Cybercab and related issues. NHTSA put the principle plainly: it needs to ensure that all of its laws are followed.

Now discipline that against itself, because both the alarmed and the dismissive readings are too easy. Self-certification is the default pathway for getting a vehicle onto American roads. It is not, by itself, a workaround. And NHTSA has opened an audit, not a stop-sale. There is no interim order and no reported pause to the rollout, so nobody should assume the service stops. Tesla, for its part, has not put any response to the investigation on the record — the only Tesla position available is its original claim that the vehicles are compliant. One more thing nobody has confirmed: whether these Austin rides carry any personnel in the vehicle at all. Fully driverless is a design claim about the hardware here, not a verified operating condition. But the hard question underneath is real. Can a vehicle built with no manual controls be self-certified against standards that presume a driver's seat, or does removing the human interface entirely require a different approval path? That is what is actually being tested, and it means the binding constraint on this vehicle is a certification pathway, not software.

The scale of it is sharper than it first looked, and smaller. Texas motor vehicle records show Tesla had 420 total autonomous vehicles registered in the state as of the Friday before launch. Only 45 of those are purpose-built Cybercabs. The figure of around a thousand that has been circulating is not the deployed fleet at all. It describes the scale of vehicles Tesla's self-certification submission covers, which is what NHTSA says it is reviewing. So the audit is mostly about an expansion Tesla has not executed yet, not about the forty-five cars already carrying passengers.

There is a precedent sitting right next to this one, and it makes Tesla's choice look deliberate. Zoox built its own steering-wheel-free robotaxi. Instead of self-certifying, it went through NHTSA's formal exemption process before deployment rather than after it. What it got in return was a two-year exemption window running from July 31st, 2026 through July 31st, 2028, with permission to deploy up to 2,500 vehicles a year. Tesla never filed an exemption petition. It self-certified Level 4 autonomy under Texas's own state rules and went straight into commercial service. That is the trade. Zoox accepted upfront federal review and a hard annual ceiling. Tesla skipped both, has no volume cap — and is now the one being audited after the fact, with no exemption already in hand if the finding goes against it.

Why does any of this matter commercially? Because deleting the wheel and pedals is the entire cost argument for the Cybercab. A two-seat vehicle with no manual interface is supposed to be a fundamentally cheaper machine to build and to operate than a car retrofitted for autonomy. If NHTSA's audit concludes those standards cannot be met without manual controls, and Tesla has to put them back in, the Cybercab stops being a different cost structure. It becomes an expensive sensor stack bolted to a small car. The remedy in that scenario is not a fine, it is a design change — and the risk sits on the vehicle architecture, not on the software. That is the premise being audited, not a compliance footnote.

So if safety can be self-graded, what about capability? That is the same question one layer down, and Google DeepMind gave an unusually honest answer with Gemini Robotics 2. Flag the date up front: it was released on July 30th, five weeks ago, so treat it as evidence rather than as news. What makes it evidence is the disclosure norm. DeepMind published task-level success rates across five different robot platforms instead of one polished demo, which is exactly what this beat has spent the week asking model labs to do.

Here are the numbers that matter. Running on Apptronik's Apollo 2 humanoid, the model picked an object off a table successfully 68.4 percent of the time. Off a shelf, 76.3 percent. Its best result in the set was 92 percent, on unscrewing a bulb.

Sit with that first number. A robot that fails one pick in three cannot run an unattended shift. And notice which task scored highest — the most constrained and most repetitive one — which is exactly the signature of task-specific engineering still doing the work underneath a general-purpose label. Two caveats before anyone trades on this. These are DeepMind's own figures, with no independent replication, and they were pulled from a company blog post rather than a benchmark anyone else ran. So do not stack them against another vendor's percentage as if the two measured the same thing. But they are the most honest capability disclosure the model layer has produced this year, and the honesty is what makes them damaging. The humanoid capex cycle is being financed against a general-purpose model that picks an object off a table roughly two times in three.

Close the loop with the one clean counterexample. Waymo now serves more than 500,000 paid rides a week. That is roughly double the 250,000 it was running less than a year ago, across more than 4,000 vehicles in fourteen U.S. cities. Zoox, by contrast, is still mapping Houston and San Diego with human drivers aboard, and its only paid ride-hailing service remains Las Vegas. The asymmetry that matters is not speed, it is permission. Every city on Waymo's list is one where a regulator, not Waymo, decided the car could run driverless. And every one of those weekly rides has a paying customer behind it. Tesla's no-controls design rests on a certification Tesla wrote itself. Be careful with the comparison, though. Waymo's ride count is Waymo's own statement, not an audited disclosure, and neither operator publishes a cost per mile or an intervention rate. So this is not a safety ranking. It is a question of who is allowed to operate, and on whose word.

What would settle this week's argument is specific. NHTSA's finding on whether the Cybercab's certification holds. Or a real intervention rate measured against the miles Tesla's Austin fleet has actually driven. Or a robotics lab publishing task success numbers from a paid deployment instead of a lab bench. Until one of those lands, the outside check this beat has been demanding turns out to be available to whoever gets to write the certification.
