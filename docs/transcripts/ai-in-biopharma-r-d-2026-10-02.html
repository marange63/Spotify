AI in Biopharma R&D

Transcript · October 2, 2026

Good morning. It's October 2nd, 2026, and this is Cautious Optimism Briefings.

Yesterday we said Roche's eighty percent Phase 3 success rate was a marker, not a verdict. Roche's own chief medical officer credited governance, not AI, for that number. Today we go down a level, from portfolio claims to individual molecules. The question is simple: what does the clinical record for AI-originated drugs actually show right now?

Start with the most advanced test case. Rentosertib is Insilico Medicine's TNIK inhibitor for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, discovered and designed on Insilico's generative AI platform. Its Phase 2a results were published in Nature Medicine. On the 60 milligram once-daily dose, patients gained a mean 98.4 milliliters of forced vital capacity. Placebo patients lost a mean 20.3 milliliters. In a disease that normally only gets worse, an improvement rather than a slower decline is a real signal.

Now the fine print, because it shapes everything that follows. That trial enrolled 71 patients across 22 sites, all in China, and treated them for just 12 weeks. The 60 milligram group was one of several dose arms. Insilico's own release describes the lung-function result as a secondary efficacy endpoint with promising outcomes. We could not find a published statement that it was statistically significant. So bank it as a promising, exploratory signal, not as proof of disease modification.

Here's what changed. The registry entry for rentosertib's Phase 3 had listed it as not yet recruiting, with an estimated start of August 30th. That date has passed. The current ClinicalTrials.gov record now says recruiting, with an actual start date of September 9th. That's about ten days behind the trial's own estimate. That is ordinary start-up noise, not a quiet slip. Insilico's Hong Kong entity is the sponsor, and planned enrollment is 320 patients.

The design is where this gets interesting. The Phase 3 is randomized, double-blind and placebo-controlled. Its primary endpoint is the annual rate of decline in forced vital capacity, measured across a full year. The Phase 2a, by contrast, read a change at 12 weeks. So the pivotal trial asks a harder question over a much longer window. Does an early lift in lung function turn into slower decline across a year? That makes it the first pivotal-scale test of whether an AI-selected target and an AI-designed molecule can beat placebo, rather than just reach the clinic faster.

Now widen the lens, because rentosertib does not sit alone. One industry tracker counts more than 173 AI-designed drug programs in clinical development as of early 2026. That compares with roughly two dozen in late 2023, a roughly sevenfold rise in a little over two years. Trackers define "AI-designed" loosely, so treat the count as directional, not precise.

Here's the line that should temper that growth. It appears in Nature Medicine, in the same paper that reported rentosertib's Phase 2a. AI-discovered drugs, it notes, have seen similar levels of Phase 2 failure as conventionally discovered drugs. And as of that paper, none had progressed through Phase 3. BenevolentAI's atopic dermatitis drug, which could not beat placebo in Phase 2a, remains the familiar cautionary example.

Put those two facts side by side. AI is reliably getting molecules into patients. It has not yet been shown to get better molecules into patients. On today's evidence, the demonstrated value is throughput into the clinic, not improved odds once there.

The next case sharpens that distinction from the other direction. Rentosertib pairs an AI-selected target with an AI-designed molecule. Recursion Pharmaceuticals' REC-4881 goes after MEK1/2, a well-validated mechanism. On September 28th, we said no efficacy figure for REC-4881 was solid enough to repeat on air. That has changed, though not because of new data. The figures come from Recursion's earlier TUPELO update, filed with the SEC. Recursion put the program back in front of investors at the Morgan Stanley healthcare conference last month.

The trial is in familial adenomatous polyposis. At week 25, median polyp burden was down 53 percent from baseline. Nine of the 11 evaluable patients kept a durable reduction. The comparison is a natural-history cohort, where 87 percent of untreated patients saw polyp burden rise on an annualized basis. That contrast is striking. But eleven evaluable patients is not a powered trial, and a historical cohort is not a concurrent control arm.

There's a deeper caveat for the AI thesis. Because MEK is validated biology, a win for REC-4881 would say more about Recursion's execution and choice of indication than about AI finding something new. It is real clinical evidence from an AI-platform company. It is not evidence of AI-discovered biology.

The more telling Recursion number isn't clinical at all. According to coverage of that Morgan Stanley appearance, Recursion has cut its cost base by about 40 percent on a pro forma basis since integrating Exscientia. The company credits AI agents, automation, and capital prioritization. That is the cleanest quoted evidence in today's record that AI is moving a number now. It's just not the number everyone wants moved. It lowers the cost per shot on goal. It does not raise the odds of scoring. And capital prioritization after a merger is ordinary belt-tightening, so the AI share of that 40 percent is unknown.

So if the odds are ever going to move, the mechanism has to sit upstream, at target selection, before a molecule exists. That brings us to a refinement of yesterday's coverage. We described the lung-cancer strategy from Stanford's Virtual Biotech paper as a proposal, not a trial result. That's still true, but there's more to it. The multi-agent system proposed an antibody-drug conjugate targeting B7-H3. According to Stanford's release, that design matched the strategy behind a real therapy that independently received FDA breakthrough status months later.

That makes it a pseudo-prospective check, the strongest form of retrospective validation. But it is one matched case, not a track record. And the release passage we have doesn't pin down the data cutoff. Matching a strategy industry already pursued shows the agents can reconstruct consensus-quality reasoning. It does not show they can find what a well-resourced team would have missed.

Pull the threads together and a pattern from recent weeks holds again. Every success-rate claim this show has traced back to its source has resolved to speed, to governance, or to cost. None has resolved to odds attributable to AI. What follows is our inference, not reported fact.

First, valuation. If Phase 2 parity holds, AI drug-discovery platforms should be valued on speed to clinic and cost per program. They should not carry a success-rate premium. That is a materially different equity story from the one most of these names tell.

Second, concentration risk. Rentosertib is now close to a single point of failure for the asset class's narrative. A clean Phase 3 would be the first hard evidence that AI-originated selection survives a pivotal trial. A miss would weigh on sentiment across AI-native platforms, well beyond Insilico.

Third, where the pressure lands. If agent-driven savings like Recursion's spread, the first squeeze falls on headcount-billed work. That means discovery and documentation services at contract research organizations. Total R&D budgets get reallocated, not grown.

Three open questions to carry forward. Does rentosertib's Phase 3 hold its enrollment pace now that it is underway? What does Recursion's November TUPELO update show against the figures we can now quote? And will anyone publish a genuinely prospective cohort, rather than another matched retrospective case?

On success rates, our read is parity today. Any real lift will most likely come from upstream target selection, and it will show up slowly, trial by trial, not in a headline. On spend, the near-term AI dividend is a lower cost per program inside budgets that stay roughly flat.
