Good morning. This is Capital Markets Radar for Saturday, October 3rd, 2026.

All week this desk has asked one question. Can the long end fall on calming news? Yesterday we named Friday's jobs report as the decisive test. The print is in, and on Treasury's own closing data, the long end did not fall. It rose.

Start with the print. The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that September nonfarm payrolls rose 29,000, with unemployment at 4.2 percent. In the agency's own words, both "changed little." There is no verified consensus to grade it against, so we are not calling it a miss.

The detail underneath is softer than the headline. The Bureau revised July and August down by a combined 60,000 jobs. July now shows an outright loss of 10,000. Wages barely moved either. Average hourly earnings rose just 0.1 percent in September. So the labor market is not re-accelerating, and it is not generating wage pressure. One month is noisy. But this is not a labor market pushing inflation higher.

So what did the bond market do with it? Treasury's daily par yield curve gives us the closes. From Thursday to Friday, the ten-year rose five basis points, to 5.17 percent. The thirty-year rose three basis points, to 5.67 percent. Even the two-year rose, by two basis points. Those levels come from Treasury's own closing series, which can sit below the screen quotes you may have heard, so focus on the day-over-day change.

The thirty-year deserves one more line. That 5.67 percent close puts the long bond at or near its highest level in roughly 24 years. It continues a week-long pattern of the long bond grinding higher, this time on soft data. So the desk scores the test as failed. The caveat is that this is one session, and one close can be noise. But the long bond had its clean chance on soft data, and it closed higher.

Here is why that matters more than one data point. Our working explanation for the long end has had three legs: resilient growth, term premium, and Treasury and corporate supply. A soft payroll print with downward revisions weakens the growth leg directly. If growth were driving the climb, this report should have pulled the thirty-year down. It did not. That leaves the structural legs standing, term premium and supply. This is the desk's inference, not an established market consensus. But when the cyclical story weakens and the long bond still rises, the price is being set by something other than the cycle.

There is a competing read, and it is the main risk to that thesis. Without a verified consensus, we cannot rule out that the market had braced for something weaker. On that view, 29,000 was a relief rather than a disappointment, and Friday was a positioning move, not a structural signal. The front end makes that read harder to dismiss, because the two-year rose too. We think the revisions and the flat wage line make the soft reading the better one. But if the next soft input also fails to move the long end, the competing read loses its footing.

Supply is the obvious candidate among the survivors. Long-dated paper has to clear somewhere, and the buyers who set the term premium are not the buyers who set the policy path. The AI buildout is one plausible claimant on that supply, because its financing runs into long-dated markets. We are not putting a figure on that today. We are saying it fits the explanation that survives once growth is removed. That matters for the outlook, because supply does not reverse on a single data print.

Now equities. We do not have a verified equity close for Friday, so the point here is structural. If stocks trade the soft labor data as policy relief, they are leaning on the front end. And on Friday the front end did not deliver that relief.

That makes the longest-duration growth names the exposed part of the market. Their valuations depend most on the discount rate. The "bad news is good news" trade only works if the long end follows the front end down. On Friday, neither end moved down. A rally built on the policy channel, with no help from policy-sensitive yields, is a rally that needs another explanation. So the equity risk is not any one day's price. It is that a soft-data rally would rest on a rate path the bond market has not confirmed.

Which puts the Federal Reserve in an awkward spot. The next decision is the October 28 meeting. A soft labor report argues against the hike path the market has been weighing. Yet the two-year, where that decision is priced, rose on the day. We have no dated futures probability taken after the release, so the tension stays qualitative. That is an uncomfortable combination for a central bank: a softening labor market, and a curve that is not easing financial conditions for it.

That tension has a direct consequence for duration as a hedge. Long Treasuries are supposed to rally when growth disappoints. That is the whole case for holding them against equity risk. Friday was as close to a growth-disappointment test as one session gets, and the long bond sold off. This desk has already flagged long duration struggling as a hedge. Friday extends that failure to a growth scare. For a 60/40 portfolio, the bond leg is not doing the diversifying job a growth-scare model assumes. For risk-parity books, which lever duration to balance risk, the gap is larger. They are levered into the exact exposure that did not rally. If that persists, the mechanical response is to cut leverage. Cutting leverage means selling duration, which adds supply to the same long end. That is how a hedge failure can feed the move that caused it.

There is a second-order point on collateral. Long Treasuries are what dealers, repo desks, and levered funds post and borrow against. When the long end rises on a soft print, that collateral loses value just when funding needs may be rising. Lower collateral values mean the same posted securities support less borrowing. Levered holders then face a choice between adding margin and trimming positions. Trimming means selling more long paper into the same weak long end. We cannot size this without verified repo data, but it is the channel to watch if the long end keeps climbing.

A word on what is missing, because absence is not a signal. We do not have verified recent data for oil, gold, copper, the dollar, credit spreads, or crypto and ETF flows. We have seen figures, but they are undated, secondary, or inconsistent, so we are not reporting them. Treat their absence here as missing evidence, not as calm.

So what settles the test? The observable is simple. If the ten-year and thirty-year close back below Thursday's Treasury levels of 5.12 and 5.64 percent, the soft print finally did its work, and the term-premium read is overdone. If they hold above those levels into September CPI on October 14th and the October 28 meeting, the structural explanation is the one to own. Either way, the answer comes from closes, not intraday swings.

Carry three things into next week. The long end rose on the labor test. The growth explanation is weaker than it was on Thursday, which leaves term premium and supply. And long duration failed as a growth hedge on the day it was tested.

That's Capital Markets Radar. Have a good weekend.
