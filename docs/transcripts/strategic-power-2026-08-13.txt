Good morning. It's Thursday, August 13th, 2026, and this week gave the gap between announced power and delivered power an actual number — and then, on closer inspection, a second number that doesn't quite agree with the first.

Bloomberg reported yesterday, citing projections from the research firm Wood Mackenzie, that grid operators and utilities are likely to commit to only about 28 percent of the 1,066 gigawatts of power capacity that's been requested nationwide for data center projects. The rest, in Wood Mackenzie's framing, is "phantom" — speculative interconnection filings that will never get built. That is a striking number, and it's the sharpest version yet of a story this show has been tracking for days: how much of the AI power boom is real capacity, and how much is a queue full of hedged bets.

But here's the catch. Wood Mackenzie has also published a separate figure set on the same topic, describing 220 gigawatts of additional US data-center demand in the pipeline. That's not a rounding difference from 1,066 gigawatts — it's a different order of magnitude entirely. The two studies may be measuring different things: one could be the entire nationwide interconnection queue, full of duplicate and speculative filings, and the other a narrower, vetted pipeline. But the underlying Wood Mackenzie methodology behind the 1,066-gigawatt, 28-percent figure sits behind a paywall, and it could not be reconciled against the firm's own other published number. So take the headline directionally — a meaningful share of announced data-center power will not get built — and treat the precise 28 percent ratio with real skepticism. Even the analyst firm supplying the number can't be shown to agree with itself.

What's not in dispute is the regulatory response, because it's on the record. Back in June, the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission issued show-cause orders to all six major U.S. grid operators — PJM, MISO, SPP, CAISO, ISO New England and NYISO — demanding they either justify their existing rules for connecting massive new loads or file reforms. Those responses are due Monday, August 17th — four days out, not this morning. And FERC's definition of what counts is specific: a large load is a single site pulling at least 50 megawatts of peak demand, connected at 69 kilovolts or above. The reform's teeth are in the eligibility bar attached to it — developers now need full site control, a construction-schedule commitment from their state siting authority, a $500,000 study deposit, and a $15,000-per-megawatt readiness deposit just to hold a place in the queue. That's a real cost on speculative filing, for the first time.

Except the six grid operators are not moving together. PJM looks set to file substantive reforms on schedule. MISO, by contrast, has signaled it will ask for a delay rather than file complete revisions — using an escape hatch built into the process, since the operators had until August 3rd to request that their proceedings be held in abeyance for up to 90 days. And the other four — SPP, NYISO, CAISO, and ISO New England — have each indicated their own filings won't land until sometime between November of this year and March of next. So Monday's deadline is really a test case for PJM, not a national inflection point. The reform is real, but it's arriving in pieces, on wildly different clocks.

So what does this mean if it plays out? If a large share of requested load really doesn't materialize, some of the utility capex and equipment orders — turbines, transformers — that were sized to headline demand risk running ahead of what actually gets built, which could leave pockets of the country with a capacity glut rather than a shortage a few years out. And it strengthens the case for behind-the-meter power — gas turbines, on-site generation — as a hedge against queue uncertainty, especially with Henry Hub gas still sitting under three dollars. That's a tailwind for gas-turbine makers and on-site generation names, and it argues for data-center siting to keep drifting toward whichever grid operators actually clear their queues fastest.

Now, the same announced-versus-delivered test shows up one input over, in critical minerals. The Pentagon and the Export-Import Bank have been running a sequential, chokepoint-by-chokepoint financing campaign for weeks now — an August 7th package alone included $1.4 billion for Sila Nanotechnologies' battery-anode manufacturing, $150 million for Niron Magnetics' rare-earth-free magnets, and $400 million tied to Sunrise Energy Metals' scandium project in Australia. That last one is worth isolating. Confirmation of the $400 million Defense Department financing sent Sunrise's stock up as much as 29 percent intraday this week, closing up 16 percent. The reason it matters: China controls close to 70 percent of global rare-earth mining and about 90 percent of processing, and for scandium specifically, there is almost no primary mine supply anywhere in the world today. This would be the first.

But — same discipline as the power story — read the structure of the deal, not just the headline. This is a 25-year debt facility, released in stages as Sunrise puts up matching equity, not a lump-sum check. Construction is targeted to start in the second half of this year, with commercial production not expected until the second half of 2028. That's two years before any scandium actually ships. The market moved on the financing; the metal is still years away. So-what here: this is a real template — the Pentagon acting as anchor lender against a single-source-China material — and it's one that can plausibly be repeated for gallium, germanium, and antimony. But announced financing is not refined metal, and the timeline matters as much as the dollar figure.

One more domain running the identical test: Taiwan. Japan's Defense White Paper, released August 4th, named China as Japan's "greatest strategic challenge" and warned Beijing could impose a blockade using its coast guard rather than its navy. That's the assessed, structural read — the balance tilting the wrong way. But the near-term activity data point in the opposite direction: Chinese air force incursions into Taiwan's air defense identification zone totaled 190 in July, less than half the peak levels from the same month in each of the prior two years. Taiwan, meanwhile, isn't treating that lull as reassurance — it just ran its largest-ever reservist mobilization, rehearsing wartime supply-chain dispersal and deliberately throttling civilian mobile bandwidth to simulate wartime conditions, while its 2025 recruitment came in at 117.5 percent of target with retention up to 87.2 percent.

The tension is genuine, not decorative. A falling incursion count could mean real de-escalation, plausibly tied to distractions elsewhere and a warmer Trump-Xi channel — though that read is analyst interpretation, not confirmed Beijing policy. Or it could just mean coercion has shifted toward less visible tools like coast guard pressure that don't show up in an ADIZ count. Markets pricing Taiwan risk into semiconductors and shipping should treat the near-term data as reason to ease, not eliminate, the tail-risk premium — while the structural case for chip supply-chain dispersal into Arizona, Japan, and Germany keeps its logic intact regardless.

Pull the thread across all three, and it's the same lesson: an interconnection queue, a mining announcement, and a defense white paper are all, in their own way, a claim about future capacity. None of them are capacity itself. The gap between the claim and the delivery is where the real money — and the real risk — sits right now.
