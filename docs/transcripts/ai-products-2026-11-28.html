Good morning. This is Cautious Optimism Briefings, your AI products update for Saturday, November 28th, 2026.

One disclosure up front. Anthropic makes the model behind this show, and one Anthropic product appears later in this briefing.

After a week dominated by who pays for agent work, today's two launches are both about trust. One is about what a vendor can see in your data. The other is about what a vendor can tell your agent to do. Neither is proven. One is an alpha with no customers named. The other is a beta that enterprise plans can't use yet. Treat both as claims on paper, not finished products.

Start with the lead. On Wednesday, November 25th, Sam Altman announced on X that OpenAI is testing something called Private Inference. Here's his language. "We're now testing Private Inference, our latest offering as part of OpenAI Private Intelligence, with a small group of customers. Stronger privacy protections will make frontier AI useful for a lot more work." Calling it the latest offering implies a product line. Treat that as a statement of intent, not a shipped portfolio. And that post is essentially the whole primary disclosure. There are no named customers, no customer count, no pricing, and no general-availability date.

The distinction worth getting precise is with Zero Data Retention, which OpenAI already offers. Per reporting from the tech site Unite.AI, Private Inference uses confidential computing and additional controls. Unite.AI puts the difference this way. "ZDR prevents OpenAI from retaining prompts and responses after processing, while Private Inference focuses on protecting that information while processing happens." So Zero Data Retention is a promise about what happens after the model runs. Private Inference is a claim about what happens during the run, while your data is inside the system.

Why does that matter? Regulated buyers in legal, healthcare, and defense-adjacent work share a specific fear. They worry the vendor itself can see sensitive data mid-inference. That fear is a central reason many have stayed on self-hosted or open-weight models. Consider Anthropic's Claude Ship, a set of agents that review, fix, and approve code changes. On Wednesday, this show reported that Claude Ship is unavailable to organizations running Zero Data Retention, the HIPAA configuration, or customer-managed keys. So the regulated buyer who most wants codified controls is locked out of that product. Private Inference is a bet on winning exactly that buyer back to a hosted frontier model.

Now the skepticism, and here our follow-up research settled something. A confidential-computing guarantee is only as strong as the customer's ability to verify it. That usually means remote attestation or an independent audit. We looked for that mechanism in OpenAI's own materials and could not find one. The most detailed technical analysis we found comes from Privatemode, which sells confidential computing itself, so read it as a competitor with a view. It calls the verification question "the single most important open question in the announcement." It also notes, quote, "No regions, no data-residency statement for the inference itself, no pricing."

The closest thing OpenAI has published covers a different system, called Private Safety Processing. It's an abuse-detection layer tied to Zero Data Retention, described in an OpenAI post from August. That post says, "OpenAI personnel do not receive access to the customer content even when it is flagged." The same post promised a technical white paper in September. Our follow-up research found no newer OpenAI update. So as far as we can find, OpenAI's most detailed privacy claim in this area still lacks its promised technical backing.

OpenAI also isn't first here, and its rivals show what's missing. Google launched Private AI Compute last year, using remote attestation to connect a user's device to a sealed cloud. Microsoft Azure's Confidential AI Inferencing is closer to an enterprise offering, though it's infrastructure you deploy on, not a hosted frontier model. Azure publishes what it calls "a publicly verifiable attestation policy." Both rivals describe how their guarantee gets checked, and for Private Inference, OpenAI hasn't yet.

There's a competing reading of the whole announcement, too. It may be sales positioning for regulated enterprise deals, announced before it's really a product. The plain takeaway: a privacy claim is only as good as your ability to check it. Today, this one can't be checked.

Suppose OpenAI does publish verifiable attestation. Two things follow, and both are our inference. First, it would weaken the privacy case for regulated industries to self-host open-weight models. That cuts against Palo Alto Networks. Palo Alto has agreed to buy a company called Applied Compute. Applied Compute's platform lets enterprises adapt open-weight models to their own data and run them in production. That deal is a bet that enterprises keep running their own models. Second, it would pressure Anthropic, whose Claude Ship still excludes the most privacy-sensitive customers. Anthropic would face pressure to offer those customers a comparable during-processing guarantee.

Protecting your data from the vendor is half of this week's trust story. The other half is protecting your agent from what a vendor's server tells it to do. That's where Anthropic's new work sits. A reminder that Anthropic makes the model behind this show.

On Tuesday, November 24th, Anthropic opened a public beta called Skills over MCP. An MCP server can now hand an agent reusable workflow instructions, not just tools to call. Per VentureBeat, Claude discovers a skill when the server connects, but loads its full instructions only when needed. The named launch partners are Shopify for store design, HubSpot for customer onboarding, and Atlassian for analyzing work.

The part that matters most is the security model, and the spec is blunt about the risk. Here's VentureBeat's account, quote. "The extension specification identifies server-supplied skills as a prompt injection risk. It requires explicit user approval for each skill before it can cause local code execution. If a previously approved skill's files change, the client must ask for approval again." End quote.

That changes what an enterprise agrees to when it connects a third-party server. It used to mean approving which tools that server exposed. Now it also means approving instructions that shape the agent's behavior. The spec treats that as its own security primitive, with a mandatory checkpoint. Compare MCP's earlier Elicitation feature, which let a server pause to ask a human but didn't require it. Here, the client must ask again whenever a skill's files change. The human gate is moving from approving an action to approving a version of the instructions.

Two caveats are worth holding onto. First, read the wording closely. The mandatory approval attaches to a skill that "can cause local code execution." A skill that only steers which remote tools get called may not trip that gate. Neither might a skill that shapes how data gets handled. Those are exactly the cases where prompt injection does its damage. That's our reading of the text, not a gap Anthropic has confirmed. Second, frequent re-approval prompts invite fatigue. Users click through. A checkpoint that fires too often stops working as a checkpoint.

Availability is thin as well. The beta is on by default for Pro, Max, and Ultra plans. Team plans need an administrator to switch it on, another case of the admin deciding what an agent may do. Enterprise support is listed only as coming soon. So the customers with the most mature security review can't use it yet. And there's no count of how many servers actually ship skills.

Here's the practical implication, and it's our inference. Skills update on the server side, outside any app-store review. So expect Shopify, HubSpot, and Atlassian to publish skill changelogs, the way they publish API changelogs today. Expect enterprise security review of these integrations to become continuous, not a one-time approval. For a security team, the thing under review is no longer a fixed integration. It's a document that the vendor can rewrite at any time. The so-what: connecting an MCP server is no longer a single sign-off. It's a standing subscription to someone else's instructions.

Three things to watch from here. First, whether OpenAI publishes an attestation mechanism, a price, or a general-availability date for Private Inference. Second, whether Skills over MCP reaches Enterprise plans, and with what admin controls. Third, whether Shopify, HubSpot, or Atlassian publishes a public history of its skill changes.
