Good morning. It's Wednesday, October 21st, and this is your digital money briefing.

For two days this desk told you bitcoin's institutional owners were stepping back. The read was that the ETF wrapper had been a net seller all month and that no new owner had shown up. On Monday, they stepped in. US spot bitcoin ETFs took in $526.6 million, the biggest single session of October, according to TFTC's flow table. Almost all of it came from one fund. BlackRock's IBIT alone drew $665.16 million that day, a figure The Block reports as well. On our arithmetic, that means the rest of the group were net sellers of roughly $140 million on the same day.

Now the counterweight, because one day is not a regime. Even after that inflow, October is still a net outflow for the group. TFTC puts it at roughly $90.6 million month to date. Treat that as provisional, because the trackers do not reconcile and the total moves with pull time. Still, the scale of the swing is clear. Yesterday this desk cited TFTC's month-to-date outflow of about $617 million through October 16th. One session erased most of that deficit. It did not reverse it.

Price is still sitting on the line this desk has watched since the middle of the month. Glassnode puts the US spot ETF cost basis near $81,500. Bitcoin opened Tuesday at $81,844, then slipped to $81,405 by 7:30 in the morning Eastern. That is straddling the line, not clearly above or below it. In Glassnode's words, a daily close below it would put the average ETF holder back at a loss and remove the nearest on-chain support. Keep the mechanics straight, though. A cost basis is an average, not an order book. A close below it hurts holders' marks, but it forces no one to sell.

So the honest read is that Monday's buyer showed up. We still cannot tell you whether that buyer was a long-term allocator or a basis trader re-entering a trade. We looked for futures basis and open-interest evidence around Monday's print and found none we could quote. We are not going to guess. And concentration cuts against the bullish reading. When one fund carries the whole day while the rest of the complex bleeds, that is a single channel of demand, not a broad one. That is an update to our thesis, not a reversal of it.

The second piece of evidence against our own framing comes from leverage itself. Across three episodes this month, this desk described leverage flushing and then rebuilding inside the range. First came a long flush, then fresh shorts, then a long rebuild. A slower gauge tells a different story. CryptoQuant's bitcoin leverage ratio, as relayed by CCN, has fallen for a fourth consecutive month to its lowest level since August 2024. CCN frames that against the anniversary of last October's crash, which CoinGlass put at $19.16 billion of liquidations.

Both readings can be true at once. A monthly ratio is a slow aggregate. Our funding and long-share readings were short-window snapshots. The book can be structurally lighter than it was heading into last October's crash, while still re-levering inside any given week. Treat the ratio itself cautiously. It is single-sourced through CCN, and CryptoQuant's own chart was not checked directly. But the direction argues the range is sturdier than this desk has given it credit for this month. Structurally lighter is not the same as calm.

If the bitcoin book is changing hands, so is the cash underneath it. The stablecoin float is still flat, at $302.3 billion per Stablecoin Beat, below May's peak of $320.6 billion. What is new is composition. Crystal Intelligence found that exchanges cut $4.6 billion of USDT holdings in the third quarter. Ripple's RLUSD grew 59 percent over the same stretch. That growth comes off a base the research does not quote, so read it as momentum, not market share. And over the last thirty days of the quarter, USDC's supply grew 1.2 percent against just 0.2 percent for USDT, according to Stablecoininsider.

That quarter-end data complicates what this desk said in yesterday's episode. We told you USDC was slipping, and that offshore distribution was beating US regulatory comfort. Be precise about the windows. Yesterday's numbers covered the thirty days into mid-October. Stablecoininsider's cover the thirty days into the end of September. In the earlier window, the regulated issuers were the ones gaining ground. So the share contest turns on which window you measure. Our one-month read was not wrong about the number. It was premature in calling a trend from one slice.

The $4.6 billion matters for a second reason. Stablecoins sitting on exchanges are trading collateral. If that much USDT left exchange balances, there is less dollar collateral on venues to absorb a liquidation cascade. That is our inference, not a measured outcome. We also do not know where the money went. Redemption, migration into USDC, and counterparty-risk hedging ahead of new rules are all live possibilities. Nothing in the research resolves which one it was.

The regulated issuers' edge depends on rules, and those rules are now being written by agencies, with Congress not having acted. The SEC's comment period on its proposed Regulation Crypto Assets closed yesterday, October 20th, a deadline on this desk's watch-list all last week. Closing that window does not produce a rule. The next step is simply the SEC reviewing what it received. Separately, the CFTC issued an advance notice on October 5th. It sketches two contemplated rules, Regulation CTX and Regulation CAM, covering crypto transactions under the Commodity Exchange Act. That notice binds no one. As of October 7th, it had not even been published in the Federal Register. So at last check, its comment clock had not started.

The more consequential move is the SEC's. It has granted temporary, conditional exemptive relief to tokenized-securities venues, running five years, through September 17th, 2031. It lets those venues trade tokenized versions of listed stocks without counting as an exchange under the Exchange Act. That is real permission. What it is not yet is real volume. Nothing in the research shows anyone trading under it at scale.

The same gap shows up next door in tokenized Treasuries. This desk has sized that category at about $15.4 billion. Usage is a different story. Across eight leading products, only about $200 million, roughly 2 percent of their value, sits in DeFi's active locked volume. Those are different datasets, so do not multiply one by the other. The point is the ratio. Tokenized Treasuries today are mostly a cash-management wrapper, not working collateral.

For anyone building on this, the footing matters. An advance notice and a time-limited exemption are weaker ground than a statute. A future Commission could narrow either, and the 2031 sunset puts a hard date on the relief. Firms spending on compliance infrastructure now are underwriting that risk. So permission is arriving faster than usage, and on ground that can be taken back.

So here is the updated thesis. The two signals this desk used all month to call bitcoin "rented, not owned" both moved against us at the margin. Institutions posted their best buying day of the month, and a slow leverage gauge sits at its lowest since August 2024. For allocators, the bull case now has evidence behind it. But it rests on one session, one fund, and one single-sourced gauge. October's ETF flows are still negative in aggregate, and price is still pinned on Glassnode's cost-basis line.

The things worth watching are concrete. First, whether Monday's inflow follows through, or reverses by the next print like an earlier up-day this month did. Tuesday's flow data was not confirmed at our deadline. Second, whether bitcoin actually closes above $81,500, rather than just brushing it intraday the way it did Tuesday morning. And one line on the calendar: the Fed's next decision lands October 28th. It has not happened yet, so it is a pending catalyst, not a data point.

That is digital money for October 21st. Talk to you tomorrow.
