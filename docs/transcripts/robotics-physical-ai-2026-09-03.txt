Good morning. It's September 3rd, and this is your robotics and physical AI briefing.

For three briefings running, this show has argued that physical AI's headline numbers are counted by the parties they flatter. Build counts, funding marks, benchmark scores — all self-reported. Today's lead breaks that pattern in one corner. Driver-out status and city approvals are granted by regulators, not by the companies themselves, so the question shifts from who discloses a number to who is actually allowed to operate. One flag before we start. Tesla holds its Austin event today, after this briefing airs. Nothing about what it says or shows is reported here.

Waymo and Zoox both announced multi-market expansions on September 1st, and they land on opposite sides of the only line that currently matters in this race. Waymo said it would begin welcoming its first public riders in Denver, San Diego, and Tampa, taking its fully autonomous ride service to fourteen cities. Waymo's own blog describes these as fully autonomous trips — the freedom, in its words, to get into a car without a driver. That's a primary claim, not press aggregation. Zoox, Amazon's robotaxi unit, said it would begin testing in Houston and San Diego, extending its footprint to twelve US locations. Press reporting says those new-market rides begin with human safety drivers aboard, and Zoox has not published a statement of its own confirming that detail. Zoox also only started charging fares anywhere, in Las Vegas, this past August. Same week, same announcement format, opposite side of the driver-out line.

That contrast is worth pausing on, because it is a harder capability signal than anything either company publishes about its models. A benchmark score is a claim the vendor makes. Removing the human from the driver's seat in a new city is a permission an outside authority grants, and it is countable from outside the company. So for the first time in a while, this sector hands you a comparison you don't have to take on trust.

Now discipline that lead against itself. City count is the wrong denominator. Waymo's new markets start at dozens of vehicles and grow to hundreds over time — that framing comes from a spokesperson quoted in the press, not from anything Waymo has published. Three new cities on that basis barely move the roughly 500,000 rides a week Waymo has been running, a figure quoted at about the same level across the past week of this beat even as the city count keeps climbing. Waymo says it's targeting one million weekly rides by year end. Getting there depends on deepening its existing markets, not on adding logos to a map. The widely cited figure of over 4,000 vehicles across those fourteen cities is press aggregation too, not something Waymo itself has published. Goldman Sachs projects the US robotaxi market growing from roughly three billion dollars in 2027 to nineteen billion by 2030 and forty-eight billion by 2035. That is useful for sizing the prize and worthless as evidence the prize is arriving, because neither operator has published a fare, a cost per mile, or a utilization number to underwrite that path.

Here's an outside data point that sharpens the permission argument. Nevada's Transportation Authority approved robotaxi permits for Clark County on August 20th. Tesla was authorized for up to 5,000 driverless vehicles and Waymo for up to 1,000. Uber got another 1,000, which it plans to run through Motional and Zoox, and Zoox separately holds its own 100-vehicle permit. That's a combined ceiling of up to 8,000 robotaxis across the county over the next year.

Read that carefully. It is a regulatory cap, not a deployment count, and it says nothing about what Tesla announces tonight. But it is a genuine tension worth naming plainly. Tesla's approved ceiling in Nevada is five times Waymo's, even though Waymo is running fully autonomous service in fourteen cities today and Tesla, on the record available before tonight, has no comparable driverless public service anywhere. Permission and delivered service are not the same thing, and right now they point in opposite directions for the same company. Notice also who the third permit holder is. Uber has no autonomy stack in that approval at all. It holds capacity and plans to fill it with someone else's vehicles. So there are three business models sitting inside one county permit: the vertically integrated operator, the manufacturer with a ceiling and no service, and the aggregator renting both. The so-what is that this race is being run on permits and operations as much as on model quality. The operator who moves fastest through regulators, insurers, and fleet depots sets the pace, and that favors balance sheets and operating organizations over model elegance. Read the Waymo-versus-Zoox gap as an organizational gap first and a technical one second.

That same permission-and-structure logic decides where the money goes, and it hands off cleanly into industrial robotics. Huntington Ingalls signed production agreements worth up to 900 million dollars with Path Robotics and GrayMatter Robotics over seven years, for shipbuilding welding and sanding automation. A shipyard is an environment where the access problem is already solved and the task is bounded. That is exactly where a nine-figure production agreement clears.

Treat the number carefully. It is a ceiling on an order book, split between two vendors over seven years, not confirmed revenue, and the split between firm work and options isn't disclosed. Annualize it and share it and the run rate is far below the headline. It also says nothing about installed cycle time, uptime, or how many welders it actually displaces. But it does set a real hurdle rate for general-purpose humanoids, and that hurdle is the useful part. A flexible robot has to beat a purpose-built welding cell on cost, cycle time, and uptime in the same yard where the incumbent already works. It has to do that on a seam a fixed cell has already been tuned for, against a vendor being paid to keep that cell running. Nobody has shown that comparison in public. Until someone does, humanoid capital is buying flexibility that no customer has yet been billed for.

Now the other side of that same trade. XPeng's robotics unit raised more than 900 million dollars, pushing its post-money valuation above 6.3 billion — the largest private physical-AI round in China to date. Note the coincidence in the headline number. Roughly the same 900 million dollars buys a shipyard's worth of contracted welding work in the United States, or a pre-revenue humanoid program in China. One is work somebody has agreed to pay for. The other is optionality somebody has agreed to finance. And the 6.3 billion is a private mark from a negotiated round, not a price anyone can trade against. No shipment or customer figure is attached to it.

It also lands as US market access for foreign-made humanoids is narrowing. In late July the FCC added foreign-produced humanoids, quadrupeds, and other mobile robots to its Covered List. That blocks the equipment authorization new models need to be imported, marketed, or sold in the United States. So the value chain is splitting by geography rather than by whoever builds the better robot. China compounds the cost curve, the US controls who gets to deploy, and no amount of model quality arbitrages that split shut.

So where does this leave the sector heading into tonight. Watch three things. Whether Waymo's per-market fleet depth actually closes the gap to a million weekly rides. Whether Zoox names a driver-out date for its new cities. And whatever Tesla puts on the record in Austin — a price, a production rate, a fleet size, a driver-out claim. Until one of those lands, the cleanest read on who's winning in physical AI isn't a benchmark and it isn't a funding round. It's who's been given the keys.

That's the briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
