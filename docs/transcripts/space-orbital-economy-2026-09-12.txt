Good morning. It's Saturday, September 12th, and this is your space and orbital economy briefing.

This sector is usually graded on what operators promise. Launch cadence, deployment counts, revenue guidance. Today we can do something rarer. The proliferated missile-warning layer finally has a price per satellite attached to it, and it falls out of a contract split that has only now become visible. That number is the spine of this briefing, and it leads somewhere uncomfortable.

Start with Golden Dome, because it has stopped being a policy program and become a priced order book. In mid-July the Space Development Agency awarded one and three-quarter billion dollars for 36 missile-tracking satellites. That headline has circulated for weeks. What we now have is the split. According to Military Times' account of the agency's announcement, L3Harris took roughly 955 million dollars for 18 of the spacecraft. Sierra Space took roughly 798 million for the other 18. The program of record matters here: these are Tranche 3 tracking-layer satellites inside the Proliferated Warfighter Space Architecture, the Space Force's proliferated low-Earth-orbit missile-warning constellation.

Do the arithmetic yourself and the split is telling. L3Harris is being paid about 53 million dollars per satellite. Sierra Space about 44 million. That is our own division of the two disclosed totals, not a quoted unit price, so hold it loosely. But the roughly twenty percent spread between two contractors buying into the same tranche is itself a signal. It prices a difference in assumed sensor capability and in who is carrying the development risk. And it is the first per-unit cost anchor this beat has had for the proliferated missile-warning layer at all.

Now the part the headline buries. All 36 spacecraft are only expected to be ready for launch by the end of 2028. That is a readiness target, not a launch date, and it sits more than two years out. So read this as a manufacturing order, not as evidence that an operational tracking layer is close. The money moves now. The capability, if it arrives on schedule, arrives in a different presidential term.

The cost question gets messier the closer you look. President Trump's own figure for the whole of Golden Dome is 175 billion dollars. The program's director, General Guetlein, has since revised that upward to about 185 billion. Set either number against the Congressional Budget Office's independent estimate from May and it stops looking like a number at all. CBO put the twenty-year cost at 1.2 trillion dollars, with the bulk of that driven by space-based interceptors, and the reporting on that estimate notes the administration's figure is widely viewed as far too low. So when you hear 175 billion attached to Golden Dome, hear the contested floor of a nearly sevenfold dispute. The interceptor layer is where CBO's money goes. It is almost entirely absent from the contracts we can currently see.

That interceptor layer did get one award earlier this year: 3.2 billion dollars in April, split across twelve companies. Spreading that much money across a dozen recipients looks like deliberate anti-lock-in procurement. It is a real structural break from how missile defense has historically been bought, and it pulls in new entrants alongside the legacy primes. But argue the other reading too. When nobody has demonstrated the capability yet, spreading the money around means no single company is being asked to prove very much. Twelve funded prototypes is a hedge, not a program. The so-what is that the disaggregation you are being sold as discipline may simply be an admission that the technical risk has not been retired.

The visible supply-chain tell sits in Fort Wayne, Indiana. L3Harris has just completed a 125 million dollar expansion there, supporting Golden Dome payload integration alongside the Missile Defense Agency's hypersonic and ballistic tracking sensor work. That is real capital going into real floor space ahead of the satellites it is meant to build. It is also where the disaggregated award structure bites. A dozen-plus recipients are now competing for the same narrow pool of infrared and electro-optical sensor capacity, and that pool does not widen because a contract was signed. Watch the sensor supply chain, not the press releases, for whether 2028 holds.

One of the smaller names capturing this money is Rocket Lab. Keep one fact in view as you hear the rest: Neutron, its medium-lift vehicle, still hasn't flown. Rocket Lab disclosed two Space Force awards alongside its second-quarter results. The larger, as reported, is 397 million dollars for the Space-Based Airborne Moving Target Indicator program. The second is 266 million for HASTE, covering up to 18 suborbital missions supporting missile defense, with the first launch promised before year-end.

The award worth pausing on is the moving-target-indicator contract. It asks Rocket Lab to build, launch, and operate the satellites. That is prime-contractor scope, not launch-services scope. It moves the company's comparable set away from pure launch provider and toward a space-systems integrator like L3Harris or Northrop. That matters for how you value it. Recurring government systems revenue carries a different multiple than launch optionality does, and it is the part of the business least hostage to the Neutron calendar. The uncomfortable version is that winning integrator-level work while your own medium-lift vehicle remains unflown relocates execution risk. It moves from a schedule we can watch closely to a program we mostly can't.

Which is the bridge to the thing every one of these programs depends on and none of them controls. Each of these satellites has to reach orbit. The twelve interceptor awardees, the L3Harris and Sierra Space tracking spacecraft, the HASTE missions. And American heavy-lift capacity outside SpaceX has thinned badly this year. Blue Origin's New Glenn has not flown since May 28th, when a static-fire test destroyed the vehicle and severely damaged the company's only operational New Glenn pad. Blue Origin says it intends to fly again before the end of the year. Repairs were still underway this month, and pad rebuilds of that severity have historically taken more than a year. Rocket Lab's Neutron, as noted, has not flown at all.

So procurement diversity at the satellite layer does not buy redundancy at the launch layer. Spreading contracts across a dozen vendors solves a vendor-concentration problem. It does nothing about the launch-concentration problem underneath it. That leaves the Pentagon in an odd position: the buyer with the most purchasing power on Earth, holding almost no scheduling leverage.

Which brings us to the provider carrying most of that manifest. Space Exploration Technologies — SpaceX itself, not a Starlink spin-off — is now a public company. Shares began trading on Nasdaq on June 12th under the ticker SPCX. The offering closed three days later at roughly 85.7 billion dollars in gross proceeds, the largest capital raise this sector has recorded. That is three-month-old context, not news. But set the two halves of today's story side by side. The Pentagon is deliberately diversifying who builds the satellites, while the money underwriting the rocket that gets nearly all of them to orbit has consolidated into one now-public company. That is the concentration argument stated in dollars on both sides.

The optimistic read is that going public means transparency. Quarterly filings finally putting a floor under Starlink and Starship economics that analysts previously had to guess at. Be skeptical of that. This was a combined listing: rockets, Starlink, and the newly merged xAI operations inside one reporting entity. Segment disclosure inside that filing is a choice the company makes, not something a listing guarantees. A consolidated line item can hide constellation capital spending just as easily as an outside estimate could.

Two things to watch. First, Starship's Flight 14. An account tracking SpaceX's FCC filings points to September 15th as the target for the first orbital Starship mission built to deploy operational Starlink satellites, and other trackers list September 18th. It has not flown as of this recording, and I am not characterizing what it will achieve. Second, watch whether HASTE's first launch actually lands inside 2026 as promised. And watch for any public pairing of those 36 tracking satellites with a named launch provider. Right now no such contract is public. That tells you the launch question for Golden Dome's hardware hasn't been asked yet, let alone answered.

That's the briefing. This is Cautious Optimism, signing off.
