Space & the Orbital Economy

Transcript · September 5, 2026

Good morning. It's Saturday, September 5th, and this is your space and orbital economy briefing.

The space economy has spent this year being scored almost entirely on its own numbers. Launch cadence, backlog, subscriber counts, production-line positions. This week the outside checks arrived. And they landed faster than the deliveries did.

Start with Golden Dome. The Space Force has selected twelve companies to develop a space-based interceptor layer. The work is spread across twenty separate Other Transaction Authority agreements, with a potential combined value of up to three-point-two billion dollars. Be clear about what that figure is and is not. It is a ceiling, not money that has been obligated or spent. Companies get paid as they hit milestones.

The provenance matters too. That number reaches us through secondary defense-trade reporting, not through a Space Force release or a filing anyone on this side could read directly. The number that will actually matter is how much of the ceiling gets exercised once the first tests happen. Reporting on the program describes a goal of initial interceptor tests before the end of this year, with orbital demonstrations in 2027. Treat that as a stated ambition rather than a booked schedule. No primary document confirms it.

Now the part that makes this the lead. On September 4th, two independent pieces appeared the same day, one in the Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists and one in RealClearScience. Both argue that the underlying concept runs into orbital mechanics that money does not obviously fix. The claim is about geometry and timing. Intercepting a missile during its boost phase from a constellation in low orbit is a coverage-arithmetic problem, not a schedule problem. Be careful with that. These are analyst critiques, published as opinion and analysis, not a Pentagon technical finding. And outside skeptics have underestimated defense-space cost curves before, repeatedly.

But notice what is different about this particular skepticism. Almost every other constraint in this sector right now is a timing problem with a known fix. A delayed rocket, a scrubbed test, a satellite still sitting in the factory. More schedule, more capital, more iterations eventually relieve all three. If the critique here holds, this is the first constraint of the week that a bigger check does not cure.

Here is the honest counter-case, and it is structural rather than technical. These awards are shaped like cheap options precisely because the concept is unproven. Twelve companies, twenty agreements, milestone payments, a ceiling nobody is obliged to spend. That is what buying optionality on a contested architecture looks like, and it is the right way to fund one. So the so-what is conditional. If the physics argument gains traction in Congress, the plausible response is not cancellation. It is money migrating from the interceptor layer toward the missile-warning and tracking layer, where the engineering is already understood. That would favour the sensor and tracking primes. The Space Development Agency's Tranche 3 tracking awards last December ran to roughly three and a half billion dollars across four teams, including eight hundred and five million dollars to Rocket Lab. A reallocation of that kind would be worth more to those names than to any interceptor developer.

Now the second item, because the same structure shows up where public equity is doing the underwriting. A bet on unproven execution, priced as though execution were already the base case. On September 2nd, Berenberg's Michael Filatov initiated coverage on three public space names in a single session. Each one was pitched on a differentiation claim rather than on revenue or margin. AST SpaceMobile was framed as the only company to have demonstrated true cellular broadband from space to unmodified smartphones. It jumped roughly eleven percent intraday. Planet Labs was framed as the only company imaging the entire Earth daily. It climbed about five percent, helped along by its own quarter, where revenue grew fifty-eight percent year over year to a hundred and sixteen million dollars. Rocket Lab was framed as the only end-to-end public pure-play in space, and was initiated the same day.

Hold the AST move against what AST actually reports. Its most recent quarter brought in thirty-one and a half million dollars, against full-year guidance topping out near two hundred million. And roughly two thirds of the fleet AST has paid to build is sitting on the ground, waiting on somebody else's launch manifest. Nothing about that launch-access constraint changed on September 2nd. The stock moved eleven percent anyway, on one analyst's framing. Note what this is not. It is one shop initiating on one day, uncorroborated so far by any other desk, and those differentiation claims are Berenberg's framing rather than audited facts.

That is the tell. The marginal holder in these names is thin and narrative-driven. The same volatility that produces an eleven percent pop produces an eleven percent air pocket. And it is exactly the window into which AST SpaceMobile, which funded itself with a convertible offering back in July, tends to raise again.

Now the other half of the picture, which is what has actually flown rather than what is being priced. Europe's best-funded new launch company failed again to get its rocket off the ground. Isar Aerospace called off its September 4th attempt at a second flight of its Spectrum rocket, reportedly on high winds at its Norwegian pad. Be fair about that. Scrubbing on wind is the system working, not a failure of the vehicle, and SpaceX scrubbed repeatedly on its own early flights too.

So don't count the day. Count the pattern. This is at least the fifth delay on this specific flight since January. The causes have ranged from a pressurization-valve problem to an unauthorized boat in the exclusion zone. Four separate root causes, none of them a repeat. Eight months, one mission, still not flown a second time. The signal is not the wind on any given day. It is the elapsed time. And the launch window is still open, so this item could be stale within days.

The so-what runs straight into the allied-versus-adversary launch question. European institutional payloads that have to fly on a schedule will keep leaning on Ariane 6 while Isar works through its punch list, and some of them will lean on American providers instead. That is the practical opposite of the launch-sovereignty case that funded Isar in the first place. I am not going to put a Chinese cadence number next to that, because nothing fresh and dateable on the Chinese commercial launch cohort surfaced this week.

The same slipping-schedule logic shows up at a longer time constant in low-Earth-orbit stations. Vast's Haven-1, once expected to fly this year, is now no earlier than the first quarter of 2027. It is one entry in a cohort of ISS successors, Starlab and Orbital Reef alongside it, that are all still pre-launch. Take that as calendar rather than as news, because the slip itself is weeks old.

What makes it matter is the fork ahead of it. NASA has said it will decide in 2027 whether to launch the U.S. Deorbit Vehicle in 2029 and begin retiring the ISS, or to extend station operations instead. An extension is the likelier outcome, and if it happens the crewed low-Earth-orbit gap this cohort risks creating never opens at all. If it does not happen, and no commercial successor is ready, China's Tiangong becomes the only continuously crewed station in orbit. That would be a soft-power win bought entirely with other people's schedule slips, rather than with any Chinese technical achievement.

So what would actually settle any of this, and when. Watch three things. First, whether Golden Dome's early interceptor testing, if it happens on the timeline the program has described, begins to resolve the physics argument, and whether Congress quietly reallocates toward tracking in the meantime. Second, whether Isar gets Spectrum off the pad inside its current window, because another scrub cycle hardens the allied-launch-gap story. Third, Starship's Flight 14, filed with the FCC for no earlier than September 15th to deploy new high-capacity Starlink satellites. That would be the first orbital Starship flight if it holds. It is a filed date, not a launch, so treat it as a calendar entry.

The through-line across all of it is this. The question this week stopped being what companies said about their own progress. It became whether anyone outside those companies could check the claim. A physics paper, a sell-side note and a wind reading are crude instruments. But they are external ones, and a sector that gets marked by outsiders is a healthier market than one that only marks itself.
