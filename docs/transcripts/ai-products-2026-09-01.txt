Good morning. It's September 1st, 2026, and today was supposed to be the day agent tokens got more expensive. It isn't.

Anthropic's own pricing documentation now says that Claude Sonnet 5's launch rate is permanent. That rate is two dollars per million input tokens and ten dollars per million output tokens. In Anthropic's words, that price, "announced at launch as introductory pricing through August 31, 2026, is now the standard price." The page then states plainly that the previously scheduled increase to three dollars and fifteen dollars per million tokens, set for today, will not occur. That was a fifty percent step-up on both sides of the meter. Sonnet 5 is the workhorse model behind most agentic and coding products on the market. So this isn't a footnote. It's a line item disappearing from a lot of teams' September budgets.

This show has spent the past month naming exactly this risk: cheap agent tokens arrive with expiry dates stapled to them. On August 29th we named the dates. OpenAI's promotional rate on its flagship is still dated to November 21st. Google's runs to January 1st. Anthropic's was supposed to lapse today. Of the three, Anthropic's just didn't fire. That is the first time this thesis has failed a live test, and it's worth being precise about what that does and doesn't prove.

Two days ago, this show argued that only an open-weight license is truly unexpiring, because a hosted price is just a vendor's promise. That still holds. The distance between the two is real: a license you already hold cannot be withdrawn, while a standard price can be restated. But a promise that cancels its own scheduled increase is a better promise than one still counting down to a date. Anthropic just moved from the second category toward the first without actually arriving.

Here's the skeptical read. Anthropic has not said why it did this. Competitive pressure, falling inference cost, and demand elasticity would all produce the same headline. Each implies a different forward path. So any motive offered here is inference, not company statement. The commitment is also narrower than it sounds. Anthropic's pricing table still lists Claude Opus 5 at five dollars input and twenty-five dollars output per million tokens, with no note attached at all. Nothing extends this to Opus 5 or any other tier.

And permanent is a word, not a contract. Standard pricing can be revised again exactly as this schedule was revised. What actually changed is that a dated, pre-announced step-up got removed. So the plain so-what for a product team is this. If your September margin plan assumed Sonnet-class inference costing fifty percent more starting today, that assumption is gone, and the budget it freed should fund expansion rather than absorption. But that applies only to the Anthropic leg. Anything you moved onto OpenAI's or Google's promotional rates still carries a countdown clock in those vendors' own pricing pages. The exposure this show named in August is reduced, not removed.

The same price floor is being built from the other direction at Google, though with a wrinkle worth flagging up front. Google's Gemini API added a stable, production version of 3.6 Flash. It made 3.5 Flash-Lite generally available as a dedicated low-cost subagent model. And it shipped 3.5 Transcribe, two dedicated speech-to-text models with speaker diarization, word-level timestamps, and custom vocabulary built in. All of that landed in late August.

The instinct is to call that structural cheapening rather than promotional cheapening: a new model shipping below the price of the one it replaces, with no lapse date attached. Google's own pricing page undercuts that instinct for 3.6 Flash specifically. Its current rate of seventy-five cents input and three dollars seventy-five output per million tokens runs only through December 31st. On January 1st it doubles, to a dollar fifty and seven dollars fifty. That is the same dated expiry this beat flagged for Google, now confirmed in Google's own figures rather than a third-party estimate. So 3.6 Flash does not make the structural case. Flash-Lite does. It's listed at thirty cents input and two dollars fifty output per million tokens with no stated expiry, and that's the tier product teams should treat as the durable floor.

The practical upshot either way is that a cheap, fast router model paired with a more capable model for final reasoning is now something teams can ship rather than diagram. Note what's missing, though. Google published no accuracy figure alongside any of these releases, and "stable" and "generally available" are status labels, not performance claims. What Transcribe does change is the vendor map. Folding diarization and custom vocabulary into the core platform quietly compresses standalone speech-to-text vendors from a category into a feature. For a voice-interface team, the build-versus-buy question stops being which transcription vendor to license and becomes whether transcription deserves a separate line item at all.

So if the meter is getting cheaper and the subagent tiers are generally available, the live question is what people are actually building with them. This month, one answer showed up in trade execution. A cluster of crypto exchanges has, within weeks of each other, wired agents directly into order placement. KTX launched a Skills Kit on August 18th, connecting agents running in Claude, ChatGPT, Codex, and Cursor to its market data, portfolio tools, and order execution. And it isn't alone. As one account of the wave put it: "Coinbase shipped an MCP server for agent trading, OKX open-sourced a 163-tool Agent Trade Kit, Kraken rebuilt around an agentic CLI, Binance released AI Agent Skills." That is essentially the whole tier-one exchange layer building the same connective tissue at once. The news isn't any single exchange's feature. It's that a general-purpose coding or chat agent can now place a real order without custom integration work.

Two caveats belong here, stated plainly rather than buried. First, that cross-exchange comparison rests on a single secondary source, not on each venue's own developer documentation. Second, none of it comes with a usage figure. No exchange here has disclosed what share of executed trades now originates from an agent instead of a human clicking buy. Treat that share as undisclosed, not as evidence of scale. And with KTX's kit dating to August 18th, this is a several-week wave arriving together, not something that happened today.

That leads to one hand-off to a story this show covered on August 29th. Anthropic's browser agent, Claude in Chrome, went generally available on every paid plan in late August. Anthropic's own testing found no successful prompt-injection attacks against its newest models when its safety classifier and content probes were active. Against an unprotected model, the same attacks succeeded 3.8 percent of the time. Both figures are Anthropic's own, and neither has been independently audited. The reason it matters again today is that the same attack surface now sits behind a live order-entry endpoint. A successful injection against a browser agent produces a bad summary. A successful injection against a trading skill produces an executed trade.

Put it together and the constraint on agent products just shifted. If mid-tier inference cost has stopped climbing, and cheap subagent tiers are shipping generally available, then cost stops being the thing that gates what gets built. Authorization, error recovery, and liability at the point of execution become the gate instead. That is exactly what these exchange kits expose without yet answering. Two things would settle it. A lab explaining why it actually cancelled a price increase. Or an exchange publishing what share of its order flow now comes from an agent rather than a person. Neither exists yet.

That's the briefing for September 1st. Have a good one.
