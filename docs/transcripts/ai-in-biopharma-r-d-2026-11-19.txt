Good morning, it's November 19th, 2026, and this is your Cautious Optimism Briefing on AI in biopharma R&D.

In the space of a week, Alphabet and NVIDIA each shipped a biology-relevant model, and they chose opposite ways to put it in a sponsor's hands. On November 9th, this show said no biopharma-specific model release had surfaced to tell us which model leads which task. Within days, two arrived. Today we work through both. Their opposite distribution logic says a lot about where this stack's money and leverage actually sit.

Start with Google DeepMind's AlphaGenome 2, published November 11th. It takes a stretch of DNA sequence and a specified cell type. It then predicts how a variant changes gene activity in that cell type, rather than averaged across a whole tissue. DeepMind trained it on over 107 million individual cell profiles. Cell-type resolution matters because tissue averaging has long obscured which cell population a disease variant actually acts through. That is a real bottleneck between a genetic association and a usable drug target.

The headline result comes from TenK10K, a single-cell benchmark built on immune cell types. There, AlphaGenome 2 found nearly twice as many causal variants as its predecessor: 30 percent versus 16 percent. That is a large relative jump, and it's the one number worth carrying out of this segment. But read it plainly. By simple arithmetic, roughly seven in ten causal variants on this benchmark are still missed. This is a big gain from a low base, not a solved problem. And it is one immune-cell benchmark, so how well the result carries to other tissues is an open question.

The comparison is also narrower than it sounds. DeepMind's own blog describes the comparison set as, quote, "the original AlphaGenome fine-tuned on single-cell data and other specialized tools." Those other tools aren't named. DeepMind reports no head-to-head against a named outside rival, such as Arc Institute's Evo lineage. So "nearly doubling" tells you AlphaGenome 2 beats AlphaGenome. It does not tell you it leads the field. The technical work is posted as a bioRxiv preprint, not yet peer-reviewed, and no independent group has reproduced the result. File it as self-reported, for now.

Access is narrower than the early coverage suggested, too. The model is available today for non-commercial research through DeepMind's API. Commercial use is, in the company's words, "in private preview for commercial research via Google Cloud," with no pricing disclosed. The only named commercial-adjacent user is Genomics Limited. Its chief executive says, quote, "We look forward to incorporating it in our Mystra platform." That is a plan, not a signed deployment. And DeepMind is explicit about the limits. It says the outputs are "intended for research use only and have not been validated or approved for clinical or diagnostic purposes."

So why spend the lead on something this hedged? Because of where it sits in the pipeline. AlphaGenome 2 is a selector-layer tool. It helps decide which variant, acting in which cell type, points to which target. That is a different job from designing a molecule. And it is the layer where this show has argued the credible near-term evidence for AI in drug development clusters: choosing better targets, not designing better molecules.

There's a peer-reviewed number behind that argument. A 2024 Nature paper by Minikel and colleagues estimated that drug mechanisms with human genetic support have a probability of clinical success "2.6 times greater" than those without. That is a historical association across drug programs. It is not a measured effect of this model. Linking AlphaGenome 2's benchmark gain to that figure is our inference, not a finding of the paper. But it is the right frame. Genetic support is one of the few inputs with a documented link to clinical success. That makes sharper variant-to-cell-type mapping worth pharma's attention, even on one retrospective benchmark. What hasn't happened yet is a target nominated prospectively with this model and then validated.

One more footnote, and it's our inference. Yesterday's lead was Isomorphic Labs' raise, which Alphabet itself joined. The same Alphabet family is now offering this genomics model to Isomorphic's competitors. That sharpens the question we raised yesterday about what a sponsor shares with a vendor that may also become a competitor.

Land it here. With AlphaGenome 2, one bottleneck between genetic association and mechanism got cheaper to attack, for immune cells, on the vendor's own scorecard. That is not yet a validated target, and not yet a peer-reviewed result.

That model reaches you through Google's API. NVIDIA took the opposite route. On November 16th, NVIDIA launched Nemotron for Science. The company says the model is "optimized to run locally on a 128GB NVIDIA DGX Spark system." That's a box inside the sponsor's own lab, not a call to someone else's cloud. Alongside it, NVIDIA released LitmusBench, a chemistry-reasoning dataset with "nearly 50,000 questions" for training and evaluating language models.

Every performance figure in the launch is partner-reported inside NVIDIA's own press release, and none is independently audited. So rank the evidence. The strongest comes from an agent built at KTH Royal Institute. It fixed 81 percent of test cases in setting up molecular simulations. NVIDIA's release adds that "every fix was checked independently by GROMACS itself." GROMACS is a physics simulation engine, so each fix was graded against ground truth, not a judgment call. That makes it the most defensible number in the release.

Next comes Therna. It reported nearly doubled accuracy in predicting protein-expression ranges, measured against frontier models. It also says "scientists preferred its explanations over those from the base model in 96% of comparisons." Keep those two claims apart. The accuracy gain is against frontier models. The 96 percent is against NVIDIA's own base model, and preference isn't accuracy.

The last result isn't biopharma at all, but it is the cleanest cost signal. Lila Sciences, working on quantum-dot materials chemistry, reported the post-trained model "exceeding GPT-6 Astra's 54%" on its task. It did that "at 10-20x lower inference cost." That is one partner-run comparison against one frontier model, on a narrow task outside drug discovery.

Here's the business read, and it's our inference. NVIDIA is not fundamentally in the model business here. It is selling the box. A capable model that needs only a 128-gigabyte desktop system is a hardware wedge into every lab that wants its assay or sequence data to stay in-house. That gives this show's running question about who owns the data and the model weights a new kind of answer. Until now, that answer came in licensing language. Now it comes in architecture.

It also carries a pattern from our other desks into the lab. Yesterday, our frontier-labs desk read frontier inference as commoditizing from inside the top tier. A domain-tuned model beating a named frontier comparison at a tenth of the cost or less extends that compression into lab science.

Two consequences follow, and both are inference. For sponsors building in-house models, a capable local model lowers the cost of fine-tuning on proprietary data without that data leaving the building. That tilts build-versus-rent toward building, at least for narrow tasks. For AI-discovery platforms whose pitch is a proprietary model, the squeeze runs the other way. When Alphabet and NVIDIA put capable biology and chemistry models in researchers' hands, the model itself is a thinner moat. What's left to defend is the engine that generates proprietary experimental data.

So the takeaway on Nemotron is this. On narrow lab tasks where a tool can check the answer, the cheap local model looks good enough, on its partners' word. That shifts the build-versus-rent math for every sponsor sitting on proprietary data.

Pull the two together. Where is the money landing today? With the toolmakers and the infrastructure, not with clinical outcomes. Alphabet earns through API access, and NVIDIA through hardware. That's our read, not a disclosed revenue line. Where's the low-hanging fruit? Narrow, tool-verifiable tasks like the GROMACS-checked simulation fixes, and genetics-led target selection, which is the AlphaGenome 2 use case. What's still unproven? Whether any of this moves Phase 2 success rates. Nothing in this week's news is a clinical readout, and no AI-discovered drug has yet won approval. On R&D spend, our inference is a tilt rather than a cut. Budgets shift toward owned infrastructure and proprietary data generation, and away from renting someone else's model.

Three things to watch. First, independent or peer-reviewed reproduction of AlphaGenome 2's benchmark. Second, disclosure of its actual commercial terms on Google Cloud. Third, a pharma sponsor publicly fine-tuning a local science model like Nemotron on its own proprietary data.

That's today's briefing. Stay cautiously optimistic.
