Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Credit & the Corporate Credit Cycle

Transcript · October 22, 2026

Good morning, it's Thursday, October 22nd, and this is your private credit briefing.

Here's the thesis. Private credit's stress is landing on equity before it lands on lenders. A survey of business development company results, published this week, shows where the squeeze is going. And the Fed's new data tell us why that is the system working as designed, not breaking down.

Start at the top of the quality ladder, because that's the real news. Blackstone Secured Lending is the name many allocators treat as the quality benchmark among listed BDCs. It has not cut its dividend yet. But management has told investors it is moving toward, in its own words, "a lower dividend level that is aligned with the fund's longer-term earnings profile." That's a pre-announcement. Its own second-quarter release, for the quarter ended June 30th, shows net investment income of 75 cents a share against a 77-cent regular dividend. That's 97 percent coverage, by the company's own math. Yet credit at the fund looks sound. Non-accruals were just 1.8 percent of the portfolio at fair value. So this isn't a credit story at Blackstone's fund. It's a rate-and-spread story. Lower base rates shrink floating-rate income, and competition for deals has compressed the spread BDCs earn. Either one alone is enough to force a reset at a well-underwritten book. The takeaway: when the quality name guides income down without a credit problem, the earnings squeeze is reaching the whole sector.

Now step down the ladder, where the same squeeze sits on top of real credit damage. This is a different cohort with a different problem. Blue Owl Capital Corp has already cut its base dividend. Its adjusted net investment income covers the new, lower payout at 110 percent. Thin, but positive. FS KKR Capital has less room. Its net investment income of 44 cents a share now exactly matches its distribution, according to a survey by 24/7 Wall St. FS KKR's own release puts non-accruals at 7.1 percent of the portfolio at amortized cost as of June 30th, against 3.8 percent at fair value. That gap is telling. By our reading, the troubled loans are already marked far below what the fund paid for them. And FS KKR's net asset value fell from 18 dollars 83 a share to 18 dollars 30 in a single quarter. That's the equity layer taking the hit in real time. FS KKR heads into the third quarter with zero cushion. Any uptick in non-accruals, or any further rate cut, forces another reduction, because there's nothing left to absorb it. Prospect Capital is the starkest case. Its own filings warn that distributions to common shareholders top net investment income. In plain terms, it has been paying out more than it earns.

One callback worth making explicit. Blue Owl's non-traded funds have been gated at a 5 percent quarterly redemption cap since April, as we covered on October 17th and again on Tuesday. Now its listed BDC, Blue Owl Capital Corp, has cut its payout too. The gates hit the non-traded investors, and the dividend cut hits the traded ones. The same platform's stress now shows on both sides of its book.

Three caveats before moving on. First, these are last-reported-quarter figures, for the quarter ended in June. Third-quarter results for this group haven't been published, so treat this as the preview, not the verdict. Second, the headline cut percentages circulating this week came from a single outlet, 24/7 Wall St., doing arithmetic on per-share dollar amounts. The companies didn't disclose them as percentages. Third, we tried to check how much of this income is non-cash payment-in-kind interest. We couldn't confirm a stated PIK share from any of these companies' releases. One secondary figure for Blackstone's fund conflicts with that fund's own filing, so we're not using it. Until PIK shares are disclosed, read these coverage ratios as a ceiling on cash coverage, not a floor.

So the real signal is the coverage ratio, not the percentage cut. Coverage, not the default rate, is the leading indicator here. Defaults lag. Coverage is already gone at the weak end and thinning at the top.

If BDC shareholders are the ones absorbing this, the Fed just told us how much of the system sits where they sit. Last week the Fed published, for the first time, a measure of private credit inside the Financial Accounts of the United States. The data run through the fourth quarter of 2025, so they measure structure, not this year's strain. Private credit loans were about 6 percent of nonfinancial business debt, up from roughly 1 percent in 2012. That's the scale number. The funding mix matters more than the size. U.S.-domiciled vehicles are funded primarily by equity, 1.1 trillion dollars of it. Bank loans to those vehicles total 349 billion dollars, roughly a third of the equity figure. That's the first official measure of drawn bank lending into this channel. It follows the IMF's proposal for bank capital charges on private-credit exposure, which we covered on October 15th. It also follows the UK regulator's look at Barclays' private-credit swap bookings, which we covered Tuesday. In one week, the bank-linkage question has moved from policy design, to a supervisory case, to official measurement.

Here's the inference, and it's ours, not the Fed's. That 349-billion-dollar figure counts drawn bank loans to the vehicles. It doesn't capture undrawn credit lines, warehouse lending to loan originators, or synthetic exposure through total return swaps. That last channel is exactly the booking question the regulator is asking Barclays. So treat the Fed's number as a floor on bank linkage, not the whole picture. What the funding mix does establish is the direction of travel. Losses move away from bank balance sheets and toward the equity holders in these funds. Those holders include pension funds, insurers, and increasingly retail wealth-channel investors. They're the same people now eating the dividend cuts and sitting behind the redemption gates. The BDC dividend cuts are that mechanism visibly at work. That's the liquidity-mismatch risk in one sentence: the least liquid, least diversified holders are the shock absorber.

Now to where the next layer of risk is being built, and who is limiting how much of it they'll hold. Securitizers are writing their own guardrails around AI-linked lending. A recent CLO anchored by PGIM was reported to cap AI exposure at 15 percent of its pool. That's the same rough limit Carlyle's Jason Thomas floated on television, which we covered October 15th. A strategist's suggestion has become a contract term. For scale, Bank of America strategists estimated in September that CLO exposure to chip and data-center term loans could reach 100 billion dollars. That compares with roughly 4.5 billion today. It's a projection, not issuance. Here's our inference. If securitizers keep capping their AI exposure, data-center debt that can't be packaged into a CLO lands back on direct lenders' own balance sheets. Those are the same balance sheets whose income cushions are already thinning.

So where does the cycle actually stand? This looks like an income-and-NAV grind concentrated in the equity layer of the lending vehicles. It's not yet a default turn. The weakest credits' maturities still cluster in 2027 and 2028, as we covered on October 17th. The biggest unresolved risk is whether wealth-channel capital keeps absorbing losses quietly while its exits stay gated.

Here's the calendar that tests the read. Blackstone and Barclays both report third-quarter results today, and neither had reported as we prepared this briefing. Listen to Blackstone for any word on the BDC dividend reset, and to Barclays for disclosure on the swap question. Then comes Ares Capital on October 27th and Ares Management on the 29th. Apollo's asset-level marks follow on the 30th, and Apollo's earnings on November 3rd. Goldman Sachs BDC and Kayne Anderson BDC report in early November. If third-quarter non-accruals rise at zero-cushion names like FS KKR, that confirms the credit-driven read and points to a second round of cuts. If the resets stay rate-and-spread driven, as at Blackstone's fund, while non-accruals hold flat, this is an earnings story, not a cycle turn.
