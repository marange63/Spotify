Good morning, it's Monday, September 7th, and this is your private equity briefing.

On September 3rd, CVC Secondary Partners closed its sixth global secondaries fund at ten billion dollars. That is the firm's largest secondaries vehicle ever. And that one fact is today's thesis. Limited-partner capital is not frozen. It is switching sides. The record sums now being raised to buy private-equity exposure secondhand, rather than to fund new buyouts, are the clearest price this industry has put on its own inability to sell companies.

Start with the arc of the raise, because the escalation is the news, not the headline number. Fund IV closed at 2.7 billion dollars in 2019. Fund V closed at 5.8 billion in 2023. Fund VI just closed at ten. That is roughly a 3.7 times increase across two vintages. Now add who wrote the checks. More than two hundred investors came into the fund. CVC's own release says roughly half the capital came from investors new to these secondaries funds. So this is not one firm's existing relationships shuffling money between its own products. Real outside money chose this lane. CVC also says its secondaries platform now runs about twenty billion euros of assets under management. That is roughly twenty-three billion dollars, so note the currency switch, and note that the figure is the firm's own characterization rather than an audited number. The so-what is simple. Capital raised specifically to buy other people's fund stakes just closed at nearly double its predecessor.

That is a pattern break worth naming plainly. This show has spent the week describing a fundraising machine that is jammed. Blue Owl's GP Stakes Fund the Sixth, targeting thirteen billion dollars, slipped roughly a full year against its own timetable. And funds over five billion dollars that closed in the first half of this year spent more than three times as many months on the road as similarly sized funds did five years ago. That figure comes from PERE and the underlying vehicles are real-estate funds, so treat it as a read-across for alternatives fundraising broadly rather than a buyout-specific number. Now set CVC's raise against that backdrop. Limited partners are not absent from this market. They are choosing a different lane. Money that might have gone into a new blind-pool buyout fund is going instead into a vehicle built to buy existing stakes at a discount. That is the strategy that actually produces liquidity events while IPOs and trade sales stay scarce.

One caution before that argument runs away with itself. A ten-billion-dollar fund takes years to assemble. So this is evidence about where limited-partner dollars have been drifting as a class over multiple years. It is not a reaction to any single quarter's exit conditions. It is a slow signal, which is exactly what makes it a credible one.

There is a fee consequence in that lane change, and it runs the other way from the headline. Secondaries money buys shorter duration and typically thinner economics than a blind-pool buyout fund. Assets bought at a discount, closer to their exit, throw off a smaller carry pool per dollar committed. So an industry re-pointing its fundraising toward liquidity strategies is growing its assets under management while quietly shrinking the future carried interest those assets can generate. That is the fee-related-earnings versus carry tension showing up in fundraising rather than in an earnings print.

There is a consolidation consequence too. EQT closed its purchase of the secondaries specialist Coller Capital on August 31st, one week before CVC's fund close. Between a buyout giant buying scale and CVC raising it, scale is now the moat in this business. The independents left face a raise-or-sell choice.

Now, if this much capital is bidding for fund stakes, the natural question is what it pays. That is the price test the volume-and-consolidation story has been missing. Jefferies' most recent full review put average buyout secondaries pricing at 92 percent of net asset value. Be precise about what that is. It is a full-year 2025 figure cited in 2026 reporting, with no comparable 2026 print published, and the widely repeated breakdown of it by fund age could not be verified to a direct source, so it is left out here. And note what the denominator is. Pricing to net asset value is pricing against the general partner's own reported mark, not against an outside fair-value benchmark.

What can be sourced is where the volume actually went, and it makes a sharper point anyway. Evercore's review of the first half of 2026 found that roughly 70 percent of secondaries volume came from funds three to eight years old. Only about 26 percent came from funds older than eight years. Sit with that split. The secondaries market is sold to limited partners as the release valve on a backlog of aging, unsold companies. But the capital is overwhelmingly clearing mid-life funds moving through their natural hold periods, not the old tail where the real inventory problem sits. The bid is real, and it is landing on the easy end of the shelf.

Be skeptical of the price test too, and for a structural reason. A secondaries clearing price is not an independent valuation. It is what a buyer with a target return and a finite deployment window will pay. So there is a loop here worth naming directly. More secondaries capital, like CVC's fresh ten billion, chases the same pool of fund stakes. That mechanically tightens discounts to net asset value. Tighter discounts then get cited as evidence that sponsor marks are honest. But the thing doing the validating is fundraising, not cash flow. And fundraising is exactly what needed the marks to look credible in the first place. A narrowing discount is not vindication. It is a bigger bid.

One caveat on CVC specifically. The release describes the fund's strategy only as two-pronged, split between buying limited-partner portfolios and doing GP-led continuation deals. There is no disclosed numeric split and no stated deployment pace. So it is fair to say a fresh ten-billion-dollar bidder adds competition in mid-market continuation vehicles, which would tighten pricing further. But that is a directional read. The split simply is not public.

Now turn to a different way managers are solving their own liquidity problem, because it is the same argument arriving through another door. General Atlantic manages roughly 129 billion dollars. It has launched an evergreen fund giving wealthy individuals access to every investment made across its current and future flagship growth funds. Reporting says the vehicle also includes select holdings from the firm's own balance sheet. That is happening while the firm revives its own listing plans. It tapped JPMorgan as lead underwriter in mid-August, targeting a listing as soon as late this year. There is no filing and no valuation range yet, so treat the IPO as reported intent only.

Here is the sharp reading. A manager is arranging its own liquidity event while its portfolio companies still cannot arrange theirs. And it is selling individual investors continuous access to positions that have no continuous market. If the balance-sheet holdings are in fact included, that is the governance question worth stating plainly. Retail money would be buying into a sponsor's own legacy positions, priced by that sponsor, in the window right before that sponsor lists itself. Nothing here alleges anything improper. It is simply a conflict worth saying out loud. The genuine upside is disclosure. An actual listing would put a growth-equity manager's fee-related earnings next to its realized carry in audited public filings, which is a ratio outsiders currently have to assemble by hand.

So where does the cycle actually stand. The gap that matters is between owners changing and companies selling. Three different things keep getting called liquidity in the same breath. A fund close is a commitment. A secondaries purchase is a change of owner. A distribution is cash in a limited partner's hands. Only the third is what investors are short of, and none of today's developments produce it. CVC's ten billion is undeployed. A tightening discount reflects a bigger bid, not more cash flow. General Atlantic's evergreen vehicle recycles existing exposure rather than creating an exit. Watch three things from here. CVC's first disclosed deployments out of the new fund. The next secondaries pricing review that carries real vintage-level data. And whether General Atlantic actually files.
