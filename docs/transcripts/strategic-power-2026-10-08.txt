Good morning. It's Thursday, October 8th, and this is Strategic Power.

The theme today is the gap between declaration and enforcement. You can see it on the water in the Strait of Hormuz. You can see it in a sanctions bill sitting on the president's desk. And you can see it inside the federal agency that's supposed to make sure America's biggest power grid has enough supply.

Start with Hormuz. On Tuesday this briefing told you there was no reliable post-strike reading on flow through the strait. We said a stalled recovery was an inference, not a measurement. That measurement now exists, and it points to a stall. Tanker-tracking data reported by OilPrice and The National shows crude crossing the strait fell twenty-seven percent from the prior week's wartime high, to about ten-point-one million barrels a day. Put that in context. That level is back to September's average, but it's only seventy-four percent of where the strait ran before the war. That's a strait running well below capacity, not one that's reopened.

One caution on that number. Other trackers report materially different flow levels and baselines for the same period. So the twenty-seven percent is one outlet's reading, not a consensus measurement. But every reading I have points the same direction, and none of them shows the strait back to normal.

Here's where it gets interesting. Secretary of State Rubio said this week that Iran has, in his words, lost complete control of the strait. He also said oil flows have returned to near prewar levels. That claim and the twenty-seven percent drop can both be technically defensible, because they measure different things. Exports from the Gulf of Oman coast and the Red Sea, which bypass the strait, have risen to six-point-seven million barrels a day. That has kept overall Middle East crude exports stable. So the region's aggregate supply looks fine. The strait itself does not. Rubio's "near prewar" holds up only if he means the region, not the waterway.

Tehran is making its own claim. Iran's parliament speaker, Ghalibaf, says the strait stays closed until seven conditions Iran has set are met. Washington isn't acknowledging that framing. And the attacks haven't stopped at a convenient reporting boundary. Citing the US Navy-led Joint Maritime Information Center, Reuters counted at least twelve attacks on crude, LPG, and LNG carriers between September 28th and October 2nd. Then on October 7th, after that count window closed, projectiles struck a tanker north of Qatar, with casualties reported. Any backward-looking tally is already understating the tempo.

The market, for now, is pricing friction rather than closure. Brent rose only about seven-tenths of a percent on Tuesday, to roughly a hundred and one dollars a barrel, per one wire report.

The so-what is twofold. First, don't take "flows are normal" at face value from either capital. The strait data and the aggregate export data tell genuinely different stories, and the gap between them is the real signal. Second, those attacks are hitting LNG and LPG carriers, not just crude. That means the shock reaches seaborne gas, which matters more to Asian and European buyers than to US crude balances. It also strengthens the strategic case for non-Gulf LNG, including US Gulf Coast capacity, as a hedge rather than a nicety.

Washington's other lever on Iran, and on Russia, just moved from rhetoric toward statute. The Senate passed the Graham sanctions bill eighty-six to eleven back in August. The House has now approved the Senate-amended version, formally the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act, by two-sixty-two to one-fifty-nine. That sends it to the president's desk. The reporting shows no confirmed signature yet, so treat this as pending, not enacted.

The substance splits into two very different tools. One is discretionary: authority for tariffs of up to five hundred percent on Russian imports, used at the president's judgment. The other is nominally mandatory: tariffs of up to one hundred percent on goods from the five largest buyers of Russian crude and gas, a group that includes China and India.

But look at the actual language, and the mandate is softer than the headline. The president would be required to impose tariffs of, in the bill's own terms, more than zero and as much as one hundred percent. A floor just above zero means an obligation to act is not an obligation to bite. A token rate would satisfy the law. Congress has legislated the posture here. The president still sets the price. That's the real story, more than the five-hundred or hundred-percent ceilings.

Now the conditional second-order effect. If the tariff were applied at a meaningful rate, India and China would have a reason to shift away from Russian crude and gas. That demand would land on the same constrained Gulf barrels we just walked through at Hormuz. A sanctions tool aimed at Moscow could end up tightening the market Washington says is back to normal.

And if the tariff ever bites China, it lands in a crowded calendar. China's rare-earth truce, which we covered yesterday, expires November 10th. A mandatory tariff aimed at China as a top buyer of Russian energy, arriving in the same window as a lapsing rare-earth truce, would put two separate US-China levers under tension at once. Neither is guaranteed to fire. But the chance that they fire together, a month from now, is the kind of compounding risk markets tend to underprice until it's already happening.

From who controls energy flows abroad to who pays for them at home. PJM, the country's largest grid operator, has a capacity shortfall driven by data-center demand. That's the same buildout behind the Google deal for eight hundred and ninety megawatts of new nuclear capacity that we covered yesterday. Now the mechanism meant to fix that shortfall has been frozen.

On September 29th, FERC accepted most of PJM's one-time backstop procurement. But it suspended the backstop until February 28th, 2027. FERC questioned how PJM planned to divide the costs among customers, which in practice is a fight over whether data centers or households carry the bill. The suspension kills the offer window PJM had planned to run from September 30th through October 21st. PJM's board has instead proposed a limited emergency auction to backfill the shortfall from its last capacity auction. That's a shortfall of roughly six-point-seven gigawatts, per Canary Media and others. Nothing in the reporting shows the emergency auction closing it. And the board's fallback stops short of requiring data centers to bring their own power.

Separately, PJM is weighing new "ride-through" reliability standards. That follows a July 22nd event in Northern Virginia, when data centers tripped offline. PJM itself called it the largest such event in its history. That reframes data centers from simply a demand problem into a reliability risk in their own right.

There are two honest readings of the FERC freeze. The first is that a cost-allocation fight could discipline speculative data-center buildout better than any auction. If the bill lands on large loads, marginal projects drop out. It would also push data centers toward behind-the-meter generation. That's an incentive, not an observed shift yet. The second reading is that a procurement vacuum until February 2027 leaves the shortfall without a funded fix. That supports elevated capacity prices and favors independent power producers sitting on existing generation.

And this is the same who-pays thread we followed in the Wisconsin rate case on Tuesday, now arriving at the federal regulator. Residential bills in PJM states stay a live political issue heading into the midterms. Either way, the binding constraint in America's largest grid is no longer the megawatts. It's who pays for them.

Keep an eye on four dates. First, a presidential signature and an effective date for the Graham Act. Second, the next Hormuz flow reading that captures this week's attacks. Third, November 10th for the rare-earth truce. And fourth, February 28th, 2027, when FERC's suspension of PJM's backstop runs out. That's where the next real measurement, as opposed to the next claim, will land.

That's Strategic Power for October 8th. Stay sharp out there.
