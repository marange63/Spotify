Good day, and welcome to your enterprise technology briefing for August 23rd, 2026.

For most of the past week, this briefing has framed data governance as the place enterprise software vendors are building their next durable margin — sold as a feature, metered as a product, pitched as a reason to trust one platform over another. This week, Atlassian gave us the inversion. A dominant collaboration vendor doesn't have to sell you governance at all. It can simply take the setting away from you.

Here's what actually changed. Atlassian's own support documentation says the company began using customer data according to a new set of data contribution settings on August 17th, across Jira, Confluence and the related cloud products. And the mechanics, laid out plan by plan on Atlassian's own page, are starker than a typical default-on story.

Atlassian splits contributed data into two categories. In-app content is the text inside Confluence pages and Jira tickets. Metadata is the layer around it — the field and workflow names, the configuration, the operational exhaust of how a company actually runs its tools. On the Free, Standard and Premium plans, metadata contribution cannot be turned off. Atlassian's language is flat: "you can't change this setting." Only on the Enterprise plan does a toggle to switch metadata contribution off exist at all. In-app content runs the other way. Free and Standard customers can turn it off, and Premium and Enterprise customers have it off by default.

So this is not a default you can flip if you know where to look. For three of Atlassian's four cloud tiers, the capability to withhold metadata does not exist in the product. An Atlassian staff member, replying officially on the company's own community forum, confirmed as much for Premium. That reply said the only exclusion path short of Enterprise is a narrow compliance exemption — customer-managed encryption keys, government cloud, isolated cloud, or HIPAA status. It also noted those exemptions are not tied to plan tier, which is exactly what makes them a different thing from an opt-out.

Two honest qualifications. Atlassian de-identifies what it collects, stripping names and emails before use, so this is not a raw-content-leak story. And retained, de-identified data can be kept for up to seven years. That seven-year figure comes from secondary reporting on the policy rather than from Atlassian directly, so hold it loosely. If it holds, it makes the opt-out asymmetric in time. What a customer withholds from here on governs future contribution, not the model that has already learned.

Here's the so-what. If withholding your own workflow metadata from an AI training pipeline requires an Enterprise contract, governance has stopped being a customer right and become a paid upgrade — a forced-upsell lever any dominant collaboration suite is now free to pull. And notice the likely response. It will not be churn. GitLab has published a switch-to-us post about the policy, but a marketing post is not a customer loss, and Jira and Confluence migrations are expensive enough that almost nobody will pay for one over a settings table. The realistic outcome is contractual. Expect data-use clauses pushed into renewal negotiations across every collaboration suite, and vendors discovering that a privacy tier lifts effective revenue per customer without shipping a single new capability.

Which raises the obvious question. How does a vendor ship a default like that and expect it to stick? Part of the answer is that most customers cannot see it happening, because most enterprises don't actually know what data they have.

On Tuesday we quoted the growth half of Salesforce's Agentic Enterprise Index — activated agents going from five to thirteen over fourteen months. That cohort was restricted to organizations running agents in production every single month of the study period, so it measures intensity among the already-converted, not breadth of adoption. Today, the half underneath it. In the same report, only twenty-seven percent of the commerce organizations surveyed said their customer data was fully unified. Forty-six percent of business-to-consumer organizations reported duplicate or conflicting customer records. And just thirty-two percent of those commerce organizations had fully defined AI success metrics at all. The survey behind those figures is 4,689 respondents from May, with no published sampling frame and no cross-vendor benchmark to check it against. It is also Salesforce marketing its own platform, which is precisely why the unflattering half is the more credible half.

That gap is the mechanism. An organization that cannot tell you whether its own customer records are duplicated cannot meaningfully evaluate what a vendor's default data setting is doing with its information. Default-on designs are not reckless bets. They are commercially safe precisely because the audience that would need to object mostly lacks the internal visibility to do so. There is a second-order read there as well. If the binding constraint on agent deployment is data engineering rather than model quality, the systems integrators capture a growing share of every agent dollar before the platform vendor ever books license revenue.

Now follow the money to where that exposure is being priced. Obsidian Security closed an eighty-five million dollar Series D earlier this month, led by Crescent Cove Advisors at a one-point-one billion dollar valuation. It crossed into unicorn territory on a single premise: governing what AI agents can touch inside enterprise applications is its own budget line now, not a feature bolted onto identity or endpoint security. In its own funding announcement — and that self-interest matters — Obsidian says more than seventy percent of its customers already let AI agents into third-party applications. That is the demand-side mirror of Atlassian's supply-side move. The access already exists, buyers are already funding tools to watch it, and no incumbent has claimed the category outright. But a one-point-one billion dollar point solution is not evidence the category survives on its own. CrowdStrike, Palo Alto Networks and Okta shipping the same capability inside what customers already buy is the likelier end state.

Zoom out one level, to the infrastructure underneath all of this. On Tuesday we covered Cloudflare's restructuring and called its "agentic AI-first" language a real cost story wrapped around an unverified causal claim. Agents Week is the product line that language was pointing at. On August 4th, Cloudflare announced Wallets, a programmable payment system built for a mundane problem: an AI agent cannot open a bank account or complete a normal signup flow, so it needs its own financial rails. Days later came Kitesurf, a browser built from scratch rather than repurposed from Chromium. Cloudflare says it uses roughly a seventh the memory and under a third the CPU of Chromium-based automation. That is Cloudflare's own measurement, on HTML-extraction tasks, not an independent benchmark at production scale. The company also says it built the thing in about twelve weeks, running entirely on its own Workers platform.

Treat that as a claim about direction, not proof of demand. There is no customer count, no usage figure and no revenue disclosed for any of it, and Kitesurf is a free beta with no stated business model. But the logic is coherent. Cloudflare is betting that autonomous agents need their own primitives — their own identity, their own payment rails, their own lightweight way of browsing — rather than human tooling with an agent bolted on top. If that bet is right, access defaults like Atlassian's don't stay confined to a settings page. They get poured into the architecture agents run on, which makes them far harder to unwind later.

Put the pieces together and the shape of this month is plain. The model layer is being commoditized. The agent-platform layer is being metered. And the layer that decides who keeps the durable advantage is whoever sets the defaults on enterprise data — because those defaults are being written right now, by vendors, while most buyers still can't tell you what data they're sitting on.
