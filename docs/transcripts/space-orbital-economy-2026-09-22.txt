Good morning, it's September 22nd, 2026, and this is your space and orbital economy briefing.

Today's spine is the gap between what the orbital economy is booking and what it is actually putting into orbit. Contracts and backlog are compounding fast. Deployed hardware is not. That gap shows up in defense space, in launch, and in commercial stations, and the mechanism is the same in all three.

Start with the lead. The Space Development Agency's Accelerated Missile Defense Tranche 3 program has put another one point seven five billion dollars into the missile-tracking layer of Golden Dome. L3Harris takes nine hundred fifty-five million dollars for eighteen satellites. Sierra Space takes seven hundred ninety-eight million for another eighteen. Both batches are due in time for a potential launch in late 2028. That sits inside the broader three-year, one hundred seventy-five billion dollar Golden Dome architecture. Keep those two numbers apart. The program total is an ambition. The award is the only part that is obligated money.

Now weigh that against what is flying. The Space Development Agency told Federal News Network this month that it has fielded sixty-three of the one hundred fifty-four satellites planned for Tranche 1. Tranche 2 has two hundred seventy satellites in production. Tranche 3 now totals a hundred and four after this award. So awards arrive on a clock measured in months, and deployment arrives on a clock measured in years.

Here is the part that cuts against what this show has argued all week. The agency's own director, asked why Tranche 1 is behind, points at software and hardware issues that forced a months-long pause in launches, plus supply-chain problems he says still persist. That is not a rocket shortage. That is a manufacturing and integration problem inside the satellite vendors. The binding constraint on Golden Dome today is the factory, not the pad.

Now the per-satellite arithmetic, because for once there is a benchmark to check it against. L3Harris's award works out to roughly fifty-three million dollars a satellite. Sierra Space's works out to roughly forty-four million. That is this desk dividing two published contract totals, not a disclosed unit price. What makes it useful is that the agency bought almost exactly this requirement once before. In January 2024 it awarded the Tranche 2 tracking layer to three teams, L3Harris, Lockheed Martin and Sierra Space, at eighteen space vehicles each, for launch no later than April 2027. Against their own prior prices, L3Harris is now charging about four percent more per satellite and Sierra Space about eight percent more, on a schedule that leaves roughly ten fewer months between signature and launch. Read that as the price of compressing a timeline. It is an inference drawn from two award totals, but it is a rare one, because the government has now paid twice for the same job in public.

And here I want to correct a framing that was tempting this morning. It is easy to read Sierra Space's win as the tracking-layer supplier base widening past the legacy primes. It is not. Sierra Space was already a Tranche 2 tracking prime back in January 2024. What actually changed between the two awards is narrower, not wider. Three teams won the 2024 round. Two won this one, with Lockheed Martin absent from the accelerated batch. The genuinely new name in this layer is Rocket Lab, which took a piece of the December 2025 Tranche 3 awards covering seventy-two satellites, alongside L3Harris, Lockheed and Northrop Grumman. So the honest so-what is this. The fastest-moving number in Golden Dome is dollars obligated, because nothing in these tranches can reach orbit before 2028, and the supplier list is churning rather than broadening.

Say the counter-case plainly, because it is strong. A two-year gap between award and launch is normal for space hardware, not evidence of failure. And a multi-billion-dollar government order book is contracted, budget-backed revenue, insulated from the commercial constellation economics that make the rest of this sector fragile. The argument here is about how the headline numbers should be read, not a claim that these programs fail.

One genuine absence is still worth flagging. Nothing in the award announcements for these tranches identifies a launch vehicle or a manifest for any of it. Not the agency's own release, not the contractor statements, not the trade coverage. The Space Development Agency buys launch separately, so that silence is not proof of a hole. But it does mean several hundred satellites across two tranches are under contract with no publicly identified ride, coming due into the back half of this decade, when Western medium-lift supply is precisely the thing in question.

Which brings in Rocket Lab, a December 2025 tranche awardee, and the clearest place to watch backlog and delivery diverge outside the defense budget. Rocket Lab's second-quarter results, reported back on August 10th, showed record revenue of two hundred thirty-four million dollars, up sixty-two percent year over year. Backlog hit a record two point three six billion dollars, up a hundred thirty-seven percent, with more than ninety missions booked. Third-quarter guidance is two hundred fifty to two hundred sixty-five million dollars of revenue, against an adjusted EBITDA loss of seventeen to twenty-three million. Backlog is compounding faster than the company is converting it into cash.

That combination is only financeable because a large part of the order book is sovereign. A government-backed backlog lets a company run losses and keep raising equity at a cost of capital that purely commercial space cannot get. The risk is that equity issuance stops being a bridge and becomes the operating model, with the sector's capital intensity underwritten by awards that have not yet converted into anything.

The bet on closing that gap is Neutron, Rocket Lab's medium-lift vehicle aimed at the part of the market Falcon 9 owns. The fairing, which the company calls Hungry Hippo, has completed all testing and integration of key subsystems, and Rocket Lab has run hundreds of hot-fires on its Archimedes engine. That is real hardware progress, and it is the first concrete Neutron update since the August earnings call. It is also a subsystem milestone, not an integrated stage standing on a pad. There is still no firm first-flight date, and Neutron has already slipped once from an original 2025 target. Backlog is the promise. Neutron is the delivery. Only one of the two compounded this year.

The same pattern shows up outside defense entirely, in a business with no government backstop at all. Vast's Haven-1, meant as an early node succeeding the International Space Station, has moved from a May 2026 target to no earlier than the first quarter of 2027. That is despite completed primary structure welds and systems integration already underway. No cause was disclosed, so I will not guess at one. What I will say, as this desk's read rather than a sourced fact, is that no commercial low-Earth-orbit station entrant has held its original date. If that holds, a gap between the station's retirement and a working successor is the base case rather than the tail risk, and NASA would be carrying that schedule risk with no obvious second source.

One more place where a signed agreement is standing in for proven demand. AST SpaceMobile has advanced its Verizon partnership into a definitive commercial agreement for space-based cellular coverage in the United States, using standard, unmodified phones, with the agreement's own language putting service starting in 2026. That is contract language, not a confirmed live-service date. It is a real distribution win against SpaceX and T-Mobile's competing direct-to-cell offering. But there is no subscriber count behind it and no revenue figure. A commercial agreement is an obligation. It is not yet a customer. That distinction bites hardest here, because direct-to-cell economics live or die on subscriber volume, not on the number of carrier logos in a press release.

Pull the threads together. What is funded and contracted this week is real. The Golden Dome tranches, Rocket Lab's order book, Vast's welded hull, AST's carrier deal. What is on orbit and earning revenue is much smaller than any of those headline totals implies. And the agency itself just answered half the open question: the near-term constraint is satellite manufacturing, while the 2028 constraint is lift that still has no name attached to it. Watch three things. Any firm Neutron flight date. Any primary Space Development Agency disclosure updating deployed counts or naming a launch vehicle for Tranche 2 and Tranche 3. And Starship's Flight 14 window, still September 28th, now carried unchanged across five straight editions of this show with nothing new attached to it.
