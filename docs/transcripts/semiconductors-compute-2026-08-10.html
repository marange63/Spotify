Cautious Optimism Briefings

Semiconductors & Compute Infrastructure

Transcript · August 10, 2026

Good morning. It's Monday, August 10th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

All week this show has chased a single question: where exactly is the AI buildout rationed? Earlier in the week it was wafers. Then it was memory. Then it was the interconnect between them. Today the answer lands on advanced packaging, and the data behind it is sharper than anything we've had so far.

Start with TSMC. CoWoS, the advanced-packaging process that fuses GPU dies to high-bandwidth memory, is sold out through the end of this year, with lead times running fifty-two to seventy-eight weeks. TSMC has been expanding that capacity roughly eighty percent annually, from about thirty-five thousand wafers a month at the end of 2024 to about seventy-five thousand by the end of last year, targeting somewhere around a hundred twenty-five to a hundred thirty thousand wafers a month by the close of 2026. Even at that pace, supply-chain estimates put Nvidia alone reserving roughly sixty percent of next year's output — on the order of five hundred ninety-five thousand wafers, the bulk of it on the CoWoS-L variant used for its largest accelerators. TSMC itself hasn't confirmed that percentage; it comes from analyst and trade-press tracking that varies source to source, so treat the exact wafer count as a directional estimate rather than a disclosed figure. What is on the record is Nvidia's response: it has gone outside TSMC entirely, enlisting the outsourced assembly-and-test houses Amkor and ASE for an additional eighty thousand wafers of packaging capacity. TSMC's own results explain the urgency — second-quarter net profit jumped seventy-seven percent year over year to a record on strong AI-chip demand, with third-quarter revenue guided to roughly thirty-seven percent growth. So the lesson is that the binding constraint on GPU supply was never lithography. It's the packaging step that turns a finished die into a shippable accelerator, and that step is now scarce enough that even Nvidia is buying capacity from TSMC's own subcontractors. That tells you something about the shape of demand, too: buyers are queuing for allocation more than a year out, so this is compute rationed by availability, not demand throttled by price.

The practical effect is that Amkor and ASE, once viewed as commodity assembly shops, are capturing a durable slice of the highest-value step in the AI hardware stack, and that should show up in their margins before it shows up in anyone's headlines. And it looks structural, not like a one-quarter spike. The advanced-packaging supply-demand gap, as wide as twenty percent earlier this year, is estimated to narrow to roughly ten percent by December as new capacity comes online — though that's an industry estimate, not a TSMC-guided figure, and even a ten-percent shortfall keeps allocation, not price, the thing customers fight over. So a market that once priced Amkor and ASE as cyclical assembly houses now has to start pricing them as gatekeepers, pulled up the value chain by the same scarcity that pins Nvidia's own supply.

That same scarcity logic runs straight into memory, the second chokepoint. SK hynix's chief executive told Reuters that 2027 will be the worst year in the memory industry's history from a supply standpoint, and that customer demand will keep exceeding the company's production capacity even beyond 2030. Samsung's memory chief separately warned that significant shortages across memory products will continue through at least 2027. Those are the suppliers' own forecasts, and suppliers benefit from signaling scarcity, so hear them as interested guidance rather than neutral fact. But the more useful tell is how the two Korean giants are structuring their contracts. TrendForce reporting confirms that SK hynix has rewritten its long-term agreements so that spot-price surges pass through in full to contract pricing, while Micron's agreements retain a hard ceiling tied to prevailing market levels from this spring. That's a real divergence in conviction: SK hynix is betting scarcity holds and wants full upside, Micron is offering customers predictability instead. Which bet pays depends on 2027. If scarcity holds, SK hynix collects the full premium and Micron leaves money on the table. If it breaks, hyperscalers reallocate purchasing toward whoever gave them the predictable number, and the revenue mix shifts the other way.

Layered onto that, TrendForce also reported back in December that Samsung and SK hynix had raised HBM3E supply prices by nearly twenty percent for all of 2026 — an annual price move, not a single-quarter jump, so don't read it as a fresh spike. The same TrendForce reporting adds that Samsung's foundry business is targeting full utilization in the second half of this year, up from a seventy-to-eighty percent range currently, driven by ramping HBM4 base-die production and by Exynos processor output, with two-nanometer order volume from customers including Broadcom expected to more than double year over year. Put together, memory suppliers are no longer describing a normal cycle. They're describing a structural shortage that reprices DRAM and HBM as a scarce input to every AI accelerator's bill of materials, not a commodity that mean-reverts.

And that scarcity doesn't stay inside the AI budget. Sustained DRAM and NAND tightness raises the bill of materials on every server and every PC, which is a quiet tax on the non-AI compute that still pays most of the industry's bills — enterprise refresh cycles get more expensive precisely as AI capex crowds the same fabs. It also hands China's memory makers, CXMT and YMTC, a structural opening: a shortage this deep lets them sell into the price-insensitive commodity segments they'd otherwise be shut out of, which advances Beijing's memory self-sufficiency push even though neither can yet do advanced HBM packaging.

So with packaging and memory both concentrated in a handful of hands, the obvious question is whether anyone can add a credible second source. The best answer this week is a small yes. Intel Foundry landed Fortinet as its first publicly named external customer under chief executive Lip-Bu Tan, manufacturing the next-generation Security Processor 6 for Fortinet's firewall line using Intel's EMIB packaging. Intel's foundry business grew thirty-one percent year over year to five point eight billion dollars of the company's sixteen-point-one-billion-dollar quarter, ahead of its own twenty-five percent growth target. That's genuine traction. But the win sits on Intel 4, a mature, already de-risked node — not on the eighteen-A leading-edge process where Intel most needs to prove it can take competitive business from TSMC. Financial terms weren't disclosed either. Still, the signal matters at the margin: a named, cost-sensitive networking customer on Intel 4 is the kind of reference that pulls other security and networking ASIC programs in to evaluate Intel as a TSMC alternative — just not yet at the node where the scarcity actually lives. So this closes the loop only partway: concentration in the packaging and memory layers isn't going away because one mature-node customer showed up.

Zoom out and the throughline is pricing power. TSMC is ceding vertical control of packaging to keep pace with demand, which means Amkor and ASE now share in that value. SK hynix is betting its balance sheet that memory scarcity persists into the next decade, while Micron hedges. And Intel's proof that it can relieve the pressure remains confined to a mature node, not the leading edge where the real bottleneck sits. That also means the geopolitical risk surface tied to Taiwan is now wider than lithography alone — a shock there runs through advanced packaging just as much as through the fabs themselves.

That's the briefing. Have a good day.
