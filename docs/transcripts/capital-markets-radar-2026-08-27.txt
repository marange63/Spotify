Good morning, it's Thursday, August 27th. This is your capital markets radar.

This week the market finally paid real money for the liquidity story it has been telling itself since Treasury's buyback announcement. On Tuesday this program called bitcoin a rates proxy — an asset trading on a Treasury operation aimed at Treasuries, not on anything that happened in crypto. We now have the flow receipt for that call. It supports the trade. What it does not support is the mechanism underneath the trade, and that gap is today's story.

Start with the price. Bitcoin hit $81,235 intraday on August 25th. That was its first print above $80,000 in more than three months. It eased back the following morning. Ether ran harder still over the same week.

Now the flow, because the flow is what is actually new. The number everyone has been citing is $2.62 billion into spot bitcoin and ether ETFs, the best week of 2026. But that window covered August 17th through the 21st. It closed four days before the high, which means it could not explain the breakout it was being used to explain.

That gap has now closed. On August 25th itself — the session bitcoin printed its high — US spot bitcoin ETFs took in a further $314 million. BlackRock's IBIT accounted for $284 million of it. Spot ether funds added on the same day as well. So the money did not stop before the top. It ran straight through it.

Be precise about what that proves. It establishes that the buying and the price move were contemporaneous, which is better evidence than a four-day gap allowed. It does not establish that Treasury's buyback caused either one. The chain everyone is running — buyback announcement, to dollar liquidity, to bitcoin — is still reporter framing rather than a flow-of-funds identity. The honest alternative reading is the one this program gave on Tuesday. Allocators re-entered a deeply drawn-down asset on a rates view, and the Treasury narrative was attached afterward. Both readings fit the same flow data. Only one of them survives if the funding mechanism never shows up.

So follow the money to its supposed source. Treasury is doubling the size of its long-end buyback operations, from a two-billion-dollar cap per operation to at least four billion, starting September 9th. Days later, CNBC reported — citing two senior Treasury officials, not a Treasury statement — that Secretary Bessent might fund the expanded buybacks by tapping the Treasury General Account rather than issuing new bills. That account has been built up to around $950 billion, far above what the prior administration targeted.

The two funding routes are not equivalent, and that is the whole argument. New bill issuance drains cash out of money markets to pay for the purchase. Drawing down the cash balance does the opposite. It releases reserves that were sitting idle at the Fed, which is quantitative easing's effect without the Fed doing anything at all. Same buyback, opposite sign on liquidity. Treasury has confirmed neither route.

Except the drawdown has not happened. The most recent Daily Treasury Statement covers August 24th. As reported from that statement, the General Account closed just under $967 billion. That was up about thirty-four billion from where it opened that same day. That is not a drawdown. That is the account still being built up, days after the leak. So the liquidity impulse this entire trade is supposedly pricing has not entered the banking system. Not one dollar of reserves has been released. The market is trading a funding plan Treasury has not confirmed, ahead of a purchase that has not started.

That split is visible in how the legs of the trade are now behaving. Yesterday this program noted that three days of escalating buyback headlines had produced progressively smaller yield reactions. Add the fourth run of the same headline and the diminishing response has turned into an outright divergence. Bonds have partly given back their initial rally, as analysts question how much firepower the operation really has. The dollar index sat near 99.15 on August 26th, still close to a three-month low, but it ticked higher on the day rather than lower.

So the two legs closest to the Treasury story have stalled. The two furthest from it, crypto and gold, are still running. When a one-trade narrative breaks into pieces, it is worth asking which piece you are actually holding. Bitcoin ETF creations are the most reversible capital on the board. They arrived on a liquidity view, and they can leave on that same view the first time the Treasury data disappoints it.

There is also a real cost building underneath the funding plan, whether or not Treasury ever pulls the trigger. Spending down cash instead of issuing bills thins the government's own buffer. It does that heading into a debt ceiling expected to bind in late 2026 or early 2027. Delay the issuance now and it still has to show up later. It shows up as a bill-supply catch-up at exactly the moment Treasury has the least room to maneuver. So even the bullish version of this story trades near-term liquidity for a nastier collision down the road.

Now turn to the one corner of markets with no daily mark to argue about: private credit. Business development companies hold most of the publicly tracked exposure, and their capital structure is currently split against itself. BDC bonds have recovered. BDC equity keeps trading at a persistent discount to net asset value, on the very same portfolios. That is not a disagreement about what the assets are worth today. It is a disagreement about their earnings power going forward.

There is a reason both can be true at once. Payment-in-kind usage across BDC loan books has been climbing. Boston Fed research, as relayed through secondary reporting, puts it at roughly six percent of loans three years ago and roughly ten percent by early this year. And this is no longer a software-lender phenomenon. Construction went from under five percent to nearly twenty over the same stretch. PIK income counts on the income statement, but it is not cash in the door. So dividend coverage is more fragile than the NAV headline implies. The bondholders sitting above that equity have every reason to feel differently about identical assets.

Here is the piece that cuts against the run-on-the-bank framing making the rounds. Blue Owl Capital Corporation II ran a tender offer, and fewer than one percent of shareholders took it. If investors believed the marks were wrong and wanted out at any price, that tender was the moment to prove it. They passed. So the discount looks less like a stampede for the exits and more like holders declining to sell at the price on offer. That is a dispute about the exit price, not a run. One tender at one fund is a data point, not a sector-wide participation rate. But it is the right kind of data point, and it points the opposite way from the narrative. Worth remembering the next time a private-credit allocation is marked at face value: the yield on the label is not the cash yield in the account.

Last, the tail risk, now with a number attached. Brent and WTI both fell roughly three percent on August 25th, Brent settling at $89.44, as the market shrugged off Washington's new sanctions push on Iran. Yesterday this program argued the Iran premium came out on probability rather than on physics. Here is the quantification. On the Monday before that selloff, only two tankers transited the Strait of Hormuz. That is the lowest daily count since early May. And the very next session, crude sold off three percent.

The physical indicator and the price are pointing in opposite directions. That is not evidence a closure is imminent. Transit counts can fall because shippers reroute voluntarily, and a single day's reading is noisy. What it does say is that the market repriced the probability of disruption while the supply constraint sat where it was. And there is a rates consequence most people are skipping. Cheap crude has been doing quiet work capping the back-up in long yields. So a genuine Hormuz disruption would not hit the inflation story first and the liquidity story later. It would hit both at the same time, in the same week.

Two things remain genuinely unresolved. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh delivers his Jackson Hole keynote on Friday, and it had not happened as of this recording. And the first of the larger buyback operations does not begin until September 9th. Until both land, this is a market pricing a mechanism it has not yet seen work.

That's your radar for today. Trade carefully out there.
