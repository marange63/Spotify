Enterprise Technology & Software

Good morning. It's Friday, November 6th, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

Thursday was a brutal tape for software, and the instinct is to call it a verdict on the AI trade. I don't think that's right. Palantir, CrowdStrike, and Okta each fell roughly ten percent, per 24/7 Wall St.'s market coverage. Only one of the three had reported anything this week. Atlassian is the exception. The market marked it down on its own numbers. Separating those two events is the spine of today's briefing, and it ends somewhere more useful than AI winners versus losers. It ends at seats versus consumption.

First, the tape. The Nasdaq Composite dropped five and a half percent on Thursday and closed in correction territory, according to Yahoo Finance. Chip stocks, in that outlet's words, took the brunt of the beating. On a session like that, a ten percent drop in a high-beta software name is close to what beta alone would produce. Nomura's Charlie McElligott said the primary catalyst wasn't a sudden deterioration in fundamentals. Vanda Research's Viraj Patel called it a total capitulation by retail investors. Others point to Tuesday's ballot results, where voters sided with data-center restrictions in nearly every contest, as The Throughline covered yesterday. Causation is contested. But none of those explanations is about any one software company's execution.

That's why Palantir's drop tells you little. Its Monday print was strong: revenue up ninety-six percent, U.S. commercial revenue overtaking U.S. government for the first time, and a raised full-year guide. The real question for Palantir is whether new bookings are refilling its pipeline as fast as that revenue is being recognized. Thursday doesn't answer it. Palantir fell in the same basket as companies with no news at all, so the answer waits for next quarter's bookings.

The security names tell the same story, and this is their second deflation in about a week. On October 28th, this show said the market was pricing security like a conclusion. CrowdStrike, Zscaler, and Palo Alto Networks had rallied double digits with no new bookings or guidance behind them. Then Fortinet reported. It raised full-year guidance for billings and total revenue, but lowered its full-year service revenue guidance below the prior range. Palo Alto and CrowdStrike fell in sympathy. On Thursday, CrowdStrike and Okta each fell roughly ten percent, with no earnings from either. For CrowdStrike, that's the bar resetting twice before it has even reported.

That changes what the coming prints have to prove. Yesterday we covered CrowdStrike agreeing to buy two AI security startups and launching an AI kill switch. After two resets, I'd expect that agent-security story to buy less credit when CrowdStrike reports in December. Investors are more likely to parse sub-metrics, the way they parsed Fortinet's service line. Thursday told you about positioning in these names, not about their businesses. But it raised the bar their numbers now have to clear.

Now to the stock that moved on its own numbers. Atlassian reported Thursday, and the quarter itself was strong. Subscription annual recurring revenue grew twenty-three percent year over year. Cloud revenue grew faster still, at thirty-one percent. But Atlassian guided full-year subscription ARR growth to about eighteen and a half percent. Per SiliconANGLE's coverage, the shares fell sharply on that slower forecast. Be careful with the comparison. Twenty-three is one quarter's year-over-year rate, and eighteen and a half is a full-year guide, so the windows differ. Still, the guide implies deceleration. That fits the pattern this show named on Monday: the market grading the forward commitment, not the quarter just delivered.

Look at what's carrying the business. Cloud is still the engine, growing about eight points faster than subscription ARR as a whole. The guide says that engine isn't expected to hold total ARR at this quarter's pace. There are two honest readings of why. One is a conservative full-year number that Atlassian has room to beat. The other is that seat-based software is maturing faster than AI add-ons can offset. Atlassian's release gave no figure on how its AI features, Rovo included, are monetizing, so there's no way yet to tell those readings apart.

One complication. An unconfirmed report says Atlassian had already guided to roughly this growth rate a quarter ago. If so, Thursday's reaction was less a fresh signal than a known number landing on a risk-off day. That would pull Atlassian partway back toward the beta story. Either way, the market is paying for forward growth in a seat-priced model, and Atlassian's own guide says that growth is slowing.

That leaves seat-priced vendors with a disclosure problem. If AI add-ons are supposed to offset seat maturation, investors will want to see them as a separate, quantified line. Atlassian's next quarter is the test of whether that line shows up in ARR at all. In seat-priced software, an AI story without its own disclosed number is starting to cost multiple.

If the doubt in software sits with seat-priced models, consumption tells a different story. Cloudflare's third-quarter revenue grew thirty-nine percent year over year. Per financial press summaries of the release, that's its fastest pace in nearly four years. That's the mirror image of Atlassian. It's acceleration, not deceleration, in a model where growth doesn't depend on adding seats. It also answers a watch item this show has carried all week. We'd asked whether usage-priced growth was starting to run into budget caps, like the hard AI spending caps Snowflake shipped on Tuesday. At Cloudflare, the answer for now is acceleration, not a cap.

Three caveats keep that honest. It's one company and one quarter. Snowflake's caps are too new to show up in a quarter that closed in September, so this is no sign yet, not the thesis refuted. And Cloudflare's growth isn't cleanly AI consumption. The reported figures don't break out how much of it comes from AI workloads. Datadog, the other half of that watch item, stays open for now. So far, the doubt in software looks like it sits with seats, not consumption.

And here's where that consumption is flowing. Menlo Ventures' enterprise AI report, which this show covered yesterday, has two more numbers worth banking. First, Anthropic and OpenAI together now take eighty-six percent of enterprise large-language-model API spend, up from sixty-seven percent a year ago. The standing disclosure applies: Anthropic makes the model behind this show. Menlo is also a venture investor with portfolio companies in this market, Anthropic among them. So treat that as a concentration of API dollars, not of enterprise adoption, and not as a neutral number. Second, on the OpenRouter platform, agents now generate eighty percent of all tokens. That's one platform, skewed toward developers and automated workloads. It's not a claim about all enterprise AI.

Put the two together, and here's my inference. Agents, not people, may be becoming the primary consumers of AI capacity. And the dollars at the model layer are concentrating in two labs. If both hold, the exposed business model is the one that charges by seat. A seat doesn't grow when an agent does the work.

That has a budget consequence. If agents drive consumption, enterprise AI budgets shift from per-user licenses toward metered usage. That favors vendors who bill on consumption. It also makes CFO controls like Snowflake's caps more central, because metered spend is exactly the kind finance wants a ceiling on. The thread under Atlassian's guide and Cloudflare's acceleration is the same one. The marginal AI dollar is metered, not seated.

Pull it together. Thursday's tape was mostly beta, a risk-off session that swept up strong operators and weak ones alike. Underneath it, the real discrimination was seats versus consumption, with Atlassian's guide on one side and Cloudflare's print on the other. Four things to watch. Palo Alto Networks reports later this month, and CrowdStrike is estimated for early December, both into a bar that has already reset. Watch Atlassian's next quarter for any disclosed AI revenue line. Read Datadog's numbers for whether they match Cloudflare's story or complicate it. And watch whether Snowflake-style caps start to show up in usage-priced vendors' guidance.

That's the briefing. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
