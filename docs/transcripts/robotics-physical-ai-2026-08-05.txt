Good morning. It's August 5th, 2026, and this is your robotics and physical AI briefing.

Industrial policy just moved to the front of the humanoid robot story. On July 29th, the FCC banned imports of new foreign-made humanoid robots, quadruped robots, and power inverters. As CBS News reported it, "The Federal Communications Commission is banning imports of new foreign-made humanoid robots and power inverters, citing national security risks, in a move that targets China." Two days later, Unitree confirmed it's proceeding with its Shanghai STAR Market IPO anyway. Book-building opened today, pricing lands tomorrow, and results publish August 14th.

Start with what the ban actually does, because the scope matters more than the headline. It only blocks new device models going forward — it doesn't touch robots already purchased or already authorized. FCC Chair Brendan Carr framed it as an effort to "secure America's critical supply chains." China's government pushed back hard: a foreign ministry spokesperson said "Protectionism does not make the U.S. more competitive, and it will only hurt the interests of U.S. companies and consumers." The reason this lands on China specifically is scale — CBS cites estimates that "China dominates with approximately 85% of the global humanoid robot market." Morgan Stanley puts China's humanoid market alone at $15 billion by 2030.

Meanwhile Unitree is moving forward with its listing regardless. It's selling 40.45 million shares — 10% of enlarged capital — targeting roughly 4.2 billion yuan raised. Citic Securities expects a post-listing valuation between 50.6 and 55.9 billion yuan, something like seven to eight billion dollars, six to twelve months out. That's a sell-side estimate, not a locked-in price — final pricing is tomorrow. Founder Wang Xingxing keeps outsized control post-listing: he and related parties hold 31.29% of shares but 65.31% of the voting rights. One caveat worth stating plainly: how much of Unitree's revenue actually depends on US sales is unresolved. The public disclosures conflict — different figures in different years — and the prospectus itself gives only an aggregate overseas-revenue line that never isolates the United States. So don't take this ban as a confirmed dent to Unitree's business yet. It's a policy signal aimed at a market Unitree may or may not need much.

Here's why the ban matters more as industrial policy than as a revenue threat, though: it's protecting a home market the US can't yet supply at anything like the same scale. AGIBOT, the Shanghai humanoid maker, rolled its 15,000th robot off the line on June 30th. Its own account calls it "not only an important milestone in Agibot's mass production and engineering delivery capabilities, but also a reflection of the broader industry's move toward scaled deployment in real-world settings." And the cadence is accelerating, not just growing — going from 5,000 to 10,000 units took AGIBOT three months, versus roughly a year to get from 1,000 to 5,000. That's better than a fourfold speedup in throughput. AGIBOT was already 2025's global shipment leader per Omdia, with 5,168 units and 39% market share.

Compare that to Figure AI, arguably the best-funded US humanoid challenger. Figure hit its 1,000th Figure 03 unit on July 23rd at its BotQ facility in California. That's real progress — Figure went from one unit a day to one unit an hour in under four months, and it reports end-of-line yield above 80% and 99.3% yield on its battery line. But set the two milestones side by side: fifteen thousand cumulative units in China versus one thousand in the US. That's roughly an order of magnitude, and it's the industrial substrate underneath this week's trade action. A couple of caveats here too — AGIBOT's count spans multiple product lines, not all full bipedal humanoids, so it's not a perfectly like-for-like comparison, and neither company has published verified data on how many of those units are actually working paid, revenue-generating shifts versus sitting in inventory or demo fleets. Units built is not units deployed. Still, the direction of the gap is the story: the US is using trade policy to protect a market its own manufacturers can't yet fill at volume, while a scale advantage that size compounds into something harder to legislate away — more units in the field means more real-world data flowing back into whoever's training the underlying models, a flywheel that outlasts any single benchmark comparison.

That gap also hands the US a bill it hasn't paid. China's dominance isn't only in finished robots. It runs deep into the actuators, motors, batteries, and rare-earth magnets underneath them. A ban on finished imports does nothing to close that, and it invites the obvious retaliation — China restricting the components and rare earths that US robotics and EV supply chains still lean on, right as a Trump-Xi summit is penciled in for September. Protecting the finished-goods market is the easy half. Standing up the supply chain beneath it is the half policy can't legislate overnight.

Now the domain where the US actually is pulling ahead: autonomous vehicles. Waymo dropped its rider waitlist in Dallas yesterday, August 4th, making the service open to anyone with the app. During the gated period that ran from February through early August, roughly 150,000 riders used the service — that's a usage count, not a safety metric, so don't read it as a disengagement or incident figure. Airport service to Dallas Love Field is still pending. Worth remembering that Waymo had to pause service across Dallas, Houston, San Antonio, and Atlanta back in May because of trouble handling heavy rain and flooded roads — and nothing so far confirms that underlying weather-handling limitation is actually fixed versus the service simply resuming. Still, going from a gated pilot to fully open public access is a real commercial milestone, and it's another data point on Waymo's stated push toward a million weekly rides. So while China leads on humanoid hardware volume, the US is ahead on converting autonomous driving from pilot to open commercial product.

Last thread: where US physical-AI value is being repriced fastest is defense autonomy. Anduril is reportedly in talks for a new funding round at around $100 billion — more than triple its mark from just two months ago, when it doubled to $61 billion on a $5 billion round led by Thrive Capital and Andreessen Horowitz. A year before that, in June 2025, it was valued at $30.5 billion. Anduril reported $2.2 billion in 2025 revenue. Run the math and a prospective $100 billion valuation would put a private drone-and-autonomy company in Northrop Grumman and Lockheed Martin territory on a small fraction of their revenue. Nothing here is confirmed — Anduril says no financing decision has been made, and the figure is sourced to people familiar with the talks, not a filing. But the pace of the step-ups, thirty-point-five billion to sixty-one billion to a proposed hundred billion in about fourteen months, raises the obvious question of down-round risk if a single major program gets delayed or lost.

Put it together and the map of embodied AI is now being drawn as much by industrial policy as by capability. China leads on humanoid hardware scale and is racing to convert that into a data and cost advantage. The US leads on autonomous-vehicle commercialization and on pricing defense autonomy at venture speed. The FCC's ban is a bet that policy can buy the US time the supply chain hasn't yet earned. Watch two dates for where this resolves next: Unitree's pricing tomorrow and its results on August 14th, and whether the FCC's rule holds up as durable, enforceable policy rather than a symbolic gesture with a wide exemption built in.
