Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Equity & the Deal Economy

Transcript · September 16, 2026

Good morning. It's Wednesday, September 16th, and this is your private equity briefing.

Apollo Global Management is in talks to buy Johnson and Johnson's DePuy Synthes orthopedics business. The reported price is close to twenty billion dollars. DePuy generated nine-point-three billion dollars in sales last year. That makes this the largest corporate carve-out to reach live negotiation in this cycle, and the largest healthcare buyout anyone has attempted in it.

Start with what we can and cannot say about the price. An analyst at GlobalData tracking the unit puts its run-rate revenue nearer ten billion dollars, and reads the reported twenty-billion figure as J&J pricing the business at roughly two times revenue. That is the one attributable pricing observation available, and it is a revenue multiple, not an earnings multiple. No verified figure exists for DePuy's operating profit or its margin. So nobody outside the room can say what this costs relative to what it earns. I went looking for a quotable enterprise-value estimate including debt, and could not stand one up. Figures above twenty billion are circulating. None of them survive sourcing, so I am not going to repeat them. The talks are private, and reporting says an agreement could come within several weeks. Nothing is signed, other sponsors are reportedly circling, and J&J retains the option to spin the unit off publicly instead. So this may never become a private equity deal at all.

Treat it as a test case regardless, because of what it says about the buy side. Apollo's flagship, Fund the Eleventh, has been raising since February against a target of twenty-two to twenty-five billion dollars. Private Equity International's own headline describes it as roughly halfway to that target. So the firm negotiating the biggest healthcare buyout of the cycle is doing it while its main vehicle for writing checks that size is still mid-raise. That does not mean the deal cannot get done. Sponsors have other levers — club partners, heavy co-investment, balance-sheet and insurance capital. I want to be precise here. No consortium, no co-investment structure, and no committed financing package has actually been reported for this transaction. I went looking for one, and it is not there. So the honest statement is that entry capacity and fundraising capacity are two separate questions, and this deal is currently answering only the first.

Here is the part that matters for anyone waiting on cash. A twenty-billion-dollar carve-out does not shrink this industry's backlog of unsold assets. It adds to it. J&J is not a sponsor distributing proceeds to limited partners. It is a strategic seller cleaning up its own balance sheet. So a signed deal creates a new asset that somebody's fund has to exit later, and produces a realization for nobody today. Hold that against the number this show ran yesterday: median distributions-to-paid-in on 2022-vintage funds at roughly nine cents on the dollar. Deployment capacity is not this cycle's constraint, and it has not been for a while.

One more beat on this deal before moving on, and it is a cross-topic one. Yesterday's private credit briefing put healthcare providers near nine-point-nine percent on Fitch's trailing private credit default index. That is tied with industrials for the worst sector in the book, against software at zero-point-six percent. Apollo would be underwriting the cycle's largest healthcare buyout directly into that cohort. This is not a prediction that the deal fails. Orthopedic implants are a different business from the healthcare-services borrowers driving that default rate. But it is a flag on financing terms and downside protection, and it belongs in anyone's diligence memo on this one. The rate backdrop is also genuinely unresolved as I speak. The Federal Reserve's decision lands at two o'clock this afternoon, after this briefing airs. I am treating it strictly as scheduled, with no outcome assumed. So any read on financing costs for a deal this size is pending, not decided.

So if that is what the buy side can still assemble, the next question is who can actually raise capital right now. The answer is fewer firms, and more slowly. PitchBook's first-half figures show the twenty largest funds took in a hundred and seventy-one billion dollars, more than half of everything closed in the period. Funds of a billion dollars or larger accounted for seventy-eight-point-two percent of capital raised, up from fifty-nine-point-one percent in 2021. Two disciplines on those numbers. Capital closed is committed, not called or deployed. And PitchBook's own framing of the half-year is bleak, which sits oddly next to a concentration chart that looks healthy. So read the barbell as a description of who is closing, not as a verdict on the asset class's health.

There is also a correction owed here to this show's own framing. We have spent the week reading fundraising slippage at individual firms — Apollo's Fund XI most of all — as limited partners passing judgment on distributions. PitchBook's data complicates that. Funds raising over five billion dollars in the first half of this year spent more than three times as many months on the road as similarly sized funds did five years ago. Three times. If the biggest, best-branded vehicles in the business all now need that much longer to close, the slippage looks structural across the whole mega-fund cohort rather than a distributions verdict on any one manager. The concentration is real and so is the slowdown, and they are not the same fact. Totals can look concentrated and healthy on a headline chart while the process underneath gets harder for everyone. That is the difference between capital committed and capital conviction, and it should change how you read every fund-close press release for the rest of this cycle.

Now turn to the exit side, where the binding constraint actually sits. Thoma Bravo agreed last month to take Accelerant, an insurance marketplace platform, private in an all-cash deal worth more than four billion dollars. The price is twenty dollars and twenty-five cents a share, a forty-nine percent premium to the prior close. Accelerant had listed on the New York Stock Exchange only about a year earlier. Two caveats before the point. The deal is agreed, not closed, with completion expected in the first half of 2027. And the founders and Altamont Capital intend to roll equity alongside Thoma Bravo, so this is partly a recapitalization rather than a clean change of hands. A forty-nine percent premium also means public shareholders were paid well, so this is not a story about value destroyed. It is a story about durability. A company that round-trips back into private ownership inside roughly twelve months of listing is evidence that at least part of this cycle's reopened IPO window was shallow rather than structural. Any limited partner counting a recent listing as proof the exit backlog is clearing should mark that down.

Pull it together and the cycle stands where it has stood all week. The entry side works. A twenty-billion-dollar healthcare carve-out can reach live negotiation in a matter of days. The two sides that actually return cash to limited partners are both rationing. Fundraising is rationing by time, forcing even the strongest brands onto a road three times longer than it was five years ago. Exits are rationing by depth, with at least one recent listing already reversing back into sponsor hands. Watch three things from here. Whether Apollo and J&J sign inside that several-week window, and in what structure. Whether J&J takes the spin-off path instead, which would take the asset out of private equity entirely. And the late-October third-quarter manager prints, read for realization pace rather than for fee-related earnings. Until one of those lands, deployment capacity keeps outrunning realization capacity, and that gap is the story.

That's your briefing for September 16th. Talk to you tomorrow.
