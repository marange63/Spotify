Cautious Optimism Briefings

AI in Biopharma R&D

Transcript · August 23, 2026

Good morning. It's Sunday, August 23rd, 2026, and this is your briefing on AI in biopharma R&D.

All week this beat has been asking one question: who actually buys an AI-designed molecule, and at what price? This week the first half of that question got an answer. The second half did not.

Start with Chai Discovery. On August 19th and 20th, Chai announced a collaboration with Bristol Myers Squibb, applying its molecular folding and design models to antibody discovery. Chai's own release calls Bristol Myers Squibb, quote, "the latest biopharmaceutical company to collaborate with Chai Discovery." Latest, because Pfizer got there first. Pfizer licensed Chai's technology on June 4th, with early access to Chai's then-unreleased Chai-3 model, plus a version tuned to Pfizer's own workflows. So in about eleven weeks, Chai went from one named big-pharma partner to two.

On Thursday, this beat carried Chai's de novo antibody benchmarks as raw capability with no named buyer attached. It now has two. One nuance to hold: the benchmarks we discussed Thursday were for Chai-2, and what Pfizer licensed was the newer Chai-3, a separate model generation. The commercial claim still stands. Two of the largest drugmakers in the world have now signed for this capability, and that is the thing the design layer has been missing — repeat demand rather than a single pilot.

Here's the hinge. Neither the Pfizer release in June nor the Bristol Myers Squibb release this week contains a dollar figure. No deal value, no milestones, no royalty structure. Be precise about what that is and isn't. Neither company published a sentence saying terms were withheld; the releases simply omit any numbers at all. And "collaboration" is not the same word as "purchase" — we do not know that Bristol Myers Squibb is paying anything up front. Either way the practical effect is the same. Pharma is acquiring this capability, and nobody outside the deal knows what it costs. There is no comparable to benchmark one antibody-design agreement against another.

So if the market can't price AI design directly, where did a price actually move this month? Not on a molecule AI designed. On one it didn't.

AbCellera, the antibody-discovery company built around large-scale computational screening, had two catalysts in the past four weeks. On July 29th came a collaboration with Vertex Pharmaceuticals on multispecific T-cell engagers for autoimmune disease. Twenty-eight million dollars in total upfront payments to AbCellera, plus milestones and tiered royalties, with Vertex funding all the R&D. Then, on August 10th, positive Phase 2 topline data for AbCellera's own candidate, ABCL635, in menopausal vasomotor symptoms. In ninety-two postmenopausal women, a single 600-milligram dose cut moderate-to-severe hot-flash frequency by 83 percent at week four, against 33 percent on placebo. Severity fell 58 percent, against 12 percent. Keep the scale in view. That is topline data, in fewer than a hundred women, at one timepoint four weeks out, after a single dose. AbCellera's shares moved sharply higher on that bundle of catalysts.

Now the reversal, and it's the point of the whole item. AbCellera's own release describes ABCL635 as, quote, "AbCellera's first pipeline program from the GPCR and ion channel platform to advance into the clinic." Not generative chemistry. That release does not use the words AI, machine learning, or generative design anywhere in connection with where the molecule came from. So the one AI-native name that actually got repriced this month was repriced on an asset the company itself does not call AI-designed, while the deals that would validate the AI-design thesis carry no price at all.

Sit with that inversion, because it has a valuation consequence. If design deals keep closing without disclosed economics, the design-platform cohort gets valued the only way that's left — on its own clinical readouts. Which means these companies get priced as ordinary biotech option baskets no matter how good their models get. AbCellera is the working example of that happening already. And it means the equity signal coming off these tickers tells you close to nothing about AI progress in the underlying science.

If the market won't price AI design, ask what pharma itself will put a number on. That's Roche. In this Q2 earnings season, chief executive Thomas Schinecker gave the only quantified AI framework any major pharma CEO put on the record, and he built it in three tiers. Tier one, everyday productivity: roughly 100,000 employees trained, about 75 percent of the workforce using AI tools daily. Tier two, core process optimization: regulatory filing timelines compressed from months to roughly a week. Tier three — the "big ideas" tier, the lab-in-the-loop work meant to raise the actual volume of new molecules entering the pipeline — carries no number at all.

That asymmetry is the tell. Roche can quantify adoption and paperwork speed. It cannot yet quantify whether AI is producing more or better drug candidates. Two pieces of discipline on that. Filing timelines means preparing the submission, not the regulator's review clock, so nothing here says the FDA or the EMA is moving faster. And this commentary reaches us through a secondary roundup published Friday, not the Roche call transcript itself. Novartis, AstraZeneca and GSK all discussed AI this season without giving figures, so treat Roche as one data point, not a league table.

It also fits a pattern that has been running five days on this beat. Novo Nordisk's adoption count across more than 25,000 employees on Tuesday. Recursion's claim of faster patient enrollment out of agentic workflows on Friday. Now Roche's months-to-a-week filing compression. Each claim is larger than the last, and not one of them carries an outside audit or a named baseline. These are executive characterizations, not validated benchmarks. Sanofi's Belen Garijo supplied the counterweight this season, refusing to commit to any specific number of late-stage pipeline discontinuations, saying the decisions will rest on, quote, "scientific needs, potential to create long-term value, and obviously ensuring the right risk profile." AI-era R&D is running alongside ordinary portfolio triage, not replacing it.

That one quantified win — filing speed — now has a regulator standing on it. The EU AI Act's high-risk provisions took effect on August 2nd, and compliance advisers read them as potentially classifying some drug-development AI as high-risk. Where exactly that line falls is unresolved. Nobody yet knows where internal discovery tooling ends and regulatory-facing tooling begins, and regulatory-facing tooling is precisely the filing machinery Roche is touting. So the fastest-moving, most auditable AI win in the industry is also the one sitting closest to the newest compliance burden. If the line gets drawn broadly, expect sponsors to wall this work off as pure internal research, or push it toward US-based development.

The same scale-versus-substance gap shows up on the data side. Tempus's TIME network used algorithmic screening plus nurse review to scan more than 1.28 million patients across 94 trials in a single year for trial eligibility. That screening produced 573 consents. Flag the provenance there: those figures come from an ASCO-linked analysis whose primary abstract I could not reach, so hold them more loosely than the rest of this briefing. But the shape is what matters. Scale at the top of the funnel is real. Scale at the bottom, meaning actual enrollment, is not. And on the larger real-world-data promise — pointing AI at messy electronic health records to surface a genuinely new indication or confirm an epidemiological estimate — there is still no dated 2026 case to hold up. That gap, not model capability, is what currently bounds the real-world-data argument for cheaper trials.

Pull the threads together. The clinical scorecard ends where it did yesterday: as of mid-2026, no AI-discovered drug holds full FDA approval. Against that backdrop, this week produced two design deals with no price attached, a re-rating on an asset that isn't AI-designed, and one CEO's filing-speed claim that just became a compliance question. The honest read is that AI value keeps sliding down the stack, away from generating molecules and toward operations — writing, filing, screening — precisely because that is the only layer anyone will attach a number to. Watch whether the next antibody-design deal, Chai's or a rival's, finally discloses terms. That is the signal that tells you whether this capability has a market, or just users.

That's the briefing. Have a good Sunday.
