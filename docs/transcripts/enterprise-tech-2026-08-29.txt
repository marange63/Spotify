Good morning. It's Saturday, August 29th, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

All week the pattern in enterprise software earnings was simple: beat, then rally. On Friday that pattern broke. SentinelOne beat on revenue and its stock fell eight percent. CrowdStrike fell about seven percent alongside it, two days after what its chief executive called the best quarter in the company's history. The instinct is to read that as the market finally pricing a margin problem in AI-era cybersecurity. Look at what SentinelOne actually filed, though, and that reading does not hold up. This is a story about a headline number spooking a sector. It is not a story about disclosed economics getting worse.

Start with SentinelOne itself. Second-quarter revenue came in at two hundred ninety-two million dollars, up twenty and a half percent year over year. That was a beat. What moved the stock was the full-year guide. SentinelOne cut its full-year non-GAAP earnings-per-share guidance from a range of thirty-two to thirty-eight cents down to thirty to thirty-two cents. That is roughly an eleven percent cut at the midpoint, and that is the number the market reacted to.

Now read the rest of the same guidance table. In that same release, SentinelOne raised its full-year revenue guidance, from just over one point one nine billion dollars to just over one point two billion. It also raised its full-year non-GAAP operating income guidance, from a range of one hundred fifteen to one hundred twenty-five million dollars up to a range of one hundred twenty-four to one hundred twenty-eight million. The company's own chief financial officer put it this way: "We exceeded all top and bottom-line guided metrics, achieved record profitability and are raising our revenue and operating income outlook for fiscal year 2027." Set that against an eleven-percent cut to per-share guidance and you have an apparent contradiction.

The way to resolve it is share count, not cost structure. When operating income guidance rises and per-share guidance falls in the same release, the gap is arithmetic. The denominator grew. The reported explanation is a higher expected diluted share count, tied to SentinelOne's own stock-price appreciation feeding equity compensation, plus foreign-exchange effects on international assets and liabilities. That explanation comes from secondary coverage, not from a company statement I can quote, so treat it as the best available reading rather than a confirmed one. But it fits the numbers SentinelOne did disclose far better than a story about AI detection costs eating margin does. The plain takeaway: there was no disclosed margin break here. There was a per-share number that fell for reasons that have very little to do with how the business performed.

That distinction got lost fast, and CrowdStrike paid for it. The reporting describes Friday's seven percent decline as a sympathy move, with no company-specific news behind it. Worth re-grounding CrowdStrike briefly for anyone who missed the print. Second-quarter revenue was one point four seven billion dollars, up twenty-six percent. Annual recurring revenue reached five point eight four billion, up twenty-five percent, and Falcon Flex, the company's bundled consumption contract, now carries over two point two nine billion dollars in ending ARR, roughly double a year ago. Reported figures for CrowdStrike's own full-year guidance point the same direction as SentinelOne's operating line, upward — though I could not trace those specific guidance numbers to the company's release, so take the direction and not the decimals. Nothing in CrowdStrike's disclosure changed on Friday. Sentiment toward the cohort did.

There is still a real gap in what CrowdStrike tells you, and Friday is exactly when it starts to bite. Chief executive George Kurtz has credited Anthropic's release of its advanced Mythos model with accelerating security demand. His words: "Ever since Mythos, we have seen growth in our business, not measured by meetings or calls, but measured by ARR, and we don't see the threat landscape subsiding." That is a demand-side claim from an earnings call, with no bookings broken out by cause anywhere in the release. Treat it as company narrative. And notice what sits on the other side of it. CrowdStrike discloses ARR, net-new ARR and Falcon Flex adoption. Those are all growth metrics. It discloses nothing about what it costs to serve AI-native detection at scale. A demand metric with no matching cost metric cannot answer the question the market started asking on Friday.

The same disconnect between narrative and disclosure showed up two days earlier, running the other way. Salesforce's Claudeforce announcement and guidance raise sent the stock sharply higher on Wednesday night. ServiceNow and Adobe rallied alongside it on Thursday with no company-specific news of their own. So: two sector-wide repricings inside forty-eight hours, one up and one down, and in neither case did the move track what the affected companies actually disclosed.

Salesforce's own numbers are worth revisiting through that lens, because they carry a similar defect in a different place. Second-quarter revenue was eleven point three billion dollars, up eleven percent, and the company raised full-year revenue guidance to a range of forty-six point one to forty-six point four billion. Agentforce annual recurring revenue exceeded one point five billion dollars, up more than two hundred forty percent. That growth is real, but note two things about it. ARR is an annualized run-rate defined by Salesforce, not recognized revenue. And one and a half billion against a forty-six billion dollar revenue guide is about three percent of the company. Here is the part that matters for earnings quality. Of Salesforce's five dollars and ninety cents in non-GAAP earnings per share, two dollars and fifty-three cents came from a mark-up on its equity stake in Anthropic. That stake is marked against an implied valuation of nine hundred sixty-five billion dollars, set in a funding round back in May. By one outside calculation, strip the gain out and adjusted earnings per share was closer to three dollars and thirty-seven cents.

Put the two prints side by side and the symmetry is the whole point. SentinelOne's per-share number fell for reasons unrelated to its operating business. Salesforce's per-share number rose for reasons unrelated to its operating business. In both cases the market traded the per-share line as though it described software.

There are two consequences worth naming. The first is for anyone holding the stocks. Salesforce's gain is an unrealized mark on a private, illiquid stake, and realizing it depends on a liquidity event that has not happened. If more incumbents take equity in the AI labs they partner with as the price of preferential access, one valuation move at one lab hits several income statements in the same quarter. That is a correlated exposure sitting inside numbers that look like operating results. The second is for anyone signing a contract. The sector is migrating toward committed-consumption and outcome pricing — Falcon Flex is the template. A buyer signing one of those envelopes is contracting against a vendor cost base for AI features that no vendor in this cohort has disclosed. If that cost base moves, the pass-through mechanism is already built into the contract.

So the falsifiable question going forward is not whether AI adds revenue to enterprise software. This week answered that, with real ARR growth at both Salesforce and CrowdStrike. The question is whether the market can distinguish a per-share number moving on dilution, currency or a private mark from a per-share number moving because the underlying business changed. On Friday, it could not. Zscaler is scheduled to report on September 3rd, after this recording, and that print is the next chance to find out whether investors read the guidance table before they read the headline.
