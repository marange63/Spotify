Good morning, it's Sunday, August 23rd, and this is the Capital Markets Radar.

We owe you a correction to our own thinking. For five straight days this show has told you that the long-end selloff was a term-premium story and a fiscal-credibility story, and explicitly not a growth or Fed-expectations story. Friday's data argues the other side. S&P Global's flash Composite PMI jumped to 56.0 in August, the fastest pace of US business growth since April of 2022. The survey now points to annualized third-quarter growth approaching 3.0 percent, against the 1.5 percent pace of the second quarter. That mapping from survey to GDP is S&P Global's own, not an independent forecaster's. It is still the first real evidence this week for a story we have spent the week dismissing.

But look at the internals before you buy the headline. This is entirely a services print. Manufacturing actually fell to 53.2, missing both the prior month and consensus. So the whole composite beat rests on one series, not two. And here is the part that should make you skeptical of the reflexive hawkish read. The fastest growth in four years arrived alongside the slowest input-cost inflation since February, and the weakest prices charged since November. Fast growth with decelerating price pressure is the signature of a positive supply shock, not an overheating economy. That combination argues against the market pricing a hotter Fed path, not for it. So the PMI is real, but it is not obviously hawkish.

Take that supply-shock read seriously for a moment, because it has a real market implication. If third-quarter growth is genuinely running near three percent, the market's implied neutral rate has to move higher. That lifts the whole curve independent of anything the Fed actually does in September. A hold at current levels stops looking accommodative and starts looking like passive tightening. For equities, a growth-driven yield backup is a completely different animal from a premium-driven one. It undercuts the rate-cut leg of this year's bull case and reinforces the earnings leg. That split is the classic setup for leadership to rotate out of long-duration AI and growth names and into cyclicals and financials. It would also mean the tight high-yield spread we have flagged for five straight runs is being vindicated by fundamentals rather than excused by complacency. That is the bull case for taking the PMI at face value. Now here is why we cannot yet.

Here is what we were actually looking at last week when we called the yield move fiscal rather than growth. That was yesterday's lead. Treasury doubled its long-bond buyback size, from two billion to four billion dollars per operation. Secretary Bessent told CNBC that Treasury was, in his words, signaling here to show that we believe yields don't reflect the underlying fundamentals. The relief lasted under 48 hours. By Friday the 10-year was back near 4.73 percent and the 30-year back above 5.2, erasing the entire announcement-day decline. And those buyback operations do not even begin until September 9th, so nothing has actually been bought yet.

The honest test for which story is right is whether that yield backup is real yields or breakevens. Real yields rising means growth. Breakevens rising means fiscal and oil. We went looking for that decomposition and came back with half of it. The 10-year TIPS real yield rose to 2.39 percent on Friday, up four basis points on the day. So real yields were moving too, not just inflation compensation, and that lends some support to the growth read. But we could not source a clean pre-buyback baseline, so we cannot tell you how much of the move since midweek was real rates versus breakevens. Treat that decomposition as genuinely open.

And there is a bigger wrinkle we have to be straight with you about. The Atlanta Fed's GDPNow model is a nowcast built on hard data as it lands, not a survey of sentiment. It put third-quarter growth at 4.03 percent on August 18th, down from 4.31 percent on August 14th. That is still far above the second quarter's 1.5 percent pace. But it was decelerating in the weeks before the flash PMI, not accelerating into it. The survey turned up over the same stretch in which the hard-data model was being marked down. So this is not clean corroboration across two sources. It is one survey rising while one nowcast cools, and the next GDPNow update later this week is the first thing that can break the tie.

Now to the variable that decides which way the inflation side of that decomposition goes. Oil resolved a question we flagged twice as attribution rather than documented fact. Bessent told CNBC that Washington will impose the toughest sanctions in history against Iran, echoing President Trump's threat of a crushing economic operation aimed at Iran's trading partners. That cause is now on the record. Brent settled Friday at 94 dollars and 39 cents, up 6.39 percent on the week, with West Texas Intermediate settling at 87 dollars and 6 cents. Here is the so-what. No sanctions have actually been imposed. This is a rhetoric-driven rally against barrels that are still being pumped, which makes it the most reversible piece of the inflation story on the board. It is also the piece most likely to make the PMI's benign input costs stale, because the survey period largely predates the sanctions leg of the oil move. Layer a real growth acceleration under a fresh oil shock and the Fed's September decision stops being a formality. It becomes two-sided. And it lands three weeks after Warsh speaks at Jackson Hole, not before it, which matters for how that speech gets read.

If growth were really the whole story, the debasement trade should have flinched on Friday's print. It didn't. The dollar index traded near 98.8 and was tracking a loss of about one percent on the week. Gold held above 4,600 dollars an ounce. And crypto extended through Saturday rather than fading, with Bitcoin closing at 78,335 dollars, up 7.26 percent on the day and capping its strongest week since 2024. Ethereum did better still, up 8.12 percent. Those crypto figures come from a single secondary tracker and were not cross-checked against on-chain data providers, so hold them loosely. Issuer concentration in the ETF flow has not broadened either, with BlackRock's IBIT still taking the large majority of it. The point is the direction, not the decimals. A hot growth print should be dollar-positive and gold-negative. It was neither. Either the market does not fully believe the growth read, or something other than growth is setting the dollar and gold.

That leaves both readings alive, and high-yield spreads near their richest levels in years are consistent with either one. So find the thing that can falsify it. If the debasement complex is really pricing fiscal risk, a growth print this strong should have hurt it, and it did not, which says the fiscal story is intact for now. If instead it is pricing nothing more than liquidity and risk appetite, a hawkish Warsh should hurt it badly. Kevin Warsh delivers his first Jackson Hole keynote as Fed chair this Friday, August 28th. He walks into a growth print he has not yet responded to. Watch whether he treats that PMI as an overheating economy or as a positive supply shock. That single read will tell you more about where the long end and the debasement trade go next than anything in this week's data. We do not have the clean decomposition today, and we are not going to pretend we do. But you now know exactly what to watch, and why it prices every asset we just walked through.

That's the Capital Markets Radar for August 23rd. Trade safe, and we'll see you tomorrow.
