Good morning. It's September 24th, 2026, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

A word on freshness before we start. None of today's material is from the last three days. Everything here is between ten days and a month old, and none of it has run on this show before. So treat this as a re-read of the memory story with new numbers, not as breaking news.

On the 19th, this show asked a pointed question. Was the market capitalizing a memory scarcity rent as if it were a permanent business model? It turns out the largest supplier of high-bandwidth memory had already answered, in coverage published on the 16th. SK hynix says the memory shortage gets worse next year. It also says demand could outstrip supply beyond 2030, even as it brings new capacity online. That answer is: permanent. Today's job is deciding whether to believe the company making the claim.

Start with the evidence for the structural case. KB Securities, in a September 7th note relayed by Digitimes, put memory inventory at Samsung and SK hynix below ten days of supply. By the same estimate, SK hynix's own inventory sits at about ten days. That is a broker's estimate, not a company disclosure. Even so, it is a fraction of the cushion this industry normally carries. On the 19th, the best we could say was that supplier inventories were at historic lows. Now there is a number.

The Digitimes report ties that squeeze to AI spending hitting three products at once: HBM, server DRAM and enterprise SSDs. HBM4 is the pressure point. It claims a disproportionate share of DRAM wafer capacity. So every wafer steered to HBM4 is commodity server DRAM that never gets made. Two earlier data points fit the same picture, and both ran here on the 19th. Nvidia's finance chief said memory prices had run ahead of the company's own expectations. And the same shortage repriced Huawei's AI accelerators. Put simply, the structural case is this. Buyers are paying more, suppliers are holding almost nothing, and the leading supplier says it stays that way past the end of the decade.

Now the case for cyclical, and it starts with the source. SK hynix is the party with the most to gain if buyers believe scarcity is permanent. That matters most right now, while 2027 memory contracts are being negotiated. Its 2030 view is a supplier's forecast of its own market. It deserves the same discount this show applied on the 22nd to Micron's forecast of a hundred-billion-dollar HBM market. And it reaches us through secondary coverage, not through a filing.

The second problem is the metric itself. Supplier inventory tells you where memory sits. It does not tell you how much memory exists. And there is quoted evidence that some of it has moved to the buyers. An analysis from SHI, written for data-center buyers, says hyperscalers pulled DRAM purchases forward and stockpiled inventory. Their fear was that waiting would mean competing for constrained supply later. SHI attributes the initial price spike, in the fourth quarter of last year, to exactly that stockpiling and pull-forward. It also cites reports that Samsung, SK hynix and Micron are asking customers for more detail on their orders and demand, specifically to curb stockpiling.

Think about what that last point means. When suppliers have to ask customers what they really need, the suppliers themselves suspect some of the demand is buffer-building. Thin supplier inventory is consistent with a genuine capacity wall. It is equally consistent with inventory migrating into buyer stockpiles. That second pattern is the classic late-cycle signature in memory, and it unwinds quickly once buyers' buffers are full. Today's evidence cannot separate the two readings. The counter-signal to watch is capex. If memory makers answer their own shortage-past-2030 call by accelerating fab spending, the structural story starts undermining itself. Nobody has put a number on that yet. So here is where the memory item lands. The shortage is real today. Structural is a claim, not yet a finding.

Now look at what's being scheduled upstream of that memory. TrendForce reported on September 14th that TSMC is targeting a 22 percent jump in 2-nanometer capacity by the middle of 2027. That takes it from 90,000 wafers a month at the end of this year to 110,000. Three-nanometer is on a similar path. It rises more than 16 percent over the same window, from above 180,000 wafers a month to 210,000. These figures come from TrendForce's supply-chain checks, not from TSMC guidance, and no capex figure comes with them. They sit on top of the packaging expansion this show led with on the 22nd, so I won't restate those numbers.

Two points on what those targets do and don't tell you. First, capacity is not allocation. My inference, not a reported fact, is that an early 2-nanometer ramp mostly serves the lead customers, Apple, Nvidia and AMD. That leaves smaller fabless designers further back in the queue. Second, and more important for today's argument, is the pull on memory. To the extent those extra wafers become AI accelerators, each one needs its own allotment of HBM. So a faster logic ramp doesn't relieve the memory squeeze. It deepens it.

That isn't a claim that memory is now the single bottleneck. As we said yesterday, memory and packaging are co-binding. The difference is visibility. TSMC's logic and packaging capacity is growing on a published calendar. Memory is the input whose leading supplier says it cannot close the gap.

If supply is the limit, efficiency is the other way to buy compute. That brings us to OpenAI's Jalapeño inference chip, co-designed with Broadcom. It was benchmarked on SemiAnalysis's InferenceX platform, in results relayed by secondary coverage on August 25th. So this is a month-old result, context rather than news. The comparison was against Nvidia's GB200 and GB300 systems, on three models: GPT-OSS 120B, DeepSeek R1 and Kimi K2.5. Jalapeño delivered 1.5 to 1.9 times more AI work per watt. It also posted 1.7 to 3.6 times lower end-to-end latency.

You may see far larger multiples in circulation, above fifty times on one measure. That figure comes from a narrow operating point. At matched, very high per-user decode speeds, a GPU's batch-throughput advantage collapses, so the ratio balloons. It describes one interactive regime, not fleet economics.

And this is benchmark leadership, not production leadership. No deployment volume has been disclosed. The chip was built for OpenAI's own workload mix, so it should do well on workloads like these. And the comparison is against Nvidia's Blackwell generation. Nvidia says production shipments of Rubin begin this fall, so Rubin, not Blackwell, is what Jalapeño will face in the field.

Still, the direction matters for the memory question. If custom silicon extracts materially more work per watt on real workloads, part of inference demand shifts. It moves from being constrained by chip availability to being constrained by economics. That is the structural demand-side force that could prove SK hynix's 2030 call wrong. Serving the same output with fewer, more efficient systems bends the demand curve for everything inside them. One caution: nothing in the reporting describes Jalapeño's own memory configuration, so don't assume it needs less HBM per chip.

So here's the picture. Logic and packaging are growing on a visible schedule. Memory is where the leading supplier tells you, on the record, that supply may never fully catch demand. If SK hynix is right, the memory bill stays inflated for everyone who buys it. That means accelerator vendors, hyperscaler custom-chip programs, and PC and phone makers alike. If SK hynix is wrong, the downside sits with investors paying structural multiples for a cyclical peak. Three things will decide it. The first is whether memory makers back their forecast by accelerating capacity spending. The second is whether customer-side inventory data shows buffers still being built, or being drawn down. And the third is Micron's fiscal fourth-quarter report, the next real read on where pricing power sits.

That's the state of the chip world for September 24th. Thanks for listening, and I'll see you next time.
