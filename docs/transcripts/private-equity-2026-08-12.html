Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Equity & the Deal Economy

Transcript · August 12, 2026

Good morning. It's Wednesday, August 12th, and this is your private equity briefing.

Q2 earnings season just closed across the big listed managers, and the headline numbers are the strongest complication yet to the drought narrative this show has been building all week. KKR posted fee-related earnings per share of a dollar thirty-two, up 34% year over year, and called it the largest monetization quarter in the firm's history — a record $848 million in realized performance income. Its fundraising machine ran just as hot: KKR pulled in $133 billion over the trailing twelve months, an all-time high for the firm, and blew through its three-year, $300 billion target in two and a half years. Co-CEO Scott Nuttall put a name on what's happening. He called the industry "K-shaped," with most of the outside attention on the unhappy leg of that letter while KKR sits on the happy one. Blackstone, Carlyle and Apollo all confirm the pattern. Blackstone's distributable earnings per share rose 26% to a dollar fifty-two, with net realizations up 27% to $414 million. Carlyle posted record fee-related earnings of $358 million and record transaction fees that more than doubled year over year, and its distributable earnings of a dollar-oh-seven a share beat the ninety-one cents the street expected. Apollo's fee-related earnings hit a record $785 million, on assets under management now around one-and-five-hundredths of a trillion dollars. So for five straight days this show has argued that realized cash isn't coming back to limited partners. This is the first real data that pushes back — but only at the very top of the industry.

Here's the distinction that matters, and it's one the managers themselves aren't making for you. Fee-related earnings are a fee annuity. They compound off assets under management regardless of whether anything actually exits. Apollo's fee income sits alongside $877 million of spread-related earnings, and that's an insurance-balance-sheet business, not a buyout-exit business. At Blackstone, private equity contributed only 44.6% of segment distributable earnings — the rest is real estate, credit, and infrastructure. So when a manager says "record earnings," the question to ask is which earnings. I went back to KKR's and Blackstone's own Q2 transcripts to find a cleaner number: a fund-level distributions-to-paid-in figure, or a realized-carry number that isolates the buyout franchise specifically. Neither company disclosed one. KKR management told analysts its exits were, in their words, "diversified across strategies, regions and asset type," with realized multiples ranging from two to twenty times invested capital. That's management's own framing, and it cuts against a too-clean story that this was all engineered liquidity. But KKR never itemized that $848 million by exit channel — strategic sale, IPO, continuation vehicle, or dividend recap. That's worth naming as a real finding, not just an absence. The industry can claim record monetization as a headline while withholding the one breakdown that would tell you whether it's genuine liquidity or capital moving between vehicles the same manager controls. Blackstone, for its part, is still sitting on $7.5 billion of accrued but unrealized performance revenue, firm-wide, and that stock grew 13% year over year — faster than the realizations it actually cashed out this quarter. The pile of unbanked carry is still building faster than it's being converted to cash.

Now look at how some of that monetization is actually being manufactured, because two deals this week show the mechanics directly. MetLife Investment Management closed a $1.2 billion managed secondary sale, anchored by funds run by Lexington Partners, moving roughly $754 million of private equity, venture, and co-investment positions — nearly eighty of them — off an insurer's own balance sheet, with Evercore advising. And in Europe, mid-market sponsor Exponent closed a seven-hundred-fifty-million-euro single-asset continuation vehicle for H&MV Engineering, an Irish electrical-engineering firm serving data centers, valuing the company at one-point-four billion euros. That one was co-led by Apollo's own secondaries arm, S3, alongside Pantheon and SQ Capital. Notice the reflexivity. Apollo shows up in the earnings story as a record-fee manager, and here again as the firm monetizing through a secondaries channel it also runs. An insurer selling to a Lexington-anchored vehicle, or a sponsor rolling a winning asset into a continuation fund its own co-lead helps price, is a murkier liquidity signal than a genuine arm's-length sale to a stranger. It's cash changing hands, but it isn't necessarily the market clearing.

That's the unhappy leg of Nuttall's K, and the aggregate data backs it up. Continuation vehicles have stopped being an emergency valve and become standard playbook: 83% of the top hundred global buyout sponsors have now used one, and repeat issuers generated 58% of last year's volume. There's a further wrinkle the earnings headlines skip. The NAV loans increasingly stacked inside these vehicles sit structurally senior to the limited partners' own equity, so the LP now stands behind fresh leverage on assets it used to own outright. Jefferies thinks the secondaries market could run near $300 billion a year within a couple of years if exit windows stay shut — a projection, not a print, but it tells you which valve the industry is betting on. Even some of the buyers are uneasy: New York City's pension system has started warning publicly that stacking continuation vehicles on top of continuation vehicles makes general partners look, in its word, "desperate." Meanwhile industry-wide distributions ran at roughly 6% of buyout assets under management in the year to last June, against a ten-year average closer to 14%. Fund close rates fell to 57% last year from 94% in 2020. That's the gap between what five scaled managers just reported and what the other few thousand general partners are actually living through.

So where does the cycle stand. The mega-manager prints are real, and they're not a fluke. Fee income and fundraising machinery at the very largest platforms are genuinely thriving — KKR's trailing $133 billion and Carlyle's $56 billion of inflows over the last year prove capital still wants exposure to brand-name scale. But that outperformance is concentrating capital at the top and starving the middle of the market. And even at the top, the industry still won't show you the one number — realized carry by exit channel, isolated from fees — that would prove the traditional buyout exit is actually healing rather than being routed through secondaries the sponsors themselves control. The catalyst to watch is Blackstone's eight pending IPO filings. It has already floated three companies since May, including its own data-center REIT, BXDC, whose $2 billion listing it called the largest blind-pool REIT IPO ever — so the pipeline isn't hypothetical. If those eight convert into real public listings with disclosed proceeds this fall, that's the first genuine third-party liquidity test since this drought began. Until then, treat record earnings as a fee story, not an exit story.
