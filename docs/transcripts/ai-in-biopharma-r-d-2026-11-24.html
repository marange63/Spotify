Cautious Optimism Briefings

AI in Biopharma R&D

Transcript · November 24, 2026

Good morning. It's November 24th, 2026, and this is your briefing on AI in biopharma R&D.

Twice last week, on the 20th and again on the 21st, this show closed by saying that nothing had measured AI's effect on clinical success. Pharma's own numbers were about speed to a candidate. The labs' numbers were about chemistry benchmarks. The big data deal was about access. And on the 22nd, the scorecard was a retrospective potency benchmark, graded by the vendor that built the model. Today that pattern breaks. A consultancy has put a number on whether AI-native biotechs actually get through the clinic more often. The questions are whether that number holds up, and whether the money is already treating a hypothesis as proof.

The number comes from Oliver Wyman's October analysis, which PYMNTS reported yesterday. In Phase II, 45 percent of AI-native biotechs' programs advanced, against 29 percent for the industry overall. That's roughly one and a half times the industry rate. Last week's figures from Novartis, Novo Nordisk and Sanofi were all about saving time. Speed only gets you to the test sooner. The odds of passing Phase II are what move pipeline economics. So if this edge is real, it matters more than every timeline claim from last week put together.

Start with what kind of evidence this is. It's a consultancy analysis, relayed by a trade outlet. It is not peer-reviewed, and no one has audited it. The sample is also small. Oliver Wyman itself says the Phase II set is 46 programs, with roughly half still running. The firm separately flags that sample as limited.

We went to Oliver Wyman's own report to see how the 45 percent was built. The headline turns out to be the stricter of two measures. The 45 percent counts programs that progressed to the next phase. Count programs that simply completed Phase II, and the firm's figure rises to 55 percent. So the coverage did not pick the flattering version. But the report doesn't state an industry comparator on that completion basis, so don't hear 55 percent as a wider gap. And with half the cohort still running, the rate can still move a long way in either direction. There's one more credibility flag in the same report. It gives two different Phase I success rates in two separate passages, and never reconciles them. That isn't fatal. But it isn't the precision of an audited dataset either.

Three harder questions stay open. The first is the comparator. The report's text never plainly says where the 29 percent comes from, or what years it covers. So treat the size of the gap as directionally real, but not precisely pinned.

The second is the definition. Oliver Wyman defines AI-native biotechs as, quote, "companies built around embedded AI discovery or R&D platforms." The firm presents that as a proxy for AI-enabled assets. It's a company-level label, not a molecule-level test. It can't tell you whether AI designed the molecule or merely picked the target. That's the selector-versus-designer split we drew on the 20th. Recursion's REC-4881, for example, is an in-licensed molecule. Recursion's AI chose its target and indication, not its chemistry. Yet a company like Recursion would plausibly count as AI-native here. So even a durable 45 percent would not, on its own, show that AI design improves the odds.

The third is selection, and this is our inference. AI-native companies, and the investors backing them, may simply choose better-validated targets to begin with. That would lift the advancement rate without the platform adding anything. Nothing in the coverage separates those two effects. So bank this as a provisional signal, not proof.

To see what one program in this category looks like, take Insilico's rentosertib, an AI-designed molecule for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. In a small trial previously published in Nature Medicine, patients on the highest dose gained 98.4 milliliters of lung capacity. Placebo patients lost 20.3. That's a genuine prospective signal. But it's one program, and it isn't this week's news. It is not a verdict on the asset class.

The real grade comes in Phase 3. Rentosertib's Phase 3 is already underway, and Recursion has aligned with the FDA on its registrational design. We covered both on the 20th. The new name is Generate Biomedicines, now cited among the companies starting late-stage studies of an AI-designed drug. So the cohort that will eventually test that 45 percent is widening. But entering a pivotal trial is not succeeding in one. No AI-designed molecule has reported Phase 3 results. And because the positive sample is so thin, one high-profile late-stage miss could reset the whole category's apparent edge. That fragility is our read, and it's the main risk to the thesis.

Now to the money, because that's where the gap between capital and evidence gets stark. Oliver Wyman's own report puts 2025's investment and licensing deals in AI drug discovery at 43 billion dollars in committed funds. That's more than triple the year before. Then, in the report's words, the first half of 2026 "added another 46 billion." On that wording, six months outran all of last year.

Two cautions on those dollars. The report doesn't say how much is equity, how much is upfront cash, and how much is milestone value that may never be paid. Nor does it define what counts as an AI drug-discovery deal. Now set the money against the pipeline. AI-discovered molecules are still only about 1 to 2 percent of the clinical pipeline. So capital is compounding into a sliver of the pipeline, on the strength of an edge measured on 46 programs. The money is pricing the hypothesis as if it were the result. One footnote: Isomorphic Labs' five-billion-dollar round, covered here last week, was announced on November 17th. So it sits outside that first-half window entirely.

Set against that, here's the layer where AI adoption is already measurable: regulatory and process work. FDA's drug center has received more than 1,500 AI-related submissions, according to a regulatory-affairs panel this year. Be careful with that count. It doesn't say what the AI did in each filing, or whether it changed a trial design or an outcome. It measures adoption, not efficacy. But the contrast is the point. As we read it, AI is already routine in the paperwork, and still unproven in the biology.

So what follows if the Phase II edge survives a larger, more mature sample? Our inference is that deal structures shift. Pharma would pay up for clinical-stage AI-native assets, and for milestone-heavy options on them, relative to preclinical design-platform deals. That favors platforms with something already in the clinic, like Insilico and Recursion, over pure discovery engines that have none, like Isomorphic. It also pushes the bottleneck further downstream. On the 21st, we argued that faster discovery shifts the binding constraint to clinical capacity and enrollment. Better Phase II odds would compound that, sending more programs into the large late-stage trials that contract research organizations run. That's a volume argument for CRO clinical operations, and again, it's our inference.

For pharma's aggregate R&D productivity, keep the scale in view. Even a confirmed edge on 1 to 2 percent of the pipeline barely moves industry-wide numbers in the near term. Any visible effect on R&D spend would come from reallocation toward in-licensing AI-native assets, not from an overall budget cut. That's our read, and it's the honest ceiling on this week's good news.

So, pulling the threads together. The biggest open questions come down to three. Does the rate hold as the running half of the 46-program cohort finishes? Does anyone independent replicate it, ideally in peer review? And does "AI-native" ever get defined at the molecule level, rather than the company level? The realistic read on clinical success rates is that the first evidence is real, encouraging, and thin. Because it touches a small share of the pipeline, the effect on pharma R&D spend is a reallocation story for years before it becomes a productivity story. Three things to watch. First, an updated Oliver Wyman rate as that cohort matures. Second, any independent replication. Third, Isomorphic's year-end target for a drug in trials, with about five weeks left and nothing reported yet.

That's the briefing. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
