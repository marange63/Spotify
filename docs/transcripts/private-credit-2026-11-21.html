Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Credit & the Corporate Credit Cycle

Transcript · November 21, 2026

Good morning, it's Saturday, November 21st, and this is your private credit briefing.

Here's the thesis up front. Banks took back the top of the lending market last quarter. Private lenders answered by holding or raising their price instead of cutting it. And one BDC's print this week shows what that strategy costs.

Per 9fin's third-quarter data, private credit's share of large-cap financings fell 15 percentage points to 40%. 9fin calls that the lowest since the third quarter of last year. The syndicated loan market took the majority back. On Monday, this desk said the bank takeback rested on a single Bloomberg report, and we flagged it as reported, not confirmed. The market-wide data now says the same thing.

The volume side tells the same story. Per 9fin, US direct-lending deal count fell to 485 in the third quarter. That's a 36% drop from the second quarter, and the second straight quarterly decline. Be precise about scope, though. This is share of large-cap deals and a count of new deals. It doesn't measure private credit's total book or its assets under management. Still, the bank takeback is now a market-wide fact, not a story about a handful of listed BDCs.

Now the mechanism. In 9fin's data, median syndicated spreads actually widened 30 basis points in the quarter, coming off a record low set in the second quarter. Private-credit pricing held flat at SOFR plus 500. That narrowed the premium direct lenders charge over syndicated paper to 220 basis points. So banks won share even as their own pricing moved up.

Here's the detail that changes the read. The coverage of 9fin's numbers ties the syndicated market's 60% share directly to refinancing. Borrowers took existing private loans out into the syndicated market. Refinancings and recaps also rose to 22% of deal activity, from 19% in the second quarter. That figure covers all deal activity, so it doesn't size how much of the share swing is refinancing versus new buyouts. But the mechanism matters. A private loan refinanced into syndicated paper is a repayment at par for the private lender, not a loss. So part of this swing is lenders getting paid back by a cheaper market, not origination simply vanishing.

Land it this way. At the top of the market, private credit's edge has narrowed to complexity and speed, not raw capital. And one quarter of share data can swing on a handful of big deals, so this is a signal, not yet a trend.

If banks won on price, the obvious move for private lenders is to cut theirs. They didn't. 9fin has private pricing flat. PitchBook LCD has it rising. Its direct-lending LBO spreads ran 475 basis points in the first quarter, 494 in the second, and 520 in the third. That's three straight quarters wider.

Covenant Review, a separate dataset with a different scope, has private-credit loans clearing at an average nominal spread of SOFR plus 549. That's the widest since the fourth quarter of 2024. These series measure different slices of the market, so don't stitch them into one line. But none of them shows lenders cutting price. Losing share while holding or widening spreads means the big direct lenders chose margin over volume. That confirms Monday's read that they ceded volume rather than match the banks on price.

There are two ways to read wider spreads on a shrinking book, and they deserve equal weight. One is discipline. Lenders are pricing the same risk higher because the borrowers who still need them will pay. The other is adverse selection. The borrowers strong enough to reach the syndicated market left. The ones who stayed pay more because they're weaker credits. Average spreads depend on deal mix, so spread data alone can't separate those two stories.

Leverage is the best tiebreaker in the data. Per PitchBook LCD, private-credit LBOs averaged 4.0 times debt to EBITDA. That held for both first-lien and total debt. Bank-syndicated LBOs ran 5.1 times first-lien and 5.3 times total.

One caution before reading too much into that. Identical first-lien and total leverage is the signature of unitranche, a single senior tranche at a single price. Syndicated deals often carry junior debt beneath the first lien. So these aren't like-for-like capital structures. Even so, private books are carrying more than a turn less total leverage, at a premium spread. That tilts the evidence toward discipline. It doesn't rule out adverse selection, because leverage is only one axis of risk.

And it cuts against something this desk said on Monday. We inferred that private lenders would be left holding the riskier tail of the market. On leverage, the evidence now points the other way. They're holding the less-levered deals and getting paid more for them. On what we can see today, that part of Monday's read was wrong, and we're correcting it.

Choosing margin over volume has a cost on the capital side. Two straight quarters of falling deal count leave lenders with fewer new loans to put money into. Historically, that's the setup in which terms give way so capital can get deployed. There's no evidence in the data that documentation has loosened yet, so treat it as the thing to watch, not a finding. The strategy also depends on the syndicated bid staying open. If bank appetite retreats, share swings back, and private lenders regain volume at their wider spreads. Whether that ends well depends on which borrowers come back.

Margin over volume also only works if the book you already hold pays its way. Golub Capital BDC's print this week tests exactly that. It reported its fiscal fourth quarter, the three months ended September 30th. Net asset value per share fell to fourteen dollars, from fourteen twenty-five.

Management's case is containment. About twenty-one cents of the drop came from markdowns on nonaccrual debt and equity. Another four cents came from equity in previously restructured companies. Management attributes the loss to a small number of credits it had already identified as problems. That split does support the idea that the damage is concentrated. What one quarter can't show is whether the problem list is growing.

The income line is where it bites. Net investment income came in at thirty-two cents a share, against a thirty-three-cent distribution. Missing by a penny is small. GAAP earnings per share, though, fell to eight cents, roughly a quarter of the payout. So credit costs, not new originations, are now the swing factor at one of the best-regarded middle-market managers. Still, one manager isn't the sector.

The buyback may be the most telling number in the print. Golub Capital repurchased 793,000 shares at an average of twelve seventy-five. That's an 11% discount to its prior-quarter net asset value. Here's why it matters. Private loans have no daily price, so when public loan prices fall, private marks don't have to follow. A listed BDC trading and buying back below NAV is one of the few market prices we get on those marks. Holders of the equity are valuing the book below where the adviser marks it.

Two qualifiers on that. A discount can reflect sentiment and fee drag, not only doubts about the marks. And buying back below NAV adds value for remaining holders only if the NAV is real. Still, it's a market price on private marks, which is exactly what this desk said on Wednesday was missing.

So where does the cycle actually stand? The competitive turn at the top of the market is now confirmed by data, not anecdote. Private lenders held or raised their price rather than chase volume down. Their deals also carry less leverage than the banks' deals do. Nothing in this week's data shows a default turn. The wider-cycle read stands where Wednesday left it. Syndicated loan prices are flagging stress, while realized payment defaults haven't moved. So for private credit, this is a share and margin story, not yet a broad credit-loss story.

The biggest unresolved risk is BDC earnings power, and this is the desk's inference, not a finding. Less top-end origination means income leans harder on the existing book. A book carrying nonaccrual markdowns leaves less room to cover a fixed distribution. If more managers print like Golub, distribution cuts or fee waivers become the adjustment.

Watch three things into year-end. First, 9fin's fourth-quarter share data, for whether this quarter was a trend or a one-off. Second, the next round of BDC prints. The question there is whether Golub's markdowns stay inside known names, and whether peers' net investment income still covers their distributions. Third, whether listed BDC discounts to NAV widen or narrow as the fourth-quarter marks come in. That's the market's running verdict on private marks.

That's the lending machine for November 21st. Stay cautiously optimistic, and enjoy your weekend.
