Cautious Optimism Briefings

Enterprise Technology & Software

Transcript · October 10, 2026

Good morning, it's Saturday, October 10th, 2026, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

Wall Street has decided that AI is an enabler for software, not a disruptor. This week's operating evidence says the real gates sit somewhere a stock price can't reach. The first is whether customers actually switch on the AI inside their own orgs. The second is whether data centers can get enough power to run it.

Start with Salesforce. Its Winter '27 release is enabling Agentforce automatically, at no added cost, for eligible editions. According to Salesforce Ben's reading of the release notes, that covers Lightning orgs on Enterprise, Performance, Unlimited and Developer Edition, plus Agentforce 1 Edition. The same report says enablement for existing orgs began on a rolling basis in early September. A Salesforce consultancy's summary of the release material puts the two main upgrade weekends on October 3rd and today. So by Monday, eligible orgs will have the Agentforce builder tools sitting in front of their admins, whether they asked for them or not.

The fine print matters more than the headline. That consultancy's guidance is blunt. Enablement makes the tools available. It does not activate any agent, and it creates no consumption charges by itself. In its words, no agent starts answering your customers on its own, and nothing goes live without being built and activated. So this is a natural experiment in the gap this show keeps tracking, between availability and adoption.

Yesterday we noted an Oppenheimer survey finding that nearly half of Agentforce triers are still testing or piloting. This rollout is Salesforce's answer to that stall. Put the tools everywhere, remove the friction, and wait for activation to catch up. The bullish read is that zero friction accelerates adoption. The skeptical read is that Salesforce is inflating the denominator because the activation numerator is slow. Both readings fit the facts we have. No one has reported an activation rate since the rollout, so there is no way to settle it yet.

What will separate them is a paid metric, and Salesforce has already given us the baseline. Its fiscal second-quarter earnings release in August put Agentforce annual recurring revenue above one and a half billion dollars. That was up more than 240 percent year over year. Crucially, that figure predates the auto-enable rollout. Any count of orgs with Agentforce next quarter will be swollen by default enrollment. The number to demand is whether that ARR growth holds up on paid consumption. Availability on Salesforce is now close to universal, so only activation and revenue numbers carry real information.

There's a second piece to the Salesforce story. According to a third-party release tracker, some Agentforce requests currently served by Google's Gemini 2.5 Pro, Flash and Flash-Lite models will be rerouted on October 20th. Which model picks up that traffic, and why, we can't confirm. The secondary accounts contradict each other, so I won't guess. What matters is the pattern. Yesterday we covered Atlassian's Rovo, which routes each task across OpenAI and other model providers on capability, speed and cost. Now Salesforce is reshuffling its own model supply on a scheduled date. Two major software platforms, on consecutive days of this show, are treating foundation models as swappable backend inputs. That weakens the pricing leverage any single model vendor holds over the platforms sitting on top of it.

The stock market isn't waiting for activation numbers. Accenture's record full-year bookings of 84.5 billion dollars, reported at the start of the month, punctured the fear that AI would shrink demand for IT services. We covered the headline figures on October 2nd, so here is the one new data point. Bookings tied to Accenture's emerging AI and data partners grew more than threefold, and revenue from those partners roughly doubled versus fiscal 2025. That's a defined partner cohort, not Accenture's whole AI book. Tripling off an undisclosed base can still be small in dollars. But it partly answers the question we raised on October 2nd, whether those bookings were AI work or relabeled transformation projects. At least some of it is genuinely AI-native.

That relief has since spread well beyond consulting. On Tuesday, October 6th, Palo Alto Networks, CrowdStrike and Fortinet all closed at record highs, with Palo Alto finishing at about 420 dollars. In the same session, the S&P 500 software and services index rose to its highest level since November 2025. On Thursday, this show called that security jump narrative rather than fundamentals. Two days on, it looks less like a security story and more like a re-rating of software as a whole. One LPL strategist summed up the mood: AI has been more of an enabler for these companies than a disruptor. Treat that as one strategist's framing, not evidence. And treat Tuesday's closes as a dated peak, not today's level.

Here's the problem. There's no company-specific catalyst behind those individual moves. No contract win, no earnings print. This is multiple expansion on a narrative. It also sits in direct tension with the credit market. Earlier this week, our private credit coverage flagged two warning signs. The first was a report of Fed examiners reviewing banks. That review was prompted in part by JPMorgan's markdowns on software loans threatened by AI. The second was redemption requests concentrating in Blue Owl's tech-focused fund. Equity holders are calling AI an enabler. Software lenders are marking the same exposure as risk. Both can't be fully right. For now, price is running well ahead of the activation proof the Salesforce story just laid out.

If the first gate is inside the customer's org, the second is at the substation. Quartz reported this week that Oracle is weighing trucked natural gas for a stalled New Mexico data center project with a 2.45 gigawatt target, amid pipeline delays. Weighing is the operative word. This is a contingency at one stalled site, not proof the whole buildout is behind. You could even read it as resourcefulness. But it's a tangible sign that physical infrastructure, not capital or demand, is now the binding constraint on turning signed contracts into running capacity.

For Oracle, that constraint lands directly on its backlog. Oracle's remaining performance obligations stood at 638 billion dollars in its latest annual figures filed with the SEC. That backlog converts to revenue only as fast as sites can be energized. If power gates capacity, the path from backlog to revenue stretches out. Site-level energy logistics then become an equity variable for Oracle. It also revises a read this show made on October 2nd. The Financial Times had reported that Tencent was prepaying about 30 percent of a chip lease with Oracle, a deal neither company has confirmed. We read that prepayment as proof Oracle held pricing power over scarce compute. This week cuts the other way. Scarcity binds Oracle too.

Back on the software side, a rich multiple is also a financing tool, and Snowflake moved to use it just before this month's rally. On September 28th, Snowflake launched 3.5 billion dollars of zero-coupon convertible notes, split between 2029 and 2031 maturities. Its SEC filing says the proceeds will pay for capped-call hedges and repurchase part of its existing 2027 convertible notes. On its face, that's liability management. The broader read, and this is my inference rather than a disclosed plan, is that a highly valued software stock is itself financing currency. Zero-coupon debt is cheap only while the equity holds up. Expect more equity-linked raises, and possibly stock-funded deals, from software vendors while that window stays open.

Put the two gates together. The market has priced AI as a net positive for enterprise software ahead of the evidence inside customer orgs and inside data centers. And default-on is becoming the industry's adoption lever. GitHub's Copilot features flip to default-on on October 22nd, as we flagged on October 2nd. So discount any adoption count from the next quarter or two for auto-enrollment. Three markers to watch. First, whether Salesforce's next earnings call shows Agentforce growth holding on paid consumption. Second, GitHub Copilot numbers after the 22nd, read against that default-on flip. Third, any concrete Oracle disclosure on when its stalled sites come online. Until then, treat the rally as running ahead of the receipts.

That's today's enterprise technology briefing. Have a great weekend.
