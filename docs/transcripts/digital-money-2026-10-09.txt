Good morning. It's Friday, October 9th, 2026, and here's the Cautious Optimism Briefing on digital money. Today's thesis in one line: the institutional flow bid on bitcoin has turned from marginal to net selling, and it now runs two days deep. Meanwhile, on the dollar side of the stack, growth is narrowing toward the stablecoin rail that carries real usage.

We start with a number we've been waiting for. Yesterday we said trackers pointed to an October 7th ETF outflow, but we declined to air a figure because none came with a source we could verify. Now one does. CoinDesk, citing SoSoValue data, puts the October 7th net outflow for US spot bitcoin ETFs at four hundred eighty seven point one million dollars. That's the largest single-day outflow since June 25th. It sits roughly two point one standard deviations below the ninety-day average daily flow, which has run at about ninety two million dollars of net inflow. That's not noise. But one bad day is not a regime, so the real question was whether the outflow would extend.

It did. A separate SoSoValue read puts Thursday, October 8th, at a further two hundred forty four million dollars of net outflows. Franklin's EZBC was the only fund that took in money that day, and only a modest amount. That makes two straight days of net selling. It also moves the test. The question is no longer whether a second day follows a bad one. It's whether a third does.

Now, who led the selling. On October 7th it was BlackRock's IBIT, which lost two hundred seven point seven million dollars, more than any other fund that day, per fund-level data relayed by NewsBTC. Fidelity's FBTC and ARK's ARKB followed, each losing a little over a hundred million. So this wasn't thin, smaller funds getting squeezed while the flagship held firm. The flagship led it. That matters because IBIT is normally the strongest source of positive flow in the group. When the biggest, stickiest pool of capital moves first, it's a different signal than redemptions concentrated in marginal vehicles.

Zoom out, and the most revealing number is this year's. Year-to-date net inflows for 2026 stand at about seven hundred million dollars, per CoinDesk. Cumulative inflows since the funds launched in January 2024 are about fifty seven billion. On our arithmetic, this year's buying is only around one percent of the total. So almost all of the wrapper's holder base is legacy capital from prior years, not fresh demand. And the momentum has turned. September alone brought two point six five billion dollars of inflows, while October is now net negative. Bitcoin is trading around eighty three thousand dollars, near a floor that has held since the rally stalled on September 21st. The read is that ETF flows follow bitcoin's price rather than anchoring it. So the "institutional floor" narrative built on the cumulative number overstates how much active sponsorship sits under the price today.

Now the fair counterarguments. The first is mundane de-risking: holders who were near breakeven taking the exit when they got the chance. The second is a basis-trade unwind rather than directional selling. We looked for CME futures open interest or basis data for this window and found none. So the basis story stays a hypothesis, with no numbers attached. And the bull case hasn't vanished. Eighty three thousand dollars has held for more than two weeks. The third quarter brought six and a half billion dollars of ETF inflows while bitcoin finished up roughly forty percent, per ViaBTC as cited by The Block. Two outflow days don't erase that run. They do mean the floor is now being tested by real selling, not just by a quiet market.

Briefly, a grading note on our own call. Yesterday we described the liquidation day around October 7th as contained, not a cascade, on a total of about five hundred fifty million dollars. The Block's count for the same episode lands in the same place, so the event checks out. But The Block also flagged a measurement problem. Binance restricted its liquidation reporting in April 2021. CoinGlass now captures only one liquidation order per second from that exchange. So this total, and every historical comparison, likely understates the true figure. Our "contained" call may still be right relative to past episodes, since those are understated too. But it rested on a floor, not a measurement. Don't lean on any liquidation number as precise.

If the flow bid on the price side is retreating, the place to look for real usage is the dollar rail underneath it. Total stablecoin supply stood at three hundred four point two billion dollars on September 29th, per a third-quarter industry report. That's about sixteen billion below May's peak of three hundred twenty point six billion. So the overall pool is still smaller than it was in the spring.

Inside that pool, growth is concentrating. Earlier this week we left open a question: is USDC gaining share inside a flat total? The same report now answers it. USDC supply grew one point two percent over the last thirty days of the quarter. That's six times USDT's point two percent. More telling, USDC carried roughly seventy percent of adjusted transaction volume in the first half. Tether carried about twenty five percent. Yet USDC holds only about a quarter of total supply. That's a usage share far out of proportion to its share of the float.

Now the caveats. One point two percent of a seventy four point six billion dollar base is under a billion dollars in absolute terms. So this is a growth-rate story, not a market-share takeover. The volume split is a first-half figure on an adjusted methodology, not a live October read. And total on-chain transfer volume was recently running down about twenty three percent over thirty days. So USDC's usage lead sits inside a shrinking activity pool. Still, for Circle, whose earnings come down to float times yield, the float side is growing at the margin, not just holding. The takeaway: the rail capturing genuine payments activity is gaining ground, even as the overall pool shrinks.

That growing rail is about to meet regulation. The Federal Reserve has proposed requiring Board-supervised payment stablecoin issuers to fully back their coins with permissible reserve assets. Short-term Treasury bills are named as qualifying. But two limits apply. Naming bills as eligible is not a mandate to hold them. And with total float still below its peak, there's no net new bill bid from this channel yet. The rule shapes composition. Supply sets size. Treasury is running its own, separate proposed rule on payment stablecoins, which is open for public comment until October 19th.

The market-structure side is at an even earlier stage. The CFTC's October 5th action on retail crypto trading is only an advance notice of proposed rulemaking, a list of questions with no rule text yet. That matters for Coinbase, because its new CFTC-registered clearinghouse, which clears fully collateralized contracts in USDC, will operate under whatever rulebook comes out of this process. And with the CLARITY Act still stalled in the Senate, there's no statute to fill the gap. So any case for Coinbase built on a settled CFTC regime is early, because the rules haven't even been proposed.

Pulling it together. The flow bid on bitcoin retreated for a second straight day, led by the largest fund rather than the smallest. And the dollar rail's growth is narrowing toward the stablecoin that actually moves money, not the one that mostly sits as float. Watch for a third ETF outflow day, and whether eighty three thousand dollars holds on a closing basis. Also watch the October 19th comment deadline on Treasury's payment-stablecoin rule. September CPI on the 14th and the Fed's meeting on the 27th and 28th are both still ahead.

That's the briefing for October 9th. Stay cautiously optimistic.
