Space & the Orbital Economy

Transcript · November 5, 2026

Good morning. It's Thursday, November 5th, 2026, and this is your Cautious Optimism briefing on the space and orbital economy.

The theme today: outside SpaceX, the momentum in orbit belongs to state-directed programs. Meanwhile, the West's public challengers head into their November reckoning on timelines set by analysts, not by their own operators.

Start with China. On November 3rd, a Qianfan broadband batch of eighteen satellites reached polar orbit aboard CAS Space's Kinetica-2 rocket, from Jiuquan. The tracker Orbital Radar describes it as the constellation's seventeenth deployment, and the largest group yet delivered by a Chinese commercial-sector launch vehicle. Still, one batch is a data point, not a cadence.

Does a record batch mean either constellation is catching up to plan? No. Count what has actually launched this calendar year, and both are well short. A mid-October launch report had Guowang at 86 satellites deployed in 2026, roughly a quarter of its plan for the year. One more Guowang batch has flown since, so today's figure is somewhat higher. It is nowhere near a pace that closes the gap.

Qianfan is further along, but still behind. The tracker KeepTrack counts 143 Qianfan satellites launched so far this year, under half of its annual target. And if a reported, unconfirmed 2027 goal of about 1,300 satellites is the real one, Qianfan is only a fifth of the way there. Both annual targets come from a plan published late last year, so they may be stale guidance. And every count here comes from third-party trackers, not the operators or Beijing, so treat them as approximate.

There's a second qualifier, and it's about the rocket. Kinetica-2 is not a seasoned workhorse. November 3rd was only its second flight ever. Its maiden launch, on March 30th, was expendable and carried a cargo-spacecraft prototype, not Qianfan satellites. So this is a two-flight vehicle now carrying operational payloads for a state-linked network. The "commercial" label is loose, too. CAS Space grew out of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. This is state-adjacent capacity, not a market-funded challenger.

So what does this tell us? Less than the headline suggests. A young launcher just flew a record batch, while both constellations run well behind their annual plans. That pattern is consistent with satellite production, not launch, being the binding constraint. But one record batch can't tell us whether rockets or satellite factories are holding Beijing back. We have no data on factory output, or on satellites waiting at the launch site. The tell will be whether batch sizes and cadence hold over the next few launches. If they do and the shortfall persists, the contest with Starlink and Kuiper shifts toward how fast China can build satellites and defend its spectrum and orbital-slot filings. And either way, deployed satellites are not yet broadband subscribers.

Now set that against the West's own second tier. Rocket Lab's Neutron and Blue Origin's New Glenn are both on the ground right now: Neutron hasn't debuted, and New Glenn is grounded after a May anomaly.

Start with Neutron, which slipped into 2027 after its tank-dome defect. What's new this week is who's setting its calendar. Citizens cut its Rocket Lab price target from $130 to $90, while keeping an Outperform rating. It now expects the first Neutron launch in late fiscal second quarter 2027.

Cantor Fitzgerald had already put Neutron in that same quarter on October 30th. Two sell-side shops converging sounds like confirmation, but it probably isn't independent evidence. Both are most likely modeling the same management commentary. Rocket Lab itself still says only "2027." The stock speaks more plainly. Rocket Lab's most recent close was $52.37, about two-thirds below its 52-week high of $151. So Rocket Lab's upcoming third-quarter report is the moment management either adopts the analysts' quarter or declines to.

Blue Origin's New Glenn is the other half of that stalled bench. The new detail is a root cause. Blue Origin says inspections traced May's static-fire anomaly to the main oxygen valve on one BE-4 engine. That's the company's attribution, not a closed FAA mishap investigation. The rebuilt tower at Launch Complex 36 is finished, and a November return to flight is the reported target. But that date comes from Aviation Week, via SatNews. Blue Origin's own official line is still "before the end of 2026."

Step back, and the asymmetry is plain. China's non-core launchers flew constellation batches in late October and again on November 3rd. Over the same stretch, Neutron and New Glenn produced a pad rebuild and analyst dates, but no flights. Every quarter they stay on the ground extends SpaceX's hold on US reusable medium and heavy lift, because national-security and constellation customers have few alternatives.

Meanwhile, SpaceX keeps pushing its next-generation hardware forward. Starship's Booster 22 completed a full-duration, thirty-three-engine static fire on October 25th. SpaceX is targeting November for Flight 15, with the first attempt to catch both the booster and the ship at the tower. Gwynne Shotwell has said the flight's FAA license is expected to cover both catches, but that grant isn't confirmed. And the prior flight, in late September, reached orbit despite one Raptor shutting down early. So even the leader is still proving reliability. But it is proving it in flight, while its would-be US rivals wait on the ground.

If the commercial market isn't pulling the challengers forward fast enough, the state increasingly is. Greenland's parliament approved a security agreement with the United States and Denmark around November 3rd. According to Reuters, via a secondary outlet, it lets the US establish two additional military bases. It also lets the US develop elements of the Golden Dome missile-defense system on Greenlandic territory. The reporting doesn't say which elements, or on what timeline. So treat it as a geographic and geopolitical expansion of the program, not a new piece of orbital hardware. On October 30th, this desk called Golden Dome a program with a hard deadline that benefits from threat headlines. It is now acquiring geography as well as budget.

And that budget has an unusual shape. The Pentagon's fiscal 2027 request carries $17.5 billion for Golden Dome. Just $398 million of that comes from the base budget, leaving roughly 98 percent outside normal base funding. A request isn't an appropriation. That split makes Golden Dome's funding path unusual, and worth watching closely through appropriations.

That state demand is also why Rocket Lab's equity story increasingly leans on defense satellites while its rocket slips. As background, last December the Space Development Agency awarded about $3.5 billion in Tranche 3 missile-tracking contracts to four companies. Rocket Lab took $805 million of that, alongside Lockheed Martin, L3Harris, and Northrop Grumman. So in the West, the demand pulling satellite builders forward sits in government budgets. That ties the growth of those tracking-layer winners to how Golden Dome's money fares in appropriations.

That leaves the purely commercial bet, AST SpaceMobile. Its third-quarter business update call is Monday, November 9th, at five p.m. Eastern. It's the clearest near-term test of whether direct-to-cell is converting from demo to durable service.

Analysts forecast revenue of about $45.7 million, up from $31.5 million last quarter. Those are estimates for a call that hasn't happened yet. Even if they land, the same consensus still has AST losing about forty-two cents a share. So the revenue line is growing fast off a small base, but it isn't yet covering the cost of building and replacing a constellation.

AST SpaceMobile's stock opened recently at $40.97, against a 52-week high near $134. That's roughly two-thirds off the top, the same drawdown bracket as Rocket Lab. Separately, law firms are soliciting lead plaintiffs for a securities class action, with a November 13th deadline. The complaint alleges misstatements about AST's capital and liquidity position. That's an allegation, not a finding. But it sharpens what Monday's call has to answer. For both public challengers, the market is pricing execution and balance-sheet risk, not the underlying technology.

Pulling it together. What's genuinely inflecting this week is Chinese constellation deployment on young, state-adjacent launchers, and Golden Dome's reach into Arctic territory. Still firmly in demo territory are Neutron's debut, New Glenn's return to flight, Chinese satellite internet turning into subscribers, and AST's revenue scaling against its capital needs. The biggest open question is whether rockets or satellite factories now bind China's buildout, and the next few Qianfan and Guowang batches should start to answer it.

For catalysts, watch AST SpaceMobile on Monday, Rocket Lab's third-quarter report for whether management names a Neutron quarter, New Glenn's November target, and Starship Flight 15's double-catch attempt.

That's the briefing. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
