Good morning. It's Wednesday, September 23rd, and this is Strategic Power.

On September 17th, this briefing told you to watch whether the President would sign the Russia-Iran sanctions bill sitting on his desk. He did, on September 18th. The White House says the law "authorizes and expands statutory sanctions, tariffs, and prohibitions on Russia and extends existing sanctions on Iran." Two things are genuinely new since that call. First, this is no longer authority waiting for a signature. It is statute. Second, the law carries a provision this beat hadn't covered: a tariff of up to 500 percent on goods of Russian origin. That sits alongside the secondary tariff of up to 100 percent on the five largest buyers of Russian crude and gas. The signing landed six days before Thursday's Trump-Xi meeting at the White House. Washington has separately been pressing Beijing to cut its Iranian crude purchases. So read this law as summit positioning first and trade policy second.

Now the mechanics, and a correction to what this beat told you last week. On September 17th we said China and India were named in the bill. That was wrong. The enacted text names no country. Baker McKenzie's reading of the law is that it "requires the President to impose targeted tariff increases of up to 100% on goods from the five largest importers of Russian crude oil and natural gas during the prior 12 months." Kelley Drye adds that the list is redetermined every 180 days. So the target list is a rolling executive determination, not a fixed designation in the law.

The timing and the escape hatch matter just as much. Per Kelley Drye, duties are to rise "within 30 days of enactment," which puts the window around October 18th. But Section 115 of the bill lets the President waive both the sanctions and the tariff provisions. And the Russian-import tariff is a ceiling, not a flat rate. The language is "up to 500 percent ad valorem." So today, no country faces a rate. What exists is a threat that renews itself every six months and that the President can waive at will.

Here is my judgment on what that means. The 500 percent tariff is probably close to symbolic on trade volume, because the US already buys little from Russia. I don't have a quoted dollar figure for those imports, so treat that as a read, not a measurement. The secondary tariff is the real instrument, and its value lies almost entirely in not being used yet. Its credibility erodes fast if October 18th passes with neither a designation nor a waiver. And there is a direct link to Thursday. Any extension of the US-China trade truce now implicitly prices in a decision not to use this law against Beijing.

One more wrinkle: Washington carved out its own supply chain before signing. According to a StratNews Global reading of the bill, it exempts activity under the US-Russia civilian nuclear cooperation agreement. It also excepts "specified imports into the US of low-enriched uranium used for nuclear reactors," plus certain medical isotopes. In other words, the one Russian import where a 500 percent tariff could actually bite is the one Washington chose to shield. This beat has been tracking nuclear as the base layer of AI capacity. The so-what is plain: Washington won't risk its own reactor fuel to make this law hurt more.

And the law lands on a war that hasn't gone quiet. On September 22nd, the same day Trump met Zelenskyy in New York, Reuters reported a Russian attack on an energy facility. It left nearly 100,000 consumers in Ukraine's Chernihiv region without power. On September 20th this beat called the announced energy truce noise. That read holds. The sanctions law is not arriving on a de-escalating conflict. It arrives on one still targeting the other side's grid.

Beijing has built its own instrument this month, and it points at people rather than cargo. China's State Council Decree Number 841 took effect September 15th. The sources here are legal and press summaries, including the National Law Review and Asia Times, not the State Council text itself. Per those summaries, authorities can now block departures for breaches of export-control or tech-trade rules that "may endanger" industrial or technological security. There is "no fixed time limit." And authorities can withhold written notice of the exit ban on national-security or criminal-investigation grounds.

Asia Times reports that the named know-how at risk includes "electrolyte formulas, N-type solar cell technology and rare earth separation." That last item matters most for this beat. Yesterday we noted that Western critical-minerals money is reaching mining and early processing, not separation. This decree targets exactly that gap. It raises the personal risk for any engineer or executive who might carry separation chemistry out of China. My read is that its main function is defensive rather than hostile to foreign executives. But defensive still costs the West. It slows technology transfer, and that stretches the timeline for Western-financed processing beyond what the financing alone implies. To be clear, there's no reported case yet of the decree being used against a named individual. This is a legal instrument sharpened, not yet fired.

So step back. Washington's new lever is a tariff on flows. Beijing's is a lock on the know-how that makes the flows possible. Both sides walk into Thursday's meeting holding a freshly activated coercive tool. One is a secondary-tariff law that resets its target list every 180 days. The other is an exit-ban decree with no expiration date. Neither has been used. Both are worth more as leverage than as action.

Both of those are threats. Here's the one thing this week that adds actual megawatts. Google agreed to fund uprates on Georgia Power's owned portion of the Vogtle and Hatch nuclear plants. Per Power Engineering, that adds "approximately 96 MW of new capacity." Georgia Power projects the deal will produce "about $900 million in benefits for customers over the life of the units." That's the utility's own estimate, so weight it as advocacy. The deal still needs Georgia Public Service Commission approval, and no in-service date has been reported. This is contracted, not delivered.

Now scale it honestly. Georgia Power itself projects an increase of about 2,600 megawatts in peak demand by the end of 2030. Ninety-six megawatts against that is under four percent. Yesterday this beat said nuclear's contribution to the AI buildout this decade is currently a price, not a megawatt. This is the first real counterexample. It qualifies that conclusion without overturning it.

The cautionary backdrop is Vogtle itself. Its newest reactors were originally projected to cost 14 billion dollars and be finished by 2017. My judgment is that uprates at existing plants are the replicable near-term nuclear lever, and new construction remains the slow, expensive one. That favors incumbent nuclear owners with regulated rate bases, here Southern Company's Georgia Power, over new-build developers in the near term. Separately, Samsung C&T has taken an equity stake in the Kairos Power reactor being built for Google. It's a small but real signal of allied industrial capital entering the US advanced-reactor supply chain, as a counterweight to Russian and Chinese state nuclear exporters.

Put the week together. Two governments spent September sharpening coercive options. One is a tariff law that resets every six months. The other is an exit-ban decree with no clock on it. Meanwhile physical capacity barely moved. The one number that did move was ninety-six megawatts, at plants that already existed.

Watch two things from here. First, whether Washington designates any top-five buyer of Russian energy before that October 18th window closes, or waives instead. Second, whether Thursday's Trump-Xi meeting produces any actual language on Russian or Iranian crude purchases, rather than more positioning.

That's Strategic Power for September 23rd. Talk to you tomorrow.
