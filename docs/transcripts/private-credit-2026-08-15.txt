Good morning, it's August 15th, and this is your private credit briefing.

Today's story is a bifurcation. The same AI wave, and largely the same handful of managers, are simultaneously financing the largest credit deals in this cycle's history and repricing the loan book that built the private credit asset class over the last decade. Don't let anyone sell you this as one clean "AI is good for credit" narrative. It's a split, and the honest question is which side is bigger.

Start with the side that's souring. Software has become the sector private credit lenders are actively avoiding. Bloomberg reports it has turned into the "black sheep" of the book, as AI threatens to disrupt the business models sitting underneath roughly 40% of all private-equity-backed loans. Apollo cut its own software exposure roughly in half during 2025, from about 20% of its book down to around 10%. And the public market has already put a number on the damage. Business development companies with 15% or more of their portfolios in software or IT posted average year-to-date equity declines of 16.8%, versus 8.1% for BDCs with under 10% exposure. That's a real dispersion, and it tracks sector concentration almost linearly.

Before going further, sit with why software is the sector getting singled out, because it's not just headline risk. Software credit was built on a specific recovery assumption. The borrowers are asset-light, so the collateral that a lender falls back on in a workout is essentially the recurring revenue stream and the intangible value of the code and the customer base. That's fine when the revenue is sticky. It's a problem when the disruption thesis is that the revenue itself is what erodes. AI doesn't just pressure a software borrower's margins. It threatens the exact asset a lender was counting on to recover in a default. So the "anything but software" turn is really a re-rating of recovery, not just of growth, and it steers capital toward asset-heavy, old-economy borrowers with hard collateral you can actually seize and sell.

Now the harder question. Is that 16.8% equity decline just a stock-price story, the market pricing risk on paper ahead of any actual loan mark? Until this week, the honest answer was mostly yes. New evidence changes that. Two second-quarter disclosures show the fund-level marks are already moving. BCP Investment Corp said roughly 47% of its second-quarter unrealized depreciation was concentrated in software and software-exposed names, even though those names make up under 15% of its portfolio. That's a loss concentration nearly three times the sector's weight in the book, and it dragged BCP's net asset value down about 7% in a single quarter, to $14.49 a share. And Blue Owl Technology Income Corp, a non-traded BDC skewed toward tech credits, posted a $77.3 million net loss for the first half of the year as unrealized losses on its software holdings widened, with net asset value per share falling to $9.70 from $10.38 at year-end.

So the marks are following the equity market down, not just the stock price. That's a genuine update, and it's the show's running "marketed number versus the harder number" test resolving one notch. The equity market was the leading indicator in the spring. The second-quarter fund marks are the confirming print catching up. But here's the caveat that keeps this from being a clean distress story. Both managers are calling it valuation-multiple compression, not credit impairment. BCP's management described a "pretty big disconnect between broader public valuations versus where things are getting done in the private markets," and said 94% of its software book is mission-critical and structurally protected. Neither disclosure ties the markdowns to a non-accrual change on any specific credit. So the underlying loans are still being reported as performing even as their carrying value falls.

That leaves the central ambiguity genuinely unresolved, and it's worth stating both readings plainly. One reading is underwriting discipline: lenders are correctly re-rating collateral on borrowers whose business models are structurally impaired, and the marks are simply telling the truth early. The other reading is a self-fulfilling squeeze. If a shrinking pool of software lenders keeps pulling back, the borrowers that need to refinance in the next year or two face fewer willing counterparties and wider spreads, and that refinancing stress can convert a still-performing loan into an actual default. The mechanism matters, because in the first reading the losses stop where the impairment is, and in the second the pullback itself manufactures the impairment. The data doesn't settle which one is running. Watch the next refinancing round for software borrowers for the real answer.

Here's the handoff. The capital fleeing software credit isn't leaving the AI trade. It's crossing to the other side of it. We led Friday with Nvidia's six-firm coalition — Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs, and KKR — aiming to mobilize over $500 billion of financing using GPU compute itself as collateral. Those are still unsigned memoranda of understanding, not funded deals, and no per-firm allocation or valuation methodology for the compute collateral has been disclosed. But pair that with Apollo's $35 billion capital solution, alongside Blackstone and banks, backing Broadcom's new AI compute platform — a deal Apollo itself called the largest private financing ever executed. Same managers, same AI thesis, now on the collateral side instead of the software cash-flow side.

So the net effect isn't diversification. It's concentration. These firms are trimming a diversified book of software cash-flow loans and rotating into a much more concentrated bet on AI infrastructure collateral tied to a handful of vendors and hyperscalers. If you're keeping score on aggregate correlation to the AI capex thesis across the private credit complex, it just went up, not down. Exiting a thousand small software credits to underwrite a few enormous compute facilities does not lower your exposure to whether the AI buildout pays off. It raises it.

Now, what does the legacy book actually say, away from software specifically? Ares Management just posted its best fundraising quarter ever, roughly 36 billion dollars raised, with 170 billion in dry powder, up 13% year over year. That's the new-money story, and it's genuinely strong. But Ares Capital, its separately listed BDC, tells the older-book story. Non-accruals at amortized cost rose to 2.4% of the portfolio from 2.0% a year earlier, and net asset value per share slid to $19.35, down 55 cents over twelve months. Record fundraising at the platform level does nothing to repair a seasoned book that's already on the balance sheet. Put a software lens on that erosion and it's exactly where you'd expect AI-disruption risk to surface first: quietly, inside a legacy portfolio, well before it shows up as a press release.

And there's a distributional point hiding in the non-traded vehicles that's easy to miss. When a public BDC's software exposure sours, at least the stock price screams it, the way that 16.8% equity decline did. Blue Owl Technology Income Corp is non-traded, so its holders never see a market price, only the reported NAV. That NAV has already dropped to $9.70. For a retail investor in a semi-liquid tech-credit fund, the mark on the statement is the only signal there is, and if that mark lags the underlying deterioration, the leak is invisible until a redemption gate or a refinancing forces it into the open. The people least equipped to see the software repricing are the ones increasingly funding it.

So where does the cycle actually stand? One technology shock has split private credit in two. On one side, software credit is being repriced, and for the first time we have fund-level evidence the marks are following the equity market down, even as managers insist it's multiples compressing, not defaults arriving. On the other side, the same managers are underwriting the biggest, most concentrated infrastructure credit bet in the asset class's history. The unresolved risk is whether the "structurally protected" framing on software holds up, or whether a narrowing lender pool turns a valuation story into a refinancing one. Watch three things from here: whether any rating agency puts a specific number on software-borrower downgrades, whether non-accruals at software-heavy BDCs actually move rather than just their marks, and whether any of Nvidia's $500 billion in coalition financing actually gets drawn rather than just announced.

That's your briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
