Good morning. It's Friday, November 27th, and this is your briefing on artificial intelligence across the biopharmaceutical value chain.

Yesterday, this show reported that Novartis's investor day, held on the 19th, declined to claim a success-rate gain from AI. Its executives said the evidence will take longer to emerge. Five days after that event, the Financial Times carried remarks from Novartis's chief executive on how far AI can really go. At one end of the debate, frontier-AI chief executives talk about curing diseases like cancer and Alzheimer's. At the other end sit the skeptics, who expect little. The first big-pharma operator to weigh in publicly lands between them, in what he calls the middle. That is a far smaller prize than private capital is priced for. And the work aimed at that prize is still entirely preclinical.

Start with the chief executive. Vas Narasimhan runs Novartis. His remarks reach us second-hand, reported by the FT on November 24th and summarized by The Decoder. He was answering Anthropic's Dario Amodei and Isomorphic Labs' Demis Hassabis. Both have suggested such cures could come on aggressive timelines. Narasimhan disputes that. He calls the realistic outcome "somewhere in the middle." Secondary outlets attach specific forecasts to him, but none in his own words, so we leave them out. Set beside the investor day, the two statements aren't contradictory. One is a projection. The other describes what has actually been measured so far. So even the most AI-forward large pharma company treats any success-rate gain as future and modest, not current.

Weigh the speaker, too. Narasimhan sits on Anthropic's board, appointed through its Long-Term Benefit Trust in April, well before these remarks. A standing disclosure: Anthropic makes the model behind this show. We are not alleging a conflict. He is also an interested party in his own right. By our read, he runs a top sponsor that is selling an AI-productivity story to investors. So his middle is an operator's position, not a neutral estimate. That fits yesterday's pattern: every quantified AI claim we've seen lately comes from someone with a stake.

Here is why the middle still matters, and this is our inference. A success-rate gain, even a modest one, matters more to pipeline economics than time saved. That's because failures dominate the cost of each approval. Saving time makes each attempt cheaper. Better odds mean fewer attempts per approval. That is the prize everyone is chasing. So far it shows up only as forecasts and self-reported numbers. Nobody has yet measured it at the level of a sponsor's overall success rate.

If the prize sits where most drugs fail, someone has to attack the mechanism behind that failure. That brings us to Flagship Pioneering, the venture firm behind Moderna. On October 29th, Flagship merged three of its AI-biology companies into a new one, called Etcelera. The three were Cellarity, Etiome, and Empress Therapeutics. The deal is four weeks old, so treat it as context, not news. Its pitch is what it calls "causal intelligence." The idea is to model what actually drives a disease, and how a drug will act in a patient, before the trial. Flagship justifies the merger by saying approximately half of drug candidates that reach Phase II fail because the industry lacks causal intelligence. That's the promoter's own figure, with no cited source, offered to justify its own deal. Treat it as a pitch, not a finding.

The more telling detail is what got left out. Cellarity's clinical-stage asset is a sickle-cell program called CLY-124. It stays outside the merger, in a separate entity. That leaves Etcelera entirely preclinical. By our read, the carve-out is a tell. Dealmakers still price a clinical-stage asset very differently from a preclinical platform, and they don't want the two blended. There is a less flattering reading. No new financing was disclosed, so this could be consolidation under funding pressure. Either way, the bet on Phase II attrition is real. It is also unproven, and years from a clinical readout of its own.

Two consequences follow, and both are our inference. First, an operator forecast in the middle gives investors an outside anchor, set below the lab-CEO narrative. Design-only platforms with no clinical exposure are the most exposed to that re-anchoring. Second, if the prize is Phase II attrition, value migrates to whoever can generate causal, human-relevant evidence before Phase II. Etcelera wants that work in-house. But it is also the opening for translational contract research organizations and tools vendors.

Now to the other end of the range Narasimhan is disputing. Isomorphic Labs is the Alphabet-backed drug designer, and Demis Hassabis is its chief executive. He told Bloomberg, quote, "What we're trying to build is a platform to do hundreds of drugs a year." That is a throughput claim about design, not about approvals. Set it against Isomorphic's own release this month. It still speaks only of "driving our first therapeutic programs towards clinical trials," with no date attached. About five weeks remain on its year-end target for first trials, and nothing has changed. So private capital is priced toward the far end of a range that an industry operator now publicly rejects.

That gap between design ambition and clinical proof raises a sharper question. Which parts of the AI stack deliver measurable value today? The answer splits between language models and specialized architectures. Anthropic's prospectus, which this show covered on the 18th, says, quote, "Claude's capabilities should help scientific research, particularly drug discovery." Note the verb. "Should help" is an aspiration in a securities filing, not a reported result. The one measured result in our record also sits on the language-model side. On the 18th we reported that Enveda, an Anthropic partner, tested a Claude-based workflow for reviewing manufacturing records. It cut review time sharply and caught every issue its human reviewers had found. That was a self-reported test on one document class. But notice where it landed: in paperwork, not in the clinic.

Now the specialized side. Isomorphic's design engine and Etcelera's causal models are both bets on the molecule and the mechanism. Neither has a patient result. By our read, the split follows from how fast each kind of output can be checked. A language model's draft of a protocol or a batch review can be checked by a human the same week. A design model's molecule, or a causal model's mechanism, can only be graded by biology. Biology grades slowly, through IND-enabling studies and then Phase II. So the measurable value today sits in the workflow layer, while the headline capital sits in the design layer. The money is betting on the slow-grading layer, and the proof so far comes from the fast one.

There is one more lever, and it runs outside the molecule entirely: real-world data. If better odds don't come from better chemistry, they could come from better indication choices and tighter trial eligibility. The obstacle is that post-HITECH health records are balkanized across systems and formats. Truveta is built for that problem. In the company's words, its data "reflects the real-world care of more than 130 million patients across all care settings and therapeutic areas." A peer-reviewed description spells out the plumbing: "Data undergo daily ingestion, normalization to standard ontologies, and de-identification." Normalization to standard ontologies is the interoperability fix. De-identification is the privacy design. By our read, that is how such platforms work within HIPAA rather than around it. We found no source this cycle that treats HIPAA as the impediment. So, on our inference, the binding constraint has moved from format and privacy to depth. Are harmonized records complete enough for an eligibility decision a regulator will accept? Truveta's AI layer launched in April, so this is background, not news. And there is still no demonstrated case of it changing an indication choice or an eligibility design. It is a candidate lever, not a result.

So, pulling the threads together. Four open questions stand out. Will Novartis put numbers and a method behind "somewhere in the middle"? Will causal platforms like Etcelera ever show prospective clinical validation? Will harmonized real-world data change a single indication or eligibility decision on the record? And will Isomorphic hit its own year-end target? On clinical success rates, the realistic potential looks like a modest, multi-year story, not a breakthrough. Nothing in the evidence argues for more yet. For R&D spend, the budget line doesn't shrink. The same dollars buy a somewhat better hit rate, slowly, so cost per approval is the number that moves. Three things to watch into next week. First, Isomorphic's clock. Second, any primary methodology Novartis publishes behind its chief executive's view. Third, whether Etcelera discloses financing or a first nominated program.

That's the briefing. Stay cautiously optimistic, and have a good Friday.
