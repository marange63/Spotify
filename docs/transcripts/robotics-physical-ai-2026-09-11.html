Robotics & Physical AI

Transcript · September 11, 2026

Good morning. It's September 11th, 2026, and this is your briefing on robotics and physical AI.

This beat has spent five straight briefings asking physical AI the same two questions. What does a deployed system actually succeed at, and does anyone pay for it? On September 10th, in a post on Nvidia's blog, Skild AI answered both at once. That is our lead. The answer is more interesting than the headline, though not in the direction the headline points.

Here is the claim as published. Skild's robot foundation model, called S1, takes a single video demonstration of a task and executes it. No weight updates. No task-specific post-training. Nvidia's post says Skild can go from recording that demonstration to autonomous execution on hardware in eleven minutes. On new multistep tasks, S1 succeeded at each step about 66 percent of the time, against 9 percent for what the post calls a similar AI system — more than a sevenfold improvement.

Now discipline that number, because Skild's own August research post changes what the comparison is. The 9 percent is not a rival product, and it is not an anonymous strawman. It is Skild's own language-prompted policy, the same architecture pre-trained on the same data, measured with no post-training at all. That configuration gets 9 percent cold. But the same post discloses what happens when you give the identical architecture conventional post-training, meaning two thousand human demonstrations instead of one video. It reaches 86 percent. That beats S1's headline 66 percent outright. Skild's own words are that post-training "does pass it eventually," and the company expects the gap to close as it scales. So the sevenfold figure is real, but it measures S1 against a deliberately untrained version of itself, not against the field. Against a conventionally trained version of the same model, S1 currently loses on reliability.

Two more things about the protocol. Skild's evaluation used long-horizon tasks of four to eight minutes, with human intervention to reset failed steps so grading could continue. So neither the 66 nor the 9 describes one unassisted run from start to finish. Both are per-step rates inside an assisted evaluation. And per-step compounds. Sixty-six percent at each step of a five-step task is roughly one completed task in eight, run end to end without help.

Listeners will recognize that rate. On September 6th this show covered DeepMind's disclosure that Gemini Robotics 2 picked an object off a table 68.4 percent of the time, and argued that a robot failing one pick in three cannot run an unattended shift. Skild's number lands in the same place. Two labs, different architectures, the same two in three.

So what the eleven minutes actually buys is not reliability. It is data efficiency and setup speed. One demonstration instead of two thousand, and eleven minutes instead of a post-training pipeline. That is a genuine product. It is simply not the one the sevenfold framing implies.

Note the channel, too. This disclosure reaches us through a blog post by Skild's own compute supplier, which has a direct commercial interest in the result. The detail that undercuts the headline sits in Skild's own research post, not in the promotional one.

The other half of the disclosure is money. Skild says it has reached a hundred-million-dollar annualized revenue run-rate, ten months after its first commercial deployment, across more than sixty deployment partnerships. Apply the usual discipline. A run-rate is an annualized snapshot, not booked revenue, and none of this is audited. But part of it is corroborated outside the Nvidia channel. Bloomberg reports that Skild's software now runs on hundreds of robots at more than sixty companies. Earlier this year that figure was eight. So the customer count has independent support, even if the dollar number remains a company claim. What nobody discloses is how many of those sixty-plus are paying production deployments rather than pilots. Here is the takeaway anyway. This is the most credible revenue claim commercial embodied AI has produced, and it landed on the model layer rather than on the machine. What those customers are buying is cheaper integration, not fewer failures.

That has a first casualty, and it isn't factory labor. It is the systems-integration shops that bill for exactly the work a one-video setup removes. Margin pressure lands there long before anyone runs an unattended shift.

Follow the money to the other side of the trade. This show covered Figure AI's compute deal with Nscale on September 4th, so take it as a comparison rather than as news. An initial commitment of 3.5 billion dollars toward Nvidia's next-generation Vera Rubin chips, with stated intent to scale past 6 billion. Skild's claimed hundred million is about one thirty-fifth of that single initial check. That gap is the shape of the whole sector right now. Revenue where the capital is thin, capital where the revenue is thin.

Which raises the obvious question. How big is the end market these machines are being financed against? Take the most commercially mature deployment in embodied AI, and the most favorable case available: Waymo. Its 500,000 paid rides a week across fourteen cities is a figure this beat has used twice already, so the new element is the target. One million weekly rides by the end of this year. Now size that against Goldman Sachs's forecast for the market it serves. Goldman puts the entire US robotaxi market at roughly 3 billion dollars in 2027. That is one bank's house view, not consensus, and TAM forecasts on this beat have a poor record. But take it at face value. It is smaller than the initial compute commitment one humanoid company signed this month, for a market Waymo has spent more than a decade building toward.

Two cautions before that lands. Waymo publishes no cost per mile and no intervention rate, so a doubling of rides is not evidence of a doubling in contribution margin. Scaling and subsidy look identical from outside. And Zoox, Amazon's robotaxi unit, is still entering Houston and San Diego with human safety drivers aboard for mapping rather than launching driverless. That is a reminder of how thin the field behind Waymo remains. So what it means: the most mature commercial application in embodied AI cannot underwrite frontier-scale compute spending on its own. The financing case has to rest on markets that do not have revenue yet.

Close on the denominator underneath all of it. By the estimate of one industry tracker, SAG, roughly 19,100 humanoid robots shipped globally in the first half of this year. That is up 272 percent from about 5,100 a year earlier. Two Chinese vendors account for about three-quarters of the total. AGIBOT shipped roughly 8,400 units, overtaking Unitree's roughly 5,900. More than 70 percent of those shipments went into industrial and commercial use rather than research. Treat that as standing context rather than today's news. It is first-half data, from a single tracker, and units shipped are not revenue-bearing deployments. But it bears directly on Skild's pitch. In-context learning feeds on real-world operating hours. If three-quarters of the world's working humanoids ship from Chinese vendors onto industrial floors, that operating data is accumulating outside the reach of the companies claiming to exploit it. No amount of US training compute solves a data-access problem.

Pull the threads together. What inflected this week is the cost of teaching a robot a new task, not the reliability of doing it once taught. Read in full, Skild's own numbers show a conventionally trained version of its own architecture still beating its flagship result. Meanwhile revenue is appearing on the software layer of this sector while capital piles onto compute and machines. That is the commercial analogue of the pattern this beat traced in defense for most of the week. Three things to watch. Whether anyone here publishes a success rate against a named outside system rather than an internal ablation. Whether any third party audits a run-rate claim in this sector. And whether Waymo's million-rides target actually prints by year-end.
