Good morning. It's Wednesday, November 18th, 2026, and this is your private equity briefing.

All week we've been cataloguing how sponsors engineer liquidity when a clean exit won't come. Continuation vehicles have reached down the quality curve. Evergreen funds have rationed redemptions pro rata. And last week, GFL Environmental, the Canadian waste hauler, rejected a take-private bid from a consortium of buyout firms. The company offered instead to help its private-equity shareholders sell their stakes. Those holders get a route to cash, but not the control premium a full buyout would have paid. Bloomberg's reporting yesterday adds a new tool to that kit. This one runs straight through the portfolio company's own balance sheet.

Two European portfolio companies are borrowing to pay their private-equity owners. Somacis is the Italian circuit-board maker. Bain Capital agreed to buy a controlling stake in it in 2024, and we've found no report of a sale since. Somacis is raising one point zero three billion euros of floating-rate bonds. Per Bloomberg, the money will redeem existing debt and fund a shareholder distribution. Triton-owned MacGregor is raising six hundred million euros for a refinancing and a dividend. Note what the Somacis figure covers. It's the size of the whole bond, not the size of the payout. Nobody has disclosed the split between the two.

Why now? The answer is the distribution drought. CNBC, citing Moody's, reported in mid-October that private-equity distributions have dropped from more than thirty percent of starting net asset value in 2022 to about twelve percent today. Callan's separate read covers 2025. It puts distributions that year at twelve percent of beginning NAV, against a long-run average of roughly twenty. Those are two different measures on two different clocks, so don't stitch them into one series. But they point the same way. Limited partners are getting cash back at roughly a third of the 2022 rate, and well below the long-run norm.

Now, how far to generalize. CNBC's October piece said the data suggest funds are increasingly pushing portfolio companies to take on debt to pay investors. Moody's says dividend-recap activity has surged since 2022. But Moody's singles out Europe, the Middle East and Africa as where it expects that activity to stay high. There's no reliable count of recap volume yet, and the US pace may actually be slower. So treat Somacis and MacGregor as named deals in a European year-end window, not as evidence of a global acceleration.

Now the mechanism, and why it differs from the rest of the kit. A dividend recap produces distributions to paid-in capital without anyone buying the asset. No buyer has tested the mark. The equity cushion inside the company shrinks by what gets paid out. And the debt that funded it stays on the company's books, waiting for whoever buys it next. Somacis's bonds are floating-rate. So the interest-rate risk on that payout sits with the company, not the fund.

Compare the other tools. A continuation vehicle trades a GP conflict of interest for liquidity. A stub-stake sale like GFL's trades away the control premium. A gated redemption simply rations access. The recap is the one tool that pays for liquidity with leverage. And it's the one that makes the eventual exit harder.

Here's why it makes the exit harder. A recap raises net debt. At any given enterprise value, that leaves less equity for the next buyer to pay for. And the asset arrives at its next sale more levered than before. Last week showed what heavy debt can do to a deal. Clayton, Dubilier and Rice and GTCR walked away from a take-private of Global Payments. The one specific obstacle an analyst flagged was a debt load swollen by a prior acquisition. That's a different mechanism from a recap, but the lesson carries. Debt taken on today becomes a feasibility problem for the next owner.

There's a fair competing read, and it deserves plain airtime. A dividend recap isn't inherently a distress signal. A company that has deleveraged and generates real cash can rationally pull some of it out through new debt. And these bonds are coming to market at all. That says the debt window is open, even while the equity exit window isn't. So the concern isn't that recaps are reckless. It's that a recap gets chosen instead of a sale. That choice quietly raises the stakes for whoever eventually has to exit.

Which leads to a second-order point worth banking now. Recap-funded distributions can lift headline DPI heading into next year's fundraising season. They do it with zero improvement in the underlying exit environment. Limited partners weighing a 2027 commitment should separate exit-sourced cash from recap-sourced cash in anything a manager shows them. That's our inference, not anyone's disclosure.

The managers themselves aren't hiding the underlying problem. On Carlyle's third-quarter call, chief executive Harvey Schwartz said, quote, "it's still going to take the industry a bit of time to digest the capital that is a bit trapped." That is the industry's own diagnosis of why a recap looks attractive right now. Note who he was talking about, though. He framed it as the industry's problem, not Carlyle's. A manager diagnosing everyone else's trapped capital is also, in part, marketing.

One correction on Carlyle. Last Friday, this briefing said Carlyle's fee-related earnings fell from a year earlier. That came from wire coverage. Carlyle's own call gave two hundred ninety-three million dollars, at a forty-seven percent margin, with no year-over-year comparison. So treat the decline as unverified, not wrong.

That's how cash gets out of old deals. Now the other end: what still gets bought.

The Financial Times reported yesterday that TPG is near a take-private of BILL Holdings, the fintech payments company. The talks could still be delayed, or collapse entirely. BILL's backstory matters here. Activist investors Elliott Management and Starboard Value became top-ten shareholders last year and pushed the company to consider a sale. BILL has since added four directors to its board. Its market value has fallen more than eighty percent from a 2021 peak of about thirty-four billion dollars. In other words, the company is now worth less than a fifth of that peak. Nothing is signed, and no premium or per-share price is confirmed. So we can say nothing yet about entry valuation beyond that public de-rating.

The shape still fits the pattern this desk drew last week. The big take-privates that stumbled were large tickets. Global Payments, the heavily levered target, lost its bidders. GFL Environmental's board refused a premium on price and chose to stay public. BILL is the mirror image of GFL. There, the board said no. Here, activists have pushed the board toward the sale table. That's our inference about what clears in this market. It isn't full-price megadeals. It's companies the public market has already marked down, brought to the table by their own shareholders. In a thin deal market, take-private supply may increasingly come from pressured boards rather than willing ones.

There's a valuation point buried here too. A sponsor buying BILL would enter at the public market's markdown. The public tape has already done the repricing. Private software companies bought at 2021 prices face no such tape. Their marks move on a manager's model, not on a buyer's bid. Outsiders can't see those marks. But a signed BILL deal would put a hard price on a de-rated public software name. Limited partners holding 2021-vintage software should ask what that price implies for their own books.

So where does the cycle actually stand? The exit-and-distribution logjam isn't closing. It's being bridged with partial tools and borrowed time, and now with borrowed money. The biggest unresolved risk is that recap-funded distributions flatter the DPI numbers managers will raise their next funds on. None of that requires the exit window to have reopened. The market is complacent if it reads any uptick in distributions as proof exits are back. It may be over-discounting if it treats every dividend recap as distress. Some of this is simply healthy companies using an open credit market.

Watch three things. First, whether TPG and BILL actually sign, and at what premium. Second, whether more European dividend recaps price before year-end. Third, whether Blackstone delivers the significant step-up in fourth-quarter realizations its own executives guided to.

That's the briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
