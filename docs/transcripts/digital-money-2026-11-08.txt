Good morning, it's Sunday, November 8th, 2026, and this is Cautious Optimism Briefings.

The headline this week isn't that crypto fell. It's that the cash is actually leaving. The on-chain dollar just posted its steepest weekly contraction of the year, and the spot Bitcoin ETFs leaked right alongside it. Those are crypto's two institutional cash doors, and both were open at the same time.

Start with Bitcoin, because price moved first. We covered Thursday and Friday's slide to a seven-week low near $76,000 already, along with the liquidation tape, so that's all the re-grounding it needs. What's new is the flow confirmation. On Friday we set a watch item. Would Thursday's ETF outflow extend into a second session, and would Farside confirm the number? Both questions have now resolved.

Farside shows a net outflow of $430.2 million on Thursday. It then lists a second outflow of $411.9 million on Friday. Those two sessions followed a single inflow day on Wednesday of just $74.6 million. By our arithmetic, that's about $842 million out in two sessions, more than ten times the inflow that came before it. And the backdrop was already weak. The first half of this year was the first negative half-year since these funds launched.

Two caveats keep this honest. Farside's same-day rows do get revised, so treat the exact figures as provisional. And two sessions are a signal, not a stampede.

There's also a competing explanation, and this one is our inference rather than a reported finding. In a deleveraging week, some ETF redemptions can be basis-trade unwinds. That's a desk that was long the fund and short the futures, closing both legs at once. If that's what happened, the outflow says more about funding conditions than about institutional conviction in Bitcoin. We can't test it today, because we don't have clean futures open-interest or funding data for those two sessions. Either way, the regulated wrapper was a net seller two days running. The product built to make Bitcoin a boring institutional holding moved with leveraged risk appetite, not against it.

The cash sitting next to that wrapper, on-chain, moved the same way, and faster. That's today's lead.

Total stablecoin supply fell $6.66 billion in the seven days through Saturday, per figures reported by Cryptonews. That's the steepest weekly drop of the year. Tether and Circle together account for about two-thirds of it. This desk has tracked the leak all month. On Tuesday we noted it predated the Binance hack. Yesterday we said it still hadn't found a floor. Now it has accelerated.

Put that in scale. DefiLlama shows the total float peaking at a record $315.5 billion in mid-May, and sitting at about $290.1 billion now. By our arithmetic, that's a drawdown of roughly $25 billion over nearly six months. One week just did about a quarter of it. One caution on sourcing. The weekly figure and the levels come from different publications, and aggregators disagree on totals by billions. So bank the direction and the rough magnitude, not the decimals. Even on that standard, this is acceleration, not drift.

Now look at the shape, because it breaks the obvious narrative. Working from the issuer-level figures, by our arithmetic, Tether's USDT shrank about 1.7 percent on the week. Circle's USDC shrank about 1.8 percent. Those are almost identical rates. If this were a flight to quality, money would have moved from offshore Tether into the more heavily supervised Circle dollar, and USDC would have held up better. It didn't. Last Tuesday we called USDC the most regulated stablecoin in the market. This week, that status bought it no stickiness at all. The remaining third of the drop came out of smaller stablecoins, which points the same way. This was a uniform de-grossing of on-chain cash, not a credit scare at one issuer.

What caused it, we can't say. The week overlapped both Binance's phased reopening of withdrawals and the broader risk-off selloff. Nothing in the data ties the contraction to either one, and we couldn't find chain-level or exchange-level evidence of where the redemptions happened. There's a measurement point too. Market cap counts what's outstanding. It isn't a direct count of who redeemed. If a handful of large holders drove it, this is trading-desk and market-maker deleveraging, not retail flight. We don't have the holder-level data to tell. So treat the cause as open.

Here's the bull case, stated plainly, because it's real. Both tokens held their peg through the steepest contraction week of the year. CoinGecko showed each within about a tenth of a cent of a dollar as the week closed. Billions of dollars of supply came off at par, in an orderly week. That's the narrow-bank structure working as designed. An orderly shrink is not a run.

That also sets the size of the Treasury read-through, and it's smaller than it sounds. Tether's third-quarter attestation shows $132.4 billion in direct and indirect Treasury bill holdings as of the end of September. Tether's float fell roughly $3 billion this week. By our arithmetic, that's a little over 2 percent of its bill book. The attestation is five weeks old, so it sets scale, not the current level. The direction still holds. A shrinking float means marginally less bill demand. But this week was a trim at the short end, not a liquidity event.

The issuer earnings story is sharper, and it predates this week. Circle's third-quarter filing shows reserve income of $657.6 million, down from $711.2 million a year earlier. Press coverage attributes that decline to lower yields on reserves, partly offset by a larger average float. Now put this week on top of it. Circle was already losing on rate. This week it started losing on float as well. That's a two-sided squeeze on Circle's core revenue line, independent of any USDC adoption story.

The same float sits under another public company. Coinbase reported an average of $22 billion in USDC held in its products in the third quarter, up 49 percent from a year earlier. Against today's USDC float of about $70.6 billion, that's a little under a third, by rough arithmetic. The windows don't match, since one is a quarterly average and the other a current spot level, so call the ratio approximate. Coinbase doesn't break out a separate stablecoin revenue line, so we can't size the dollar sensitivity. But the exposure is clear in direction. The stablecoin business that Coinbase bulls point to as its durable, non-trading line sits on exactly the float that just contracted.

One more channel, briefly, and it's the venue at the center of last month's stress. Binance's recovery from its roughly $6.3 billion hack is still incomplete. As of Friday's update, eight days after the theft, Hyperliquid's HYPE token was still missing from the list of restored withdrawals. Binance's own initial findings trace the breach to a compromised engineer's computer and credentials stolen from a wallet operations engineer. Our read is that this was an operational, human failure, not a cryptographic one. That's the strongest argument for regulated custody wrappers over exchange balances. The awkward part is the first half of this briefing. Those regulated wrappers didn't hold on to money this week either. All of the Binance detail is the exchange's own account, with no outside audit in what we have. The takeaway stands regardless. Recovery from the largest exchange theft on record is still not finished.

Pull it together. Yesterday we argued that crypto's stress was relocating, into the equities, funds, and corporate balance sheets built around Bitcoin. This week's data shows the next stage. Money left through the ETF door and the stablecoin door at the same time. So far it's leaving in an orderly way, at par and without a break. But it is leaving. Three things to watch from here. First, whether stablecoin supply prints another down week. Second, whether the ETF outflow run extends past two sessions. Third, whether Binance finally restores HYPE.

That's Cautious Optimism Briefings for Sunday, November 8th. Stay cautiously optimistic.
