Good morning. It's Friday, November 6th, and this is your private credit briefing.

On Wednesday this desk told you the loan books still reported as broadly benign, and that the stress was sitting in the funding. That read no longer holds. A rating agency's surveillance data, published yesterday, puts the deterioration back inside the books.

KBRA's third-quarter Middle Market Compendium came out November 5th, and the news is in ratings migration, not the default count. Downgrades hit a record 121 in the quarter. That lifted KBRA's downgrade rate to 15 percent, from 11 percent the quarter before. New assessments rated CCC-plus or lower jumped to a record 31 percent, up from 19 percent in the second quarter. One caution on that second record. KBRA itself notes that some new assessments may reflect its first review of a seasoned loan. So part of the CCC jump may describe which existing borrowers are coming in for a rating, not what lenders are originating today. The downgrade record has no such escape hatch. Those are loans KBRA already watches, moving the wrong way.

Now the headline rate, which invites overstatement. KBRA's default monitor posted its largest quarterly gain on record. That pushed the rate by count to about 3.6 percent. It's the highest of 2026, but still below the early-2025 peak of 3.9 percent. So call it a 2026 high, not an all-time record. And KBRA describes the monitor as a forward-looking gauge. It counts borrowers in payment default, plus those that likely would be without sponsor or lender intervention. It's a stress measure, not a pure missed-payment rate.

Where the rate really bites is at the bottom of the market. KBRA's smallest middle-market borrowers run at 5.8 percent, by both count and debt. The whole portfolio runs at 3.6 percent by count and just 2.4 percent by debt. That's the segment where valuation marks are least observable and most dependent on manager discretion. It also fits something this desk argued on Tuesday. As stronger credits refinance out into the syndicated market, the private book is left holding its weaker tier. KBRA's split doesn't prove that mechanism. But it shows the stress concentrating exactly where the mechanism says it should.

Then there's leverage, and this is the number to sit with. Median leverage across KBRA's middle-market book rose to 6.2 times. That's its first increase in more than two years, and it came in a quarter of record downgrades. There are two ways to read it, and today we can't pick one. The late-cycle reading is that lenders are loosening terms to win deal flow even as credit quality worsens. The less sinister reading is that earnings are softening at existing borrowers. In that case the same debt load simply measures as higher leverage, with no change in lending behavior at all. KBRA doesn't attribute the rise to either cause. One steadying detail: median interest coverage held at 1.6 times, so the leverage uptick hasn't yet reached debt service. So the deterioration is now measurable inside the loan books, and it's worst exactly where nobody can see a price.

That read has company. Last Saturday we covered Fitch's record 6.3 percent private credit default rate, and Fitch said its middle-market book drove the increase. KBRA now finds the stress in the same place, with a different portfolio and a different definition. Treat that as corroboration of where the stress sits, not a second rate to stack on the first.

And the money keeps coming regardless. On Wednesday, Blackstone announced the final close of its fourth European direct-lending fund, with 13 billion euros of investable capital. Note the packaging. That figure spans the fund and related vehicles, and it includes anticipated leverage. The capital is going into closed-end institutional vehicles. Meanwhile, trade coverage describes some US direct-lending funds hit by redemption requests over software exposure and loan quality. One link, and it is inference only. More committed capital chasing the same pool of deals is one plausible reason leverage is ticking up in KBRA's data.

That software story deserves a second look. KBRA's two most stressed sectors are Commercial and Professional Services, and a combined Health Care Services and Technology bucket. Together they accounted for 16 of the quarter's 35 additions to the default monitor. KBRA doesn't break out software, so its data can't refute the software narrative. But business services isn't a software story. The strain looks broader than the market's tidy explanation implies.

So does the agency tape show up in the managers' own books? This week's answer is: unevenly. Start with Blue Owl's listed BDC, the print we were still waiting on Wednesday. Non-accruals came in at just 0.8 percent of the portfolio at fair value. At cost, they're 2.9 percent. Management said no new issuers went onto non-accrual this quarter. And PIK fell to 9.9 percent of total investment income, from 10.7 percent. By its own headline measures, Blue Owl had a cleaner quarter.

The cost-to-fair-value gap is the part to watch. On Wednesday we called a one-point gap at Blackstone's BCRED the valuation question in miniature. Blue Owl's gap is roughly twice that. There are two live readings. One is that Blue Owl is marking its troubled names down hard. That's conservative, and it argues against the complaint that private marks never move. The other is that the loss already embedded in the book is larger than the 0.8 percent headline suggests. And "no new non-accruals" is management's own claim, which sits awkwardly against KBRA's record downgrade quarter.

Contrast that with Kayne Anderson's BDC. Its non-accruals rose to 3.6 percent of debt investments at fair value, from 2.7 percent at the end of June. It also booked a 14.9 million dollar realized loss tied to a restructuring. Same asset class, same quarter, opposite direction. Then there's Barings' BDC. Net investment income came in at 26 cents a share, exactly matching its dividend. That's not a cut. But there's no cushion left, so any further non-accrual drift goes straight to the distribution. The takeaway: private credit is not a monolith this earnings season. Manager selection is doing more work than the asset-class label, and the marks are the variable that matters.

Public loans show what an actual price signal looks like. Per PitchBook LCD data, the leveraged loan distress ratio jumped 96 basis points in October, to 8.01 percent. Meanwhile, the ratio of downgrades to upgrades eased slightly, to 1.09 times. Some of that distress jump may reflect market-wide price moves rather than credit deterioration. And the easing downgrade ratio is real evidence against a broad turn in syndicated credit. Still, the mechanism holds. In a market that trades daily, price moves ahead of downgrades and defaults. Private credit has no equivalent signal. That absence is exactly why the next item matters more than its size suggests.

An unidentified holder is auctioning roughly 500 million dollars of data-center loans, about a dozen of them, per Bloomberg's report as relayed by other outlets. The package includes debt tied to Vantage Data Centers and CyrusOne. It also includes a 25 million dollar slice backing Project Jupiter, the Stargate campus in New Mexico. Oracle has already sent that site's developer a force majeure notice. Bids of at least 10 million dollars are due November 19th.

This is the AI-credit arc moving from insurance to outright sale. Last Saturday, banks were seeking credit insurance on AI debt, and some insurers said they were nearly full on data-center exposure. On Wednesday, Waymo's financing showed private lenders pricing at the wide end of middle-market spreads. Now comes one of the first sales to bundle data-center loans exclusively. A market, not a manager's model, finally gets a vote on AI-capex debt.

This cuts both ways, and no clearing price exists yet. A weak print would be the first hard evidence that private marks on data-center debt lag reality. A print near par would support the industry's claim that the underlying project credit is sound, despite public-market jitters. Here's my inference on where that price travels. A traded level on loans tied to named operators hands other holders of similar paper an observable comparable. Direct lenders, BDCs, and insurance-backed vehicles would then have to reconcile their model marks against it. Two limits, though. The seller is unknown, and rebalancing is as plausible a motive as distress. And the Oracle notice is tied to one site, so don't read it as a verdict on Stargate or on data-center credit broadly.

Pulling it together. The cycle has moved from stress in the funding to measurable deterioration inside middle-market loan books. It's concentrated in the smallest borrowers, and it's arriving alongside a leverage rise nobody has explained. It is not yet a payment-default turn, and it is uneven across managers. The biggest unresolved risk is that the weakest, least-observable borrowers are deteriorating while leverage rises. And the only people marking that paper are the lenders who made the loans. Watch three things. First, the November 19th auction bids. Second, the calendar third-quarter BDC prints we don't yet have in hand, including FS KKR and Golub. Third, whether Barings' dividend coverage holds next quarter.

That's the briefing. Have a good weekend.
