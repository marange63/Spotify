Good morning, this is your Cautious Optimism Briefing on semiconductors and compute for Sunday, November 22nd.

We owe you a correction first. Yesterday this desk said nothing in the record pinned Friday's chip selloff on a single catalyst. That was wrong. On Friday afternoon, Reuters reported that SEC enforcement staff are, in its words, "scrutinizing potential accounting issues" at Nvidia. The report rests on two anonymous people briefed on the matter. Reuters could not determine when the inquiry began, or which accounting issues are under review.

The Wall Street Journal reported earlier in the week that the SEC was probing Nvidia, but it couldn't say what the probe was about. Reuters is the first to put the word accounting on it. Neither party has confirmed the report on the record. An SEC spokesperson declined to comment. Nvidia's spokespeople did not immediately respond to requests for comment. Reuters itself notes that investigations are not evidence of wrongdoing, and that many close with no enforcement action. The inquiry's stage is unreported, so call it an inquiry, not a formal investigation. No restatement and no charge has been reported.

Now the market move, and why we call the report an accelerant rather than the whole story. Per market reporting, Nvidia was already down about 6 percent intraday on Friday before the Reuters story hit. That slide came from a broader AI selloff. Nvidia then closed down 14 percent, at a new one-year low. So the report didn't start the fall. It more than doubled it.

For scale, the S&P 500 fell 3.7 percent the same day. Nvidia's drop was nearly four times the broad market's. On our read, much of that gap is Nvidia-specific. But a crowded, high-beta stock would have lagged badly anyway. The evidence can't yet separate a suspect beat from a routine staff inquiry that a jittery tape amplified.

Here's why it matters beyond one stock. On November 21st, this desk named what would falsify our read that AI demand hadn't broken. It was a guidance cut from a major vendor, or an actual order cancellation. Friday brought a risk our scorecard had no line for. The question isn't whether hyperscalers are still buying. It's whether the reported numbers can be relied on. This is the company that just reported fiscal third-quarter revenue of 115.8 billion dollars, up 103 percent from a year ago. That's the cleanest demand signal in the AI buildout. An inquiry doesn't say that number is wrong, and nothing reported does. But on our read, it now carries a credibility discount it didn't carry a week ago.

Follow that discount down the supply chain, and the stakes widen. TSMC's packaging plans and the memory makers' HBM commitments are priced, in part, off Nvidia's reported demand. Suppose this report firms up into a formal investigation. Those suppliers wouldn't lose orders on day one. They'd lose the signal used to size the buildout. That's conditional inference, and we're not guessing at what the SEC is examining. The so-what: the question has moved from how much AI silicon sells to whether the reported sales can be trusted.

If the question at Nvidia is whether its revenue can be trusted, the question in Washington is where revenue ends up. On that front, AMD just became easier to price.

First, what Washington's rule does. The Commerce Department's new rule, issued November 16th, requires licenses for AI chips and servers sent, in TechCrunch's summary, "to data centers anywhere in the world if they may provide compute to companies in China or other restricted countries." It also requires a license for advanced chips sold to companies headquartered in high-smuggling-risk countries, Singapore among them. We call that route the corridor: US silicon reaching Chinese users through offshore data centers.

Back on November 17th, this desk said that corridor was unsized. None of the big merchant vendors published a current figure for it. This week, a short seller filled in one vendor. Per Reuters' coverage of Hunterbrook Media's report on AMD, the chipmaker billed customers in China and Hong Kong 7.75 billion dollars in its last fiscal year. That was 22.4 percent of its revenue.

AMD also billed 4.28 billion dollars in Singapore, one of the rule's named countries. On our own arithmetic, Singapore alone is about 12 percent of AMD's revenue. Together, China, Hong Kong and Singapore come to roughly a third of AMD's sales.

Contrast that with Nvidia. As this desk reported on November 17th, Nvidia switched its geographic reporting from billing location to customer headquarters. Singapore no longer appears in its breakdown. Reuters notes that this reporting change has drawn renewed press scrutiny. Nobody has reported that it's what the SEC is examining. So AMD is now the one vendor whose Singapore billings the market can see.

Two caveats matter more than the headline. First, these are fiscal 2025 figures, relayed through a short seller's report, not a fresh AMD disclosure. Second, and this is our read, billing location is not end use. Singapore billings often reflect regional hubs, distributors or contract manufacturers, not where the chips are deployed. The new rule keys instead on where the buyer is headquartered and who receives the compute. So this is a proxy for AMD's exposure to the rule, not a measure of revenue at risk. And the proxy can miss in both directions. Hub billing would overstate the exposure. A China-headquartered buyer invoiced somewhere else would understate it.

Here's the asymmetry worth banking, again as our read. AMD's visibility cuts both ways. It's a real overhang for AMD, and it makes AMD the easiest name in the group to short. Meanwhile, its peers' corridor exposure stays invisible. Until vendors disclose by end customer, the market will price this corridor by proxy. And the most transparent vendor will wear the most visible discount.

But exposure only matters if the rule reaches where the compute goes. And reporting out Saturday found an edge it doesn't reach.

This week the export perimeter moved outward three times. First it went to the compute destination, with the data-center rule described earlier. Next it moved into the fab. Since Thursday, a second Commerce rule has removed TSMC's ability to self-certify that a customer's design falls outside US export controls. Covered Chinese customers now need a US license for advanced-node orders. Then, by allegation only, the perimeter reached into after-sale firmware. Hunterbrook discloses a short position in AMD. It alleges that AMD firmware kept reaching IEIT Systems, a blacklisted Chinese server maker. It also says nearly identical files appear in servers from three companies that aren't blacklisted. AMD denies it, and the claim is unproven.

Fortune's reporting out Saturday locates the layer the rule still doesn't touch: the model. In Fortune's words, "U.S.-owned data centers filled with U.S. chips still rent capacity out to inference providers, which use that capability to serve Chinese large language models to global users." In plain terms, the chips never leave US-controlled data centers. A Chinese-built model still serves a global customer base.

Treat this carefully. It's a single secondary report. It names no providers, no models and no capacity figures, so don't try to size it. There's also a genuine counter-reading. The hardware stays in US hands, and the Chinese lab is never the customer. On that view, this may sit outside what export control was meant to police. Calling it a loophole is a framing choice, not a settled fact. Either way, the rule governs who rents the compute, not whose model runs on top of it.

Now the second-order effect, which is our inference and stays unsized. China's access to the hardware is constrained. Its models can still compete for global usage on American GPUs. So some slice of US inference demand comes from the popularity of Chinese models. Suppose Commerce's next move closes this layer. The first hit wouldn't land on Chinese chip buyers. It would land on the utilization of the US inference providers renting out that capacity. The so-what: closing that gap would carry a domestic cost.

Here's the scorecard, by the dimension that actually moved. On integrity of reported numbers, Nvidia has a new and entirely unproven question mark. On disclosed China exposure, AMD is now sized by proxy, while its peers remain a guess. On policy reach, the rule has a gap at the model layer. And on demand itself, the record is still intact, with no reported guidance cut and no canceled order.

What to watch next. First, any on-record word from Nvidia or the SEC confirming or denying Friday's report. Second, any guidance from Commerce on whether serving a foreign model counts as providing compute under the rule. Third, whether any vendor starts disclosing exposure by end customer rather than billing address.

That's your briefing. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
