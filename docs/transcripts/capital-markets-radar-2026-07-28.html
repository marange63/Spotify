Capital Markets Cross-Asset Radar

Transcript · July 28, 2026

Good morning, it's Tuesday, July 28th, and here's your capital markets radar.

All week we've been saying the Fed-hike scare was really an oil story, not a Fed story. On Monday, oil confirmed it. Brent crude fell 8.7 percent to close at $88.36 a barrel, and WTI dropped 7.5 percent to settle at $82.61, after Iran reportedly said it would suspend attacks as long as the US pause in hostilities holds. That single session unwound nearly two weeks of an escalating US-Iran conflict that had pushed crude above $100 and put Strait of Hormuz shipping at risk. We called this a pause, not a reversal, on the 25th, and said traders weren't buying it yet on the 27th. Now the de-escalation has enough follow-through to move price by high single digits in a day, one session before the Fed's July 29th decision. So the mechanism that had been doing the market's work for it just went into reverse.

Here's why that matters for rates. CME's FedWatch tool had a 25-basis-point July hike priced at just 10.7 percent on July 15th. By July 22nd that had more than tripled to 34.7 percent, and later readings put it as high as 46.5 percent, all as oil's spike fed straight into near-term inflation expectations. Goldman Sachs was framing the meeting, even before Monday's crude collapse, as roughly a 40 percent priced probability of a hike — uncertain enough that either a hike or a hold would be a large surprise by historical standards. Now, a crash of this size in the input that was doing the repricing should mechanically ease the case for a hawkish surprise. But I want to be careful here: I don't have a clean, verified reading of where hike odds actually sit after Monday's move. The reporting pointing to a sharper post-crash drop couldn't be confirmed against the source itself, so treat that as directional color, not a number to trade on. What we do know is the odds that got us to the high-30s and mid-40s were built entirely on an oil shock that has now partly unwound. That's the story — not a confirmed post-crash print.

So don't declare the hike dead. The Fed meets Wednesday with spreads and duration pricing that still reflect real two-way risk, and a committee that could just as easily look through a single-session commodity swing as chase it. The skeptical read cuts both ways, in fact. The same market that spent two weeks over-reading an oil spike into a 25-basis-point-hike scare could now be over-reading the reversal into an all-clear. Iran's suspension is explicitly conditional on the US pause remaining in place. That's not a ceasefire, it's a truce that both sides can break with one headline, and if it breaks, the entire chain — oil, hike odds, risk appetite — snaps back within days.

And here's the backdrop that sharpens the stakes on that two-way risk: credit. High-yield spreads are still sitting below 300 basis points, a level seen only about five percent of the time since 2000, and investment-grade spreads are in the bottom decile of everything since the financial crisis. That's a market priced for nothing to go wrong. It means there's almost no cushion in credit for a hawkish surprise. If the Fed hikes tomorrow, or holds but signals more tightening ahead, spreads this tight have far more room to widen than to compress. The asymmetry runs one way. Tight spreads pay you a little if the benign path holds, and punish you a lot if it doesn't — which is exactly the shape of the whole setup into Wednesday.

One tell that the market hasn't fully bought the all-clear: gold. Gold rose about 1 percent toward $4,100 an ounce on the very session oil collapsed, even though a lower inflation impulse and reduced tightening risk should, on paper, be a headwind for a non-yielding asset like gold. Gold is well off the roughly $4,700 peak it hit in late April at the height of the war scare, but a $4,100 handle holding through an oil crash tells you the geopolitical premium hasn't been fully unwound. If the de-escalation holds and the Fed leans hawkish tomorrow anyway, gold's next move depends on whether real rates or safe-haven demand wins that tug of war.

Now, the same de-risking-into-the-Fed impulse shows up in crypto, and it's worth a beat because the signal there is about positioning, not spot price. US spot Bitcoin ETFs took in $33.79 million in net inflows for the week of July 20th through the 24th. Spot Ether ETFs pulled in $103.9 million over the same week — roughly three times the Bitcoin total — the third straight week both categories logged net inflows. Ethereum touched a two-month high above $1,970 on July 27th, while Bitcoin sat essentially flat in a $65,000 to $65,300 range. Look inside the Bitcoin number and it gets more interesting: BlackRock's IBIT lost $95.5 million and Grayscale's GBTC lost $83.7 million in net outflows for the week, even as the broader Bitcoin ETF category stayed net-positive. That means the two largest, most established sponsors are bleeding share to smaller issuers while the category as a whole holds up. Read that as a concentration story as much as a bullish-Ether story — institutional allocators aren't necessarily rejecting Bitcoin, they're diversifying away from the biggest wrappers, and doing it into Ethereum's utility case: stablecoin settlement, DeFi, tokenization rails, rather than a pure price bet. It's one week of flow data, so treat the rotation as a signal to watch, not a confirmed trend, but it's the second week running this tilt has shown up, and it's sharper than last time.

One more thing worth a placeholder before the Fed takes over the tape entirely. The other live event landing in the same window as the FOMC is this week's Microsoft, Amazon, and Meta earnings. We've already walked through Alphabet's capex raise to $195 to $205 billion, and the roughly $800 billion single-day hit to Magnificent Seven market value that came with the AI-capex selloff — that's not new information, so I won't re-litigate it. What matters now is that those three prints are the actual test of whether the market's new skepticism toward AI capex without near-term profit conversion is an Alphabet-and-Tesla-specific story, or a sector-wide repricing of the entire hyperscaler cohort. That verdict is still open.

So here's where that leaves cross-asset positioning heading into Wednesday. Two contingent events are stacked in the same forty-eight hours: whether Iran's conditional pause actually holds, and what the Fed does with a genuinely two-sided setup. If both resolve benign, the relief we saw Monday in oil, rates, and risk appetite should hold and even extend. If the pause breaks, everything that just eased — hike odds, duration, risk appetite — re-tightens together, and gold's stubborn bid near $4,100 will look less like an anomaly and more like it was the smart money the whole time. Watch oil first. It's been the swing factor all week, and it will likely tell you which way Wednesday breaks before the Fed does.

That's the radar for July 28th. Trade carefully into the decision, and I'll see you next time.
