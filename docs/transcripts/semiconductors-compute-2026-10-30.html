Good morning. It's Friday, October 30th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

All week this desk has said the same thing. Intel's third-quarter print would be the test of whether any real volume sits behind the Tesla 14A headline. That print landed yesterday. The answer is thinner than the headline suggested, and it means correcting something we told you on Wednesday.

Start with what Intel's own earnings release, filed with the SEC, actually says. Total revenue was 16.5 billion dollars, up 21 percent from a year ago. That's a solid top line. The forward guide is softer. Intel is forecasting fourth-quarter revenue of 15 to 16 billion dollars, so even the top of the range sits below the quarter it just reported. That is a sequential step down at every point in the guide. It's not the shape you'd expect from a company about to be lifted by new foundry volume.

The Foundry segment is where the Tesla question lives. It brought in 6.7 billion dollars, up 58 percent year over year. On its face, that looks like traction. But the release names no outside customer anywhere. So most of that revenue is likely intersegment wafer sales, meaning Intel's own product groups buying from Intel's own fabs. Segment growth can't, on its own, tell you outsiders are arriving.

On process, the release points to the Intel 14A 0.9 process design kit, released in October. That's genuine engineering progress, and it's what prospective customers need to start designing. But a PDK is an invitation to design. It is not a commitment from anyone to buy wafers.

Now the part that matters more: what the filing doesn't say. Intel names no external foundry customer. Not Tesla, not anyone else. The only relevant language is a forward-looking risk factor. It describes Intel's "efforts to secure product design wins with and demand commitments from potential significant external customers." That's a company describing an unresolved effort, not announcing a win.

Here's the correction. On Wednesday this desk said the Tesla engagement was now framed as Intel's own statement. That was wrong. Intel's filing never says it. The Tesla claim rests on press reporting only. On the ladder this desk uses, running from announced to sampled to qualified to volume production, it doesn't clear the first rung in Intel's own words.

And even if Tesla is confirmed, keep it in proportion. Tesla is a physical-AI and automotive buyer, not a hyperscaler-scale wafer customer. A win there would say more about Intel reaching niches outside TSMC's core than about Intel competing for AI accelerators.

The timing makes the silence louder. Intel's annual report says customers are expected to begin making 14A decisions in the second half of 2026. We are now inside that window, with two months left, and still no name. As this desk noted Wednesday, that same annual filing warns Intel may pause or discontinue leading-edge development if it can't land a significant external 14A customer. The window and the warning are now running on the same clock. Note too that yesterday's release gives no 14A production date at all.

The equity story, at least, resolves cleanly. The 23 billion dollar equity raise in yesterday's release is the August 20 billion dollar offering this desk cited Wednesday, plus the underwriters' full option. One deal, not two. So it isn't a sign that Intel's funding is accelerating.

On manufacturing, Intel's annual report says only that 18A yields continue to improve, a direction with no level attached. On capital, the money coming in is equity, not customer prepayments.

Put the pieces together and Intel's foundry is being financed by its shareholders, not its customers. The equity is already in the door. The customer side is a risk factor acknowledging the company is still trying. For the CHIPS-era reshoring thesis, that's the uncomfortable part. The second Western leading-edge source everyone wants Intel to become is, for now, an option funded by dilution, not a business anchored by orders.

And the bar Intel has to clear keeps rising. On its October 15th call, TSMC said its N2 family's annual capacity will more than double in 2027. That's two weeks old, but it's the right yardstick. A prospective 14A customer isn't weighing Intel against today's TSMC. It's weighing Intel against a much larger one.

The equipment data says the industry's build-out is physically under way. ASML shipped 121 lithography systems in its third quarter, up from 91 in the second, roughly a third more in a single quarter. That's happening even though tool supply is itself tight. On the 24th, this desk covered Lam Research saying its own shipments will stay constrained into next year. When tools are scarce, the fabs that are funded and already have customers lined up are the ones best placed to absorb them. So the incumbents' capacity lead is being poured in concrete now, while Intel's 14A is still a design kit.

That has a pricing consequence. A credible second source is what disciplines a dominant foundry's wafer pricing. Until Intel names an external customer with real volume, buyers negotiating with TSMC have no alternative to point at. So customer leverage on TSMC wafer prices doesn't improve in the near term, even as N2 supply loosens.

TSMC also said CoWoS advanced packaging is still customers' "number one choice," while it works on cost reduction. So even as wafer capacity eases, the binding constraint on shipping finished AI accelerators sits in packaging. More leading-edge wafers don't automatically mean more shippable chips. That packaging chokepoint is where TSMC's pricing power is most durable.

And that TSMC capacity is exactly where Nvidia's next platform ships. At GTC Berlin this month, Google Cloud announced A5X bare-metal instances built on Nvidia's Vera Rubin NVL72 rack-scale systems. General availability is scheduled for November. Nvidia said back in March that seven Vera Rubin-family chips were in full production. Now there's a dated, named customer availability window to hold that claim to.

Be precise about what a GA date is. It's a cloud availability commitment, not a shipment count, and no volume figure comes with it. But it's also the first hard schedule test since a spec change this desk covered on Tuesday. Nvidia quietly cut the HBM4 memory bandwidth listed for each Rubin GPU by about 13 percent. Reports differ on why. One says memory suppliers are struggling to hit the original speeds. Another blames delays in Nvidia's own Rubin rollout. Either way, it's a warning sign for the ramp. If November holds, that's the first evidence the spec revision didn't become a schedule slip. If it slips, look at memory first. On Wednesday's call, SK hynix said its DRAM and HBM volume is effectively sold out through 2029, so there's little slack to absorb a miss.

So the contrast is stark. One side of this ecosystem is converting a roadmap into a calendar date at a hyperscaler. The other is converting a process design kit into unnamed prospects.

A short watch list. AMD reports on November 3rd. In its last reported quarter, AMD's data-center revenue was 6.7 billion dollars, up 107 percent. That's the same dollar figure as Intel's Foundry segment, except all of it came from outside customers. The question is whether the MI350 and MI400 ramp converts bookings into revenue at scale. Watch whether Intel or Tesla ever puts a name and a number behind the 14A engagement in an actual filing. Watch whether Google Cloud's November date for Vera Rubin holds. And watch November 10th, when China's pause on expanded rare-earth export controls is due to lapse.

That's the briefing. Have a good Friday.
