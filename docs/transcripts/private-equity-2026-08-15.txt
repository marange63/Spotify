Good morning. It's Saturday, August 15th, and this is your private equity briefing.

For three straight sessions this show has been telling you that the industry was manufacturing its own liquidity — GP-led secondaries, NAV loans, transactions where the sponsor is effectively both buyer and seller and nobody outside the deal sets the price. This week broke that pattern. Two real, arm's-length transactions actually printed: a contested public-company takeout in Europe, and the largest leveraged buyout in history closing its books. Both are genuine deployment, not liquidity engineering. And both tell you something uncomfortable about what a "private equity deal" even means anymore.

Start with EasyJet. Apollo Global Management has won a bidding war for the UK budget airline, and this is worth dwelling on because contested public-to-private auctions have been rare this cycle. Castlelake had circled EasyJet for months, working up through five successive bids, its final indication landing at six pounds ninety per share. Apollo came in above it, at seven pounds fifteen, and on August 6th Castlelake confirmed it would not counter. EasyJet's board, advised by Evercore, intends to recommend the offer unanimously. That price is an 81 percent premium to EasyJet's undisturbed close of three pounds ninety-four back in late May, before the offer period began. The board's own framing was that the offer delivers, in the chairman's words, immediate, certain and attractive value — a tell that even a well-run listed company now prefers a locked-in cash exit to the execution risk of staying public. An 81 percent premium, delivered through actual competing bids, is real third-party price discovery — exactly the thing the secondaries market has lacked all week, where GPs set continuation-vehicle prices with no outside buyer to check them.

Two things temper that read. First, this is not closed. It's a scheme of arrangement, which under UK takeover practice needs both a shareholder vote and sanction from the High Court, on top of aviation-license clearances and merger-control or foreign-investment approval across eight separate countries — the UK, Austria, Egypt, Germany, France, Italy, Malta and Spain. That is an unusually long regulatory runway for a take-private, and completion isn't expected until the end of the first quarter of 2027, roughly seven months out. Reporting also suggests the deal is being structured to satisfy EU rules requiring majority European control of EasyJet's operating certificate, with the founding Haji-Ioannou family retaining its stake alongside Apollo. But the specific mechanics of that structure — what stake Apollo actually ends up holding, how any EU-domiciled vehicle is arranged — aren't independently confirmed with a sourced figure, so treat that as directionally likely, not established. Same with the financing: EasyJet's own offer documents list both a debt commitment letter and an equity commitment letter, confirming the deal is leveraged, but the actual split between debt and equity in Apollo's bid isn't disclosed anywhere I can verify, so no leverage multiple to report today.

There's a second-order read in that founder rollover regardless of the EU mechanics. Sponsors are increasingly structuring take-privates to retain insider or strategic capital rather than cash the incumbents out entirely. That's a hybrid ownership model, and it looks less like the clean sponsor-owns-the-whole-thing buyout of the last cycle than like a partnership between a financial owner and the people who built the asset. Hold that thought, because the second deal takes the same mutation much further.

But first, the more interesting point on EasyJet itself: airlines are exactly the kind of cyclical, capital-intensive operating business sponsors mostly avoided after 2020. Paying up 81 percent for one is a signal about where exit-starved capital is willing to go, not just about EasyJet's quality. Which raises the obvious question — why now?

Part of the answer is dry powder. Apollo reported a record 82 billion dollars of dry powder at its last quarterly print, sitting against an exit environment that has stayed frozen for most of the industry. Capital that large doesn't sit quietly forever. When the traditional exit window — IPOs, strategic M&A — stays shut, and a fund's investment period has a clock on it, sponsors widen the aperture on what they're willing to underwrite. EasyJet, cyclical and previously off-limits, fits that pattern: it's cash-generative, it's scaled, and at the right premium it's a place to put capital to work rather than let it sit idle waiting for multiples to reset further. The so-what here is that a bidding war moving price up, not down, tells you sponsors are competing for scarce quality assets rather than waiting each other out — a live test of whether other UK-listed boards start soliciting competing offers instead of accepting the first one that shows up.

Now the other fresh deal, and it takes the "what even is a buyout anymore" question further. The 55 billion dollar take-private of Electronic Arts closed on August 4th and the company delisted from Nasdaq, after an SEC filing dated July 30th confirmed every regulatory approval needed had been obtained. Widely described as the largest leveraged buyout in history — though I'd flag that superlative is asserted in reporting rather than independently verified here. What matters more than the size is who actually owns the company now. The consortium's planned post-close stakes are 93.4 percent for Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund, 5.5 percent for Silver Lake, and 1.1 percent for Affinity Partners. Read that again: the private equity firms hold roughly six and a half percent of the equity combined. This is not a classic GP-majority-control buyout with the sponsor calling the shots and collecting carry on a controlling stake. It's a sovereign wealth fund acquisition with financial sponsors along for the ride as minority co-investors and operators.

That matters for how you think about risk and returns in this asset class. When the largest deals in the market are sovereign-anchored rather than GP-majority, illiquidity risk sits overwhelmingly on a state balance sheet that doesn't answer to limited partners or report a net IRR. Governance and control run through the sovereign investor's priorities, not a standard GP playbook aimed at a five-to-seven-year hold and an exit. And it should make you skeptical every time "private equity AUM" or "megadeal volume" gets cited as evidence the industry is thriving — an increasing share of the biggest headline numbers is sovereign capital riding alongside a fee-earning GP, not classic fund-level LP money underwriting a controlling buyout.

So where does that leave the cycle. The deployment side is genuinely opening — real transactions, at real, contested or fully-disclosed prices, backed by dry powder that had been sitting idle. That's a legitimate change from a week defined by manufactured, unpriceable liquidity on the exit side. But look at what's actually being deployed into: a cyclical operator sponsors used to shun, and a sovereign-financed megadeal where the PE firms are minority passengers. Both are the classic majority-control buyout mutating into something else — in one case a partnership with rolled-over insiders, in the other a state balance sheet with sponsors as hired operators. The near-term catalysts that will tell you whether this is a real inflection or a one-off: whether EasyJet actually clears that eight-country regulatory gauntlet on schedule, and whether any other listed mid-cap boards follow EasyJet's lead and start soliciting competing sponsor bids rather than taking the first offer.

That's the briefing. Have a good weekend.
