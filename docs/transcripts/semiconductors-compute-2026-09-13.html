Good morning. It's Sunday, September 13th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

All week this beat has located the binding constraint on AI hardware in something physical. Something you could buy, build, or wait out. Logic yields, then memory allocation, then packaging slots, then Nvidia's two hundred seventy-nine billion dollar memory prebuy. Friday broke that pattern. The thing that moved two of the world's largest memory makers was not a factory and not a contract. It was a piece of software.

Here is what happened. On September 10th the Chinese lab DeepSeek released V4.1 Flash, along with a technical report titled, in part, Pushing the Limits of KV Cache Compression. The central number is this. The model's global key-value cache, the part that has to sit permanently in high-bandwidth memory while the model is serving, is now eight hundred ninety bytes per token. That is roughly a quarter of what the prior generation, V4-Flash, required.

A second technique in the same report, which DeepSeek calls SWA Bounded Replay, attacks the other tier. It cuts the persistent cache, the part that spills out to SSD or host memory, to roughly an eighth of the old footprint. So the claim is a fourfold reduction in the HBM tier and an eightfold reduction in the storage tier, on the cache specifically.

One honest note on sourcing before those numbers travel any further. This show could not retrieve DeepSeek's own PDF directly. The figures reach us through aggregators reproducing the report, which agree with each other and with the financial press coverage. That is stronger than a secondhand paraphrase. It is still not the primary document in hand.

The commercial expression of all this is the price. Reported off-peak pricing puts cached input at three tenths of a cent per million tokens. The tempting comparison is the older V4-Pro at sixty-six cents per million off-peak input tokens, but that is a non-cached line, so it is not like-for-like and I won't pretend otherwise. The point survives without the ratio. Cached inference is now being sold at something close to free.

On Friday, shares of SK hynix and Samsung fell more than three percent in Seoul. The market read the release as evidence that AI memory intensity, per unit of compute, could decline structurally rather than scaling roughly with model size. That proportionality assumption is what three years of HBM capacity planning is built on.

Now slow down, because the market moved faster than the evidence justifies. Three discounts apply. First, a key-value cache reduction is not a total memory reduction. Weights, activations and batching still set the floor, and a five hundred fifty-two billion parameter mixture-of-experts model is not a small-memory model by any definition, even if it activates only sixteen billion parameters per token on decode. Second, this is one lab's architecture. Nothing in the record shows another frontier lab adopting the same approach, so calling it an industry trajectory is premature. Third, and this is the sharper point, these are DeepSeek's own published numbers about DeepSeek's own product. The standard discount this show applies to any vendor's self-reported figure applies here at full force.

There is also one specific number worth killing outright. A claim is circulating that this implies roughly thirty-one percent less AI flash demand. That is not a modeled forecast from any analyst house, memory maker or hyperscaler. It is one unnamed commenter's arithmetic, assuming the key-value cache accounts for thirty-five percent of a single flash-demand projection and then multiplying by DeepSeek's own reduction ratios. The outlet reporting it labelled it a scenario. Treat it as scenario math, not as a forecast, because that is exactly what it is. So the honest read of Friday's move is that a three percent decline is the market pricing a tail risk, not revising a thesis.

If the claim were fully true, when could it actually bite? That is where Micron's numbers become useful, not as news, since this show has already covered the quarter, but as a timing bound. Micron's HBM3E and HBM4 are fully booked through calendar 2027, with demand extending into 2028, and management has said plainly that it has no line of sight on when supply catches up with demand. That converts the story from "AI memory demand is falling" into something narrower and far more defensible. Any efficiency effect from V4.1 Flash-style compression cannot touch contracted volume before the 2028 negotiation cycle. What Seoul repriced on Friday is a 2028 risk, not a 2026 or 2027 one.

And there are two separate channels through which that risk could land. It could show up as lower contracted units in 2028. Or, more likely and considerably faster, it could show up as the same units at lower prices, because buyers stop bidding aggressively for scarcity the moment they believe an alternative exists. The second channel hits margin rather than volume, and margin is precisely where memory equities currently earn their premium multiple. A sold-out order book protects revenue. It does not protect the multiple. That distinction is the whole of what a three percent move is telling you.

Now follow the money to where it would actually go. If inference genuinely becomes less memory-bound over time, the scarcity premium in this industry does not disappear. It migrates. On Friday this show made the mirror-image argument: that an easing packaging bottleneck would transfer the premium toward the memory makers. Run it in reverse and an easing memory constraint pushes the binding constraint back toward compute silicon, toward packaging integration, and toward whoever captures the workloads that just got cheaper.

That is where Broadcom fits. Broadcom holds an estimated seventy percent of the custom AI-accelerator design-services market. Call that an industry estimate, not a company disclosure, because no primary Broadcom filing stands behind it. It formalized a long-term agreement with Google in April to co-develop TPUs through 2031, and its confirmed custom-chip customers now include Google, Meta, OpenAI, Anthropic, and, newly, Apple. No node, no volume and no timeline has been disclosed for the Apple project, so nobody should size it. The logic is straightforward. Cheaper, lighter inference widens the range of workloads that a fixed-function custom accelerator can serve economically, which favors the design layer that builds those chips over the merchant-GPU layer that sells general-purpose silicon.

That said, resist overreading it. Some analysts frame Broadcom's position as a structural threat to AMD's merchant-GPU business. That is interpretation, not a disclosed competitive outcome. And the efficiency argument cuts against Broadcom too, because a memory-light inference stack lowers the barrier to entry for smaller accelerator vendors just as much as it favors the incumbent design house. Efficiency reshuffles which bottleneck binds. It does not hand the win to any single design model.

There is a policy read here as well, and it is the uncomfortable one. The lab that produced this gain is Chinese, operating under US export controls on advanced accelerators. Compute scarcity is a forcing function. When you cannot buy more silicon and more memory, you spend your engineering effort on needing less of both. And the output travels in a way the hardware does not. An export licence can stop a shipment of chips. It cannot stop a technical report from being downloaded. Keep that to the mechanism, though, because nothing in the record establishes that American labs are behind on cache compression, or that they have even pursued this particular approach. The narrower and sturdier claim is this. Controls that restrict compute raise the return on algorithmic efficiency, and efficiency is the one output of this industry with no supply chain to interdict.

So where does that leave the week. This show has spent recent sessions discounting bullish statements of intent. Broadcom's gigawatt customer schedule, AMD's twelve committed gigawatts, Qualcomm's sixty billion dollar vesting ceiling. Each was a number measured by its own author. Friday is the first real test of whether that same discount gets applied when the self-reported number is bearish instead of bullish. It should, because DeepSeek's compression ratios are exactly that kind of claim, and a figure is not more trustworthy for being inconvenient to somebody else.

The forward test is simple. Watch for a second frontier lab shipping the same key-value-cache approach in a released model. Or watch for any memory maker actually changing its 2028 capacity language on the record. Until one of those lands, the memory supercycle is intact on contract and contestable only on multiple.
