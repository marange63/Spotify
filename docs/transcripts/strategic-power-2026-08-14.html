Good morning, this is Cautious Optimism Briefings, your strategic power update for Friday, August 14th, 2026.

Taiwan's largest annual war games, Han Kuang, wrap up today after a ten-day live-fire phase built to test whether the island's forces can sustain twenty-four-hour defense operations without a break. Two days ago, on August 12th, Taiwan's navy and coast guard ran their first-ever joint anti-blockade drill: navy and coast guard vessels jointly escorted a simulated merchant ship, while a minesweeper cleared a safe channel to guide it into port. That is a rehearsal of the exact scenario planners worry about most, a Chinese blockade that starves Taiwan of trade rather than a direct invasion. This cycle also marked the first live-fire outing of every one of the 108 M1A2T Abrams tanks the US finished delivering in April, and the debut of Taiwan's new Littoral Combat Command, stood up July 1st to integrate anti-ship missiles, coastal defense, mobile radar and unmanned systems into one structure. So this is the most American-modeled defense posture Taiwan has ever fielded.

Now, on the reservist numbers, a correction to yesterday's briefing. Yesterday we called this Taiwan's largest-ever reservist mobilization. That framing does not hold up. The three thousand reservists sometimes cited for last year were assigned to a single unit, the Army's 206th Infantry Brigade, not the exercise's total. Taiwan's Defense Minister has said the full 2025 Han Kuang drills actually mobilized up to twenty-two thousand reservists, testing brigade-scale backup capacity. So this year's five thousand, spread across two brigade-level units, is not the largest call-up Taiwan has run — if anything it is a narrower, more concentrated one. The real story is not more bodies. It is testing deeper integration of fewer reserve units under US-style command doctrine.

What is real and quantified is what happened overnight. As of six a.m. local time today, Taiwan's Ministry of National Defense detected nine PLA aircraft sorties, six of which crossed the median line into three of Taiwan's air defense identification zone sectors, along with twelve Chinese navy vessels and two official ships operating around the island. No source ties that activity directly to the exercise's conclusion — it is Taiwan's routine daily detection count, and it happens to land on Han Kuang's final day. Treat it as concurrent pressure, not a confirmed response. Either way, the message for markets is the same: Taiwan remains the single largest tail risk to the leading-edge chip supply chain, and every rehearsal of anti-blockade doctrine is a reminder that TSMC's output sits inside a live geopolitical fault line, not a settled one. That is exactly why the structural case for dispersing leading-edge chip production into Arizona, Japan and Germany holds its logic regardless of any single day's incursion count. And for the US defense industrial base, every American system Taiwan adopts — the Abrams lineage runs through General Dynamics — argues for a sustained arms-sale flow rather than a one-off order.

From defending the chip supply, follow the thread to the minerals behind it. On July 24th, China's Ministry of Commerce placed fourteen European Union companies on its export control watchlist, barring them from further shipments of Chinese dual-use materials. The listed firms span eight countries, including Germany, France, Italy and Poland, and the action is widely read as reciprocal retaliation for the EU's twenty-first sanctions package against Russia, announced a day earlier, which itself added fourteen Chinese and Hong Kong firms to Brussels's restricted list. This follows a similar ten-firm action against US companies back in June. Put together, it confirms that Beijing's softening of its most aggressive rare-earth controls last October was tactical, not strategic — the chokepoint is still live, and Beijing is now willing to wield it company by company against both Washington and Brussels simultaneously. No shipment denials have been documented yet at the named firms; this is a listing and licensing action, not yet proven supply disruption. But it lands at an awkward moment for Europe, which is trying to rearm against Russia using defense primes and industrials that depend on Chinese rare-earth magnets. That is a direct tension: Europe cannot easily rearm against one adversary while remaining exposed to another for the inputs to do it. For those primes it is an input-cost and permitting risk arriving just as European rearmament budgets are climbing.

Now to the US side of the base layer, where there is finally a funded step rather than another press release. Centrus Energy has agreed to supply low-enriched and high-assay low-enriched uranium to X-energy from its American Centrifuge Plant in Piketon, Ohio, to support X-energy's small modular reactor and fuel deployments. In exchange, X-energy will prepay Centrus to help expand its domestic enrichment capacity. No dollar figure has been disclosed, so the scale can't be quantified, but the structure matters: this is capital changing hands to build capacity, not just an announcement. Separately, the Department of Energy has named five potential host states — Idaho, Louisiana, Oklahoma, Tennessee and Utah — for a proposed Nuclear Lifecycle Innovation Campus covering the full nuclear fuel cycle. Keep those two developments distinct. The Centrus deal is a real, if unquantified, commercial commitment; the DOE shortlist is only a siting exercise, with no funding or permit attached yet. Still, together they mark a genuine step in rebuilding the domestic enrichment base the US ceded to Russia's Rosatom over three decades, and that base is a precondition for the small modular reactor fleet hyperscalers are counting on to power data centers with firm, carbon-free electricity. A funded domestic supply of high-assay fuel takes one of the biggest execution risks off the SMR timelines that data-center power deals depend on. It also makes Centrus a scarce asset in its own right — the only scaled, licensed commercial enricher on US soil, and the natural beneficiary as Rosatom is phased out of the American fuel supply.

One more thread, briefly, on that same power buildout. Texas froze its data-center interconnection queue on August 3rd, ordering an audit of more than 1,800 proposed projects that together claim over 474 gigawatts of grid capacity — more than five times the state's record peak demand. That freeze sits alongside a deadline that lands this coming Monday, August 17th: the sixty-day clock FERC gave all six major grid operators, including PJM and MISO, to justify or reform how they handle large-load interconnection. Nothing about that deadline has resolved yet, so treat it as an upcoming decision point, not a result. But the direction of travel is consistent with everything else in this briefing: regulators and grid operators are starting to actively separate real projects from speculative queue positions, the same distinction between announced capacity and delivered capacity that runs through Taiwan's rehearsals, China's mineral leverage, and America's nuclear fuel restart.

Pull those four threads together and the picture is this: a war game, an export listing, an enrichment deal and a grid freeze are all, in their own way, claims about future capacity. Most of them are still just that, claims. Today, for the first time in this arc, one of them — the Centrus-X-energy prepayment — became funded delivery instead of a press release. That is the distinction worth carrying into next week, as Taiwan stands down from its drills and FERC's deadline comes due.
