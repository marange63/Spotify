AI in Biopharma R&D

Transcript · September 20, 2026

Good morning, and welcome to Cautious Optimism Briefings for Sunday, September 20, 2026, on AI across the biopharma value chain.

For most of this week, this briefing has made one claim. The claim was that the field's first outside grader would be Insilico's rentosertib, and that its China Phase 3 would not print before 2027. I want to amend that today. A nearer grader showed up on September 14, and it has a regulatory date attached. The catch is that it may grade something different from what the money is pricing. That catch is the whole story.

The event is Takeda's announcement that the FDA accepted the new drug application for zasocitinib, an oral TYK2 inhibitor for moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis. The agency took it under Priority Review. Takeda's release puts the target action date in the first quarter of calendar 2027. That is the same year the rentosertib readout could arrive at the earliest, and the evidence behind it is far heavier. Rentosertib rests on a small, twelve-week Phase 2a. Zasocitinib has two completed Phase 3 trials in hand.

Here is what those trials showed, per Takeda's release. In the first study, 71 percent of zasocitinib patients reached clear or almost clear skin at week 16. Placebo was 11 percent. The active comparator, apremilast, was 32 percent. The second study replicated that almost exactly, at 69 percent.

Takeda also says both studies met their co-primary endpoints and all 44 ranked secondary endpoints. That is about as clean a Phase 3 package as this industry produces.

Now the complications, because they matter more than the numbers. The comparators were placebo and apremilast. Apremilast is a modest oral bar. The data as reported contain no head-to-head against Sotyktu, the TYK2 inhibitor already on the market. And TYK2 is a target human biology had already validated before this program existed. So these trials measure a well-behaved molecule against known biology. They do not measure anyone finding new biology.

There is a third complication, and this is the point I most want you to hear. I am not going to call zasocitinib AI-designed. I am not going to call it computationally designed either. Takeda acquired the TYK2 program from Nimbus Therapeutics in a deal struck in December 2022, with four billion dollars in guaranteed cash. That much is documented. What is not documented anywhere I can quote is that a computational platform designed this molecule. Takeda's release does not mention Nimbus, computational methods, or AI at all. The only computational language I can source is Schrodinger's 2023 release about its share of that sale, and it describes a physics-based platform in general terms without saying the platform made this drug.

Schrodinger's documented position is also narrower than the narrative wants. Its stake in Nimbus was 3.8 percent on a fully diluted basis at the end of 2022, and it took a one-time cash distribution of 111.3 million dollars when the sale closed. I found no sourced royalty or milestone right for Schrodinger on this drug. So I am not going to sell you a windfall the filings do not contain.

The amended claim therefore reads like this. A regulatory grader with a date now exists. Whether its verdict counts as evidence for computational drug design depends on a provenance fact the sponsor is not stating. The so-what is that the field's best-evidenced late-stage asset cannot cleanly be claimed by the field.

That leads to the second-order effect, and it runs the wrong way for transparency. Suppose approval lands. The FDA will certify a molecule. Nothing in the review will credit a method, and Takeda gains nothing by volunteering one. So expect less visibility into where computation was load-bearing, not more. Every future AI-designed claim gets harder to audit, because the label and the review will both be silent on it.

Now the regulatory mechanics behind that silence, because the real question is what the FDA credits at all. Drug Discovery News reports that only 35 drugs, biologics and vaccines have incorporated real-world evidence into applications since 2016. Over the same period, more than 250 premarket device authorizations did. Treat that as reporting, not as an FDA statistic. And the FDA's December 2025 update, the one that lets sponsors submit real-world evidence without identifiable patient-level data, is a device policy. Drugs are described as a possible future step.

There are two ways to read that gap. The familiar one says the regulator is conservative. The other says the regulator is correct. Device claims are mechanical and checkable. A drug efficacy claim is not. An agency that accepted computed evidence on the efficacy question would be accepting the one thing nobody has validated prospectively. There is also a causation problem worth holding onto. Sponsors may simply not be trying, because a case-by-case credibility negotiation can cost more than a conventional trial arm. The so-what is blunt. An approval certifies the molecule, and the platform gets nothing.

The bar that computed evidence has to clear is meanwhile taking shape in the commercial data layer. Flatiron published its VALID framework in April. It reports variable-level metrics that benchmark model output against expert human abstraction, and Flatiron says it no longer releases a machine-learning-extracted dataset without it. That favors incumbents who can afford the human abstraction to benchmark against. On the harder question I have to be plain with you. I found no quantified evidence that real-world data has improved trial success rates or cut R&D cost. What exists is qualitative, and much of it comes from vendors.

That brings us to the money, which sits on exactly the layer that just did not get certified. In May, Isomorphic Labs raised a 2.1 billion dollar Series B. Its release says the company has identified viable candidates with, quote, unprecedented speed. It names no candidate and no indication. Reuters reported in January that Demis Hassabis expected first clinical trials by the end of 2026. That was a year later than the end-2025 target he had given before. Roughly three months now remain on the new date.

Here is the mismatch. Zasocitinib, whatever its provenance, suggests the design layer can produce a late-stage winner against a validated target. The private capital is stacked on platforms promising considerably more than that. So the market is paying platform prices for the half of the problem where evidence already exists. The half that kills drugs is target choice and human biology, and nothing this week touches it. There is a generous reading of the Isomorphic slip. A serious organization refuses to file a weak IND. The unforgiving reading is that design was never the binding constraint. A first trial by year end is the near-term test, and a second slip would be the first hard test of a 2.1 billion dollar mark against a calendar.

The other half of the money question is intellectual property, and there is a cleaner answer there now than there was. In January, Boltz and Pfizer announced that Boltz would refine its foundation models on Pfizer's extensive historical data to create exclusive models. These are biomolecular models, the structure and affinity layer, not a language model. The release adds that Pfizer will retain full ownership of all compounds discovered or developed with the support of the Boltz models and platform.

That is the emerging template. The base model is open-weight. The exclusive fine-tune runs on the sponsor's own data. The sponsor keeps the compounds. It protects the data and the asset without building a frontier model in-house. It also cuts both ways. A sponsor retains ownership precisely when the model vendor has no clinical economics to claim. That is the design-layer vendor's problem in one sentence. Its value stays in a services contract rather than in pipeline equity.

Let me pull the threads together. Three things are open. First, whether an approval will be read as evidence for computation at all, given that nobody is claiming it. Second, whether a first-in-clinic date from a lab that has already missed one can hold. Third, whether real-world data will ever earn regulatory credit in drugs the way it has in devices.

On clinical success rates, nothing today moves the probability. Zasocitinib is a strong molecule against known biology. It does not tell us whether a model can choose a target that works in patients. And real-world data has not yet shown a measurable effect on trial success.

On R&D spend, the near-term direction is clearer. Design-layer gains shorten chemistry cycles and lower the cost per candidate. My inference is that this lands in operating expense, not in success rates. So large-cap margins can improve while pipeline economics stay exactly where they were. R&D spend falls faster than the odds improve, and that gap is what investors should be watching.

That is the briefing for September 20. Thanks for listening, and see you tomorrow.
