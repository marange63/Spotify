Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Credit & the Corporate Credit Cycle

Transcript · September 9, 2026

Good morning, it's Wednesday, September 9th, 2026, and this is Private Credit.

The place to start is a flow that reversed direction. In the second quarter of this year, Wall Street banks refinanced nine deals out of the private credit market and back into the syndicated loan market. Only three deals moved the other way, from banks to direct lenders. Call it roughly three to one in the banks' favor. The clearest single example is Catalent. It refinanced four point one billion dollars of private loans into syndicated term loans, and saved itself about two hundred twenty-five basis points doing it. Those private loans were originally written by Ares Management and Blue Owl Capital. One vintage note before we build anything on that. The figures come from Bloomberg's Credit Weekly, published in early August and covering second-quarter activity. Treat it as a month-old read, not a September print.

Here's the analytical turn. All week this briefing has narrated deterioration inside private credit books without a clean mechanism for it. Software loans marked below cost. The non-accrual borrower count widening. A Blue Owl BDC position in the specialty-materials borrower Loparex, marked down to about a nickel. Each time, we attributed it to a specific vertical or to ordinary vintage seasoning. This flow data offers a different explanation. What follows is our inference, not a finding in the source. Nobody in that data set characterizes the credit quality of the borrowers who left. But the logic is hard to avoid. Borrowers are leaving private credit for the syndicated market roughly three times as often as the reverse. The ones who can leave are, almost by definition, the ones a bank can underwrite more cheaply. That means the book left behind isn't necessarily getting worse in isolation. It is being selected for weakness. The strongest names walk out the door for cheaper money, and the residual portfolio is whatever stays. Deterioration in the average, in other words, may be partly arithmetic rather than cyclical.

Two things would falsify that read. An inference you cannot break isn't worth much. If third-quarter flow swings back toward direct lenders, this was one quarter of noise. And if someone publishes ratings or leverage data showing the departing borrowers are no stronger than the ones staying, the selection story collapses outright. Neither test has run yet.

The counter-case is real, and it deserves full weight. A single quarter's reversal is not proof of a structural turn. On the full-year figures as reported, 2025 flow ran in private credit's favor. So this could simply be oscillation between two markets that trade share depending on relative pricing and risk appetite. Banks winning deals on price this quarter may say more about bank balance-sheet appetite returning than about any judgment on borrower quality.

There is a systemic angle worth sitting with regardless of which read is right. Bank commercial-and-industrial loan growth ran at a fourteen-point-two percent annualized rate in the second quarter, per that same Bloomberg piece. A year earlier it was four point four percent. That is more than a tripling of pace. Banks are visibly re-warehousing leveraged credit risk that the decade-long migration to non-banks was partly designed to move off their balance sheets. Regulators have spent years mapping private credit as a shadow-banking problem sitting outside the perimeter. If banks are taking the better half of that book back onto balance sheet, the map is wrong in both directions at once. Risk inside the perimeter is growing, and risk outside it is getting worse in composition.

Now watch what the industry is doing in response. The supply side is already adjusting. Blue Owl Capital's direct lending has fallen to roughly thirty-five percent of its assets under management as of July. Two years earlier it was about half. Data-center and infrastructure lending is filling the difference. One sourcing caveat on that percentage. It reaches us through reporting about Blue Owl, not through a disclosure from the firm itself. We flagged Monday that non-traded BDC fundraising had collapsed eighty-two percent year over year. Read the pivot against this week's refinancing data and it looks different. Less like opportunistic diversification. More like a retreat from a middle-market fight the banks are currently winning.

The complication is what gets carried into the new business. A track record built underwriting diversified sponsor cash flow is now being extended in two directions. One is collateral-dependent asset-based finance. The other is lending correlated to hyperscaler capital spending decisions. Neither has been tested by a real downturn. The one genuine liquidation test asset-based lending has had was First Brands, whose senior secured first-lien paper we quoted at around sixteen cents on Sunday. So what this means: the asset class is redefining what it does faster than its own performance history can validate the new definition.

One second-order effect is worth naming, and naming as a hypothesis rather than a fact. If sponsors now have a credible, cheaper bank alternative, they gain leverage in every repricing conversation. That should compress new-issue direct-lending spreads as lenders fight to keep the business. It would also cut against something this briefing reported earlier in the week. We read the widening toward roughly S-plus-500 on new deals as underwriting discipline returning. Compression driven by bank competition is the opposite story about the same number. There is no verified third-quarter spread print either way. So hold both possibilities open, and treat the next quarterly direct-lending pricing wrap as the tiebreaker.

The funding side of this story answers a question we left open earlier in the week. Where does the replacement capital for retreating retail money actually come from? Part of the answer is insurance balance sheets. Specifically, Bermuda-based life and annuity reinsurance sidecars, now estimated at three hundred seventy-five billion dollars in assumed liabilities. That is roughly quadruple where the market sat in 2021. One distinction matters here. That is total assumed liabilities, sized by insurance trade press rather than by a rating agency or a regulator. It is not a private-credit figure.

Here is what nobody can tell you. Most of these sidecars do not publicly disclose their investment allocations. Morningstar DBRS has said private credit exposure is likely present across many of these vehicles. The actual amounts are not visible in any public filing. So the honest framing is that the exposure is unknown, not that it is large. The opacity itself is the finding.

Regulators, to their credit, moved first here rather than last. The NAIC adopted a new actuarial guideline in August of last year. It requires insurers to test the adequacy of their offshore reinsurance. Bermuda's own regulator tightened disclosure and asset-modeling rules this year. That cuts against the standard systemic-blind-spot framing. But narrowing a channel is not the same as seeing through it. Here is why that matters for detection. If a private credit stress event does materialize, it may surface first as a solvency question at an offshore reinsurer. Not at a rated U.S. insurer, and not at a public BDC, where the disclosure actually exists. That inverts the usual monitoring order. The vehicles we can see would confirm the problem late. The vehicles we cannot see would register it first. The tightening cuts the other way over time, too. Stricter disclosure and asset-modeling rules make sidecar-held private credit more expensive to carry. That argues for slow repatriation or re-rating of those assets. It is a deleveraging risk sitting inside the annuity-funded lending model itself.

So where does this leave the cycle? The payment layer in private credit is still grinding rather than breaking. But today's argument isn't really about payments. It is about composition and visibility. On one side, a residual direct-lending book increasingly selected for being unable to refinance somewhere cheaper. On the other, that same book funded in growing part by capital sitting in vehicles that disclose almost nothing about what they hold. The biggest unresolved risk is exactly that combination. Worse average credit quality meeting less transparent capital, at the same time. Watch three things from here. First, whether third-quarter refinancing flow keeps running toward the banks. Second, the November batch of third-quarter BDC non-accrual and payment-in-kind disclosures. Third, whether Loparex, that specialty-materials borrower marked to a nickel, files Chapter 11 once its forbearance window lapses this month.
