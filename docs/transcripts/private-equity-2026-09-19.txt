Good morning, and welcome to the Cautious Optimism private equity briefing for Saturday, September 19th, 2026.

The most useful thing that happened this week is a sale where the money came from outside private equity. H.I.G. Capital agreed to sell General Datatech, known as GDT, to the London-listed reseller Softcat, at an enterprise value of one point zero five billion dollars. H.I.G. has owned GDT since 2021. Its own release says EBITDA doubled under its ownership.

Softcat is paying for this partly with a planned three hundred and fifty million pound equity placing. So the buyer is going to the public equity market for the cash. The buyer's CEO put GDT's EBITDA at around eighty million dollars this calendar year. That is a trade-press quote, not a filing. On that number the price works out to roughly thirteen times, which is my arithmetic and not a disclosed multiple.

The so-what is simple. This is a real change of control, and public investors funded the cash.

Now the discipline. Nobody has disclosed what H.I.G. paid in 2021. Doubled EBITDA with no entry price tells you nothing about multiple on invested capital. So H.I.G.'s limited partners cannot compute a return from this release. And the deal is signed, not closed. It is expected to close by the end of the first quarter of 2027, so no cash has reached anyone's account yet.

Now the version where the sponsor keeps the pen. New Mountain has been an investor in the healthcare data company Datavant since 2014. This week Neuberger Capital Solutions and KKR agreed to take a significant minority stake in it, and New Mountain keeps control. That is twelve years of ownership, and the transaction is not a sale and not an IPO.

The terms were not disclosed, and the deal is expected to close in the fourth quarter of this year. So I will not tell you whether New Mountain took cash off the table, or whether this is money into the company. What we can say is the structure. It is the primary-market cousin of a continuation vehicle, the theme this show ran on Monday, Tuesday and Friday. Someone new buys in, the sponsor keeps control, and no third party sets a price on the whole asset.

The third case is the shortest, and it is not yet a transaction. Vista Equity Partners is reported to be weighing options for Finastra, the company it formed in 2017 by combining Misys and D and H. The report comes from Reuters, citing unnamed people, and the review is at an early stage. It could end in a sale, a stake sale, or a merger. There is no certainty of a transaction, and Blackstone is among the firms understood to be assessing a possible bid.

One source said conventional earnings multiples could put the value as high as twelve billion dollars. Another said expected EBITDA this year is about six hundred and fifty million. That is roughly eighteen and a half times on my arithmetic. We do not know whether the twelve billion is enterprise value or equity value. That is a sponsor-side aspiration, not a clearing price.

Now pull those three together, because ranking them by who set the price is the point. A listed strategic set the price on GDT, and that produced cash. On Datavant the sponsor kept control, kept the mark, and got structure. Finastra is a test case still pending.

One of those three breaks the pattern we have been building all week. Here is the pattern. On Monday EQT completed its purchase of Coller Capital, one of the few large independent secondaries buyers. On Tuesday CVC closed a ten-billion-dollar secondaries fund. On Friday Blackstone was reported to be in conversations to arrange a secondary on its own eleven-billion-dollar property fund. All of that was liquidity manufactured by the general partner.

The break is GDT. This week's cleanest exit was priced by a listed buyer funding itself in public markets. That has a second-order cost. If distributions now depend on a strategic's ability to issue equity, then private equity's cash is levered to public issuance conditions. That is the exposure the asset class sells itself as diversifying away from. If the equity window shuts, this channel shuts with it.

I would not call it a thaw. The Q2 numbers from PitchBook, published in July, cut the other way. Sponsor-to-sponsor exits fell fifty-seven percent quarter over quarter to twenty-four point five billion dollars. Sales to corporates fell sixty-three percent to thirty-eight point five billion. So one strategic buyer is a data point, not a channel.

Now to the buy side, where the price-setting runs the other way. Brookfield agreed to take private the ASX-listed Reliance Worldwide, the plumbing and heating products maker, at an enterprise value of about two point eight billion dollars. Brookfield's own release gives that figure. One outlet headlines two point nine, so I am using Brookfield's number.

The price is four dollars seventy-five Australian a share. A secondary write-up puts that at a thirty-one and a half percent premium to the undisturbed price, and twelve point one times FY26 adjusted EBITDA. Those two figures are from that write-up, not from Brookfield's release. The money comes from the Brookfield Capital Partners strategy and its affiliate Brookfield Business Corporation. It is expected to close in the first quarter of 2027.

What matters is how it got there. The board rebuffed Brookfield at four fifteen, four twenty-five and four fifty Australian, before the fourth approach at four seventy-five cleared. That is roughly a fourteen percent increase over the first bid. So the seller, not the buyer, set the clearing level.

Apollo's Kleinman gave the same diagnosis from the buyer's side of the table. On September 9th he said the real obstacle is sellers being unwilling to accept the price the market is offering. Reliance is a public board doing just that, and winning.

Here is the entry-price paradox. Insurance broker Baldwin went private this week at roughly twenty times EBITDA, to a permanent-capital buyer rather than a fund. Reliance is at twelve point one. GDT is near thirteen. Finastra, if it ever prints near the top of that range, is high-teens. Price discovery at entry is abundant this week. Price discovery at exit is scarce. That asymmetry is why holding marks stay unfalsifiable.

There is a limit on the comparable. A twelve-times industrial and a thirteen-times IT-services deal bracket one end of the book at defensible levels. They say nothing about software, which is where much of the aged inventory sits. So the markdown risk is in the gap between industrial prints and software marks. Finastra is the first real chance to see that gap tested.

Next, who is funding all of this. Goldman Sachs Alternatives announced the final close of West Street Capital Partners IX at nine point six billion dollars. That is a final close, not a target. The predecessor closed at nine point seven billion in 2022, according to PE Insights, not Goldman. So the flagship is flat after four years.

Goldman's release leads with eleven point seven billion for the vintage. It reaches that number only by adding more than one point six billion for West Street Asia Equity Partners I and five hundred million for co-investment. Growth came from adjacent vehicles, not from a bigger flagship.

Read the composition, not the headline. The release cites significant commitments from Goldman and its employees and does not size them. So fee-paying outside capital is smaller than nine point six billion by an unknown amount. Goldman also did not disclose a target, so we cannot say whether it hit or missed. And a flat nominal number after four years is a cut in real terms.

Siguler Guff makes the contrast at the small end. It closed more than three billion dollars for its small buyout strategy. Over two point three billion is commingled, and nearly seven hundred million is separately managed accounts. The word record is the manager's own. But capital is arriving through customized accounts and adjacent vehicles, not the classic ten-year blind pool.

For fee-related earnings, that broadens the revenue base. It does nothing for the carry LPs are waiting on. Carry still depends on the same exit window.

So where does the cycle stand? Better than a week ago on one measure, and more fragile on another. This week the exit bid came from outside private equity, which beats a week of sponsors selling to themselves. But that bid is only as reliable as a public equity placing. And the biggest unresolved risk is still the logjam between marks and clearing prices.

Two things will confirm or refute the read. The first is whether Vista actually runs a process on Finastra, and where it prices. The second is the late-October and early-November manager earnings. Blackstone guided on July 23rd that Q3 net realizations would slow sequentially, with a robust fourth quarter and 2027. If that fourth-quarter pickup does not show up in the prints, this week was an outlier.
