Good morning. It's Tuesday, August 11th, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

On Monday, this show flagged an open question about agentic AI in the enterprise: does it kill existing SaaS seats, or does it become a new, separately priced line that vendors can point to as proof of real adoption. We specifically said to watch Salesforce's Agentforce for the answer. Two data points landed since, and together they close that question further than expected.

First, the adoption side. Salesforce published the second edition of its Agentic Enterprise Index this week, built from aggregated usage data across thousands of businesses on its platform. In February of 2025, the average Salesforce business had five agents activated. By April of 2026, that number was thirteen. Weekly employee usage of those agents tripled over the same stretch. And over 2025 the average agent went from handling two distinct skills to six.

The count itself is the least interesting number here. The one that actually signals platform maturity is deployment time. In February of 2025, standing up a new agent took roughly four days on average. By April of 2026, that had dropped fifty-three percent, to just one point nine days. That is not a story about enthusiasm. Enthusiasm shows up in survey answers. A fifty-three percent drop in build time shows up when the tooling itself gets good enough that deploying an agent stops being a project and starts being a configuration task. That is the signal worth banking: agentic AI, at least inside Salesforce's ecosystem, has moved from pilot behavior to routine operational behavior.

Now, the caveats, because this is Salesforce's own telemetry, not an independent audit. It measures Agentforce usage specifically, not agentic adoption across the industry, and Salesforce has every commercial incentive to publish numbers that show acceleration. The index also reports that businesses running agents in digital commerce saw four times the online sales growth of businesses that didn't. Treat that as correlation, not causation. The merchants sophisticated enough to deploy agents early are probably also the merchants already growing fastest for other reasons. Selection bias, not proof of agent-driven lift.

Here is where this briefing initially expected to land: adoption metrics without a matching revenue disclosure, meaning the harder question — does any of this actually show up as recurring revenue — would stay open. That turned out to be wrong, and the correction matters.

Salesforce's most recent earnings call, for the quarter ended April 30th, covers the same window as this usage index. And on that call the company disclosed that Agentforce annual recurring revenue surpassed one billion dollars for the first time. Total company revenue for that quarter was eleven point one three billion dollars, up thirteen percent year over year, an acceleration from the low-double-digit growth Salesforce had been posting. Current remaining performance obligation — the backlog of contracted-but-not-yet-recognized revenue — was thirty-three point six billion dollars, up about fourteen percent, and management explicitly attributed that growth in part to what they called continued momentum in Agentforce, Data 360, and Slack. Some reporting puts the combined Agentforce-and-data-products figure as high as three point four billion dollars, but that composite number is less firmly sourced than the one billion Agentforce-only figure, so weigh it accordingly. The point stands either way. This is not a case of adoption telemetry standing in for revenue. The revenue is disclosed, quoted, and growing alongside the usage data, in the same window.

That closes the loop we opened Monday in a specific way. On Monday we noted ServiceNow's AI line, Now Assist, had just crossed a billion dollars in annual contract value. Salesforce's Agentforce has now crossed a billion in annual recurring revenue. Two of the biggest enterprise-application incumbents each now carry a roughly billion-dollar AI line reported as a distinct product, not folded into the base subscription for free. For a thesis that said autonomous agents would collapse seat-based SaaS, that is the wrong-way evidence piling up: so far the incumbents are attaching agents as a priced add-on, not being disintermediated by them.

One number to actively leave out. A claimed hundred-and-seventy-fold increase in agent-handled customer service sessions has been circulating in secondary coverage, but it could not be traced to an exact sentence from Salesforce itself. Drop it. When a figure that clean can't be sourced to a primary quote, the right move is to leave it out rather than repeat it with a hedge.

So what does a genuine adoption-plus-revenue story like this actually change. Two second-order effects are worth naming. The first is about who loses. If the time to build and deploy an agent keeps collapsing toward zero, the systems integrators and consultancies that currently bill for agent build-outs have a shrinking service to sell. That's the same dynamic that hit low-code platforms a decade ago: the easier the vendor makes the tooling, the less anyone needs to pay a third party to operate it. And the margin on that implementation work is exactly the kind of high-rate advisory revenue that's hardest to replace once it goes. Watch consulting-revenue commentary from the Accentures and Deloittes of the world over the next few quarters for early signs of that compression.

The second effect is competitive pressure on disclosure itself. Salesforce didn't just deploy agents — it published usage telemetry and tied it to a disclosed ARR line. That raises the proof bar for everyone else claiming an agentic AI story. Microsoft's Copilot Studio, ServiceNow's agent orchestration, and Workday's own agent initiatives will increasingly be measured against Salesforce's willingness to show deployment-time and usage data rather than intent surveys or customer-count claims. Expect more vendors to start publishing comparable telemetry, simply because the market now has a template for what credible agentic-adoption evidence looks like.

Worth one line of context on scale. Microsoft's Azure cloud business crossed a hundred billion dollars in annualized revenue in its most recent quarter, and Microsoft 365 Copilot's paid seat count has been climbing sharply. Both already covered on this show, but the backdrop matters here: the infrastructure underneath agentic workloads is scaling fast enough that Salesforce's adoption numbers aren't happening in a vacuum. They're riding a cloud capacity buildout that's still accelerating.

And a reminder from last week, because it cuts the other way. Datadog disclosed that its largest customer — a leading AI company using seventeen of its products, which had just signed a nine-figure renewal — is cutting usage starting this quarter. That renewal-then-cut pattern is the counterweight to keep in mind here: even large, contractually locked AI-linked spend can reverse quickly, without churn, once a customer optimizes its own stack. Salesforce's adoption and revenue numbers are real today. Whether they compound the way Wall Street will start pricing them to compound is a separate question, and one quarter of disclosed ARR doesn't answer it.

Put it together and the honest read is this. Agentic AI has visibly crossed from pilot to standing production inside at least one major enterprise platform, and for the first time the usage data and the revenue data point the same direction in the same window. But the value in that shift is migrating toward whoever owns the platform and the telemetry — not toward the integrators who used to get paid to build the thing, and not automatically toward permanence, given how fast an AI-linked revenue line can bend the other way once a big customer decides to optimize.

That's the briefing for today. Thanks for listening, and I'll see you next time.
