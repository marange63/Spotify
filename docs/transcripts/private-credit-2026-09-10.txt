Good morning, it's Thursday, September 10th. This is Cautious Optimism Briefings, and today we're back underneath the credit machine.

All week the stress has kept moving down the stack. From marks, to recovery, to funding, to the promise a vehicle makes about how fast it will give you your money back. Today it moves again. And this time it splits rather than converges.

Start with the split itself. Cliffwater's Corporate Lending Fund, the largest interval fund in the private credit market, has capped redemptions at five percent for the third consecutive quarter. Investors asked to redeem approximately sixteen percent of shares in the third quarter. That's essentially flat against roughly seventeen percent in the second. Be precise about which part is the news. The quarterly request rate is not accelerating. The persistence is. Three straight quarters in which the cap has bound is the story.

That breaks a read this briefing gave on Tuesday. We told you then that KKR's non-traded income trust had seen third-quarter redemption requests fall to about two and a half percent of net asset value, down from a level that breached its five percent cap the quarter before. We called that the retail liquidity mismatch easing at the margin. Cliffwater says otherwise. The correct word for the complex right now is dispersion, not easing. Some vehicles are stabilizing while the largest one in the market is not, and an average across the two would tell you nothing useful.

There's a second, quieter number underneath the headline, and it matters more to an allocator than the scary sixteen percent. Cliffwater discloses that investors who have requested cash since the first quarter have received approximately seventy-eight percent of the capital they sought. Hold that next to what Blackstone disclosed for BCRED, which we covered on Monday: roughly seventy-five percent of requested capital returned over two quarters. Two independent multi-billion-dollar vehicles have now converged on the same answer. You get about three-quarters of what you ask for, and you get it late.

That is the structure functioning exactly as written. A binding cap is not a breach. It's the mechanism doing its job. But one disclosure was an anecdote, and two convergent ones make it a parameter you can underwrite. So the allocator question isn't whether gating is frightening. It's whether the illiquidity premium you're being paid compensates for a toll that now shows up quarter after quarter, at scale, at more than one manager. Cliffwater's chief executive has said the fund remains committed to acting in the best interests of its shareholders. That's true, and it's beside the point. The price of exit is the point.

And be clear about what this evidence doesn't establish. Nothing here tells us whether the redemption pressure is genuine credit-quality fear or simply wealth-channel investors rebalancing out of private credit after a strong run in public equities. That split changes how bearish the story is, and it isn't settled. What would settle it is the fourth-quarter tender data across the rest of the evergreen complex. If requests rise at vehicles whose own books show no deterioration, that's headline contagion in the wrapper rather than a judgment on the loans, and Ares, Blue Owl, Apollo and KKR all run vehicles exposed to exactly that.

So if retail capital is paying a measured toll to leave, who steps in to replace it? We argued yesterday that the answer increasingly runs through insurance balance sheets, and that most of those vehicles disclose nothing about what they hold. This week the public bond market offered a hint about how that exposure is being priced anyway.

A Bloomberg analysis of non-financial, high-grade corporate bonds finds about one trillion dollars of paper trading at spreads unusually wide relative to what its credit rating alone would imply. Five hundred eighty billion of that is in the US, and nearly four hundred billion in Europe. That's happening while the headline market looks placid. The average spread on the US high-grade index sits at zero point seven eight percentage points, within a few basis points of its lowest level in a quarter-century, and sixty-day spread volatility is near a five-year low. So the aggregate says nothing is wrong, and underneath it a trillion dollars of individual credits disagree. That's the same pattern this briefing flagged on Tuesday in BDC non-accrual data, where a headline rate of under one percent sat on top of a loan marked down to a nickel. The aggregate statistic hides the risk. Now it's doing so in the public, mark-to-market bond market rather than the private, mark-to-model one.

Part of that dislocation reportedly involves insurers, and here the reporting is thinner than it sounds. Bloomberg's language is that insurers "are being penalized for their exposure to riskier private credit." That's the whole of it. No basis-point figure, no benchmark, no named issuer anywhere in the piece. We went looking for corroboration from a rating agency, from the NAIC, or from an insurer's own filing that names private-credit exposure as a driver of 2026 spread widening, and we could not find one. So treat this as a reported characterization, not a measured spread. It is genuinely suggestive — an invisible exposure carrying what may be a visible price — and it is exactly the kind of claim that gets repeated into fact without anyone checking the magnitude. We're not going to do that.

There's a second reason to be careful, and it separates the technical from the fundamental. Six hyperscalers now make up about five percent of the US high-grade bond index, double their share of two years ago, as AI capital-expenditure issuance accelerates. That concentration is pushing managers to rebalance away from those names, which distorts spreads on bonds that have nothing to do with AI or with private credit. A bond trading wide because an index-concentration rule forced a seller into it is a different animal from a bond trading wide because the market doubts the borrower. Don't read all trillion dollars of that as a credit signal.

That same hyperscaler issuance is the bridge to the week's other development: investment-grade supply is running at a genuinely record pace. High-grade issuers have set monthly records in half of 2026's eight months so far, including each of the past three. Dealers are forecasting roughly two hundred fifteen billion dollars of high-grade issuance this month, against a prior September record of two hundred seven and a half billion. That's a forecast, not a printed month. The reporting attributes part of the pace to issuers pulling borrowing plans forward ahead of expected higher funding costs, which is classic late-cycle behavior in an otherwise calm market.

The consequence for private credit is a composition problem, not a growth story. If the strongest AI-adjacent credits can fund cheaply and in size in the public bond market, then private credit's much-touted role in bridging the data-center financing gap isn't a proportional slice of that buildout. It's the back-loaded, harder-to-underwrite residual — the projects and borrowers the public market and public equity declined. That's the same adverse-selection logic we applied to direct lending yesterday, when banks refinanced three loans out of private credit for every one that went the other way. Now it's running one market over, in AI infrastructure. And it has a tail: debt front-loaded today thins the primary calendar later and concentrates a maturity echo in the 2029-to-2031 vintages, which is precisely where the private-credit residual will be sitting too.

One policy thread to close on. In August, an executive order directed federal agencies, and the Labor Department in particular, to work toward expanding defined-contribution retirement plans' access to private credit and other alternatives. That rulemaking is still pending. The obvious read is that headlines about gated interval funds should chill the push. The industry's counter-argument is just as plausible: a target-date fund inside a 401(k) has no quarterly tender queue at all, so plan-level illiquidity isn't the same problem as a retail interval fund's. Which way the Labor Department goes is a real catalyst, not a foregone conclusion.

Pull it together. Six sessions running, the payment layer in private credit — the actual cash interest getting paid — is still grinding rather than breaking, even as the structure around it deteriorates. Marks, then recovery assumptions, then funding queues, and now visibility. The biggest unresolved risk remains a liquidity mismatch that hasn't met a real mark-to-market test. What happens if a manager has to sell loan positions at a discount to marked NAV to meet a cap, rather than simply gating around one. Watch three things: fourth-quarter tender numbers across the evergreen complex, November's third-quarter BDC non-accrual and payment-in-kind disclosures, and whether the Labor Department actually moves while this evidence accumulates.
