Cautious Optimism Briefings

Strategic Power, Energy, and Institutional Change

Transcript · October 22, 2026

Good morning. It's Thursday, October 22nd, 2026, and this is your Strategic Power briefing.

Here's the thesis. After weeks of hyperscalers bidding for power plants that already exist, one is now putting its name on new nuclear. But it's a target, not a build. And the same gap between an announced deal and a binding transfer runs through Washington's negotiation over Lukoil's foreign assets.

Start with the callback. In the last two briefings, this desk made the same argument about hyperscalers and electricity. Google's deal with Constellation and Oracle's subscription to Point Beach mostly reallocate firm power that already exists. Yesterday we put it bluntly: a subscription adds no new megawatts, it reallocates them. Today's deal is the first in this run that targets new generation.

Amazon, the reactor developer X-energy, and American Electric Power say they will target building twelve of X-energy's Xe-100 small modular reactors. The site is the retired Breed coal plant in Sullivan County, Indiana. Each unit is an 80 megawatt high-temperature gas-cooled design. Twelve of them works out to roughly 960 megawatts, the headline figure the companies use. Amazon is backing the development work, and says it would be the first customer to buy power from the plant once it's operational. This is the second project under an Amazon and X-energy collaboration that aims to add more than five gigawatts of new nuclear capacity to the US grid by 2039. So this is new generation, not a reshuffled contract. That's a real break from the pattern. Now test how real it is.

Here's the skeptical turn. The operative words in Amazon's own announcement are "will target building." Amazon "would be" the first customer "once operational." Those are the verbs of an intention, not a construction permit. The announcement discloses no price, no contract length, and no in-service date for this plant. The only date on the table is 2039. That date belongs to the whole five-gigawatt goal, not to Indiana. And twelve units of a first-of-kind reactor design is a fleet bet on a technology without a US commercial operating record.

The coal-site story is tempting too. A retired plant may come with transmission, water, and a community used to hosting generation. But nothing in the announcement confirms this site still carries usable interconnection rights. Treat that advantage as a hypothesis until a filing shows it.

There's also a less flattering reading. In a market where every hyperscaler needs to be seen securing supply, a development target is partly a signal, and partly a cheap option on future capacity. Neither reading is proven wrong yet. The test that separates them is concrete: a final investment decision, and a construction license application. So what: Amazon has committed capital and reputation to getting a reactor fleet built. It has not yet committed to a reactor fleet.

Now size it. TrendForce, a single research firm, projects a global supply-demand gap for data-center power of 268 gigawatts. Set Amazon's entire goal of more than five gigawatts against that. Even the full program, built on schedule, covers only around two percent of the gap. And it arrives on a 2030s horizon. Demand forecasts cut the other way too. On Monday, this desk reported that PJM's independent market monitor found about 42 percent of the data-center load growth PJM had forecast over a two-year window had been canceled or delayed. So the gap itself may be softer than the headline. Either way, the conclusion holds. This deal is option value on 2030s supply, not relief for the power crunch AI is creating right now.

The second-order effect is about where AI capex actually lands. When grid interconnection stalls, more of the AI buildout shows up as power-generation spending, not just chips and servers. And when a hyperscaler funds reactor development directly, its partners' fortunes start to track that hyperscaler's decisions. Name the exposed parties: X-energy and AEP. Their upside on this project runs through Amazon's appetite for power, not through ordinary utility rate-base growth. That cuts both ways. If Amazon's spending cycle turns, a development target is the easiest thing to slow.

There's a broader balance-sheet story, and this part is our inference. A company with Amazon's cash can underwrite first-of-kind generation that a debt-financed independent power producer would struggle to finance. That widens the gap between the few platforms that can self-fund firm power and everyone else bidding for the same scarce electrons. So what: power, the AI buildout's real bottleneck, is starting to sort companies by balance sheet as much as by chip supply.

The same question now runs through Russian oil. Who actually ends up owning a physical asset? And does a deal on paper become a binding transfer?

Washington's sanctions on Lukoil and Rosneft have moved past license extensions into something more consequential. It's now a negotiated sale of Lukoil's foreign holdings, with the Kremlin openly at the table. Three things are new. First, according to Izvestia's English edition, the US authorization for negotiations over selling Lukoil International GmbH has been extended to November 21st. That date rests on a Russian state-linked outlet, not a Treasury document we could find, so hold it a little loosely. Second, Carlyle, the private equity firm that had agreed to buy those assets, has withdrawn. Reuters headlined it as Carlyle dropping out of "the race." That implies other bidders remain, though none is named on the record. Third, on October 21st, Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said the license needed for a sale "could be extended without difficulty." Kremlin aide Yuri Ushakov said Russia and the US are discussing the assets' future. For scale, the portfolio was initially valued at about 22 billion dollars.

Quick callback, not new ground. The separate license letting Lukoil's non-Russian stations keep operating already runs to April 2027. It still bars any transfer of funds to a person or account inside Russia. Keep that constraint in mind, because it's the test between two readings. One reading: the negotiation is the sanctions regime doing its job. It forces a real sale of non-Russian assets while walling the money off from Moscow. The other reading: a state-brokered sale, with the Kremlin at the table, is how ownership gets relabeled without Russia losing much of substance. Carlyle's exit doesn't settle it. Private capital may have balked at the legal risk, or the price may simply have been wrong. Two facts will settle it: who the buyer is, and whether sale proceeds can reach Lukoil in Russia at all.

The stakes run past Lukoil. However this sale resolves becomes the template for whether Western sanctions can force real divestment of Russian-linked foreign assets. That matters to the European countries where Lukoil's foreign businesses operate. Bulgaria is one, where a separate license covers Lukoil's local entities. It also matters for the credibility of the next secondary-sanctions threat, whoever it's aimed at. So what: a sanctions program built to force divestment now hinges on a buyer nobody has named, and a deadline Moscow says is easy to move.

Pull the thread across both stories, and the pattern is the same. In Indiana, the test is whether a development target becomes an operating reactor. With Lukoil, the test is whether a negotiation becomes a binding transfer of ownership. Announcements are cheap. Construction licenses, interconnection rights, and verified ownership changes are not.

Watch three things. First, whether Amazon, X-energy, and AEP follow this target with a final investment decision and a construction license filing. Second, whether November 21st produces a named buyer for Lukoil's foreign assets, or just another extension. Third, the Fed's October 28th decision, still pending, as the backdrop for what capital-heavy power projects cost to finance.

That's Strategic Power for October 22nd. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
