Cautious Optimism Briefings

Space & the Orbital Economy

Transcript · October 2, 2026

Good morning. It's Friday, October 2nd, and this is your orbital economy briefing.

On Wednesday we argued that value in space is pooling around one company, SpaceX, across launch, transport, and data networks. Today we test that thesis against three counterweights. The carriers and regulators in direct-to-cell. A defense prime in missile tracking. And China's two state constellations. The honest answer is that the thesis gets refined, not reversed.

Start with AST SpaceMobile, the direct-to-cell operator. On September 30th, AST announced that its next three commercial satellites, BlueBirds 14, 15, and 16, had left its Texas factory bound for Cape Canaveral. On Wednesday we framed AST's choice as scale or sale. Either it builds out its own constellation, or it ends up selling itself or its spectrum to a larger player. This shipment is evidence for building. The spending this show has tracked for weeks is starting to show up as hardware.

But keep the pace in proportion. No launch date is attached to the shipment yet. AST's last satellites to reach orbit were BlueBirds 11, 12, and 13, on a Falcon 9 back on August 5th. That leaves roughly thirteen in orbit, against the target of about forty-five we cited last week. Batches of three, trucked to the Cape, is craft production, not mass manufacturing. And commercial service timing remains company guidance, not an achieved milestone. Shipped is not launched, and launched is not service.

The more consequential front is regulatory. AST's CEO, Abel Avellan, met with FCC Chairman Brendan Carr to pitch wider direct-to-device spectrum access. That same week, at its September 30th open meeting, the FCC adopted a satellite spectrum order. It's worth being precise about what that order does.

The FCC opened a bit over a thousand megahertz of new satellite-broadband spectrum. The bigger slice sits in the 12.7 gigahertz band, for downlinks to consumer dishes. Coverage of the order describes that as a more than 25 percent increase in downlink spectrum. A second slice, in the 42 gigahertz band, serves gateway stations. Further bands are now out for comment.

Now the limits. This is a general capacity order. It explicitly covers terminals on planes and ships. It names neither AST, nor SpaceX, nor T-Mobile, and it is not a direct-to-device rulemaking. So the vote did not pick a winner between AST's carrier-partner model and the vertically integrated SpaceX and T-Mobile approach. The rulemaking that could decide that fight hasn't happened yet.

What the order does create is a next step for SpaceX. SpaceX still has to file a modification application and win FCC approval before it can use the new frequencies. That filing is where a general order turns into Starlink capacity. A bigger downlink pool matters most to whoever can light it up first, and SpaceX is already lined up at the door. So this order can widen SpaceX's lead in home and mobility broadband, even while the direct-to-cell question stays open. How quickly the Commission acts on SpaceX's application will be the first real read on whether regulators are a brake on SpaceX or an accelerant.

What did sharpen this week was the war of words. AT&T's CEO, John Stankey, told Axios on Tuesday that SpaceX's plan to put small cellular base stations alongside residential Starlink dishes is, in his words, "not a viable strategy." His reasoning: "it would cost as much to do that as it would to build a macro network to be able to handle those capabilities." He also raised a legal snag. Quote: "you can't radiate cellular signals from somebody's house without their permission, unless somebody changes the law on that."

Read that as incumbent positioning. AT&T is defending its own macro-network economics. Its objection is about cost and law, not a technical dismissal of satellite coverage. There's also a less flattering read for AST. The carriers may simply be hedging, ready to buy capacity from whichever constellation delivers coverage first. Today, that would still be SpaceX. The so-what for AST shareholders is that the decisive lever in direct-to-cell is now spectrum rules and carrier alliances, not launch cadence. A regulatory ruling could move the stock more than a launch. And that's an arena where SpaceX's manufacturing edge doesn't automatically win.

The same concentration question looks different one layer down, in missile tracking. L3Harris is moving its Hypersonic and Ballistic Tracking Space Sensor design into production with minimal changes. It's building eighteen of these satellites under a $955 million order from the Space Development Agency, as part of the Golden Dome missile-defense architecture. Be clear on timing. The order was awarded in July, and the production decision was reported in mid-September. This is not a new contract win.

Still, it corrects Wednesday's framing. We said Golden Dome money was pooling at SpaceX, citing Bloomberg's reporting, via secondary coverage, of more than eight billion dollars in related awards. L3Harris's order is roughly a tenth of that. So it refines the thesis rather than overturning it. By our own arithmetic, not a disclosed unit price, the order works out to roughly $53 million per satellite. And "minimal changes" cuts both ways. It de-risks the schedule. It also locks in a design before Golden Dome's architecture has settled.

The accurate picture is layered concentration. SpaceX holds launch, transport, and the data network. A legacy prime like L3Harris keeps the specialized sensor layer, where qualified payload heritage still matters. For newer defense entrants without that heritage, this reinforces the subcontract-or-team squeeze we described on Wednesday.

The largest counterweight isn't American at all. China launched the 26th group of its Guowang broadband constellation on September 23rd, on a Long March 8A from the Hainan commercial launch site. SpaceNews puts Guowang at 226 satellites as of September 16th. On Tuesday's show we cited 204, from the tracker Orbital Radar. Don't read that as growth. It's two trackers disagreeing over roughly the same period. That gap of about twenty satellites is itself the lesson: Chinese constellation counts aren't yet auditable from outside.

China's other state-backed constellation, Qianfan, sits at 256 satellites in the same SpaceNews count. The more telling detail is the rocket mix. In the second half of September, Guowang flew on a state Long March 8A. Qianfan flew on two different commercial rockets, LandSpace's Zhuque-2E and Orienspace's Gravity-1. Starlink, by contrast, rides one dominant vehicle family. SpaceNews describes a busy fourth-quarter manifest ahead, though that's a characterization, not a dated schedule. Our read is that this breadth gives Beijing redundancy and a growing claim on orbital shells and spectrum. But neither constellation has published subscriber or service data. For now this is a slot-and-spectrum land grab, not a working competing network.

Meanwhile, SpaceX is opening a front none of these rivals contests: orbital computing. Its Starmind constellation, filed with the FCC in late January for up to one million satellites, would carry Nvidia's Vera Rubin NVL72 platform, at a peak compute payload of 150 kilowatts per satellite. That is roughly one rack of compute in orbit, which is why the filing reaches for a million. None of this is this week's news, and no thermal, radiation, or cost figures have been published. It's a spectrum and orbital claim long before it's a compute business. But it plugs into the power-constraint story running through our other briefings this week. If delivered electricity binds AI growth on the ground, orbital compute is SpaceX's bet on sidestepping it. For SpaceX shareholders, that's a third demand driver for Starship cadence, after Starlink replenishment and defense. And every new driver deepens the concentration that today's counterweights are pushing against.

So where does that leave Wednesday's thesis? What's genuinely moving is physical. Direct-to-cell satellites are shipping, missile-tracking satellites are entering production, and Chinese constellations are flying on several rocket families. What's still a demo is AST's commercial service, any service from China's constellations, and computing in orbit. Each counterweight today is a shipment, a lobbying meeting, or a production order, not a working alternative at scale. The open question is whether spectrum regulation, rather than rocket cadence, becomes the real brake on SpaceX's concentration.

Three things to watch. First, an actual launch date for BlueBirds 14 through 16. Second, SpaceX's modification application for the new 12.7 gigahertz spectrum, and how fast the FCC approves it. Third, any FCC rulemaking aimed specifically at direct-to-device spectrum, rather than general satellite capacity.

That's your orbital economy briefing. Talk tomorrow.
