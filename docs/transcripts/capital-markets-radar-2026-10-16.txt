Good morning, it's Friday, October 16th, and this is your Capital Markets Radar.

Here's the frame. On Thursday, producer prices came in at nearly double the forecast, and the bond market didn't sell off. The tape is betting that credit fear outranks inflation. That bet deserves a hard look before you accept it at face value.

Start with the number that should have moved everything. The Bureau of Labor Statistics said Thursday that the Producer Price Index for final demand rose 1.3 percent in September. Moody's Analytics had expected 0.7 percent, so the print came in at nearly double. Energy did the heavy lifting at the headline level. The final-demand energy index jumped 10.8 percent on the month. So this is supply-side inflation. And it lands on a Fed that only just resumed hiking in September.

Here's the part that matters more than the headline. Strip out food, energy and trade services, and producer prices still rose 0.5 percent on the month. Year over year, that core measure is up 5.0 percent. Now set that against core consumer prices from Wednesday's CPI report. The annual rate there is 2.6 percent, and that sounds tame. The momentum doesn't. As we reported yesterday, core CPI rose 0.3 percent on the month. That matched August's already-hot pace instead of cooling, and it lifted the annual rate from 2.4 to 2.6 percent. On Tuesday, this desk cautioned that an energy-led headline might not move core. Wednesday's core CPI undercut that caution once. Thursday's core PPI undercuts it a second time. These are different baskets, so five percent against 2.6 isn't a like-for-like gap. But the direction is a pipeline signal. Cost pressure at the producer level hasn't fully reached the shelf yet. The so-what: consumer core inflation may not have peaked with September's print.

Don't take that as a clean forecast, though. Pass-through from producers to consumers is never one-for-one. Margins absorb some of it. And part of that core PPI figure likely reflects energy-intensive transport and intermediate costs. Those fade if oil fades, and that's a live possibility. As we reported yesterday, Brent dropped on reports that the US may ease restrictions on some vessels carrying Russian oil. The year-over-year figure also carries base effects. So treat the producer-consumer gap as a warning light, not a call on where October's CPI lands. That report is due November 10th.

Now, the bond market looked straight past all of this. On Wednesday, the CPI release pushed the ten-year Treasury yield up 9 basis points to 5.304 percent, per Quartz. That left it just below its 24-year high. On Thursday, the move reversed. As we reported yesterday, CNBC had the ten-year down almost 13 basis points. That more than erased Wednesday's 9 basis point spike. Yesterday we argued that duration demand had shifted from an inflation story to a credit and growth scare. Thursday's producer print was the first hard test of that read. The hotter number did not send the long end back toward its high. One honest caveat. Sources disagree on whether Thursday's yield move was driven by the PPI or was a delayed unwind of the CPI spike. So the defensible claim is narrower than the headline. The long end didn't sell off on a hot print. That's not proof the market has stopped caring about inflation.

There's a rational version of this trade, and it deserves airtime. An energy-led producer shock can be demand-destructive. It squeezes margins and real incomes before it ever embeds in wages. On that view, the bond rally isn't complacency. It's a growth-scare trade, and a sensible one. The other view is that core pass-through is real, and the long end has simply paused. Two things would separate those views. The first is whether energy prices fade. The second is whether core pass-through shows up in October's consumer prices. The so-what: the bond rally is defensible, but it's a wager on demand destruction, not proof that inflation is contained.

The stakes are high for multi-asset portfolios. Since Thursday, duration has been working as a hedge against equity and credit risk again. That's the hedge sixty-forty and risk-parity allocators lost while yields climbed to multi-decade highs. If producer-price pass-through pushes the long end back up, they lose it again, within days of getting it back. The so-what: the bond hedge is only as reliable as the inflation data allows, and Thursday's data didn't help it.

That same bet showed up in equities. Thursday's tape split sharply by index construction. The Dow rose 1.1 percent and the Russell 2000 gained 1.4 percent, according to Stephen Kates's market note on Substack. Meanwhile, the cap-weighted S&P 500 and the Nasdaq barely finished positive, weighed down by big tech. On Tuesday, this desk called the equal-weight S&P's first positive week in eight a lean toward broader participation, not a measured rotation. We said it needed another data point. Thursday is that second data point, and this time it's measured. Small caps and the Dow outperforming while cap-weighted indexes stall is what you'd expect if money is leaving concentrated AI-exposed names. The breadth call now has a number behind it.

Still, call it two data points toward broadening, not a confirmed factor rotation. One session is not a regime. The Dow is price-weighted, so a 1.1 percent gain can hinge on a handful of expensive components. And a small-cap bid on falling yields is the textbook duration trade. It's a bet on rates, not a standalone vote on breadth. If long-end yields resume climbing on producer-price pass-through, the small-cap leg unwinds right alongside the bond leg. The so-what: breadth and rates aren't two independent confirmations. They're one bet, wearing two costumes.

What's pulling money out of mega-cap tech is a credit ladder. It starts at the weakest credit, CoreWeave, with default swaps above 1,000 basis points. It runs up the quality scale to Oracle, a far stronger name, at a record 288 basis points. The open question is whether that stress has reached the top rung, the investment-grade hyperscalers. Some reports say swaps on names like Amazon, Meta and Nvidia hit record highs. Our follow-up research could not confirm a current level for any of them. And for an AA name, the level matters more than the record label. A record off a thin, low base can simply reflect lenders hedging AI-project exposure. That's not the same as doubt about Amazon's or Meta's solvency. The so-what: the stress has climbed the quality ladder in label, not yet in level.

So here's the setup for the Fed's October 28th decision. That meeting hasn't happened, and I'm not predicting it. But the Fed now faces supply-driven inflation and widening AI credit spreads at the same time. Hiking into a credit scare risks a policy-error narrative. Holding with core producer prices up 5 percent risks looking behind the data. What's clear is that the market's two big trades this week both made the same bet. Buying duration and buying small caps each say credit and growth fear outrank inflation risk. That's a coherent trade if this energy shock destroys demand. It's a fragile one if core pass-through still has room to run.

Watch two things into month-end. First, do Oracle's and CoreWeave's credit spreads keep widening? That would say the credit story is deepening, not plateauing. Second, does the long end give back Thursday's rally as the PPI print gets digested? That would say duration isn't done repricing to inflation. Right now the market is treating these as separate stories. They may be the same one.

That's the radar for today. Trade carefully, and I'll see you next time.
