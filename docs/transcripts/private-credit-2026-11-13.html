Private Credit & the Corporate Credit Cycle

Transcript · November 13, 2026

Good morning, it's Friday, November 13th, 2026, and this is your private credit briefing.

The argument over whether private credit's default numbers are real has left the commentary pages. This week it showed up in sector-wide data, and in the courts.

Start with a callback. Last Saturday this desk said there was no industry-wide non-accrual figure to test the single-manager prints against. Now there is one. PitchBook LCD tracks debt held by BDC borrowers affected by non-accruals. That includes loans that are still performing but sit with a troubled issuer. On that measure, the total nearly doubled year over year, to eighteen point eight billion dollars. That's three point six percent of total BDC debt.

Be precise about what that number is. It's an exposure-to-troubled-borrowers figure, not a non-accrual rate. It will run well above the ratios individual BDCs report in their own filings, so don't compare the two like for like. And a doubling from a low base is a turn in direction, not proof of a crisis. But the sample is broad. PitchBook's first-quarter version of this series covered two hundred thirteen BDCs, run by a hundred and nine managers. So this is no longer a story about Goldman's BDC or Barings. It's the sector.

The new turn is where the critique is now being argued. Mayer Brown's November litigation update covers amended securities complaints against BDC advisers. Across several of them, plaintiffs allege the same pattern. A troubled loan came off the non-accrual list. It was replaced with new debt or equity carried at or near full value. In several instances, plaintiffs say, the new paper was carried at a hundred percent of principal. In some cases the adviser reversed write-downs it had already taken, so the net effect looked neutral. Plaintiffs say this made credit quality look better than it was.

Hold that carefully. No defendant is named in what we have, and these are allegations in amended complaints, not findings. The plaintiffs' bar has every incentive to frame an ordinary workout as concealment. And a liability-management exercise that keeps a viable borrower out of bankruptcy can be good lending. So the question these cases turn on is narrower. Was the replacement paper marked near full value without evidence the business could support it? That remains alleged, not shown. The size of any masking effect across the sector is inference too. Nobody has audited it.

Here's why litigation matters more than commentary anyway. Win or lose, the suits raise the cost of aggressive non-accrual and valuation policy. That can push advisers toward faster recognition and lumpier markdowns across the industry. That's an implication, not a forecast. So bank this. The masking critique now has a sector-wide number behind it and a legal vector pushing on it.

Now the bellwether check, Ares Capital. Its third-quarter filing puts net asset value at nineteen dollars and thirty-one cents a share. That's down from nineteen ninety-four at the end of 2025. GAAP net income was forty-four cents a share for the quarter. So the NAV decline is about three percent over nine months, not a single-quarter shock. Some of it may be spread or mark movement rather than realized loss. But slow erosion at the sector's reference book is exactly what a sector-wide drift looks like. And it isn't uniform. Blue Owl's listed BDC reported no new issuers added to non-accrual this quarter. The average is drifting the wrong way, but the spread between managers is wide.

Now the same mechanics show up in the public loan market. That complicates something this desk said last Saturday. Our line then was that on defaults, the public market was healing and the private one wasn't. Fitch now reports that distressed debt exchanges made up a record seventy-nine percent of 2026 defaults in the CLO portfolios it rates. The prior record was sixty-nine percent, set in 2024. That's a share of defaults, not a default rate. And exchanges aren't hidden from the count, because Fitch treats them as defaults. They're simply now the dominant form default takes.

The benign read deserves its strongest case, and Deutsche Bank supplies it. Its model-based measure of CLO defaults includes restructurings and distressed exchanges. It put the rate at one point one percent through September 1st. That's about half the two point five percent at the same point a year earlier. So even with exchanges counted, defaults in the syndicated loan pool roughly halved. That's the best evidence against a broad turn in the syndicated credit cycle. It also suggests part of Fitch's record share is arithmetic. When bankruptcies fall, exchanges become a bigger slice of a smaller pie.

Back to the private side, where definitions decide the headline. KBRA's direct-lending index is dated November 4th, and it's relayed by The Lead Left rather than drawn from KBRA directly. It puts the non-accrual rate at two point three percent for direct lending, and one point seven percent in the lower middle market. That looks like the cleanest number in today's data. But KBRA excludes shifts to PIK and covenant violations from that measure. Those are exactly the channels this desk has argued carry the stress a non-accrual print misses.

Don't stack these three measures. KBRA, Fitch and Deutsche Bank use different universes and different definitions, and differencing them would be false precision. What they agree on is this. Default rates are low in both the public and private loan markets. And in both, stress is increasingly resolved by negotiated exchange rather than bankruptcy. If that's right, headline default rates in both markets understate how much capital structure is being rewritten. The real loss signal sits in recoveries and re-default rates. Nothing in today's data quantifies either one. That's the open question.

None of that is slowing the money. Direct-lending funds raised about a hundred and twenty-nine billion dollars in the first nine months of 2026, according to Preqin data cited by Reuters. That's up thirty-five percent from a year earlier. Part of that growth may reflect a soft 2025 base, or the timing of a few large closes. The insurance channel is widening too. Standard Life has a new UK pension-risk-transfer partnership with CVC, Prudential, Goldman Sachs and MS&AD. It carries up to two billion pounds of commitments, and "up to" is a ceiling, not deployed capital. It carries the annuity-funded lending model into UK pension risk transfer. It also raises a question the reporting doesn't answer. How arm's-length is an insurer funding its partners' own deal pipelines?

Set that against the retail channel. As this desk laid out yesterday, semi-liquid vehicles sold to wealth clients are still rationing exits. So the funding split is widening. Institutional and insurance capital is accelerating in, while retail capital queues at the door.

Now separate the marketing from the money. Commitments aren't deployed capital. And they certainly aren't returns net of losses and fees. A record raise locks in management-fee growth whatever happens to the loans beneath it. Here's the desk's inference, not a measured fact. Record commitments chasing a thinner deal pipeline tend to show up first in terms, meaning leverage, covenants and documentation, before they show up in defaults. One signal cuts the other way. On Tuesday this desk noted that Blue Owl's listed BDC was pricing its new loans wider. So the evidence on underwriting discipline is mixed, not settled. Either way, that new capital is going to work in the same market where troubled-borrower exposure just nearly doubled.

So where does the cycle actually stand? This is not a hard-default turn. It's a cycle where stress is increasingly resolved by negotiated exchange and measured by contested definitions. Meanwhile, a record vintage of capital is being underwritten against loss assumptions calibrated to those same definitions. That gap is the biggest unresolved risk. It sits between what the headline default and non-accrual numbers show, and the recoveries nobody is measuring yet.

Watch three things. First, ING's data-center loan auction. The bank is seeking competitive bids on about five hundred million dollars spread across twelve data-center loans. It's part of a growing market where banks move AI-buildout debt to private credit firms, asset managers and insurers. Bids are due November 19th. Where that paper clears is a live price test for data-center debt once it leaves a bank's balance sheet. Second, the remaining third-quarter BDC prints this desk has been waiting on, including FS KKR and Golub, read for PIK mix as much as for non-accruals. Third, whatever comes next in the BDC adviser litigation.

That's the lending machine for November 13th. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
