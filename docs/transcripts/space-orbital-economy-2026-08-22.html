Space & the Orbital Economy

Good morning. It's August 22nd, and this is your space and orbital economy briefing.

On Monday, we passed along a warning that Golden Dome, the Pentagon's homeland missile defense architecture, could halt when the new fiscal year begins in October. We flagged it then as reported second-hand and not confirmed by a direct quote. It no longer is. General Michael Guetlein, the program's director, told the Space and Missile Defense Symposium in Huntsville on August 11th, in his own words, quote, "If they don't figure it out, there's no Golden Dome because there is no funding." That is not a spokesperson softening a risk. That is the man running the program describing the conditions under which it stops.

Here's the structure behind the warning. The Pentagon has requested 17.5 billion dollars for Golden Dome in fiscal 2027. Only about 398 million of that sits in the regular base budget. The remaining roughly 97 percent depends on a second reconciliation package that has not passed Congress. Normally, when appropriations run late, a continuing resolution extends last year's spending levels and everything limps along. Not here. A continuing resolution extends an existing annual base top line, and Golden Dome doesn't have one to extend. The usual Washington shock absorber has nothing to grab onto. So this isn't a spending-level problem. It's a funding-architecture problem, and it has a date on it: October 1st.

Hold two readings of Guetlein at once. A program director publicly saying his own program stops is unusual and self-damaging, which cuts against pure budget theatre. But it is also precisely the move you make when you want to force a reconciliation vehicle through. Both readings can be true, and neither one is a vote. His remarks are a statement at a symposium, not an executed budget outcome.

Be precise about what's actually exposed. A lapse in new funding is not a stop-work order on contracts already signed. The awards already obligated for the proliferated tracking layer keep flowing, and nobody has described them as at risk. What's exposed is forward build rate, tooling, and working capital for the next round. That's where the second-order effect lives. Contractors who have already tooled production lines against Golden Dome build rates carry labor and inventory against dollars that have not been released. A prime with a balance sheet absorbs that for a quarter or two. A newer entrant funding growth out of cash flow cannot, which quietly advantages the incumbents in the next round of awards. The other likely response is a re-weighting: primes and newer entrants alike shift effort toward the broader missile-warning and proliferated-architecture lines that do have multi-year base funding. If that happens, the announced Golden Dome demand signal never converts into the constellation build rate the sector has been capitalizing.

Which brings us to the demand side, because that's where this shows up on a specific balance sheet. Rocket Lab is the clearest case. Earlier this month, the Space Force awarded Rocket Lab 397 million dollars to develop, launch, and operate "Flatellite" surveillance satellites under the Space-Based Airborne Moving Target Indicator program, part of a 615 million dollar package split three ways. Independent reporting names Neutron, Rocket Lab's still-unflown medium-lift rocket, as the launch vehicle for that award. So one revenue line now carries two separate risks stacked on top of each other: whether Congress appropriates the money, and whether Neutron actually flies. And these compound. The probability the revenue arrives on schedule is the product of the two, not whichever one looks better. On the vehicle side, the company's own language is that stage-one tank production remains aligned with delivering Neutron to the launch pad in the fourth quarter of this year. Note what that sentence actually is. It's a pad-delivery target, not a launch-date commitment. Those are different promises.

One correction to how this has been described elsewhere. Rocket Lab also signed Kepler Communications this month as Neutron's first named commercial customer. That's a single dedicated Neutron mission, not a multi-launch package. The five-launch Neutron, three-Electron agreement that's been circulating alongside it is a real contract, but it's a separate deal, announced back in May, with a different and still confidential customer. Worth being precise, because it's the difference between one Neutron-dependent revenue event and five. The compounding-risk argument survives the correction. The Kepler piece of it is simply smaller than it's been made to look.

We won't re-run Rocket Lab's quarterly numbers here. The point today is the composition of the backlog, not its size. And there's an honest counterweight. Rocket Lab's Space Systems division, satellite manufacturing rather than launch, is where revenue is actually being recognized right now, and the Flatellite award covers building the satellites as well as flying them. The launch leg can slip without the whole award slipping with it. So the equity is becoming a levered bet on two things the company doesn't control, a launch date and a congressional vote, but it isn't existential the way it would be if launch were the only leg.

Step back, and the pattern this week is that the binding constraint in national-security space isn't a rocket at all. It's whether the money clears. And if American capital for orbital infrastructure is stuck in procedure, it's worth noting where capital isn't stuck. AST SpaceMobile has landed a Japanese government-backed joint venture with Rakuten, in which Tokyo's support takes the form of subsidies covering up to half the costs of satellite procurement, launch, and ground-facility construction. AST paired that with a billion-dollar convertible note raise due 2034 to fund the rest. A foreign government is underwriting half the capital stack of a private constellation, on schedule, while the Pentagon's flagship space program can't get its own appropriation through regular order. That's the inversion worth sitting with, and it cuts against the standing assumption that Western capital markets fund space faster than state balance sheets do.

But don't over-read the subsidy. AST is still running a beta service, with roughly three thousand active digital cells across the continental United States. Halving the capital cost of a constellation changes the denominator in the business case. It doesn't prove the end market shows up. And a billion dollars of convertible debt is the market pricing dilution, not conviction. State money lowers the hurdle rate. It doesn't create demand.

Last thing to watch, and it's close. SpaceX is targeting Flight 14 of Starship no earlier than August 28th, per an FCC filing. We told you on the 18th that this would be a booster catch attempt. That framing needs correcting. Per the filing's own description, the catch attempt is on the upper-stage Ship at the launch tower, and it comes bundled with the vehicle's first insertion into Earth orbit, alongside another deployment of roughly twenty Starlink V3 satellites. That's a harder milestone than what we described. Two caveats on it. An FCC filing establishes a communications window, not an FAA-licensed launch date, and this program slips routinely. And even a clean flight is a test flight, not a proven, repeatable service, no matter how the Starlink deployment goes.

So where does that leave the sector this week. The constraint isn't engineering ambition, and it isn't manufacturing throughput. It's whether appropriated cash, launch dates, and contested capital converge on the same calendar. Three things to watch: whether the reconciliation package passes before October 1st, whether Rocket Lab produces a firm Neutron launch date rather than a pad-delivery target, and whether Flight 14 actually flies on the 28th. None of those depend on more engineering. All of them depend on money and a calendar.
