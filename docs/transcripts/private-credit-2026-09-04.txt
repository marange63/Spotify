Good morning, it's Friday, September 4th. This is Private Credit.

The cycle's stress this week isn't showing up in a default print. It's showing up in the two promises this asset class actually sells to its buyers: periodic liquidity, and senior-secured recovery. Both got marked down in the past three days by outside forces, not by the managers' own valuation committees. Let's take them in order, starting with liquidity.

On September 3rd, the two largest semi-liquid vehicles in the private-credit wealth channel both re-gated their quarterly redemptions. Start with Blackstone's BCRED, a $77.2 billion fund. It took in redemption requests equal to about 10% of shares outstanding, or $4.3 billion, and is honoring only its standard 5% cap. So roughly half the investors who asked to leave this quarter are made to wait.

Cliffwater's Corporate Lending Fund is the other one, and the pressure there is heavier. At $31 billion, it saw investors seek to pull about 16% of shares. It too is capping at 5%, which means those investors get back only about one-third of what they asked for. This is now the third consecutive quarter both funds have hit their caps.

Before you read that as a credit event, it isn't one. These vehicles are working exactly as designed. Semi-liquid structures were built to meet only a fraction of redemption demand in any single quarter, and gating is the contractual feature, not a breakdown. Neither fund has disclosed why investors are heading for the exit. It could be return dissatisfaction, liquidity needs elsewhere, or portfolio reallocation, and we simply don't know. Nor are the two request rates strictly comparable, given different tender mechanics and investor mixes. So don't call this a run on the underlying loans. Call it a structural mismatch between an illiquid asset and a periodic-liquidity wrapper, and that mismatch is the actual story.

It's worth resisting the performance explanation too, because the numbers don't support it either way. The only return BCRED discloses is a 9% annualized total return on its Class I shares since the fund's 2021 inception. That's an inception-to-date figure, not a third-quarter mark. And the most recent aggregate NAV in the disclosures is $42.8 billion as of June 30th, a Q2 number. So there's no fresh performance print to blame the redemptions on, which pushes you back toward the structural read rather than a return-chasing exit.

The reason the structure matters is that the gap is compounding, not clearing. BCRED carried $2.3 billion of unfulfilled Q2 tenders straight into the Q3 queue, on top of this quarter's new demand. Cliffwater's cumulative math is worse. Since Q1, investors trying to exit have received only 78% of the capital they requested, against a backlog that now runs roughly $15 billion. Three straight quarters of 5% caps means the unmet balance rolls forward and grows, rather than resetting each quarter.

There's a fresh wrinkle on BCRED specifically. Its own new subscriptions slowed to nearly $750 million of inflows this quarter, even as Blackstone said its other private-wealth products saw stronger fundraising momentum than the second quarter. That's the redemption queue starting to show up on the other side of the ledger. Growth is throttling down at the same fund that's gating exits, and it's lagging Blackstone's own wealth-product trend. This is the first fund-specific evidence, rather than industry inference, that managers may have to slow the intake to manage the backlog.

Here's the so-what. Retailization's biggest constraint isn't fundraising appetite. It's whether the liquidity promise holds up under sustained two-to-three-times demand, and three quarters in, it hasn't. That directly complicates the push to route wealth-channel and 401(k) money into these same periodic-liquidity structures. If retail investors conclude that 5% caps are structural rather than episodic, the marketing gets harder exactly where the industry wants the next leg of growth to come from.

There's a second-order effect worth naming. When the primary redemption door stays half-shut for this long, capital looks for a side door. Persistent gating tends to push exiting investors toward the secondary market for private-credit fund stakes. There, a buyer will take the position off your hands immediately, but only at a discount to stated NAV. That discount is itself a market price on the same assets the funds carry at model marks. The wider it goes, the more it signals that outside buyers value this paper below where the valuation committees do. That's the same tension running through the whole week: an outside party pricing private credit below the manager's own mark.

Now, from the liquidity promise to the recovery promise, where a bankruptcy just delivered the harder lesson.

First Brands' remaining Chapter 11 cases formally converted to Chapter 7 on September 1st. Judge Christopher Lopez's order came a week after his August 24th bench ruling denying confirmation of the debtors' liquidating plan. Eleven months after the company filed, most of its roughly $1.1 billion in new-money DIP financing was gone. That new money sat inside a larger $4.4 billion facility, alongside a $3.3 billion roll-up of existing debt. And at least $222 million of obligations incurred during the bankruptcy itself remained unpaid. Those claims sit at the very top of the priority waterfall. They are super-priority, bankruptcy-incurred dollars, the most protected money in the entire capital structure, and even they couldn't be fully covered.

That's the real lesson here, and it's transferable well beyond one auto-parts liquidation. The failure wasn't a waterfall problem. Priority did its job, and these claims sat first in line. The failure was a collateral-value problem. The assets backing the DIP simply weren't worth what the marks assumed. Think about where that bites hardest. Asset-based lending and receivables finance is a fast-growing corner of private credit. In those strategies, recovery assumptions are built almost entirely on collateral marks, not on enterprise cash flow. First Brands is now the one real liquidation data point arguing against those model-based marks. Rating agencies and CLO managers underwriting similar structures should be stress-testing against this case specifically.

And the transmission runs one way here. Private marks can lag a public liquidation, not lead it. First Brands is a realized outcome that model-based collateral marks elsewhere have not yet had to confront. The longer those marks hold above what a forced sale would actually fetch, the sharper the eventual re-rate if any of these asset-based books is genuinely tested.

Conversion also changes who controls what happens next. An independent Chapter 7 trustee now controls estate claims and settlements. That's not the debtor, and not a debtor-proposed litigation trust. It includes any insider or lender-liability clawback actions, and the case deadlines have reset. That trustee hasn't been named yet in what's been reported. So how aggressively and how fast those claims get pursued is a genuine unknown, not a forecast. It's a live tail risk for any lender or sponsor with First Brands-adjacent exposure. And final recovery clarity for junior creditors could now slip well past the near-term catalysts the market is watching.

Put the two threads together, and here's where the cycle actually stands. The binding risk in private credit right now isn't the payment layer. Defaults and non-accruals remain the metric everyone quotes as benign. The binding risk is the promise layer underneath it. Can you get your money back on schedule? And is the collateral actually worth its mark when a real liquidation tests it? This week, outside forces answered both questions less favorably than the managers' own materials would suggest.

Watch three things from here. First, whether Q4 redemptions gate again or finally clear. Second, who gets named trustee in First Brands, and how fast clawback litigation moves. And third, the Q3 BDC non-accrual and payment-in-kind prints due in November, which will show whether the payment layer is still holding while the promise layer erodes.
