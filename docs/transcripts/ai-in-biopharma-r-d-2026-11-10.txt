Good morning. It's November 10th, 2026, and this is your Cautious Optimism Briefing on AI in biopharma R&D.

Today's thread is own versus rent. For weeks, this show has described big pharma buying AI discovery capability as cheap options. Yesterday a drugmaker bought one of the engines outright. The catch is that this engine was never cheap to rent, and that changes what the deal actually tells us.

Start with argenx, the immunology company. In a 6-K filed yesterday, argenx said it will acquire the rest of Unnatural Products, a macrocyclic peptide company it already partly owns. The price is 550 million dollars upfront. Up to 595 million dollars more is contingent on development and regulatory milestones. By our arithmetic, that contingent piece is slightly more than half of the roughly 1.15 billion dollar headline. So this is still partly an option. It just carries a much larger strike, paid on day one.

Look at how argenx describes what it's buying. The release calls Unnatural Products' engine "a combination of parallel experimentation and machine learning." Parallel experimentation comes first. Our read is that this is a wet-lab loop with a model layer on top, not an AI platform in the trade-headline sense.

Now the detail that complicates the clean story. The release says argenx "already holds a minority stake" in the company. It frames the deal as building on "more than two years of collaboration and investment." CEO Karen Massey put it plainly, per MedCity News: "After working together for more than two years, we also know there is a strong fit in how we work." On November 5th we called GSK's approach to Chai Discovery the template. Test the vendor on your own bench, then sign. argenx is the strongest version of that template yet.

The second complication cuts against how this show has framed the trend. Per BioXconomy, Unnatural Products had already signed a deal with Novartis worth up to 100 million dollars in upfront and pre-IND milestone payments. It also has partnerships with Merck and with BridgeBio. The Novartis figure is a ceiling, and the upfront share isn't broken out. Still, this platform was already commanding serious rental terms from large pharma. So argenx's check is not the first real money this engine has seen. What's new is ownership. argenx is buying outright a loop that other drugmakers were paying to access piece by piece.

That leaves an open question. Neither the release nor the coverage says what happens to the Novartis, Merck and BridgeBio programs after close. This show has asked twice, on November 6th and 8th, who owns the data and model weights under an AI license. Buying the company should settle that by construction. But if those partner programs survive, argenx could own a platform that still earns milestones from other drugmakers. That's our inference, and it's unresolved.

Now the skeptical turn. The price is not validation. argenx names no programs, no targets, and no hit-rate or oral bioavailability data. Nor does it say how much of the engine is machine learning versus brute-force parallel chemistry. In the release's words, the company is "developing a portfolio of therapeutic macrocycles against high-value and traditionally difficult-to-drug targets." That's the only portfolio language. So the deal tells you argenx, after two years inside the data, judged the loop worth owning. It does not tell you the molecules work in people.

There's also a live alternative reading. The release twice frames the deal around oral and long-acting medicines. One line describes the aim to "develop oral or long-acting medicines for people living with serious immune-mediated diseases." On that reading, argenx is buying an oral-modality pipeline and a team for its immunology franchise. The machine learning could be incidental to the price. The disclosures can't separate those two stories.

Either way, the own-versus-rent split inside argenx is telling. argenx is one of Chai Discovery's design partners, as we noted on November 5th and 7th. So it rents AI design access in antibodies, its home turf. Massey called the peptide capabilities "highly complementary to our antibody engineering." Our inference: you rent model access where your own data and wet lab already supply the edge. You buy the engine in a modality you can't build. For AI-discovery equities, that makes this a takeout comparable, but a narrow one. It rewards platforms that own a wet-lab data loop in a modality big buyers lack, not pure-software design vendors. And outside platforms shouldn't read an insider's price as what an arm's-length buyer would pay them. The takeaway: the moat being priced is the engine that generates proprietary experimental data, not the model.

The same day, the other way to buy AI showed up. Pierre Fabre, the French drugmaker, signed a collaboration with XtalPi to design small molecules de novo. The work starts in oncology, with possible extension to dermatology and rare disease. Financial terms weren't disclosed. It's Pierre Fabre's second AI design partnership this year, after an earlier deal with Iktos. That could be deliberate hedging, or a sign that no single vendor has yet earned exclusivity. Either way, Pierre Fabre is renting many rather than owning one.

What renting buys has a ledger, and Recursion's collaboration with Sanofi supplies one. Yesterday we called Recursion's latest Sanofi milestone a beat, not a run-rate. Per Recursion's third-quarter update, Sanofi optioned the first development candidate from the collaboration. It's described as a potential first-in-class oral small molecule against a dual target in immunology. Sanofi's total upfront and milestone payments under the collaboration have reached 144 million dollars. More than 300 million dollars in further milestones remain, plus double-digit royalties if a drug is approved.

Notice the convergence: both Sanofi's candidate and argenx's bet point at oral immunology medicines. This comparison is our framing, and it isn't apples to apples. A collaboration fee stream bought work across multiple programs. An acquisition price bought a company. But the scale gap is the point. The Sanofi ledger to date has delivered one optioned candidate for 144 million dollars. argenx paid nearly four times that upfront, by our arithmetic, for an engine with no disclosed candidate. Renting is a slow, metered path to an asset. Owning is a large bet that the loop keeps producing.

If the owned asset is data, the clinic is where the other half of it lives. And here we owe you a correction. On November 7th we said Bristol Myers Squibb and Pathos were licensing Alzheimer's and dementia data from Tempus AI for 55 million dollars. We also called the 137.5 million dollar oncology figure a ceiling. Per secondary reporting on a summary of Tempus's SEC filing, both points were wrong. BMS will pay 137.5 million dollars in fixed payments through the end of 2031, for oncology data access, Tempus's Lens software and related services. So it's a committed cost, not a ceiling. The 55 million dollar neurology deal belongs to Pathos, with fees tied to delivery of up to 500,000 records.

That correction changes the read. A traditional drugmaker has made a fixed, annuity-like data commitment. An AI-native developer is paying per record delivered. By our arithmetic, that's a ceiling near 110 dollars per neurology record. So data is being priced as a budgeted input at both ends of the industry. Willingness to pay still isn't proof the data raises trial success rates.

One clock to watch while the capital moves. Demis Hassabis said in January that Isomorphic Labs expects its first clinical trials by the end of 2026. As of our latest research, there's no publicly confirmed dosing. Roughly seven weeks of the year remain. A first trial would be the cleanest translation proof the design-model side has produced. A miss wouldn't show the platform fails. It would show how long it takes to turn structure prediction into an IND-ready candidate.

Pulling the threads together. The capital in today's stories went to two kinds of owned input. One is an experimental loop in a new modality, bought outright. The other is clinical data under fixed, multi-year contract. Design-model access, by contrast, is still rented piecemeal, from Chai to XtalPi to Iktos. On clinical success rates, nothing here moves them yet. Deal prices and data contracts are bets on attrition, not measurements of it. On R&D spend, our inference is a change in form, not size. AI spend is moving from license and milestone fees into acquired capabilities with integration costs. It's also moving into fixed data commitments that sit in the budget for years. Four things to watch. Does the deal close by year-end, as argenx expects, and do the companies then disclose programs? What happens to the Novartis, Merck and BridgeBio partnerships? Does Isomorphic hit its year-end target? And do Pierre Fabre's XtalPi terms ever surface?

That's today's briefing. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
