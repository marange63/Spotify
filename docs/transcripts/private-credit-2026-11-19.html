Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Credit & the Corporate Credit Cycle

Transcript · November 19, 2026

Good morning, it's Thursday, November 19th.

This week the Fed put its own name to something the deal tape has been showing for weeks. And the bids that could put a first price on part of it are due today.

Start with the Fed. Its November Financial Stability Report added a new section, titled "Leverage in the Data Center Buildout." According to Fortune's coverage of the report, none of the five prior editions, going back to 2024, had one. That alone tells you where supervisory attention has moved. Then, on November 16th, in a speech in Milan, Governor Michael Barr put a number on it. In his words, the "potential maximum nominal value" of these off-balance-sheet obligations now exceeds three trillion dollars. He means long-term contracts, lease commitments, and residual value guarantees. Keep that number in its place. It is a ceiling on notional commitments. It is not a measured debt load, and it is not a loss estimate. And a report section plus one governor's speech is analysis, not a rule. Nothing binding follows from either.

Here is the chain behind that ceiling. Developers use those leases to finance construction. The debt behind that construction shows up in the market. Per Fortune's relay of the report, issuance tied to advanced computing rose to more than six hundred eighty billion dollars by October. That's about five times the hundred thirty billion issued in 2023, and it's the one pace figure worth holding onto. The riskier slices of that debt, the Fed says, sit with high-yield bond funds, life insurers, business development companies, and other private debt vehicles. The Fed also says private-credit deals make the full total harder to measure. There is no dollar figure for the private-credit slice. That absence is the finding, not a gap to be filled in.

The location of the risk matters more than its size, and Barr explains why. Opacity, he said, "can exacerbate selloffs when investor sentiment shifts, as it is hard to know where risk resides." Here's this desk's reading of that mechanism. When nobody can identify the holders, a sentiment shock gets repriced across everything that looks similar, not just the actual exposures. It's the same visibility frame Bank of England governor Andrew Bailey used last Friday, speaking as chair of the Financial Stability Board. He asked how connected private credit must become before it poses a stability risk. Now the Fed has applied that question to AI debt specifically. So bank this. The Fed can count the issuance, but it still can't map the holders.

The obvious next step is to assume losses would land first in those private marks, in BDC and insurer books that don't trade. Barr's own framing complicates that. In these asset-based financings, stronger counterparties guarantee the resale value of the data centers or GPUs pledged as collateral. Barr says those guarantees represent "the degree to which hyperscalers and GPU producers might absorb losses in the event of defaults." Fortune's relay gives the concrete case. On leased GPU capacity, Nvidia generally covers the gap between the lease's guaranteed minimum value and what's actually recovered. So the first direct loss on a default may fall on big tech and the chipmakers, not on the debt holders. What stays live for private lenders is slower and indirect. Think developer leverage, and what happens to debt service if a lease isn't renewed or the collateral loses value. That indirect channel is this desk's inference, not Barr's claim. The so-what is that private credit's exposure here looks less like a default problem and more like a pricing problem.

That brings us to today's live price test, because the handoff the Fed describes is being priced today. Bids of ten million dollars or more are due in ING's auction of roughly five hundred million dollars of data-center loans. An SMBC strategist named the likely buyers as private credit investors, asset managers, and insurers, buying for the expected higher return. That's the vehicle set the Fed just named. No result is known yet. A discount would be an early price on risk that has almost no public pricing. A clean clear near par would push back on the worry. Either way, one bank's sale can't by itself prove a broad bank retreat from data-center lending.

If the risk is landing in private vehicles, the next question is whether anything forces those vehicles to price it. That's where Fitch's latest BDC data matters, and it cuts against this desk's own line. Yesterday this desk said the binding risk isn't leverage, it's liquidity mismatch. Fitch's numbers, as relayed by Funds Society, push back on that. Fitch tracks fifteen non-traded BDCs. Thirteen of them saw fewer redemption requests in the third quarter than in the second. Requests are still elevated, averaging nine point eight percent of shares. Nine of the fifteen were above five percent. Fitch says it will take several quarters for the average to fall below five percent. It also says these BDCs have enough liquidity to cover several quarters of redemptions at the cap. There's no second-quarter average in the coverage, so we can't say how far requests fell, only that they fell almost everywhere. Ares chief executive Michael Arougheti said the redemption queue "is shrinking quarter over quarter." Take that for what it is, a manager describing his own book. But the direction is real, and the run-risk version of yesterday's thesis is weakening.

That cuts both ways. Fewer forced sales means less chance of fire-sale contagion. That's a genuine stability positive, and exactly what a regulator wants. But forced selling is also the mechanism that makes a private book print a price instead of holding a model mark. That mechanism is losing strength right when it would be most informative.

Liquidity is calming. Credit is not. Eddie O'Neill co-heads global leveraged credit at KKR. In a Bloomberg Intelligence interview, relayed by FA Magazine, he said he expects the distress ratio to climb toward eight to nine percent. His universe is U.S. and European leveraged loans plus high yield. That's a forecast, not a measured print, and the exact definition of his measure isn't confirmed. It's also broader than the U.S. loan gauges this desk cited yesterday, so don't read it as a precise update to those numbers. What's notable is the source. Yesterday's forward call, from JPMorgan, came from a bank. Today's comes from a lender. A firm that raises capital to deploy into dislocation isn't a neutral forecaster, but it is calling stress in the market it lends into. The coverage of the interview adds one sharper detail. It cites worries about the most fragile borrowers, from inflation and rates to the business impact of AI. Those worries have pushed the yield gap between triple-C names and stronger junk to its widest in years. So stress is concentrating in the weakest tier, not spreading evenly.

Now put the three pieces together, and this is this desk's inference, not a fact in any of these releases. Redemption pressure is easing. Forward distress calls are stacking up. And the Fed has just said it cannot see exactly where the riskiest AI-buildout debt sits. So the mechanism that would force private books to test their marks is weakening, just as the case for testing them gets stronger. The counter-case deserves equal footing. Calm redemptions buy time, and time lets managers work out problem loans without dumping them. Still, a setup where nothing forces the question is quieter than a liquidity run, and arguably more dangerous.

So where does the cycle actually stand? Realized defaults haven't turned. Yesterday's prints showed syndicated payment defaults flat, and private credit's own default gauge moving sideways. Forward calls, first from a bank and now from a major lender, keep pointing the other way. The biggest unresolved risk in private credit today isn't leverage. On Barr's own telling, it isn't mainly a direct default loss for debt holders either. It's opacity: AI-buildout tranches marked to model, inside vehicles nobody outside them can fully map. Three catalysts would confirm or refute that read. First, the ING result, whenever it surfaces. Second, the next quarterly redemption data, measured against Fitch's several-quarter path back below five percent. Third, November's loan-distress and default prints, which will test KKR's forward call.

That's the lending machine for November 19th. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
