Good morning. It's Friday, July 31st, 2026, and this is your briefing on robotics and physical AI.

This week the decisive variable in embodied AI wasn't a model release or a funding round. It was government policy, moving in two directions at once — Washington walling off part of the humanoid supply chain, and Washington opening the door to a new class of driverless vehicle. Let's start with the wall.

On Tuesday the FCC added what it calls "advanced robotic devices" to its Covered List, the mechanism that decides which foreign-made electronics can receive FCC equipment authorization. Here's the important correction to how this has been reported: this is not, in the FCC's own text, a China-specific robot ban. The order defines the covered category simply as mobile robots, "such as humanoids and quadrupeds," and the underlying national-security determination applies to foreign-made products "regardless of the nationality of origin." No country and no manufacturer is named anywhere in the FCC's own fact sheet. That matters, because the same rule would formally apply to a future European or Korean humanoid maker too — it's a nationality-neutral, category-based rule that happens to land hardest on China today because of who currently ships the most units.

And that current market reality is stark, according to press reporting on industry shipment data — not the FCC's own document, which cites no shipment figures at all. Six of the top ten humanoid manufacturers are Chinese, accounting for roughly 87% of all humanoid robots shipped as of this past January. Two of those firms, Unitree and AGIBOT, each imported about 5,000 units last year, out of some 15,000 shipped globally. So even a neutral rule, applied to today's supply chain, functionally hits Chinese suppliers hardest.

Two details temper the "ban" framing further. First, the order blocks only new device-model authorizations going forward — it doesn't recall anything already on the market, and it doesn't touch federal government purchases at all. So Unitree's H1 Pro, which just launched commercially in Europe on July 22 with Asia and North America launches following in August, isn't pulled from shelves; new models simply can't clear FCC authorization going forward. Second, the FCC built in an explicit escape hatch: a foreign producer can still win authorization for new devices if the Department of War grants "Conditional Approval" after a security review. That's a case-by-case lever, not a blanket door-closing — the practical bite of this rule depends entirely on how aggressively that review gets used.

The FCC frames the risk not as manufacturing dependency but as data and cybersecurity exposure — networked robots that could be commandeered or used to surveil their environment — and it notes this is now the third device category, after drones and consumer routers, added to the Covered List this way. Chairman Brendan Carr cast it as the agency acting, in his words, "in lock step with our national security agencies" to secure America's critical supply chains. So what to take from this: it's a real, structurally significant step — the first time the humanoid category itself has been treated as a security risk — but it's a slower, more contingent squeeze than a flat prohibition, and it lands seven weeks after the Pentagon put Unitree on its list of Chinese military-linked companies.

Now the other side of that decoupling. While Washington restricts finished robots coming in, Beijing has been tightening what goes out. China's Ministry of Commerce widened its dual-use export controls to 14 firms across the European Union by July 24, following similar curbs on ten U.S. companies in June. Rare-earth permanent magnets — neodymium, dysprosium — sit inside the actuators and motors of essentially every humanoid on the market, Chinese or Western-branded. The IEA has projected that full enforcement of controls like these could put trillions of dollars of downstream global production at risk, though that's a warning about a hypothetical full-enforcement scenario, not a number that's happened. The through-line here: the FCC can block the finished Chinese robot, but it can't yet source the rare-earth magnet inside a Figure or Apptronik unit. Western humanoid builders are exposed on both sides of this decoupling — locked out of cheap Chinese finished goods, and still dependent on Chinese-concentrated inputs.

Now pivot from wall to gate. On Thursday, NHTSA granted Amazon's Zoox what the company is calling the first-ever commercial exemption for a purpose-built robotaxi — a vehicle with no steering wheel and no pedals, distinct from Waymo's approach of retrofitting conventional cars. The exemption caps Zoox's commercial fleet at 2,500 vehicles per year for two years, under what NHTSA describes as "enhanced, adaptable oversight" that can evolve as the technology matures. Zoox plans to start charging for rides first in Las Vegas, with no confirmed date yet beyond "soon." Some headlines have framed this as a 5,000-vehicle approval — that appears to be the two-year cap compounded, not a single annual number, so take the 2,500-a-year figure as the conservative, sourced read. And Zoox still needs separate permits from California's Public Utilities Commission and DMV before it can operate there commercially — this is federal operating authority, not a green light everywhere, and it's not disclosed unit economics either. No fare, no cost-per-ride, no revenue figure has come with it.

Why this matters beyond Zoox: NHTSA has effectively created a repeatable exemption structure for vehicles built from scratch without human controls, rather than case-by-case waivers. That's the precedent Tesla's from-scratch robotaxi ambitions, and any other no-human-controls program, will now need to clear. It could also become a long-run cost advantage for Amazon's approach versus Waymo, which still has to retrofit consumer vehicles.

Which brings us to the counterweight. Also this week, Waymo resumed freeway operations across its entire service area, including construction zones, after a two-month pause. The company had voluntarily recalled nearly 4,000 robotaxis in June after at least 13 incidents of vehicles driving into highway sections closed for construction. Software updates are now in place, and Waymo — which runs roughly 500,000 paid rides a week by outside estimates, a figure not directly disclosed by the company itself — is resuming service starting with Phoenix. And note where Waymo is heading next: on July 8 it named four new markets, one of them Las Vegas — the very city where Zoox plans to charge its first fares. Retrofit and purpose-built are about to meet head-to-head on the same streets. The lesson running through the AV story all year is the same: the binding constraint on autonomous vehicles at scale isn't model capability, it's the long tail of edge cases like a construction zone that doesn't match the map. Zoox just cleared a regulatory hurdle Waymo already has; Waymo just relearned that clearing the hurdle once doesn't mean the tail is closed.

Pulling the threads together: this week policy was the accelerant and the brake in the same breath. The FCC is walling off — carefully, not absolutely — the Chinese finished-goods side of the humanoid supply chain, while China squeezes the input side back. NHTSA opened a repeatable path for purpose-built robotaxis while a construction-zone recall reminded everyone why that path stays narrow. Watch three things next: how the Department of War actually uses its Conditional Approval discretion, whether Zoox's Las Vegas rides actually start charging fares, and whether China's export tightening reaches further into the robotics supply chain specifically. That's where the real signal will show up next, not in another demo video.
