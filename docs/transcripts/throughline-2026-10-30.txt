Good morning, this is The Throughline for Friday, October 30th, 2026.

Today's headline: Amazon confirmed the hyperscaler cash squeeze is an industry story, and the market sorted the spenders by earnings delivery, punishing Amazon and Alphabet for misses and rewarding Microsoft for a beat.

Eleven briefings shipped today, running from the macro backdrop through the AI and compute stack to the applied frontier.

Strategic Power opens with four contests fought this week on paper rather than on the ground. The FCC advanced a plan to more than double satellite-to-phone spectrum. Carriers are fighting to carve nearly 300 megahertz out of it, even as Verizon's CEO says satellite can't compete. Banned Nvidia chips found by accident in China, a UK defense target funded by reshuffling rather than new money, and a Japan-Germany minerals statement with no tonnage all share one gap: the rule is running ahead of physical reality.

Capital Markets Radar moves to numbers that just landed, with Amazon's trailing free cash flow swinging to a 42.4 billion dollar outflow, even though operating cash flow rose 29 percent. The market sorted the spenders by earnings, not capex: Amazon and Alphabet missed and were punished, while Microsoft beat and was rewarded, a narrower and more reversible signal than an AI capex top. Outside mega-cap, neither volatility nor crypto flows confirm a broader risk-off.

Digital Money carries a separate shock: Binance suffered the largest exchange theft in crypto's history, roughly 6 billion dollars, which Binance says may trace to a compromised engineer's laptop. The telling detail is what got frozen: Tether and Circle froze over two billion dollars of stablecoins within about a day, while the stolen bitcoin and ether can't be clawed back. Binance has pledged to cover every loss, but its own reserve disclosures now leave ether under-backed.

Private Credit and the Corporate Credit Cycle sees fundraising outrunning lending in a top lender's own numbers for the first time: Blue Owl raised 49 billion dollars of new capital, yet net deployment in its direct-lending book was slightly negative. Management blames soft M&A, but the desk worries the strongest borrowers are refinancing out at par and leaving weaker credits behind. And the Bank of England is examining whether Barclays misrepresented illiquid private-credit loans, so the marks question now has a bank leg.

Private Equity picks up the same theme from the deal side. Thrive Holdings, financed solely by Apollo funds, is taking ADT private again at about 7.6 billion dollars of equity, the third workaround this week for a sponsor-to-sponsor market that has stopped transacting. The desk's read is that capital keeps arriving at the biggest managers but recycles inside private markets rather than flowing back to limited partners.

That's the macro backdrop. Now the AI and compute stack, where supply, not demand, sets the pace.

Semiconductors and Compute Infrastructure checked whether Tesla is really a customer for Intel's 14A process, and Intel's own filing doesn't say so. It names no external foundry customer, only a risk factor about an unresolved effort to win one. The desk is also correcting Wednesday's episode, which said Intel had framed the Tesla deal itself. That claim rests on press reports alone.

A standing disclosure for the next two desks: Anthropic makes the model behind this show. Frontier AI Lab Competition has the price war moving from cost per token to cost per finished task: OpenAI's new Terra halved its list price but cut cost per completed task roughly fourfold, by its own numbers. Anthropic went a different way with a 40 percent federal discount, which the desk reads as buying government lock-in without touching its list price, while Google sat the fight out, gating its newest model to cyber defenders.

AI Products and Applications sees this week's launches extending where agents can reach, not adding another chatbot. Apple is opening Keynote, Pages, and Numbers to outside agents through the Model Context Protocol. What it hasn't said is how users will approve that access. Liquid AI claims a long-context memory cache about a third the size of its chosen rival's, which decides whether that work fits on a phone rather than a server.

Enterprise Technology confirms the pattern: AWS's backlog hit 578 billion dollars, growing at a triple-digit rate, making it three hyperscalers out of three where demand isn't the constraint and GPU supply is. The market's new lens is cash conversion, which is why Alphabet had its worst day as a public company despite cloud revenue roughly doubling.

Now the applied frontier, where industrial planning is outrunning proof.

Robotics and Physical AI has Boston Dynamics asking Korean suppliers, led by Hyundai Mobis, to prepare for purchase orders covering ten thousand Atlas humanoids, with reduced reliance on China among the stated priorities. But the buyer is Hyundai itself, and nobody has disclosed whether the actuators still depend on Chinese rare-earth magnets. The real sovereignty test is the magnet layer, not the assembly address.

Space and the Orbital Economy closes the tour with a press report that US intelligence sees China favoring lasers for counterspace. The desk argues that shifts value toward cheap, replaceable satellites. The rockets meant to diversify launch away from SpaceX are moving hardware but won't commit to dates, leaving an analyst and the trade press, not the operators, to name the calendar.

One change versus earlier this week. On Wednesday, this show traced a financing ladder from CoreWeave's bonds up to a chip vendor backstopping its customer's lenders, and on Thursday it flagged Alphabet's negative free cash flow. Today the cost reached the hyperscalers themselves: three of the four biggest AI spenders show negative free cash flow on at least one measure, and Microsoft, the exception, just sold 25 billion dollars of bonds.

That's The Throughline. Have a good weekend.
