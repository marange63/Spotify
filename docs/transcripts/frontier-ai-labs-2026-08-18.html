Good morning. It's Tuesday, August 18th, and this is your frontier AI labs briefing.

Model capability at the top of the leaderboard has been essentially frozen for about a week now. So the real competition has moved down a layer, into how these labs are financing the compute buildout that capability depends on. And yesterday that competition produced its most concrete data point yet: the actual structure of Nvidia's guarantee behind OpenAI's new Ohio data center.

Nvidia and OpenAI announced a twenty-year lease at SB Energy's PORTS-Pike campus in Pike County, Ohio. The initial buildout is 4.25 IT-gigawatts, with an option to scale to 8 IT-gigawatts, and OpenAI is the customer for the full amount. Nvidia is putting $1.5 billion directly into SB Energy and at least $4.2 billion into regional grid infrastructure, and SB Energy commits to building at least 10 gigawatts of new power generation. None of this comes online quickly — capacity arrives in phases starting in 2028.

Here's the number that matters most: this deal caps Nvidia's guaranteed exposure at roughly $105 billion. That is a real collapse from where this story started. Earlier reporting had Nvidia in talks for a backstop as high as $250 billion for a 10-gigawatt buildout. Then the Wall Street Journal reported Nvidia was trimming that to under $120 billion. The deal that actually signed came in lower than even that trimmed figure. So the trend across the summer wasn't a stabilization around a big number — it was a steady walk-down, and it landed well below the last trial balloon. That is the story: not the size of the guarantee, but the direction and the persistence of the shrinkage. Even the best-capitalized chip supplier in the world has a ceiling on how much vendor-financed risk it's willing to carry.

And structurally, this isn't the blank-check backstop the headline number implies. Reporting on the underlying filing describes it as a capped residual value guarantee — Nvidia is backing the value of the finished buildings, not directly promising to cover OpenAI's rent. Payment only triggers if OpenAI defaults or goes insolvent, and even then, SB Energy has to first try to re-lease the site at the same price, then try to sell it, before any shortfall becomes Nvidia's problem. Nvidia also chooses the remedy — it can assume the lease, force a re-letting, push a sale, or defer for up to a year. And OpenAI has separately agreed to reimburse Nvidia for anything it actually pays out. So the real tail risk here isn't a simple missed payment. It's a scenario where OpenAI is both insolvent and the real estate has lost value at the same time. That's a narrower, more contingent exposure than a flat "$105 billion backstop" headline suggests — worth remembering next time that number gets cited as pure downside risk sitting on Nvidia's books.

One honest caveat on sourcing: this $105 billion figure and the mechanism behind it trace to secondary reporting characterizing an SEC filing, not a directly quoted Nvidia statement. Nvidia's own release talks in gigawatts and the $1.5 billion equity and $4.2 billion grid figures, not a single headline guarantee number. Treat it as well-sourced but not primary-quoted.

What this confirms, regardless of the exact mechanics, is that Nvidia has moved beyond being a chip vendor. It's now an equity investor in the power company, a funder of grid infrastructure, and a guarantor of real estate value — all wrapped around a single customer. That deepens Nvidia's circular exposure to OpenAI's success. If AI demand disappoints, Nvidia isn't just selling fewer chips, it's holding an equity stake and a contingent guarantee tied to the same outcome.

Now, the other side of this trade is Anthropic, and its financing strategy looks almost nothing like OpenAI's. Where OpenAI negotiated a direct, disclosed guarantee from its chip supplier, Anthropic has been quietly building roughly $71 billion in chip-lease debt through special-purpose vehicles over just the past sixty days, and keeping it off its own balance sheet. There were two deals: a $35 billion package in June arranged by Apollo Global Management and Blackstone Credit and Insurance, and a second roughly $36 billion package reported in early August, though that second one is still described as preliminary rather than confirmed closed.

The structure matters here too. Broadcom backstops the senior tranches — about $6 billion in the top slice, another $24 billion just below it — effectively lending those tranches its own investment-grade credit rating. A $4.5 billion junior tranche carries no backstop at all, and trades at 8.5 percent, which tells you where the market actually prices the risk once Broadcom's name comes off. Set against this: Anthropic's own annualized revenue run rate, in the reporting tied specifically to this financing story, is about $19 billion, against estimated compute spending of roughly $45 billion a year. Note that other reporting this spring cited a higher revenue figure, north of $30 billion — that's likely a different measurement date, not a contradiction to resolve here, but it's a gap worth watching.

And there's a second-order worry baked into the structure itself. When tens of billions in compute debt sit inside special-purpose vehicles rather than on the lab's own balance sheet, equity investors can't easily see the true leverage building up in the AI ecosystem. That opacity rhymes uncomfortably with the off-balance-sheet vehicles that masked risk before 2008 — not a prediction of the same outcome, but a reason to treat headline lab valuations with some skepticism about what's hidden underneath them.

So look at what both labs are actually doing. OpenAI got a direct guarantee from its chip supplier, structured with layers of recourse back to OpenAI itself. Anthropic built a private-credit structure that keeps tens of billions in debt off its own books, with Broadcom absorbing the senior risk. Different instruments, same instinct: neither lab wants AI compute risk sitting nakedly on its own balance sheet. That's now a real competitive dimension, separate from model quality entirely — call it capital-structure sophistication. And it raises the same unresolved question in both cases: if AI revenue growth disappoints, who actually eats the loss? For OpenAI's guarantee, contractually, it loops back to OpenAI itself unless it's insolvent. For Anthropic's SPVs, it loops back to Broadcom's balance sheet on the senior tranches, and to whoever's holding that 8.5 percent junior paper on the rest. Neither answer is "nobody."

There's a third financing route in play too, and it's the one that would actually bring outside public capital into this picture rather than routing risk between insiders. OpenAI is reportedly targeting a roughly trillion-dollar IPO around September, following a confidential S-1 filing in May, with a valuation band reported between $852 billion and above $1 trillion. Treat the timing and valuation as targets, not settled facts — and treat the revenue and loss figures floating around that filing as aggregator summaries of a confidential document, not primary text. But directionally, an IPO of that scale would be the first time public markets, rather than chip vendors and private credit funds, get to price all of these financing assumptions at once. That's the real test coming.

So the scoreboard this week isn't the benchmark leaderboard — it's the debt stack. Nvidia found its ceiling on vendor financing. Anthropic built an off-balance-sheet structure to avoid having one. And OpenAI is about to find out what public markets think of the whole arrangement. Watch the cap tables, not just the capability charts.

That's the briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
