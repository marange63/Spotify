Good morning, it's Wednesday, August 26th, and this is your AI products briefing.

For a week now, this show has told you the AI price war lives in the cheap tiers. This week it reached the top of the stack.

Start with OpenAI. On August 21st, OpenAI cut the API price of GPT-5.6 Sol, its flagship model. Input fell from five dollars to four dollars per million tokens. Output fell from thirty dollars to twenty dollars. In OpenAI's own words, that is twenty percent lower input pricing and thirty-three percent lower output pricing. One sourcing note: the announcement came through OpenAI's developer community forum rather than a filed pricing page, so treat it as an OpenAI-authored post, not a formal disclosure. The cut also covers Fast mode, Batch and Flex processing, and eligible ChatGPT Work and Codex credits. Here is the detail that matters more than the headline percentage. This touches metered API and credit usage only. Pro, Plus and Business subscription usage is explicitly unchanged, and OpenAI says so in its own words. So OpenAI is not giving consumers a discount. It is buying developer volume.

Compare that to last Friday, when this show reported Anthropic canceling a planned price increase on Claude Sonnet 5, alongside OpenAI's earlier eighty percent cut to its budget-tier Luna model. Both of those were mid-tier and bottom-tier moves. This one is different. This is OpenAI discounting the model at the very top of its own lineup, weeks after launching it. The flagship no longer commands flagship pricing, at least not for the next three months.

Now the skeptical turn. OpenAI's stated reason for the cut is a single line about improving efficiency. It does not cite competition from Google, or from Chinese open-weight labs, anywhere. Any competitive framing you hear elsewhere, including from this show, is analyst inference rather than an OpenAI statement. And the new pricing is guaranteed only through November 21st. No OpenAI source addresses what happens the day after. That makes this a customer-acquisition expense with a scheduled expiry, not a durable price cut. Notice also which token type got the deeper discount. Output, at thirty-three percent, against input at twenty. Output tokens are exactly what a long-running coding agent burns through fastest. So this is a targeted subsidy for agentic workloads, not a general goodwill gesture.

Think about where the repricing risk actually lands, because it is not on OpenAI's income statement. It lands on the wrapper and developer-tool companies in between. Their input cost is temporarily low, which invites them to price aggressively to win customers now. Then that cost resets upward on a date they do not control and cannot cleanly pass through. A coding-agent startup quoting flat monthly seats today, against four-dollar input tokens, has effectively written an option it does not own. November 22nd is the strike date. The promotion is OpenAI's marketing spend and somebody else's margin risk.

Google is running the identical play with a different expiry date. Gemini 3.7 Flash shipped on August 13th, tuned specifically for coding and agent work rather than as a general-purpose upgrade. It launched at seventy-five cents per million input tokens and three dollars fifty per million output tokens. That is roughly half the standard rate that takes effect on January 1st, 2027, according to VentureBeat's reporting on the launch. On Google's own AutomationBench, an enterprise workflow automation test, the new model scores about thirty percent against seventeen for its predecessor. That is a real delta. But start from seventeen, and even a near-doubling still leaves the model failing something like seven of every ten enterprise automation tasks. Treat the improvement as directionally real and the capability as still limited. These are also Google's own numbers, with no independent reproduction anywhere. What is reliable here is the pricing structure, not the score. Two labs, two workloads, two published expiry dates. Neither is lowering prices. Both are renting habits.

One short beat on where that flagship is being sold. OpenAI's GPT-5.6 family, Sol, Terra and Luna, is selectable inside Kiro, AWS's agentic coding environment. That is a tool built by a cloud competitor, not by OpenAI or Microsoft. Joint OpenAI and AWS testing claims an eighty-two percent reduction in the cost of a completed coding task on a benchmark called Terminal-Bench. Take that carefully. It is two interested parties measuring their own integration, with no independent baseline. The rollout timing is genuinely unclear as well. Kiro's own blog post is dated mid-July, while several outlets describe an August rollout six weeks later. So call it a standing arrangement rather than fresh news. The point survives either date. OpenAI is placing its flagship inside a rival's developer tool, treating model licensing as a distribution channel independent of ChatGPT and its own Codex product.

So if tokens are getting cheaper, and models are getting better at agent work, what is actually still blocking enterprise agent deployment? It is not price.

Anthropic has shipped its answer on the client side of Claude. Following July's move to a stateless version of the Model Context Protocol, the standard that lets agents talk to outside tools and data, Anthropic added three things that matter more than the protocol change itself. First, enterprise-managed authentication. An IT admin can now provision an agent's connectors organization-wide through an identity provider like Okta or Entra, the same way they govern any other piece of enterprise software. Second, MCP tunnels, still a research preview, which let Claude reach a server inside a private network without exposing it to the public internet. Third, MCP Apps, which let a connected server render interactive interface elements inside a Claude conversation instead of returning plain text. Anthropic also says MCP has passed four hundred million monthly SDK downloads, a fourfold increase this year, with over nine hundred and fifty servers listed in Claude's connector directory. The exact ship date of that announcement is not independently confirmed, so hold the timing loosely.

Notice what none of those three features are. None of them make the model smarter or the tokens cheaper. They clear procurement and security gates: who is allowed to provision a connector, and whether an agent can reach an internal system without putting it on the public internet. That is a larger expansion of what an agent can actually touch than any price cut this month, and it arrives with no adoption evidence attached to it.

Here is the callback that carries the argument. Three days ago, this show reported that the group stewarding the MCP standard admitted, in writing, that its authorization model still assumes a person approving access in a browser. No standardized agent identity, no version number, no ratification date. Anthropic has now shipped its own proprietary answer to that exact gap, ahead of the neutral standard. That is worth sitting with, because MCP was donated specifically to avoid single-vendor control. Every day the vendor-neutral identity fix stays a roadmap item, the shipped version accumulates the customers and the lock-in.

Keep the download figure in perspective, though. SDK downloads are the weakest adoption metric available. Continuous-integration pipelines and transitive dependencies inflate them, and a download is not a request. The nine-hundred-and-fifty-server directory measures supply, not how many of those servers carry production traffic. And Anthropic is the sole source for all of it.

One further consequence is worth naming. Once MCP servers can run serverless, and can be governed like any other piece of enterprise software, shipping one becomes cheaper than maintaining a bespoke API integration. That commoditizes the integration layer. Margin that sits today with connector vendors and systems integrators starts drifting toward whoever owns the client and the directory.

Put the two halves of today together. The labs are renting agentic workloads with discounts that expire on named dates, November 21st for OpenAI and January 1st for Google, while the same industry quietly builds the layer nobody discounts. Identity, authorization, private network access. Price is the promotion. Plumbing is the product. Watch what happens to agent economics when the discounts lapse, and watch whether the neutral standard closes its identity gap before more of that plumbing gets built proprietary.
