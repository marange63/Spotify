Good morning. It's Tuesday, July 28th, and this is your frontier AI labs briefing.

We flagged July 27 as the date to watch this week: the day Moonshot AI's full Kimi K3 weights would go public and finally let outsiders independently test the capability claims built up around it. The weights actually landed a day early, on July 26 at around 7:30 PM Eastern. That timing detail matters less than what it means structurally. Once open weights are on Hugging Face, they cannot be pulled back. Whatever leverage anyone hoped to apply to this release before it happened is gone. The only leverage left now runs through deployment, access, and export routes, not the model itself.

So the catalyst landed. But the thing it was supposed to enable, independent verification, still has not shown up, and that gap is the real story today.

Start with the model itself. Kimi K3 is a 2.8-trillion-parameter mixture-of-experts model, though only 16 of its 896 experts activate on any given token, and it carries a one-million-token context window. Here's the number that should reframe how you think about open weights generally: the full weight file runs to roughly 1.4 terabytes, which implies something on the order of eighteen top-tier 80-gigabyte accelerators just to load the model into memory, before you've run a single query. That is not a laptop exercise. That is a hyperscale-operator exercise.

This is the point the last few days of this feed have undersold. The running assumption has been that open weights commoditize inference, pushing frontier-adjacent capability toward cheap, dispersed compute. Kimi K3 complicates that. At this scale, self-hosting frontier-tier open weights is available only to operators who already have hyperscale accelerator fleets. So the release doesn't democratize compute demand, it concentrates it among the same handful of players who can already afford dozens of top-tier chips sitting idle to load one model. That's a data point in favor of sustained accelerator and high-bandwidth-memory demand, not against it. If the largest open model in history can only be self-hosted by the same firms already buying the most silicon, then open weights are not the pressure valve on the compute buildout that the commoditization story assumes. They are another reason the buildout keeps going.

Now, the license. New reporting fills in a gap that mattered: this is not simply free-for-all. Moonshot's published license requires any licensee whose aggregate revenue exceeds 20 million dollars over a rolling 12 months to sign a separate commercial agreement. And any product with more than 100 million monthly active users, or more than 20 million dollars in monthly revenue, has to prominently display the name "Kimi K3" on its interface. Internal use and traffic routed through Moonshot's own hosted API are exempt from those triggers, and that API itself is priced at 3 dollars per million input tokens and 15 dollars per million output tokens. So there's a real fork here: run it yourself at hyperscale cost and license complexity, or just pay Moonshot directly at a price still well under Western flagship rates. Either way, "open" and "free" are not the same word.

Sit with what that revenue threshold does. Almost any enterprise serious enough to want frontier-tier capability in production is already north of 20 million dollars in revenue. So the exact customers with the money and the chips to self-host are the same customers the license pushes back toward a direct commercial deal with Moonshot. The open drop is real, and it hands researchers and smaller players something genuinely valuable to study and fine-tune. But at the scale where this model actually earns money, the release still routes the commercial relationship back to Moonshot, either through a signed agreement or through that hosted API. The weights are public. The revenue path is not fully open, and it was engineered that way.

Now the caveat that should anchor everything else about K3. Before the weights went public, third-party leaderboards had K3 ranked second on Vals AI and third on Artificial Analysis's Intelligence Index, and Interconnects' Nathan Lambert used that to argue the open-to-closed capability gap has compressed from six-to-nine months down to something like three-to-five. All of that is pre-release leaderboard data. The entire point of shipping the weights publicly was to let independent labs test the model themselves. As of this recording, no evaluation run against the actual July 26 released weights has surfaced.

The closest thing we do have cuts the other way. A joint UK AI Safety Institute and US CAISI assessment, published July 23, three days before the full weight drop, tested Kimi K3 specifically on cyber capability, using API access from its earlier July 16 unveiling rather than the released weights. It found K3 performs significantly below the most recent frontier cyber-capable US models: roughly 32 percent against about a 59 percent average on one exploit benchmark, and it stalled at step 17 of 32 on a cyber-range simulation versus an average of 28.5 for frontier systems. It did beat the prior leading open-weight model, so it's still progress within the open camp. But the same assessment flagged that K3's safeguards allow assistance with agentic cyber exploit development, which is exactly the kind of concrete, government-sourced finding that feeds directly into the ongoing US export-control and distillation dispute, independent of whether general capability parity holds up. So the honest read is this: the one independent, government-grade check available doesn't confirm the leaderboard story, it complicates it, and it doesn't even test the model that's actually public now.

That government finding is also why the timing of the release matters for policy, not just for benchmarks. Once the weights are permanent and global, there is nothing left to embargo at the model level. A finding that K3's safeguards will help with exploit development does not give Washington a lever over the weights, because the weights are already everywhere. What it can shape is the layer around them: which cloud providers host it, which chips are allowed to run it, and which distribution and transshipment routes stay open. So expect the US response to move off the model itself and onto deployment, access, and compute access, because that is the only surface left to act on. The distillation dispute earlier this week is now technically checkable against a public model, and still unchecked.

Briefly, on the competitive dimension inside China itself. Hours after DeepSeek's V4 general-availability rollout last week, Alibaba's Qwen team previewed Qwen 3.8-Max, reportedly a 2.4-trillion-parameter model, which would make it larger than K3 if it ships at full weight. That's preview-only, single-sourced, and unverified, but it shows how compressed the release cadence has become among Chinese labs, each dropping frontier-scale announcements within hours of a rival's launch. The title of largest open-weight model in history, which K3 just claimed, is already being contested from inside its own ecosystem, before anyone has even finished testing the incumbent.

Pulling this together: the compute story cuts against the popular commoditization narrative, the license story means self-hosting still has real cost and friction attached, and the capability story is still an open question the industry hasn't actually answered yet, despite having the weights in hand to do it. Once public, the model is permanent, so the fight from here shifts to who can verify it, who can safely deploy it, and who controls the routes it travels through, rather than whether it should have been released at all.
