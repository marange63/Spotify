Good morning, it's Friday, August 14th, and this is your Capital Markets Cross-Asset Radar.

We've spent two days refusing to call the Fed's live hike-versus-hold debate, waiting on the data to settle it. Yesterday it settled. July's Producer Price Index for final demand came in flat month over month, against a consensus call for a 0.2% rebound, and the annual rate slowed to 4.7% from 5.5% in June. Underneath, services prices rose 0.2% while goods prices fell 0.7%, so the disinflation is broad, not a single volatile component. That's the second soft inflation print in two days, after Wednesday's cool CPI, and it's a real trend, not a one-off.

Two independently reported trackers of CME's FedWatch tool both show September hike odds compressing hard through the week, though they don't land on quite the same number. One has hike odds falling from 55% before CPI, to 42% after CPI, to 32% this morning after PPI. Another has the September split at 63% hold versus 37% hike. Different snapshots, different moments, but the same direction: hike odds have gone from roughly a coin flip earlier this week to somewhere in the low-to-mid thirties now, depending on which tracker you're reading. Call it compressed to about a third, not eliminated.

Equities took that as unambiguous good news. The S&P 500 closed at a record 7,798.99, up 0.65% on the day, after touching an intraday all-time high of 7,816.70 — the index's first-ever close above 7,800. The Nasdaq added 0.81% to 26,803.03, and the Dow rose 0.13% to 53,839.99. The 10-year Treasury yield settled at 4.639%, easing on the soft print. And gold, which has been running on this same fading-hike-odds bid since earlier in the week, held above $4,400 an ounce for a fourth straight session — a continuation of the trade, not a new catalyst, but confirmation it survived contact with the actual data.

Here's the caveat worth sitting with. A hike tail in the low thirties is thinner than it was Monday, but it isn't zero, and three FOMC members dissented hawkish at the July meeting. High-yield credit spreads, meanwhile, sat at 2.71%, or 271 basis points, as of Wednesday, just before PPI — still tight, still complacent, unmoved by any of this. Investment-grade issuers spent the past week front-running that tight window with heavy supply, and nothing in the last two prints has given credit a reason to reprice wider. So you've got a record, low-volatility equity tape pricing near-certain relief sitting on top of a rates and credit complex that hasn't fully repriced away the hike risk. The relief is real. It's just being read more aggressively in stocks than anywhere else in the capital structure.

That gap between the calm surface and what's still priced underneath shows up even more clearly in currencies. The yen is testing the same intervention line that Tokyo and Washington defended three weeks ago. USD/JPY traded near 159.45 to 159.53 on Thursday, which means the pair has now retraced roughly 48% of the decline the record coordinated intervention drove in late July — essentially half the move undone within eight trading sessions. The mechanics haven't changed: the Fed's policy rate sits at a midpoint of 3.625%, the Bank of Japan's at 1%, a gap of 262 and a half basis points, and that spread is exactly what funds the carry trade that keeps pushing the yen back toward 160. Market practitioners treat 160 as the trigger line for a fresh round of intervention. So this is a live test of whether late July's move actually broke the trade's economics or just reset its entry point. If the latter, the calm equity tape is sitting on a currency fuse that could force a violent, policy-driven unwind of carry positions funding leveraged bets well beyond Japan — that's the kind of event the current low-VIX pricing isn't built to absorb.

Now, the other input to yesterday's disinflation story: oil, which moved in the same direction but for a more troubling reason. Brent crude fell about 2% to $87.17 a barrel and WTI fell 2.2% to $81.41, ending a six-session rally, even as the U.S.-Iran conflict and the Strait of Hormuz risk remain unresolved. That's the tell — this wasn't a supply story easing, it was a demand story weakening. OPEC cut its 2026 global demand growth forecast to 580,000 barrels a day, its fourth consecutive downward revision, and the IEA trimmed its own demand outlook this week too. Lower energy costs feed straight into the same disinflation trade lifting equities and pressuring hike odds, so in that narrow sense it's supportive. But a fourth straight demand downgrade sitting alongside an active supply shock is a more cautious signal about global growth than the market's risk-on tape is currently reflecting. For the cross-asset read, the same lower-energy tailwind helping large-cap equities is a warning flag for growth-sensitive cyclicals and commodity-exporting emerging markets. Good for the Fed trade, less good for the soft-landing thesis if it keeps compounding.

Shifting to market structure and digital finance, where today brings a genuinely new piece of plumbing. The SEC has scheduled an open meeting today to vote on proposing what it's calling Regulation Crypto — the first formal crypto rulemaking of the Atkins era. The proposal would create a tailored offering regime letting qualifying crypto projects raise capital through token sales without triggering full securities registration, and a related sandbox initiative would explore 24/7 on-chain trading of tokenized securities. I want to be precise here: this vote hadn't happened as of this recording, so treat the outcome as unknown — don't assume passage. What's confirmed is the setup itself: the SEC moving via formal rulemaking rather than waiting on a stalled Congress, and historically that kind of rulemaking takes twelve to eighteen months from proposal to final rule even if today's vote opens it. So even a successful vote today is the start of a long process, not new market structure by Monday.

Closing out the digital-asset beat, Strategy — the company formerly known as MicroStrategy — keeps selling Bitcoin to fund its own capital structure. It disclosed selling 1,690 BTC for $108.6 million in the first full week of August, at an average price of $64,262 a coin, bringing year-to-date sales to 6,948 BTC. The proceeds went partly to fund $52.4 million in dividends on its STRC preferred stock and to repurchase $52.3 million of that same preferred. This is worth flagging alongside this week's rotation toward Ether over Bitcoin in ETF flows: the market's largest corporate Bitcoin holder, built on a permanent-accumulator identity, is now a recurring seller servicing its own funding structure — a supply overhang that exists independent of whatever the ETF flow data says about institutional demand.

So pull it together. Two soft inflation prints have genuinely thinned the Fed's hike tail and pushed the S&P to a record above 7,800, but that relief is priced very unevenly — equities are all in, rates and credit are more measured, and the yen carry trade is rebuilding right at the line that forced intervention three weeks ago. Watch that 160 level, and watch what the SEC actually does with Regulation Crypto once the vote is in.

That's the Capital Markets Cross-Asset Radar for August 14th. Have a good weekend.
