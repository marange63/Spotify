Good morning. It's Sunday, September 6th, 2026, and this is your private credit briefing.

Here's the frame for this morning. This asset class publishes a default rate every quarter, like clockwork. It almost never publishes a recovery rate. This week, on one credit, it did.

The credit is First Brands, the auto-parts platform whose collapse a year ago exposed invoice-factoring fraud across multiple shadow lenders. On Friday we told you a bankruptcy judge had converted the case to Chapter 7 liquidation, rejecting a reorganization plan built on litigation recoveries. We argued then that the failure was about collateral value, not about where anyone sat in the payment waterfall. Yesterday we said the marks across BDC loan books were finally moving in the right direction, but that direction alone doesn't tell you the level. Now we have a level. First Brands' first-lien debt, senior secured and first in line to get paid, is being quoted at around 16 cents on the dollar, according to broker runs.

Sit with that qualifier for a second, because it matters. Sixteen cents is a broker quote on a still-liquidating estate, not a final, realized recovery. It's the market's probability-weighted guess at what the Chapter 7 trustee eventually claws back through litigation. Litigation recoveries are about the most dispersed input in all of bankruptcy, so this could move either direction from here. It's also worth remembering how this case started. Invoice double-pledging: collateral pledged more than once, to more than one lender. That is a collateral-verification failure specific to asset-based lending. It is not an indictment of sponsor-backed cash-flow direct lending generally. So First Brands bounds the downside for one type of structure. It doesn't reprice the whole asset class.

But even with those caveats, 16 cents is not where a senior secured claim is supposed to land. And this isn't the first data point on this credit. It's the second leg down. One BDC holder's own disclosures show this same first-lien paper carried at 94.3 percent of principal in June of last year, before the fraud surfaced. By the end of September 2025, just after the original Chapter 11 filing, that same lender had already marked it to 35.2 percent of principal. So the market applied a severe discount the moment the fraud came out, a full year before this week's liquidation-era quote. What's happened since is roughly another nineteen points of value going out the door. Read that as the final, adjudicated confirmation of a discount already applied provisionally, not as a fresh shock out of nowhere.

The arithmetic behind the number is visible now, too. The debtor-in-possession facility that private-credit and bank lenders extended to fund the case ran roughly one billion to one point one billion dollars. By the time of conversion, one legal analysis describes that new money as gone. Against that, itemized private sales of the company's business lines — Walbro, the Toledo molding operation, Horizon, and two smaller units — totaled roughly 214 million dollars, plus one more sale at an undisclosed price. And at least 222 million dollars of costs the bankruptcy case itself ran up were still unpaid at conversion, stacked ahead of any general recovery. Run that math and 16 cents on senior secured paper stops looking strange. Collateral realization, not priority mechanics, is the binding constraint here. Being first in line only matters if there's enough at the front of the line to matter.

That's the reason this number travels beyond one company. In cash-flow lending, recovery is a function of enterprise value, and enterprise value at least gets tested by a sale process for the whole business. In receivables and inventory finance — the structure private credit has been growing fastest into — recovery is a function of whether the collateral exists and was pledged exactly once. First Brands failed that second, more basic test. Private-credit and insurance marks on similar asset-based structures elsewhere have never been tested by an actual liquidation. So a discount that has never been applied could be sitting in those books. Name who holds it: BDC shareholders, and the insurance balance sheets funding annuity liabilities with this paper. What a senior claim is worth on paper and what it's worth when it's actually tested are two different numbers, and this week we only learned the second one, for a single name.

Now, that's the credit that got tested. What about the ones that haven't been?

Here the picture is more mixed than a single headline suggests, and it cuts against something we argued yesterday. We'd said the deterioration looked concentrated in one vertical — software credits getting marked down — rather than a genuine cycle turn. The breadth data from the second quarter pushes back on that. The number of distinct borrowers with at least one non-accrual tranche at the top ten BDCs rose by eleven, to 101, over the quarter. That's a widening headcount, not a deepening of the same few names. Blue Owl's own default rate hit 2.8 percent in the second quarter, its highest level in at least five years. And Fitch Ratings puts the broader U.S. private-credit default rate at roughly 6 percent as of the end of May. Treat that last one as a separate measurement entirely. It is a rating agency's estimate, not a BDC's own non-accrual share of cost. So don't blend that 6 percent with the 3.95 percent non-accrual figure from the top-ten BDCs. The gap between them is definitional, not informational. And worth saying once: all of this is June 30th data being read in September, so it's already a couple of months stale.

Our concentration thesis survives on the markdown data — the deepest cuts are still clustering where they were. But it is under real pressure on the breadth data. A rising borrower count looks more like vintage seasoning working through 2021 and 2022-era loans on schedule than a single sector's problem. Breadth is the variable that would actually break our read, and breadth just moved.

So if the count of troubled borrowers is rising but the reported losses mostly aren't, ask where those borrowers are going instead of default. Mostly, they're going into amend-and-extend. And here the data is unforgiving. A study out of Oxford this year looked at 89 recent liability management exercises — the negotiated maturity pushes and priming transactions that let a stressed borrower avoid a formal restructuring. More than 80 percent of them defaulted again within 36 months, many ending up in formal bankruptcy anyway. Handle that carefully. It is a small sample, and it reaches us secondhand through a legal-academic summary rather than the underlying study. It is also loaded with selection bias in the other direction. Companies that need a liability management exercise are already the worst credits in the pool, so a high subsequent failure rate is partly about who gets selected into the sample. And the 36-month window means much of that sample predates the current rate environment. Read it as evidence that these exercises postpone rather than cure, not as proof they cause the eventual default.

But postponement has a consequence for the headline number everyone quotes. A stressed private-credit borrower has two exits. One is liquidation, where First Brands now prices the outcome at 16 cents. The other is amend-and-extend, where more than four in five borrowers apparently end up defaulting again anyway. The industry overwhelmingly chooses the second path. That means the benign-looking default rate this asset class advertises is largely a statement about which workout technique lenders prefer, not a statement about borrower health. And because liquidations produce an observable market price while amend-and-extend does not, the more the industry leans on extension, the fewer real price observations exist to discipline anyone's marks. A low default rate mechanically produces less price discovery, not more comfort.

So here's where the cycle actually stands. The payment layer — who's current, who's not — is deteriorating slowly and unevenly. The recovery layer, the thing this asset class is actually underwritten and marketed on, has essentially one hard observation this year, and it was bad. The biggest unresolved risk is asset-based and collateral-marked private lending that has simply never been tested by a real liquidation. Watch two things next. First, whatever the First Brands trustee actually recovers through litigation. Second, the third-quarter BDC non-accrual and payment-in-kind prints landing in November, which will tell us whether that borrower count keeps widening.

That's the briefing. Talk to you next time.
