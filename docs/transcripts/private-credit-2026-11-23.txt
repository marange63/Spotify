Good morning, it's Monday, November 23rd, 2026, and this is your private credit briefing.

Two things changed in the last few days. Credit started pricing AI compute risk by who actually stands behind it. And the core direct-lending book just showed a default turn.

Start with the price. According to reporting relayed by Twenty-Four Seven Wall Street, Apollo and Goldman Sachs priced about forty-four billion dollars of senior and subordinated notes on November 20th. The money buys Nvidia chips for SpaceX. The pricing detail traces to a person familiar with the sale, not to an issuer filing. Even so, it's the clearest public credit price yet on AI compute risk, since ING's data-center loan auction still hasn't reported. The notes back a roughly forty billion dollar master finance lease. SpaceX made a binding commitment to that lease in October, with an entity managed by Apollo. Per Apollo's own release, lease schedules start only when the GPUs are delivered, in the first half of 2027.

Now read the curve those notes priced on, because two gaps carry the argument. Giving up seniority earned only about ten basis points of extra yield. Two extra years of tenor cost about sixty. Here are the levels behind those gaps. The senior secured notes due 2033 priced at about eight and a quarter percent. The subordinated Class B notes, also due 2033, paid only that sliver more. The shorter notes, due 2031, priced at about seven and two-thirds percent. So two extra years of collateral life cost far more than a full step down the capital structure.

That puts the real question to this deal: who actually stands behind the risk? SpaceX is a strong borrower, but it isn't a hyperscaler. The desk's read is that this market isn't pricing your place in the stack. It's pricing how long the GPUs stay valuable, and whether SpaceX keeps paying. There are two live explanations for the thin junior premium. One is that the risk is binary. If SpaceX or the collateral fails, senior and junior holders get hurt together. That leaves little reason to charge much more for junior paper. The other is that heavy demand for yield squeezed the junior premium regardless of risk. The first reading says seniority here protects less than its label implies. The second says the junior buyers are being underpaid.

We can't yet call eight-plus percent wide or cheap. Ratings and Treasury spreads are circulating for this deal, but none is confirmed in anything we can verify. Nor has the credit support been disclosed. We can't confirm a SpaceX parent guarantee, or any Nvidia backing on the chips. And nobody has said who carries the coupon between pricing and delivery in 2027. That gap matters, because lenders are funding well ahead of the cash flows meant to repay them. If SpaceX's lease obligation is unconditional, this is a price on SpaceX credit. If noteholders ultimately lean on the GPUs, it's a price on obsolescence, and that's a very different risk.

One more gap. The three reported tranches add up to roughly thirty-two billion dollars, against a headline of about forty-four. The rest of the package hasn't been broken out. Reported totals don't even agree from one outlet to the next. So don't treat any blended yield on the whole deal as known. The anchor investors, at least, are confirmed. BlackRock, Goldman Sachs Asset Management, and accounts advised by KKR anchored the deal. This is a real price for SpaceX-grade, GPU-collateralized debt. It is not yet proof that AI credit broadly clears at that level.

And the head of the firm that led this deal says many AI borrowers couldn't get near it. Apollo's chief executive, Marc Rowan, spoke on CNBC last week. He described Nvidia as performing a banking function, a guarantor function, for the neoclouds. If that function gets taken away, he said, access to capital for a large number of neoclouds is going to disappear. Fed Governor Michael Barr made the mirror-image point last week. He said guarantees in these financings show how far hyperscalers and GPU producers might absorb losses in the event of defaults. Rowan supplies the other side of that idea. The guarantee that protects lenders in a loss is also a single point of failure for funding. Pull it, and the lending stops before any loss arrives.

Weigh Rowan's words as those of an interested party. Apollo just showed it can term out strong-obligor AI risk at over eight percent. A story in which weaker borrowers lean on Nvidia flatters Apollo's own lending model. Still, the distinction he draws is the right one. SpaceX brings its own credit standing and its own collateral. Many neoclouds are effectively borrowing Nvidia's credibility. So AI credit is splitting between borrowers who stand on their own and borrowers standing on someone else's guarantee. If that guarantee weakens, this desk infers, the second group's marginal lender becomes costlier structured private credit, or nobody.

Now step away from the AI frontier. The ordinary direct-lending book just broke a line this desk has held since last Wednesday, that realized defaults hadn't turned. Houlihan Lokey's third-quarter Private Credit DataBank is the break. On Houlihan's default measure, size-weighted defaults rose to one point five percent of outstanding loan principal. In the second quarter it was zero point eight percent, so it nearly doubled. That's Houlihan's own tracked universe, not a census. Houlihan's framing is that the stress is not evenly spread. Borrowers under one hundred million dollars of EBITDA saw their size-weighted rate rise to three point nine percent, from three percent.

Here's where this desk pushes back on that framing, and the arithmetic is simple. Start with the second quarter. The whole book defaulted at zero point eight percent, while small borrowers ran at three percent. So even if no large borrower had defaulted, small borrowers could be at most about a quarter of the book. A quarter of the book rising nine-tenths of a point adds only about a quarter point to the total. But the total rose seven-tenths of a point. So larger borrowers must account for most of the rise. On this math, their own default rate climbed by at least six-tenths of a point. That's this desk's arithmetic, not Houlihan's. It assumes the mix of large and small borrowers held roughly steady between quarters. A shift in principal toward small borrowers could explain part of the move. Even so, the bifurcation story that comforts allocators, that it's only the small borrowers, looks narrower than advertised.

The other half of this story is how defaults show up, not just how many. Fitch's October private credit default rate, out last week, eased to six point two percent. Fitch's gauge runs far higher than Houlihan's because it uses a different universe and counts interest deferrals and payment-in-kind as defaults. And on the headline, it moved the other way. The composition is less comforting. Deferrals and PIK in lieu of cash made up forty-nine percent of the default events in Fitch's count. So close to half of what Fitch calls a default is a borrower deferring interest or paying it in more debt. That isn't a resolved loss. At the smallest borrowers, the two gauges disagree too. Fitch's issuers under twenty-five million dollars of EBITDA eased slightly, to ten point seven percent. Houlihan's small-borrower bucket rose. They use different size bands, definitions, and time windows.

Here's the desk's inference across both. When roughly half of booked defaults are deferrals and PIK, income keeps accruing without cash arriving. The recovery assumptions inside private marks haven't met a cash outcome. Marks can look stable simply because nothing has forced the test.

Pull the threads together. The credit cycle isn't turning through a wave of headline bankruptcies. It's turning quietly, inside private books that don't mark to a daily tape. SpaceX's GPU notes show that AI term debt now has a real price, but only for borrowers with standing of their own. Many others still run on Nvidia's implicit guarantee. Underneath that, the ordinary direct-lending book just posted a clear default acceleration on Houlihan's measure, even as Fitch's headline eased. On Fitch's count, much of the stress is arriving as accommodation rather than resolution. The biggest unresolved risk is that AI-linked and middle-market books alike are being carried on guarantees and PIK income that haven't faced a cash test. Watch three things. First, whether the SpaceX lease moves from binding commitment to a closed deal, and whether the GPUs arrive on schedule in 2027. Second, Fitch's November print, and whether the PIK and deferral share moves past half. Third, fourth-quarter BDC marks. If this desk's arithmetic is right, stress should start showing in upper-middle-market books, not just the small-borrower ones.

That's the lending machine for November 23rd. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
