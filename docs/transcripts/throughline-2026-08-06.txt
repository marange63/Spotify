Good morning, this is The Throughline for Thursday, August 6th, 2026. The single most important development across today's briefings: Demis Hassabis stepped down as CEO of Google DeepMind, and the market didn't read it as a promotion — Alphabet shares fell roughly 5 percent, the second similarly sized AI-talent selloff in six weeks, and it caps a run of departures and missed deadlines that now reaches the top of the org chart.

Start with the macro backdrop. Strategic Power has spent four straight days tracking the gap between announced authority and delivered capacity, and today that shows up in trade law itself: the Senate voted 86 to 12 to advance a bill authorizing, not requiring, tariffs up to 200 percent on Russia's oil buyers, while Taiwan is prosecuting a real chip-smuggling ring with a law that doesn't yet exist to charge it directly. The one verified physical fact in the mix is Aalo Atomics' test reactor achieving criticality on July 4th, a genuine engineering milestone still five times smaller than the commercial unit it's meant to justify.

That same authority-versus-delivery pattern runs straight into Capital Markets Radar, which spends today debunking its own working theory. The reflexive read after Meta's rough earnings was that Microsoft and Amazon got rewarded for free-cash-flow discipline Meta lacked — but Microsoft's free cash flow actually fell 23 percent year over year and Amazon's flipped negative for the first time in recent history. The market is discriminating on cloud revenue and backlog, not the metric everyone assumed, so don't extend that framing into the next earnings cycle. Copper hit a fresh record on tariff-driven hoarding rather than real demand, and credit spreads sit near their tightest decile of history with almost no cushion if Friday's jobs report disappoints.

Digital Money picks up the plumbing underneath all of it: Morgan Stanley downgraded Circle on a structural bet that tokenized money-market funds will strip stablecoin issuers of their reserve yield, and BlackRock launched exactly the products built to capture it — a repricing of who owns the float, not a shrinking of it, since the underlying Treasury demand just changes hands to a more conventional owner.

Private Credit and the Corporate Credit Cycle has that same discrimination showing up in loan pricing: CoreWeave's GPU-financing loan cleared 125 basis points wide of initial talk, and Proofpoint's refinancing got twenty-some new protective covenants over fears AI could erode its software moat — while an investment-grade data-center loan from Blackstone's QTS sailed through untouched. Lenders are pricing AI risk selectively, not uniformly.

Private Equity and the Deal Economy closes the loop on Q2 earnings season: Carlyle's record distributable earnings turn out to be fee-driven, with realized carry of just 115 million dollars, confirming KKR's monetization quarter was the outlier, not the start of a broad thaw. Every major manager hit a fee record this quarter; none broke out a comparably large realized-carry number to match.

Now the AI and compute stack, where the announcement-versus-delivery test runs from silicon to the boardroom. Semiconductors and Compute Infrastructure has Apple's reported talks to qualify CXMT as a fourth DRAM supplier collapsing on price — the Chinese entrant wouldn't undercut Samsung or SK hynix — which argues the feared memory price floor doesn't drop this year and incumbent pricing power stays durable into 2027.

Frontier AI Lab Competition carries today's headline: Hassabis becomes Chair of DeepMind and Alphabet's Chief Scientist, with Koray Kavukcuoglu running Gemini day to day but reporting to Sundar Pichai — no CEO title anywhere in the announcement. It caps a stretch of Gemini 3.5 Pro missing its third straight deadline and researchers like Noam Shazeer and John Jumper leaving for OpenAI and Anthropic, the very labs Google competes with. Google's answer is a leapfrog bet: skip the stuck model, pretrain Gemini 4 instead.

AI Products and Applications reframes the same authority question in legal terms: a Ninth Circuit ruling let Perplexity's Comet agent keep operating on Amazon, finding the user, not the agent vendor, is the one accessing the site — a precedential win for agent builders, while a Rust project policy this week drew the opposite line, banning AI-authored code from its core repository outright. Enterprise Technology shows the market rewarding delivered results over unproven claims in real time: software stocks whipsawed more than ten percent both directions inside a week, with Palantir's earnings beat resetting sentiment across the whole cohort while the underlying substitution question stays unresolved.

Finally, the applied frontier, where the same proof-versus-promise test plays out in hardware. Robotics and Physical AI has Symbotic posting a genuine swing to GAAP profit on warehouse automation, while Unitree's Shanghai listing discloses the sector's first real humanoid unit economics — profitable, at scale — and Tesla's Optimus sits on the other end, its own CEO calling the production ramp impossible to predict.

Space and the Orbital Economy has the same gap at orbital scale: the Space Force just committed a $397 million national-security payload to Rocket Lab's Neutron rocket, which has never flown, and Amazon's Leo constellation forfeited spectrum priority after missing its FCC deployment deadline by more than a thousand satellites.

AI in Biopharma R&D closes with Pathos AI's two oncology deals, where the AI's real contribution is deal selection, not molecule design — and the widely repeated $2.3 billion headline rests almost entirely on one disclosed $125 million upfront, the second deal's terms undisclosed.

Here's the thread worth naming, because it's now run four straight days and today it reached the boardroom. From tariff authority Congress hasn't shown it will use, to a free-cash-flow story that didn't survive its own numbers, to a rocket with zero flight heritage carrying a defense payload, to a Nobel laureate's exit dressed as a promotion — the market and this show are both still asking the same question every single day: what's actually been delivered, versus what's merely been announced. Today, for the first time, that question cost a CEO his title.
