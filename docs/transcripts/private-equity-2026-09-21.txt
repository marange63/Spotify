Good morning, and welcome to the private equity briefing for Monday, September 21st, 2026.

The lead is a contest for GFL Environmental, and the structure matters more than the size. Bloomberg reported on September 16th that two consortia are lining up to take the waste company private. KKR, Blackstone, and Energy Capital Partners have teamed up on one side. Brookfield and IFM Investors are on the other, though one report describes them as still considering a rival group rather than having filed a formal bid. A decision could come in the coming weeks, and the chief executive, Patrick Dovigi, is willing to roll all of his stake into any deal.

Bound the sourcing before going further, because it is thin. This is Bloomberg reporting from people familiar. Nothing has been announced, and no consortium has disclosed an offer price, a premium, or a financing package. The interest itself is not new either. GFL said in its July 29th release that it had received, in its words, unsolicited preliminary expressions of interest from multiple parties, and it formed a special committee of independent directors. The company framed that interest as a valuation disconnect, which is management's framing and not neutral evidence. What Bloomberg added this month is the cast list.

On scale, Bloomberg's figures are a market value of about 18 billion dollars plus 10 billion dollars of debt. Adding those gets you to roughly 28 billion dollars. That sum is my arithmetic, not a reported enterprise value, and both inputs are approximate. It would still make this one of the year's largest leveraged buyouts.

Now the arithmetic on price. GFL guided in July to adjusted EBITDA of about 2.29 billion dollars for this year. Against that, the 28 billion sum works out to roughly twelve times. Treat that as a floor, not a print. The market value already reflects months of bid speculation and carries no takeover premium. So any winning offer lands above twelve times, and I would not set that number beside last week's entry multiples as a like-for-like comparison.

Here is the analytical point. A three-sponsor club is a capacity statement, not a confidence statement. The reporting itself notes that pairing up spreads the cost and risk, and can help firms line up enough debt financing to get the deal done. When three of the largest names in the industry share one cheque, the binding constraint is the size of the equity and the depth of the debt market, not appetite. The chief executive's rollover helps here, because it shrinks the cash equity the bidders must write. The real cheque size will only show once terms emerge.

The so-what is that this is what the top of the market looks like when no single fund can underwrite an asset alone. It also carries a second-order cost. If a deal signs at that scale, it tests leveraged loan and high-yield capacity for jumbo sponsor paper. And the eventual exit for an asset that size is an initial public offering or another consortium.

That brings up who is actually bidding. Brookfield and IFM are infrastructure capital, and Energy Capital Partners is energy-infrastructure capital. So for the year's largest buyout contest, the marginal buyer is again not a classic buyout fund. Last week's Baldwin deal made the same point, with a permanent-capital buyer setting the price at roughly twenty times earnings. When the marginal bidder has no fund clock, a drawdown fund either loses the auction or wins at a price its own hold period struggles to support. And winning at that price thins the cushion further, because the return then has to come from operational change alone, with neither leverage nor multiple expansion left to do the work.

The club structure also revives an old argument. Limited partners pushed back on consortium deals after 2007 over concentration, because several of their funds ended up owning the same asset. That concern lands badly now. Bain's midyear report says distributions to institutional investors ran at just 13.4 percent of net asset value in 2025, against an average of 25 percent between 2010 and 2021. Bain also says one in five limited partners is reducing its strategic allocation to buyout funds. Cross-fund concentration is hardest to absorb when cash back is this thin.

The same pattern shows up at a smaller scale on the entry side. The National Basketball Association amended its private equity policy on September 18th to let a firm and its executives invest in the same franchise at the same time. That clears the way for Joshua Kushner and Bob Iger to buy the Lakers from Mark Walter at a record 12.5 billion dollar valuation. Two cautions. The deal was struck last month, so the rule change removes an obstacle rather than creating a transaction. And Thrive Eternal is not a conventional private equity fund, so this says little about fund economics. What it does show is a gatekeeper widening the bidder set, and a record price on a scarce asset. Price discovery at entry is abundant and rising. At exit, it is scarce.

Now the other side of the ledger, and it involves one of the same names. On September 17th, Bloomberg reported that Blackstone is facilitating a formal secondary sale for investors in one of its Blackstone Property Partners funds, a United States vehicle with 11 billion dollars in net asset value. Blackstone is taking, in the report's words, a more hands-on role than usual. That 11 billion is the fund actually transacting. It sits inside a 57.7 billion dollar Property Partners strategy made up of several perpetual funds, and the larger number is not what is changing hands.

The new fact is a fee cut. Property Partners cut management fees by 30 percent for investors who kept redemption requests below a certain threshold, according to documents from a California pension. That is a gate by incentive rather than a gate by contract. Open-ended funds like this typically process withdrawals quarterly with no hard cap. Blackstone is paying investors to stay in the queue instead of leaving it.

Two caveats. This is core-plus real estate, not buyout, so the read-across is to semi-liquid evergreen structures generally, not to private equity marks. And everything traces to one Bloomberg scoop, re-reported by aggregators. We have no secondary pricing, no discount to net asset value, no buyer names, and no redemption volume. The absence of a disclosed discount is not evidence that there isn't one.

Here is why the fee rebate matters for manager economics. Fee-related earnings can be spent to suppress redemptions. Carry cannot be manufactured. A manager that cuts its own fee to slow the queue is telling you redemption demand exceeds what the portfolio can fund through sales, and it is paying for that with the earnings line that supports listed manager valuations. If evergreen private equity vehicles adopt the same tool, reported fee stability partly reflects how much redemption pressure managers are paying to hold back. Blackstone's flagship wealth vehicle, BXPE, raised 2.4 billion dollars in the second quarter and has net asset value above 25 billion dollars. That is the franchise that would need the tool next.

Put the two halves together. In one week, the same firm appears in a consortium bidding at the top of the market and is paying investors to hold off at the door of a perpetual fund. That is one manager on both sides of the liquidity trade, and it is the wedge between manager economics and what limited partners actually receive.

So where does the cycle stand? Entry is being repriced upward, and at escalating scale. Exit is still being engineered by the same managers. The cost of today's full prices does not show up in cash returned now, against a distribution rate that on the latest full-year read was 13.4 percent of net asset value. It shows up in the distributions-to-paid-in of 2026-vintage funds, five to seven years from now. The bill is deferred, not resolved. And distributions measured as a share of net asset value carry the manager's own marks in the denominator, so even that number flatters.

The counter-argument is that timing, not structure, is the problem. TPG said in August that the volatile macro backdrop had temporarily impacted the timing of realizations across the industry, and that it expects the cadence to accelerate towards the end of this year and into 2027. That may prove right. But it is a manager's forecast, and it is the claim the coming prints have to test.

Three catalysts would confirm or refute the read. First, whether GFL's special committee signs a deal, and at what premium, since that sets the entry marker. Second, whether the Blackstone secondary prints near or below net asset value, because that is a free market check on the marks. Third, the third-quarter manager earnings, due from late October into early November, which are the next hard read on realization pace. Until an exit of comparable size prices, the biggest unresolved risk is the gap between loud price discovery on the way in and silence on the way out.

That is the briefing for today. Thanks for listening, and see you tomorrow.
