Good morning, it's September 15th, 2026, and this is your briefing on artificial intelligence across biopharma R&D.

All week this beat has made the same argument without a single number attached to it. The argument is that AI's measured gains in drug discovery sit upstream, in the cheap part of the funnel, and that nobody outside the companies themselves has ever checked whether the clinic is actually going better. Today, for the first time, a cross-company tally arrives. It is more favorable to the AI-native cohort than this beat has been. It is also far thinner than its own headline suggests, and that thinness is the story.

Start with the base rates, as reported by BioSpace citing a PitchBook analysis. AI-native biotechs are clearing Phase I at eighty to ninety percent. The industry average sits between forty and sixty-five percent. That is a real gap, and a large one. It is also exactly the shape you would expect if generative design is good at producing molecules with clean pharmacokinetics and manageable toxicity. That is the kind of thing a model can learn from structure and chemistry data alone, without ever touching human disease biology.

Then look at Phase II, where the same analysis puts the AI cohort near forty percent against an industry average of twenty-nine. Still ahead, on paper. But Phase II is the first real test of whether the drug does anything in a patient. Picking the right target for a given disease is a biology problem, not a molecule-design problem. So the shape of this data is coherent. AI is very good at making molecules that behave. It is not obviously better than anyone else at picking targets that matter in humans.

Now the number that has to come before the percentage, which is the denominator. BioSpace's own reporting states that AI-focused biotechs have completed just ten clinical trials so far. Ten. A forty-versus-twenty-nine-percent Phase II delta computed on a base that small is not a measurement. It is an anecdote with a percent sign on it.

There is a second, more precise-sounding tally circulating in the coverage, counting assets and completed Phase II programs across dozens of companies. We went looking for its primary source and could not find one, so it is not in this briefing. That is worth saying out loud, because in this field the more specific-sounding number is often the less well-supported one. There is also a selection problem that none of these tallies adjusts for. AI-native biotechs deliberately pick well-precedented targets and well-behaved chemistry for their first programs, specifically to de-risk them. That alone would inflate the Phase I pass rate with zero contribution from any model.

That thinness matters more once you look at where the money goes. The ProMarket analysis of this same data puts the cost of bringing one drug to market at roughly two point six billion dollars, and notes that the figure is dominated by clinical testing and the failures inside it, not by discovery. Against that backdrop, PitchBook is projecting that AI could roughly double the end-to-end odds of a drug reaching approval, from around eight percent to eighteen. Note the wording carefully. The quoted phrase is that the odds "may increase." That is a forecast, run forward from the Phase I improvement, not an observed result.

Here is the uncomfortable corollary, and it is the one the coverage skips. If you raise the Phase I pass rate and leave Phase II untouched, you have not made the pipeline more valuable. You have pushed more candidates into the expensive stage that still kills most of them. That is more costly failures per dollar of discovery spend, not fewer. The logical response would be capital rotating away from pure discovery engines and toward whatever attacks translational failure: biomarker-driven patient selection, trial design, PK/PD modelling. Say it plainly. That rotation has not happened. The money is still upstream.

If the measured gain is upstream, the next question is who actually pays for it. That brings us to Schrödinger, on this beat for the second time in six days, after last Thursday's Tectora spinout. Schrödinger has launched Bunsen, an agentic AI co-scientist. It pairs a language-model orchestration layer with the company's decades-old physics-based free-energy simulation engine, on infrastructure co-engineered with NVIDIA and Google Cloud. Bristol Myers Squibb has announced a collaboration to deploy it, with no financial terms disclosed. This is a concrete answer to where the workhorse-versus-specialized-architecture line sits today. The language model plans and executes the workflow. The physics engine, not a foundation model, does the binding and structure work.

Now the number that puts that architecture debate in perspective. Schrödinger's second-quarter annual contract value was twenty-nine point six million dollars, up twenty-seven percent year over year. That is real, recurring, auditable revenue, which is a rarity in this sector. It is also a reality check on what the industry is actually willing to pay for the discovery layer that so much AI-native biotech valuation rests on. Twenty-nine million dollars, at the most established computational-chemistry incumbent in the business, is not nothing. It is also not a number that justifies multi-billion-dollar platform marks. And Bunsen itself is still early access, with full commercial release targeted for later this year. No benchmark has been published against pure machine-learning structure predictors. So treat it as a distribution and packaging advance until proven otherwise.

Large pharma is telling a version of the same upstream story. On Pfizer's February earnings call, chief executive Albert Bourla described the company's AI savings as improved productivity rather than simple cost-cutting. At JPMorgan's healthcare conference in January, AstraZeneca cited more than fifty percent faster target design and validation in early discovery. Both of those are January and February statements, not today's news, and both are the same category of claim as the base-rate data. They are internal, unaudited, and they measure cycle time or cost, not probability of success. If that is where the durable AI value sits for the next few years, large-cap pharma margins can expand steadily while pipeline economics do not move at all. That is a very different equity story from the one AI-native discovery platforms are sold on.

One asset actually stands to grade all of this, and it is the one this beat has followed all week. Rentosertib is the Insilico Medicine molecule now dosing patients in a three-hundred-and-twenty-patient Phase III trial for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. Insilico calls it the world's first pivotal trial of a generative-AI-designed drug. That is the company's own framing, and this beat has already flagged that the "first" claim does not hold up cleanly against other AI-designed assets already in Phase III.

What matters is the evidence underneath it. The Phase IIa data showed a ninety-eight-milliliter gain in forced vital capacity at the high dose, against a modest decline on placebo over twelve weeks. But the confidence interval on that effect ran from eleven milliliters to a hundred and eighty-six. In other words, the true size of the benefit is barely pinned down going into a pivotal study. On safety, sixteen of seventy-one patients discontinued early, and seven of those involved liver injury or dysfunction. The trial is also China-only, so the read-through to an FDA or EMA filing is unresolved. This is the one program in the field with a non-sponsor grader attached, on a multi-year clock, and it is nowhere near a settled win.

So where does that leave the field. For the first time, there is an outside number on the cheap half of drug development, and it is genuinely good: AI-native programs clear Phase I at roughly eighty-five percent against an industry rate closer to half. There is still no comparable number for the expensive half. That is the clinical testing that consumes most of the two point six billion dollars and decides whether any of this changes how many drugs reach patients. Until that number exists, the honest read is cautious optimism about design efficiency, and real skepticism about anything sold as a change in the odds of success.
