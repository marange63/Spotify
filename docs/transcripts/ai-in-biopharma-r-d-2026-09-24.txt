Good morning. It's Thursday, September 24th, 2026, and this is your AI in biopharma R&D briefing.

This week's primary documents don't move any clinical needle. Instead they reprice the inputs of AI drug development. One sets what real-world data costs. One sets who owns the economics of a designed molecule. And one goes after how long the clock runs before a candidate even reaches a patient. None of it changes a success rate. All of it changes who captures the savings.

Start with the data layer, because it comes with the rarest thing on this beat: hard numbers filed under securities liability. Recursion Pharmaceuticals filed an 8-K on September 15th amending its master data-license agreement with Tempus AI. The term now runs six years instead of five. Recursion gave up its right to terminate for convenience. The annual fee resets to a flat 14 million dollars for each of years three through five. At least 4 million of each payment must be cash, and the rest can be stock or cash at Recursion's choice. And Recursion gets access to fewer de-identified records than before.

Recursion's original filing from November 2023 supplies the before-number. Under the 2023 deal, those same three years would have cost between 66 and 126 million dollars. The amendment makes it 42 million flat. That is a cut of at least a third, and possibly two-thirds.

It's tempting to read the stock-payment option as a new win for Recursion. It isn't. The 2023 agreement already let Recursion pay every fee in cash or stock, at its own election, from day one. If anything, the new 4 million dollar cash floor tightens it slightly. The real news is the size of the cut, not the currency.

Keep the scale honest, too. Recursion reported 556.8 million dollars of cash and restricted cash at the end of June. Saving somewhere between 24 and 84 million over three years matters. It does not make or break that balance sheet.

Now the callback. On Saturday the 19th, we said capital was pricing real-world data as a durable asset. We pointed to the Datavant minority stake and to Tempus's 36 percent growth in data-licensing revenue. This filing complicates that read. The most prominent AI-native buyer of that data just negotiated a much smaller bill.

But there's a competing interpretation, and it matters. Recursion gave something up too. It extended the term and surrendered its exit option. Tempus may simply have traded price for certainty. It now has a counterparty it cannot lose, on a contract that can still be settled largely in Recursion stock. That reading actually supports the durable-asset case rather than undercutting it. One contract cannot tell us which concession was worth more.

The second-order point for data vendors like Tempus follows directly. Non-cancellable, multi-year terms make data-licensing revenue look steadier. But when part of it can arrive as customer equity, it also carries counterparty and share-price risk. So watch the quality of Tempus's reported licensing growth, not just its rate. Note, too, what the contract actually meters: a capped count of de-identified records. That makes real-world data a quota the buyer can shrink, not an open pipe. The filing is silent on data linkage and HIPAA, so it adds nothing on those constraints.

There's a second leg to the deal. Recursion licensed its own TxFM RNA foundation model to Tempus for oncology use, for two years. Secondary reporting puts that at 12 million dollars plus additional pathology data. That figure does not come from the filing itself. The interesting part is the structure, not the size. The flow between an AI-native biotech and a real-world-data company now runs both ways. Recursion pays for less data, and gets paid partly in data for its model. Read that model income as financing, not as product-market fit. The so-what: this is one contract, not a trend, but it's hard evidence that for an AI-drug-discovery company, cash is scarcer than data.

If the data layer is being repriced by the buyer, the design layer is being repriced by the seller. AbbVie and Iambic Therapeutics announced a multi-year collaboration on September 21st. It spans immunology, neuroscience, and oncology. It is built around Iambic's Enchant v3 model, paired with its structure-based design tool, NeuralPLexer. Iambic calls the platform "molecular superintelligence," which is marketing. The substance is that Enchant v3 is a multimodal transformer trained on more than 6,000 molecular properties. Iambic's pitch is that it optimizes potency, selectivity, and drug-like properties together rather than one at a time.

Be clear about what that number measures. It counts training labels. It is not a hit rate, a cycle-time reduction, or a wet-lab result, and no prospective validation is attached. The release describes an upfront payment and success-based milestones, but sizes neither. No targets have been named. So this could be broad platform access rather than committed programs. An undisclosed deal should not be read as a large one.

What the AbbVie release does disclose is tiered royalties on net sales of anything the collaboration produces. That's the detail worth sitting with, because it revises our own framing. On Sunday the 20th, we held up Boltz and Pfizer as the emerging template. In that deal, Pfizer keeps full ownership of the compounds, and the design vendor's value stays in a services contract. Iambic, by contrast, keeps a claim on pipeline economics. So did Orbis, in its tiered-royalty deal with Novo Nordisk on the 17th.

So the template is not settling. Design vendors appear to be splitting into two camps. Service providers get paid and walk away. Royalty holders keep skin in the outcome. Which camp a vendor lands in looks like it tracks bargaining power more than the science. For large pharma, that turns fixed discovery headcount into variable, success-contingent cost. That can lower spend per candidate without changing the odds. And note the streak. Novo-Orbis, then Novo Nordisk's Claude deal with Anthropic, and now AbbVie-Iambic. That makes three large-pharma AI deals in about a week with no disclosed cash at signing. The so-what: big pharma is paying for AI-native design mostly in contingent currency right now, not upfront dollars.

Neither of those repricings matters much if the clock to the clinic doesn't move, and that's where the FDA stepped in. On September 15th, the agency opened its Expedited IND Pilot. It pairs sponsors with what it calls Qualified Research Institutions. Those are academic medical centers, CROs, and health networks. The goal is to overlap steps that normally run in sequence: IRB approval, site contracting, and trial activation. FDA's framing of the problem is blunt. In its words, first-in-human trials "may take up to two years to complete in the United States." Applications close October 30th. The agency expects to select eight to ten sponsor-institution pairs for the first cohort.

Be precise about what this is and isn't. It's not an AI program. The FDA release sets no AI eligibility criterion, so any benefit to AI-native developers is inference, not policy. It's small. Eight to ten pairs won't move industry-wide timelines for at least a year, and the pilot is untested. And it attacks sequencing and site logistics, not the biology that actually kills drugs in Phase 2. Getting to first-in-human faster just means reaching the expensive failure point sooner. If it scales, the binding constraint moves from discovery speed to the Phase 2 readout, which is exactly the clinical data most AI platforms don't yet have. So-what: this buys capital efficiency, not better odds.

Pull the threads together. Three questions stay open. First, does Tempus's certainty outweigh Recursion's price cut, or is this the start of AI-native buyers pushing back on data costs generally? Second, do design vendors like Iambic keep winning royalties, or does bargaining power settle the split toward one template? Third, does the FDA pilot scale past its first ten pairs?

On clinical success, this week adds no evidence either way. The outcome test is still Insilico's rentosertib, whose Phase III readout is at least a year out. So here is the standing view. Capital this week flowed to design, but the lower-hanging fruit for the odds sits at either end of the chain. One end is choosing which biology to bet on. The other is choosing which patients to enroll, which is where real-world data on eligibility and indications earns its keep. Design engines and faster IND logistics mostly buy speed and cost, not probability. My call: success rates improve, but modestly and slowly, and the proof will take years.

On spend, this week's shift from fixed commitments to variable, success-contingent cost points to flat total R&D budgets with a lower cost per candidate. Recursion's smaller, harder-to-exit data commitment is that shift in miniature. The next test is whether other data buyers follow its lead.
