Good morning. It's Monday, August 17th, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

We're leading with a private equity story that reads as a verdict on public markets' judgment of enterprise software. Reuters reported last Thursday that Silver Lake has held talks in recent months with Workday about taking the HR and finance software company private. No deal has been reached, and both companies have declined to comment, but the market reaction alone was the story: Workday shares surged nearly 18% on the day, adding about $8.1 billion in market value and pushing the company from a roughly $43 billion market cap toward the low fifties. Apply the typical 25% take-private premium analysts use for deals like this, and you get an implied equity valuation of about $53.75 billion. That would put a Workday buyout in the same league as Silver Lake's own Electronic Arts take-private, which EA's own announcement last year valued at an enterprise value of approximately $55 billion. So this isn't hypothetical scale-talk — it would be one of the largest software leveraged buyouts ever attempted, backed against a precedent Silver Lake itself just set.

Here's why that matters beyond deal-size bragging rights. Workday is still down about 15% year-to-date even after last week's pop. Investors have spent this year questioning whether seat-based subscription pricing survives the shift to AI agents that don't need a named human license to do the work. That's been the backdrop for this whole week of enterprise-tech coverage — ServiceNow trimming headcount, seat-based multiples compressing across the HCM and ERP cohort. What's new here is that a buyer is finally putting real capital behind the other side of that trade. Silver Lake, presumably underwriting Workday's cash flows with leverage rather than a growth multiple, is betting the public market's AI-disruption discount on stable, sticky enterprise software is overdone. That distinction matters, because a leveraged buyer gets paid back out of free cash flow, not multiple expansion. It only needs Workday's existing customers to keep renewing — not the growth story the public market is now doubting. The read-through landed immediately: Salesforce shares fell roughly 2.7% in trading the very next day, per MarketBeat, as investors extended the same "which seat-based incumbent gets discounted next" question to the rest of the group. That reaction was tangled up with some profit-taking after a prior AI-optimism rally, so treat it as directional rather than a clean causal read. Either way, the message from the buy side is that durable subscription revenue commands real value from a private buyer, even when the public market keeps applying an AI skepticism discount to it.

But don't read this as "seats are dying" being definitively wrong, or as a category-wide rescue. It's better read as the market discriminating on franchise quality. The clearest counter-evidence sits inside Microsoft's own numbers from three weeks ago: on its late-July earnings call, Microsoft disclosed that Microsoft 365 Copilot has crossed 30 million paid seats, with net seat adds more than doubling sequentially. That is not a company losing pricing power to agents — that's a category leader still expanding seat count at an accelerating rate. So the honest synthesis is that the market isn't condemning seat-based software as a model, it's punishing the specific franchises it doubts can defend those seats against agentic substitution. Workday, ServiceNow, and the rest of the HCM and workflow-software cohort are being asked to prove they're Microsoft, not the alternative. Silver Lake's bet is effectively that Workday can clear that bar even if the public market currently prices it as if it can't.

If that premium does get paid, watch what it does to the rest of the sector. A completed Workday buyout would remove a major public comparable for HCM and ERP software entirely, and it would almost certainly embolden private equity to go hunting through the rest of the depressed seat-based cohort — names like Salesforce and other subscription incumbents trading below where their cash generation alone would justify. That's how these things tend to move: one buyer proves the leverage math works on a franchise the public market wrote off, and the rest of the industry gets re-underwritten one name at a time. To be clear, that's a real possibility to track, not a confirmed pipeline; nothing beyond Workday has been reported. But the logic of the trade, once one buyer proves it out, tends not to stay a one-off.

Now, the other side of this question — if agents really are going to erode how much software value seat licenses capture, where does durable value actually settle? One fresh, concrete data point from last Thursday: MongoDB launched an Atlas Managed MCP Server, a fully hosted Model Context Protocol connection that wires coding agents — Claude Code, Codex, Grok Build, Devin — directly into live operational data inside Atlas, with nothing for a customer to install or run themselves. Access is governed through the same credentials and permissions teams already use for Atlas, so administrators keep one place to control what agents can touch. This is a launch, not evidence of adoption — MongoDB has disclosed no usage or revenue numbers yet, and the underlying connectivity was already possible through open-source MCP servers. What's new is MongoDB shipping it first-party, hosted, and governed, rather than leaving agent connectivity to third-party wrappers.

The strategic logic is worth spelling out. As coding and workflow agents increasingly write and execute the application logic itself, that logic becomes commoditized and interchangeable — any agent can generate a CRUD app or a report. And that shift is already measurable, not speculative. On that same late-July call, Microsoft said GitHub Copilot has reached 50 million users, and that one in three pull requests on GitHub now involves an agent. When a third of code contributions already route through an agent, the question of what those agents may read and write against stops being a research problem and becomes an operational one. What doesn't commoditize is the operational data those agents need to read and write against, and the governance layer controlling what they're allowed to touch. By making itself the managed, governed front door for agent access to live data, MongoDB is positioning itself as infrastructure agents depend on rather than software agents might replace. If that thesis holds, expect Snowflake, Databricks, and Elastic to ship comparable governed MCP offerings before long — this becomes table stakes for any vendor that wants to remain the default operational data layer once coding shifts decisively toward agents.

Put the two stories together and you get a coherent picture of where this week's evidence points. Private equity is willing to pay up for enterprise software franchises whose cash flows and customer lock-in it trusts to survive the agent transition, even while the public market keeps discounting the category broadly. And the vendors best positioned to defend their relevance aren't necessarily the ones with the flashiest agent features — they're the ones sitting on the data and governance rails that agents have no choice but to route through. Seat-based pricing isn't dead. It's being re-priced, franchise by franchise, based on who actually controls the layer an agent can't work around.
