Cautious Optimism Briefings

Strategic Power, Energy, and Institutional Change

Transcript · August 2, 2026

Good morning, it's Sunday, August 2nd, and this is your strategic power briefing.

The US-Iran war is now in its sixth month, and this week it widened from one energy chokepoint to two. Iran's Revolutionary Guard declared the Strait of Hormuz closed back on July 12, and it has stayed that way in practice ever since — a strait that formerly carried about 20 percent of global oil and gas exports. Around July 20, a Houthi group added a second front, imposing a blockade on Saudi-flagged shipping in the Red Sea and striking at least two tankers there. Hormuz alone normally carries about a fifth of the world's oil and gas exports; the Bab al-Mandeb corridor carries 10 to 12 percent of all global maritime trade. Both are now degraded at once. Saudi Arabia's response came on July 30: it convened defense ministers from 43 countries in Riyadh and stood up a 14-signatory Multinational Maritime Defense Alliance, including Turkiye, Kuwait, Bahrain, Qatar, Egypt, Pakistan, and Jordan, tasked with protecting the Bab al-Mandeb, the Red Sea, and the Gulf of Aden.

New reporting overnight sharpens how real that alliance is. It's not just a communique. The coalition has an assigned command structure — a Joint Operations Coordination Center and a General Secretariat, both based in Saudi Arabia — and its planned naval capability is built around the Royal Saudi Navy's Western Fleet, based in Jeddah, backed by the Royal Saudi Air Force. That's a genuine step past pure symbolism. But the same reporting is explicit that nobody knows when it activates: it is not clear yet when the coalition will be ready to run actual escort operations, and it isn't yet clear whether the other thirteen signatories are contributing their own ships or simply their names. So the honest read is a real command structure with no confirmed operational start date, and mostly Saudi hardware behind it so far. Separate the framework from the force — markets should price that gap, not close it prematurely. And if it does hold together, it's a rare case of Gulf states pooling hard security on their own account rather than under a US umbrella — a structural shift in regional power worth tracking apart from the war's daily news cycle.

Oil has been trading that same uncertainty. Brent spiked to roughly $95.30 a barrel on July 23, up about 36 percent on the month, as tanker transits through Hormuz collapsed — shipping trackers put daily transits around 10 ships versus a normal 88. By July 30, as word of the coalition and rerouting spread, Brent eased back to $87.30. Then, on August 2nd, came the biggest single update: President Trump posted on Truth Social that he's canceling a planned large-scale strike on Iran, citing the "perimeters of a deal" and pressure from Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman — and he tied that deal explicitly to what he called the immediate, complete reopening of the Hormuz strait. That's the president's own on-record statement, which is a step up from the live-blog aggregation this claim carried a day earlier, but there is still no independent White House or State Department confirmation. Treat it as reported, not settled. The pattern here matters more than any single headline: the war premium in oil is embedded, and it snaps hard in both directions on news — up on strikes, down on diplomacy — which is itself the signal that this isn't priced to a stable equilibrium yet.

That volatility has already migrated somewhere more durable than the oil futures curve: the Federal Reserve's own policy statement. On July 29, the FOMC voted 9 to 3 to hold rates at 3.5 to 3.75 percent — a notably large three-way hawkish dissent, with the Cleveland, Minneapolis, and Dallas Fed presidents all preferring a quarter-point hike instead. The committee's statement named the Middle East conflict directly, citing "elevated uncertainty that owes, in part, to the conflict in the Middle East," and attributed elevated inflation partly to "supply shocks that have driven price increases in certain sectors, including energy." That's the loop closing in real time: a chokepoint shock becomes a shipping and insurance cost, becomes an inflation input, becomes a line in a central bank statement, becomes a live argument inside the committee about whether to tighten into an oil shock. The dissent signals genuine division, not a decision — don't read it as a pre-commitment to hike. But three regional presidents publicly breaking with the chair over an energy-driven inflation call is unusual, and it means higher-for-longer is now a real possibility layered on top of the geopolitical premium itself.

There's a durable winner in all this, and it sits apart from the daily war headline. A Gulf risk premium that markets expect to persist strengthens the case for the American barrel as the safe alternative. Permian and Gulf of Mexico crude, and US LNG sold under long-term contract, become the supply that doesn't have to transit a contested strait. That's a structural tailwind for US oil services and LNG exporters that outlasts any single diplomacy headline — the mirror image of the risk premium. The same disruption that lifts Brent also re-rates the geography of safe supply toward the US Gulf Coast, which is exactly the kind of slow-moving, durable shift that matters more than the next round-trip in the price of oil.

That combination — an energy risk premium plus a Fed reluctant to ease — lands directly on the balance sheets building America's AI infrastructure. PJM, the mid-Atlantic grid operator, is already rationing power access rather than assuming supply catches up: its board proposed a one-time backstop capacity auction, bidding September 30 through October 21, to fill a 6.8 gigawatt shortfall from its 2028 delivery-year auction, raising the cost cap to $555 per megawatt-day and offering 15-year contracts to attract new generation. None of that is law yet — it's a board proposal awaiting FERC and state approval, the classic gap between announcement and actual megawatts on the grid. But the direction is clear, and it's the same direction the energy shock points: financing costs for gas turbines, nuclear restarts, and behind-the-meter generation are all rising at once, at the exact moment PJM says it needs new capacity fastest. Data-center power access is becoming the binding constraint on AI buildout, arguably ahead of chip supply itself.

There's a second, separate inflation front compounding the same week. The reciprocal tariff deadline hit on August 1 with no extension: country-specific rates up to 25 percent on Japan and South Korea, 32 percent on Indonesia, 36 percent on Thailand, and a proposed 50 percent rate sent to Brazil, with exemptions carved out for countries that already struck deals. Those figures come from secondary trade trackers rather than the primary Federal Register text, so treat them as illustrative of the direction rather than precise to the decimal. But directionally, it's a second, non-energy source of price pressure landing on the Fed's desk in the same week as the oil shock — which only complicates an already-divided committee's read on where inflation goes next.

Step back and the throughline is this: strategic power this week ran through control of physical chokepoints — a shipping lane, a grid interconnection, and now the financing cost of both. The war premium in oil is real but reversible on diplomacy; the Fed's response to it is not yet decided; and the infrastructure that depends on cheap, abundant power and cheap financing is absorbing the uncertainty in real time, whether or not the headlines resolve this month. Watch for whether the Saudi coalition actually activates naval operations, and watch the next FOMC statement for whether that three-way dissent grows or fades — those are the two threads that tell you if this settles or escalates.

That's the briefing. Have a good Sunday.
