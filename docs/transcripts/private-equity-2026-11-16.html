Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Equity & the Deal Economy

Transcript · November 16, 2026

Good morning, it's Monday, November 16th, 2026, and this is your private equity briefing.

On Thursday, we told you North American exit counts were on pace for a record year. We also flagged what that data didn't give us: a dollar value. The value data is now in. It tells a meaningfully different story, and it means revising our own read.

Start with EY's third-quarter US PE Pulse. EY puts US exit value at 131.0 billion dollars for the quarter. That's up from 65.2 billion in the second quarter. Value doubled in three months. Over the same stretch, EY says the number of exits stayed broadly stable. So the average exit got much bigger, while the market got no broader.

EY also tells you where the value sits. Three exits above 10 billion dollars each made up about 30 percent of the quarter's value. EY doesn't name those three deals or say how they exited, and we won't guess. But the shape is clear. Nearly a third of a quarter's exit value came from three transactions. That's a composition effect, not a broadening of the exit market.

One distinction before going further. Exit value is gross transaction value. It isn't cash in a limited partner's account. A sale becomes DPI only after it closes, and only after debt is repaid and the sponsor takes its share. So even the doubled headline overstates what actually reaches LPs.

Now the exit count, and here's the bottom line first. The direction of the count depends on whose data you use. The concentration in value does not.

Take CohnReznick, working from PitchBook data on completed US deals. It counts about 1,311 PE-backed exits through the third quarter. That's down roughly 34 percent from the same stretch last year. Yet CohnReznick has disclosed exit value up about 19 percent year to date. Once again, value is up while breadth is down.

So here's the revision. Thursday's Mergermarket count, for North America, pointed toward a record year. CohnReznick's completed-deal count, for the US alone, is down by a third. Both can be true at once. The geography differs. CohnReznick counts only closed deals, and Mergermarket's tally may catch deals earlier in the process. CohnReznick also warns that its latest quarter will likely be revised up as more closings are recorded. So the exit count is less settled than either headline implies. What every dataset in front of us does agree on is this: value is concentrated at the top, and breadth is unproven.

Here's the inference we'd draw, and we'll label it as ours. If three deals above 10 billion dollars carry nearly a third of a quarter's exit value, aggregate distributions now hinge on the megadeal tier. A megadeal exit needs a buyer, and financing, at megadeal size. On Saturday we covered the biggest recent test of that buyer pool. Clayton, Dubilier and Rice and GTCR walked away from their pursuit of Global Payments. That was a sponsor buying, not a sponsor exiting. And the reason for the walk-away is still thinly sourced. So it's one data point, not a trend. But at that size the pool of buyers is short by construction, so each walk-away matters more. That makes the distribution recovery lumpier and more fragile than a doubled headline suggests.

There's a second-order cost to that concentration. A value-weighted rebound makes industry data look healthier than the median LP's experience. Managers will carry the doubled number into the 2027 fundraising season. The LP in a median fund will be looking at a DPI that hasn't moved at anything like that pace. That gap between marketing and cash is where the next round of fundraising friction is likely to show up. For the median fund, with a median-sized company, the exit window has not obviously reopened.

When the median company can't find a third-party buyer, managers manufacture one. That's the handoff to secondaries, and there's genuinely new evidence here too.

ION Analytics reported on November 11th that continuation vehicles are no longer reserved for sponsors' best assets. ION ties the shift to longer holds. In its data, the average hold at exit reached 6.4 years in the third quarter. Every holding-period dataset this show has cited in the past week points the same way: longer, not shorter.

Read that alongside the median-deal problem from the lead. A mid-market sponsor with an aging asset, and no strategic buyer or IPO in sight, now has an option once kept for its best company. That does put cash in the hands of the LPs who sell. But the asset stays with the same manager. So DPI built this way says less about the health of the exit market than it appears to.

Now layer in the pricing. Jefferies' first-half 2026 secondary market review, as summarized in reporting, put volume at a record 118 billion dollars. That's up 15 percent on the first half of last year.

The more important number is price. Jefferies has average pricing for LP portfolio stakes flat at about 87 percent of net asset value. That's consistent with where it sat at the end of last year. Flat pricing is the case against distress. If the secondary market were cracking, you'd expect that discount to widen, and it hasn't. Keep in mind it's an average across all strategies, not a buyout-specific discount.

But don't lose the other half of that number. Eighty-seven cents on the dollar is also the market's own clearing price on private marks. A selling LP who wants cash now gives up roughly 13 cents of every reported NAV dollar. That's orderly. It is not evidence that the marks are right.

Here's the risk we'd flag underneath that stable price, and again, it's our read. Jefferies puts dedicated secondary dry powder at 290 billion dollars at mid-year. That's down from 327 billion at the end of last year, even as volume hit a record. So buyer capital is being consumed faster than it's being replenished. And that's happening just as continuation vehicles reach further down the quality curve. We have no second-half pricing data yet, so this is forward-looking. But if that combination persists, the 87 percent clearing price is the number most likely to move next.

The primary side of the machine, though, is telling the opposite story. With its third-quarter results on November 5th, Carlyle told investors it expects at least 20 billion dollars of fourth-quarter inflows. That could bring its full-year 2026 fundraising to about 60 billion. Carlyle has also set a target of more than 200 billion dollars of cumulative inflows from 2026 through 2028.

Two caveats. That's firm-wide guidance spanning credit and insurance alongside private equity, not a buyout-fund close. And the 60 billion figure depends on fourth-quarter money that hasn't been booked yet. Even so, at least at Carlyle, primary targets are still rising. Meanwhile, the secondary capital that recycles liquidity out of older vintages is shrinking. The money is being raised for the new funds. The bottleneck sits in clearing the old ones.

So where does the cycle actually stand? This is a top-heavy recovery. Exit value is real, but it's concentrated in a handful of megadeals. The exit count that would signal broad health is contested across providers. And the industry's engineered liquidity is widening in scope, even as the dry powder behind it thins.

The biggest unresolved risk is still the exit-and-distribution logjam for the ordinary, median-sized portfolio company. Where's the market complacent? In treating a doubled exit-value number as a reopened exit window. Where might it be over-discounting? In reading the secondaries boom as distress, when pricing says the opposite, for now.

Four catalysts will confirm or refute this read. The first comes this Friday, November 20th, at Partners Group. Global Value is the firm's flagship evergreen private-equity fund, sold to wealthy individuals. In the second quarter, redemption requests there ran at about twice its five percent quarterly cap. Third-quarter requests hit the cap again.

Holders are voting on a plan to split Global Value in two. One sub-fund is built to sell holdings in an orderly way and return cash. The other is built to compound. This is a re-run, because October's first vote fell short of its turnout requirement. This time the bar is lower. Each sub-fund keeps the same quarterly limit, so a yes vote doesn't widen the exit. It sorts the queue. The selling sub-fund is due to start November 30th. Our read is that a yes makes it a live test of whether a semi-liquid vehicle can sell its way out of a redemption queue. A no leaves a gated fund with no structural fix, and the gate as its only tool.

The second catalyst is the next secondary pricing print after a flat first half. Third are the fourth-quarter manager prints in January and February. That includes whether Blackstone delivers the significant step-up in realizations that management guided to on its October call. And fourth is EY's fourth-quarter Pulse, which will show whether exit value broadens beyond a few megadeals.

That's your private equity briefing. Have a good week.
