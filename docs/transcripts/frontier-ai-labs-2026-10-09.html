Frontier AI Lab Competition

Transcript · October 9, 2026

Good morning, and welcome to Cautious Optimism Briefings. It's Friday, October 9th.

All week, the capability claims from the frontier labs have been vendor-reported, and token prices have kept falling. Google counted its own wins for Gemini 4 Argon, thirteen of eighteen benchmarks. Anthropic reported its own computer-use numbers for Haiku. Mistral published its own cyber scores for Large Four. Today the contest moves to two things nobody outside the labs can audit yet. One is claims made ahead of verification. The other is compute financed with borrowed money and public equity.

Start with OpenAI. This week it posted hundreds of families of claimed mathematical results from a model it has not released. A spokesperson said nearly all of them came from one prompt to a single AI agent. That claim is OpenAI's own, and nobody can check it without the model. The headline items are a claimed resolution of the four-dimensional Kakeya conjecture, and a result on what's called the quasi-Riemann hypothesis. Be precise about that second one. It is a narrower zero-free-region claim, not the Riemann hypothesis itself. Then, within a day, OpenAI withdrew three manuscripts. A sign error invalidated a cancellation argument, and with it the construction used by two dependent papers.

Here's where the claim runs ahead of the verification. Scientific American reports that forty-two percent of the results are coded in the formal-proof language Lean. Decrypt counts only a hundred and sixty-two of seven hundred twenty-two papers carrying a computer-checked main result. That's about twenty-two percent. The two numbers don't reconcile, and they may be counting different units. That's the deeper story. Even the figure meant to anchor the skepticism is itself contested. Treat any single verification percentage as a moving snapshot, not a settled number.

Avoid both easy readings of the retraction, too. Three withdrawals out of hundreds looks like a low error rate. But that rate only covers what has actually been checked, and most of the release has not been machine-checked. So the retraction doesn't prove the results are junk, and the volume doesn't prove a step change.

The mathematics community isn't waiting quietly. MIT's Andrew Sutherland says that until OpenAI releases the model and people can replicate the results, claims about one-shotting problems with a single agent should be treated as unverified. His shorter version is, "we should ask for receipts." Terence Tao's complaint is about process. Problems, he says, are being solved autonomously by AI prompters who have no interest in the broader field itself. And the Association for Human Mathematics put it more bluntly still. Releasing over seven hundred files at once, it said, is not a demonstration of scholarship, but a demonstration of power.

There's a steelman here, though. An advisory group hosted by the Institute for Advanced Study had asked labs to stop testing advanced problems on proprietary models. It also asked for machine-readable links between plain-language writeups and formal proofs. OpenAI didn't meet that bar. TechCrunch reports that just ten of the seven hundred nineteen manuscripts released the model's chain of thought. But a Lean pipeline that has formally checked somewhere between a fifth and two-fifths of these results is itself real, checkable progress. That pipeline may prove a more durable advantage than the headline conjectures.

Now weigh the timing. The release lands the same week Google's Argon claimed the benchmark lead, and while OpenAI's more capable Astra successor sits shelved. My inference is that narrative positioning is a plausible motive. The second-order cost is credibility. If verification keeps trailing announcement, claims from unreleased models get discounted by default, and that discount spills onto OpenAI's other claims. The reward shifts to labs that ship something testable. So the bottom line on the lead: OpenAI is asserting a research lead that nobody can yet hold it to.

What OpenAI actually shipped to the public this week is a different kind of move. GPT-6, in its Sol and Luna variants, is rolling out to everyone in ChatGPT, across free and paid plans globally. It replaces GPT-5.6. OpenAI's own Preparedness Framework classifies this release as High capability in both cybersecurity and biological and chemical domains. Neither model reaches High in AI self-improvement. And that label is OpenAI's self-assessment under its own framework, not an outside finding or a regulator's approval.

Compare that with Google, which has kept Argon gated to its Fairwind cyber-defense program and US government partners. Each lab can call its choice responsible. But Google's caution doubles as delay, while OpenAI takes the installed base. And OpenAI's High rating doubles as a capability boast, shipped to the entire free tier at once. So the trade is clear. OpenAI is answering a benchmark argument with distribution, not with a better score.

Shipping to everyone takes compute, and that's the week's other story: who's paying for it. SpaceX is reportedly in early talks with banks and investors to raise forty billion dollars to buy Nvidia chips. The reported split is about ten billion in bank loans and thirty billion in investment-grade debt. Investment-grade lenders are underwriting SpaceX's launch and Starlink cash flows, not xAI's model economics. That makes xAI's compute access a cross-subsidy on a sister company's credit. The competing reading is that this is ordinary capex financing by a cash-generative company. Either way, lenders rather than model revenue now set the pace, and it's a direct demand signal for Nvidia that doesn't depend on xAI's own sales.

Two more Musk moves fill in the picture. Musk says Tesla and SpaceX, not TSMC, will build and run the planned Terafab chip plant in Texas. That's his own statement, and TSMC hasn't confirmed it, but the intent is vertical integration rather than renting allocation. Memory is scarce even inside his own companies. Tesla cut its AI5 chip's memory in half and trimmed AI6 by about a third. The reported reason is to preserve supply for Optimus robot production.

None of the SpaceX financing is signed yet. Early talks can still shrink or fall apart. But it extends a pattern running across our desks this week. Earlier, it was Broadcom's reported lending behind Anthropic's TPU leases, and then private-credit talks behind OpenAI's custom chip. For disclosure, Anthropic makes the model behind this show. The takeaway: frontier compute increasingly sits on borrowed balance sheets, not on the labs' own revenue.

China's leading open-weight labs are reaching for a different pool of money: public equity, not private credit. Moonshot AI, the maker of the Kimi models, has reportedly closed its final private round at about a fifty billion dollar valuation. It's working toward a Hong Kong listing in the first quarter of 2027, weighing a raise of up to five billion dollars. Bank of America is reportedly coordinating, with CICC, Deutsche Bank, and Goldman Sachs as sponsors.

DeepSeek is the update. On Wednesday we reported a round of at least eighty billion yuan. Bloomberg-sourced coverage now says it could stretch to a hundred billion yuan, about fifteen billion dollars, with Tencent and CATL among the investors. Call that a range widening upward, not a new round.

That matters on two levels. First, it narrows a capital-access gap that used to run clearly in the US labs' favor. Second, it raises an open question. What exactly does an open-weight lab sell to public shareholders, when anyone can host the weights and compete on serving cost? The answer is probably Kimi's consumer distribution and API business, not the weights. And keep the numbers in proportion. A fifty billion dollar mark set in a final private round is a negotiated figure, not a market price. Neither Moonshot nor DeepSeek has filed a prospectus.

Running the scorecard. On research claims, OpenAI is asserting, unverified. On distribution, OpenAI gains, with GPT-6 now live for every ChatGPT user. On safety posture, Google and OpenAI are diverging, and both postures are self-assessed. On compute access, xAI is financing through SpaceX's credit. On capital access, China's open-weight labs are opening a path to public equity. And Meta is silent again this cycle, which is itself worth noting.

What to watch. Whether the Lean-verified share moves, and whether more manuscripts get withdrawn. Whether OpenAI releases the model or its prompts. Whether SpaceX's financing actually gets signed, and on what terms. And whether Moonshot or DeepSeek files a prospectus.

That's the briefing. Have a great Friday.
