Good morning, it's September 8th, 2026, and this is your private credit briefing.

Start with the cleanest single-name proof yet of the argument we've been building all week: a benign-looking portfolio statistic can sit right on top of a near-total loss. Blue Owl's flagship public BDC, OBDC, has been marking a loan to specialty-materials maker Loparex, and the path tells the story. The second-lien tranche was marked at roughly 88 cents on the dollar at year-end 2025. By the end of the first quarter this year it was down to about 63 cents. By the second quarter it had collapsed to roughly a nickel. The first-lien tranche moved even more violently. Valued around par at year-end, it's now marked at 22 cents, and essentially all of that loss landed in a single quarter. Loparex skipped a June interest payment on its second-lien debt and is operating under forbearance through September. Moody's has called the company in default and warned a Chapter 11 filing is plausible. To be precise, that's a rating agency's opinion, not a filed bankruptcy. No petition has been confirmed as of today.

Here's the part that should worry you more than the write-down itself. OBDC's overall non-accrual rate, the number everyone quotes, is just 0.8% of the portfolio at fair value. But OBDC also discloses that same metric measured at cost, and on that basis it's 2.8% — more than triple. The fair-value number is the flattering one, and it's the one that circulates. We also now know OBDC's second-quarter net asset value per share fell fifteen cents, from $14.41 to $14.26, with management attributing the move primarily to Loparex. That's a real but modest hit in isolation, about one percent of NAV across a $15 billion book. And that is exactly the point. A single loan can go from money-good to a nickel inside six months, and the portfolio-level arithmetic barely twitches. That's not evidence the risk was small. It's evidence the aggregate statistic is a bad instrument for finding it. If you're underwriting BDC exposure on the headline non-accrual print alone, you're one Loparex away from being wrong.

So is that idiosyncratic, or is it the system? Fitch's latest default study says system. The trailing twelve-month private credit default rate hit 6.1% through July, versus roughly 3.8% for leveraged loans and 2.7% for high yield. That's private credit defaulting at close to double the syndicated market it was supposed to be the safer alternative to. But don't fixate on 6.1% itself. That's barely moved from 6.0% the month before, and we've already covered the headline level. The number that actually tells you something new is the composition. Over half of Fitch's counted defaults in the broader study were driven not by missed payments in the traditional sense, but by interest deferrals and the introduction of payment-in-kind. Of ninety-nine default events in the trailing period, eighty-one were first-time defaulters. Put those together and you get an uncomfortable read. The industry's preferred workout technique — extend, defer, let the coupon accrue as PIK instead of cash — is now itself the mechanism generating the default count. So the headline rate isn't just high. It's understating the underlying deterioration, because deferral is doing double duty as both the treatment and the diagnosis. Consumer products borrowers are the sharp edge of this, with an 11.1% default rate, up from 5.9% a year ago. So the Loparex story isn't a rogue name. It's what the aggregate data was already telling you, just with a face attached.

Now, where is this same capital actually being put to work right now? Two deals from the past week bookend the answer, and the contrast is the argument. Waymo is negotiating its first-ever debt raise, targeting more than $3 billion from PIMCO, Blackstone, and Sixth Street, arranged by Goldman Sachs. This is unrated paper, pricing at a spread of more than 500 basis points over the benchmark, financing an autonomous-fleet buildout with no ratings history to lean on. At the same time, Anthropic is finalizing an expansion of its bank-led revolving credit facility to $15 billion, up from $2.5 billion a year ago and above its own roughly $10 billion target. That facility is led by Morgan Stanley alongside Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan, and Citigroup — the same four banks leading Anthropic's IPO. Neither deal is confirmed closed. Both were still described as nearing finalization in the past week, so treat the terms as pending.

But the shape of it is already visible. Banks are keeping the best AI credit — a company with hyperscaler-grade backing and an imminent IPO — and upsizing its revolver sixfold on investment-grade-like terms. Meanwhile a capital-intensive, unrated, AI-adjacent borrower goes straight to private lenders at a wide spread. That's the traditional hierarchy reasserting itself: banks take the top of the structure, non-banks take what's harder to underwrite. Except this isn't diversified sponsor cash flow, the bread and butter of direct lending. It's concentrated, hard-asset, long-duration exposure to a single counterparty's capex plan. And it's underwritten by the same managers who are simultaneously discovering that their existing marks on software and specialty-materials credits haven't been tested by an actual downturn. So the question worth sitting with isn't whether Waymo's paper gets done. It's whether it's being priced and underwritten as if it were ordinary direct lending, when the risk profile is closer to project finance wearing a private-credit label.

Close the loop on the funding side. KKR's non-traded fund for EMEA and Asia-Pacific clients, KKR-Income Trust I, saw third-quarter redemption requests fall to about 2.5% of net asset value, down from a level that breached the fund's 5% quarterly cap in the second quarter. KKR will pay out $34.7 million to cover those third-quarter requests, plus $10.5 million carried over from the second quarter, against a since-inception annualized net return of 10.3%. That's a genuine data point of stabilization at one vehicle. But across the broader private credit industry, investors sought more than $14.5 billion in redemptions in the second quarter, and only $8.6 billion was actually distributed. That figure is sourced through secondary reporting, so treat it as directionally indicative rather than gospel. It tells you the liquidity mismatch in these semi-liquid wrappers is easing at the margin, not resolved.

So where does that leave the cycle? The payment layer — cash coupons actually going unpaid — is still grinding higher gradually. The binding risk is the promise layer: marks that haven't met a real downturn, an aggregate non-accrual statistic that can hide a single name's near-total loss, and a default rate whose composition shows the industry's own workout playbook inflating the count. Layer onto that a fresh wave of unrated, capex-heavy AI lending sitting inside the same funds as the credits already being marked down, and you have managers doubling down on the exact valuation methodology the Loparex episode just called into question. Watch three things from here. First, whether Loparex actually files Chapter 11 once forbearance lapses this month. Second, the November round of third-quarter BDC non-accrual and PIK disclosures. And third, whether the Waymo and Anthropic facilities close on the terms currently being reported.

That's the state of the credit machine. Thanks for listening, and I'll see you next time.
