Good morning, it's Sunday, August 9th, and this is your Capital Markets Radar.

Let's start with the crack, not the rally. Public high-yield spreads have been priced for perfection all week, and today that warning finally gets a quantified trigger underneath it. The ICE BofA U.S. High Yield index sits around 270 to 280 basis points, in the richest decile of its own history against a long-run median near 450. Investment grade trades at 81 basis points, BBBs at 100, so the high-yield-to-investment-grade ratio is roughly three and a half times, actually in line with its long-run average. In other words, public credit is telling you nothing unusual is happening.

Private credit is telling a different story. Morgan Stanley's own outlook, dated back to March, projects direct-lending default rates climbing to 8 percent, against a 2 to 2.5 percent historical average. That's a standing forecast, not a fresh print this week, but it's now got real data behind it. Blue Owl Capital Corporation, the business development company known as OBDC, filed its June 30th results this past week, and the primary release shows non-accruals rising to 2.8 percent of the portfolio at cost, up from 2 percent at cost just one quarter earlier. At fair value the picture looks calmer, actually easing to eight tenths of a percent from 1 percent, and that gap is the point: the positions that went bad have already been marked below cost, so they shrink as a share of fair value even as they grow at cost. KBRA separately projects the 2026 private-credit default rate by volume at 2 percent, versus 1.5 percent in 2025, with CCC-rated borrowers rising and consumer, retail and healthcare roll-ups the most stressed pockets.

Here's the mechanism that matters for your positioning. Public high-yield prices continuously, every day, in a liquid secondary market, which is why that 270-to-280 basis point spread reflects real-time sentiment. Private credit marks to model, on a lag, which is exactly why deterioration shows up first in non-accruals and payment-in-kind income at the BDC level, not in a headline spread. Blue Owl's quarter-over-quarter non-accrual jump is one data point, not a sector-wide confirmation, and the rest of the major direct lenders, Ares, Blackstone's BXSL, Goldman Sachs BDC, haven't reported their June quarter figures yet. But it's a real, primary-sourced crack, and it lands right as Morgan Stanley's 8 percent forecast is being tested rather than just repeated. The rest of BDC earnings season runs through September, and that's your dated catalyst: watch whether Blue Owl's move is idiosyncratic or the first domino.

Think about what confirmation would actually do. If the coming BDC prints show non-accruals broadening beyond the software names everyone already knows about, the second-order move isn't in the default rate itself. It's in net asset values getting marked down, listed BDCs trading at wider discounts to those NAVs, and a fresh round of scrutiny on how much the private mark-to-model regime has been flattering itself against public high-yield's continuous pricing. And there's a knock-on for the real economy. Direct lenders nursing rising non-accruals tend to pull back on new commitments, which quietly tightens financing for sponsor-backed borrowers at the very moment the Fed and the public credit market are signaling easier conditions. That's a bifurcation worth flagging: the headline says credit is loosening, but the marginal private borrower could find the money getting harder to raise.

The so-what here is straightforward. A credit-spread repricing is a bigger risk to record-high equities than the rate path itself, because public high-yield is doing almost none of the discounting work that equities, also at records, aren't doing either. There's far more room for that spread to widen than there is for it to tighten further.

Now, why is the market willing to sit this tight in the first place. Friday's jobs report is the backdrop, and it's worth one pass, not a full re-litigation since we've covered it already this week. Payrolls fell 23,000 in July against a consensus call for an 83,000 gain, and prior months were revised sharply lower, leaving a cumulative shortfall of about 252,000 jobs versus what was expected. Unemployment actually fell to 4.1 percent, likely a participation-rate quirk rather than genuine strength. The market read this as tightening risk coming off the table: September rate-hike odds on CME FedWatch dropped from 55 percent to 44 percent, while the odds of a hold jumped from 45 to 56 percent. The 10-year Treasury yield fell to 4.65 percent, and the S&P 500 closed at a record 7,758, its 26th record close this year. That dovish repricing is the permission structure letting credit stay this calm.

You can see that same rate-relief trade showing up on the other side of the risk spectrum. Spot Bitcoin ETFs added just under 99 million dollars on Friday, a fifth straight day of inflows, and spot Ether ETFs pulled in nearly 50 million, a fourth straight day. That's a real reversal, this streak follows a first half of 2026 in which Bitcoin ETFs bled 5.4 billion dollars in net outflows, their first negative half-year since launching in 2024. But look at where the money's actually going: BlackRock's IBIT alone captured 479 million dollars of a 626 million dollar three-day haul earlier in the week, roughly three-quarters of the entire category's inflows. This isn't broad institutional demand returning to crypto as an asset class, it's concentrated flow into the single most liquid wrapper, trading as rate-and-liquidity beta off the same jobs print, not as an idiosyncratic hedge. If BlackRock's flows reverse, the category's headline number reverses with it.

Gold is telling the same story from the safe-haven side of the ledger. It's back up near a two-month high, around 4,343 dollars an ounce, up better than 2 percent on the day and nearly 28 percent year over year, as the dollar index slipped to about 99.6. Worth keeping in perspective: this is a re-approach to the highs, not a new record, since gold still sits well below its 2026 peak. But gold rallying in lockstep with equities and Treasuries, on the same catalyst, is the tell that this is a real-rate story, not a flight-to-safety bid. The marginal buyer is trading the path of Fed policy, same as the Bitcoin ETF buyer, same as the equity buyer at a record close. It's one trade wearing three costumes.

So pull it together. The entire cross-asset complex, equities at records, gold near multi-month highs, Bitcoin ETFs snapping back from a rough first half, is pricing the same dovish rate story off one jobs report. Underneath all of it, the one place with genuinely quantified deterioration and a dated catalyst is private credit, where a major BDC just posted a real quarter-over-quarter jump in non-accruals against a backdrop of tight public spreads that aren't pricing any of it. The tell to watch over the next several weeks isn't the Fed. It's whether the rest of the direct lenders report the same non-accrual trajectory Blue Owl just did, and whether high-yield spreads themselves start to move before the earnings do.
