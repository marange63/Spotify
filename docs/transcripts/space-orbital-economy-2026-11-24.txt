This week the binding constraint on the orbital economy isn't ambition. It's who gets to occupy contested capacity on time, and what it costs you when you don't.

Start with the story this desk has tracked since November 15: ULA's Vulcan Centaur. That day, Vulcan failed about ten minutes after liftoff from Cape Canaveral. All 40 Amazon Leo satellites on board were destroyed. ULA calls it the company's first total mission failure. It still hasn't named a cause. That mission was the first of 38 Vulcan launches Amazon bought for its Leo constellation.

We flagged Amazon's recovery launch as the catalyst to watch on three straight updates. Here's where it stands. Amazon's own mission page now targets a flight called LE-04 for today. It's a 36-satellite launch on an Arianespace Ariane 64, out of French Guiana. It had been set for yesterday, and it slipped a day. As of this recording, trackers list its status as "to be confirmed." So treat it as pending, not as a success. Amazon's backup ride has already slipped once.

Now the part that's genuinely new, and it starts with a correction. On November 17, we said no FCC enforcement action or Amazon waiver request had been reported. That was wrong. The FCC granted Amazon a conditional waiver on June 5. We've now read the order itself.

Under its license, Amazon had to deploy 1,616 satellites by July 30 of this year. It missed. The waiver keeps the license in force, but it attaches a penalty. Satellites Amazon launches after that date lose their status as an "earlier-round system" in the processing rounds where Amazon was first licensed. They are reassigned to a later priority tier against any system the FCC authorizes in future rounds. In plain terms, Amazon keeps its license. But every satellite it launches late gives up its seniority over newer entrants in the FCC's pecking order.

Then there's the clock. The demotion lasts until March 2028. Amazon can pull that in to October 2027. To do it, Amazon has to certify that it has built the satellites and booked their launches. Or the penalty ends the moment Amazon certifies that half its first-generation constellation is launched and operating. The separate deadline to deploy the full constellation is untouched.

There's a second-order effect in that structure, and it's our inference. Because the penalty is time-bound, Amazon holds a cheap lever. A procedural filing buys back five months of standing. Amazon has every incentive to file it early, well before its deployment numbers would justify confidence.

Now the arithmetic, and this is our math, not the FCC's. Per Orbital Radar's tracking, Amazon had 396 production satellites in orbit before the Vulcan failure. Getting to the FCC's milestone means roughly 1,220 more. One caution on that count. A tracker counts satellites in orbit, while the FCC counts satellites launched and operating, and the two can differ. Either way, Amazon's Vulcan block was meant to carry most of that gap. Its first flight just failed.

There's a more charitable reading, and it deserves a hearing. A waiver that keeps the license alive is the FCC accommodating Amazon, not punishing it. The real risk here is time, not revocation. But time now has two prices. Every slip delays revenue and stretches the constellation's payback. And every slip also costs regulatory standing. Meanwhile, the Space Force pause on Vulcan national-security launches continues. That pause has been in place since a February booster anomaly. So Vulcan's problems aren't resolving on any front. Amazon's launch clock and its spectrum clock are now the same clock.

The same regulator, in the same week, made the pie bigger. On November 19, the FCC voted unanimously to add 50 megahertz of Ka-band spectrum for earth stations in motion. These are moving terminals talking to non-geostationary satellites. The vote takes the contiguous block to 250 megahertz. That lowers a real barrier for markets like aviation and maritime connectivity. But an allocation isn't a subscriber. New spectrum is worth most to whoever already has satellites flying to use it. Amazon, arriving late and at a priority discount, sits on the wrong side of that dynamic twice. By our read, regulatory scarcity and launch scarcity now reinforce each other in the incumbent's favor.

And even the operator with the most satellites up is flying below its own disclosed capacity. SpaceX's Starship Flight 15 deployed 26 Starlink V3 satellites. Then the launch tower caught its upper stage, a first and a real reusability milestone. But SpaceX's IPO prospectus, as relayed by Converge Digest, says a single Starship launch should eventually deploy up to 60 V3 satellites. Be fair about what that gap means. Up to 60 is an eventual ceiling, not a near-term promise. The current loads may reflect test-campaign caution rather than a hard limit. Still, it's the same announced-versus-realized gap, now showing up at the top of the industry. The takeaway is that the capacity shortfall isn't one company's regulatory mess. It runs through the whole sector right now.

So where does new Western launch supply come from? Rocket Lab's Neutron order book is the clearest answer, and it cuts both ways. We covered the headline on Saturday. Rocket Lab booked more than 515 million dollars of Neutron contracts in the third quarter. It also raised the base price to 70 million dollars a launch, all before Neutron has flown.

What's new is the shape of that book. The manifest now stands at 16 missions, and the next open slots fall in the 2030s.

Read generously, that's real demand validating an unflown rocket. Read skeptically, it says something about planned early cadence, and that reading is our inference. If that manifest fills the calendar into the next decade, the implied early flight rate is low. That caps how fast Rocket Lab's launch revenue can scale, even if every slot holds. And these are signed contracts, not recognized revenue. ICEYE, one of the new customers, booked three dedicated launches that don't start until late 2028. For Rocket Lab holders, that's backlog for the back half of the decade, not next year's numbers.

The other new booking matters for a different reason. The Space Force bought one Neutron launch, under its SB-AMTI program. Buying a slot on a rocket that hasn't flown is the government hedging against single-vendor concentration. That's exactly the risk Vulcan's grounding exposed. It's a sensible hedge, but a small one. The plain takeaway is that new Western medium-lift capacity isn't arriving in volume before the end of the decade. That's the supply side of Amazon's problem, and the source of Rocket Lab's pricing power.

Pulling the threads together. What's genuinely inflecting is how regulatory scarcity and launch scarcity reinforce each other, in favor of whoever is already flying. What's still a demo is Neutron, which hasn't flown, and Starship at full V3 loads. The biggest open question is how long Amazon's priority discount actually bites. Does Amazon certify its way out early, or ride the clock into 2027 or 2028?

Four catalysts are worth watching. First, whether today's LE-04 launch actually clears and deploys. Second, whether ULA names a cause and a return-to-flight plan for Vulcan. Third, a date for Starship's next flight. And fourth, December 14, when final proposals are due in NASA's commercial space-station competition.

That last one comes with a courtroom attached. Axiom sued Vast in June under the False Claims Act, in federal court in Houston. The suit centers on a NASA mission-planning document that Axiom alleges Vast copied. Those are allegations, and whether the Justice Department has intervened isn't established. But a legal fight over paperwork rather than hardware is telling. Heading into the deadline, the technical gap between the leading ISS successors looks thinner than either company would like.
