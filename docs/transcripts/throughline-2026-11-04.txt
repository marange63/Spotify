Good morning, this is The Throughline for Wednesday, November 4th, 2026.

Today's headline: the AI trade is now being priced in the bond market, with Meta seeking at least twenty billion dollars in its first investment-grade sale since April, at spreads that widen the longer investors have to wait.

Ten briefings shipped today. We'll start with the macro backdrop, move through the AI and compute stack, and close on the applied frontier.

Strategic Power finds the grid now deciding where AI gets built. In October, Microsoft walked away as anchor customer for a data center in Loughton, England, because the local utility couldn't deliver enough power until the early-to-mid 2030s. In Texas, Governor Abbott's audit of a 474-gigawatt interconnection queue is turning first come, first served into verify or be denied, while the biggest builders, like Crusoe in Abilene and xAI in Mississippi, are leaving the queue for converted jet engines.

From the power grid to the bond market. Capital Markets Radar puts numbers on that slope: Meta's initial guidance steps up from one hundred thirty basis points over Treasuries on the five-year to one hundred eighty-five on the ten-year. The desk reads that as the market charging for the length of the AI payback, and notes that CoreWeave and Oracle, the names leaning hardest on outside capital, are each down more than half from their 2026 peaks. It also corrects Oracle's Monday drop to almost nine percent, not the five percent this show repeated yesterday.

From public credit to private. Private Credit and the Corporate Credit Cycle finds the strain in the money behind the loans: Apollo posted a GAAP net loss of about 1.06 billion dollars on rate-driven marks at its Athene insurance arm. Meanwhile, Blackstone's flagship non-traded BDC met only about 75 percent of requested redemptions within roughly ninety days, which the desk calls a backlog, not a run. Reported credit metrics are stable, so the stress sits in funding and in older loans, not in defaults.

Private Equity closes the macro tour with the other side of that Apollo print. Realized performance fees came in nearly triple the second quarter's, and Apollo says they reflect private-equity realizations. That's one manager and one quarter, but it's the first real data point against the desk's capital-in, no-cash-out framing.

That's the macro backdrop. Now the AI and compute stack, where the financing question turns into a bill for memory.

Semiconductors and Compute Infrastructure has AMD answering the test the desk set yesterday, with data-center revenue up 97 percent and five major customers now running workloads on its Helios racks. But gross margin landed right on guide at 56 percent, which the desk says partly breaks its thesis that accelerator makers absorb the rising cost of HBM memory. Lisa Su did flag memory inflation as a live pressure, so the real test comes when next year's memory contracts reset.

A standing disclosure for the next two desks: Anthropic makes the model behind this show. Frontier AI Lab Competition leads with security. OpenAI has engaged Cathedral Systems, the startup that says it hacked into Anthropic, to stress-test its own defenses, the same week a Reuters investigation reported years of internal security warnings at OpenAI. Both accounts are contested, and the desk's read is that neither lab gained ground on enterprise trust, while incumbents with signed security paperwork, like Microsoft, did.

AI Products and Applications finds control over AI agents becoming its own product, sold separately from the model. Snowflake's Cortex AI Gateway, now generally available, puts hard spending caps on agent traffic across providers, and CrowdStrike agreed to buy the agent monitor Fiddler for a reported 400 million dollars. And athenahealth made Claude the first assistant patients can link to their own medical records, but the record holder, not the lab, controls that on-ramp.

Enterprise Technology leads with Oracle's twenty billion dollar raise, split evenly between new stock and new bonds, which knocked the shares down Monday before a rebound Tuesday morning. The desk reads the rally as relief at a known number, not endorsement, since twenty billion is about three percent of Oracle's backlog and more raises are the base case. And Microsoft expects under five percent uptake on its buyout offer, so payroll is not where it's funding AI.

That's the compute stack. Now the applied frontier, where two newly public companies just faced their first real tests.

Robotics and Physical AI has Agility Robotics' SPAC merger winning its shareholder vote, but most of the SPAC's original trust cash walked out before close, leaving Foxconn as the real anchor investor. Meanwhile, North American robot orders rose nearly 25 percent in the third quarter, led by chip and electronics makers, whose orders nearly doubled. The dependable money is going to narrow, provable automation, not bipeds.

Space and the Orbital Economy has SpaceX's first results as a public company, with Starlink now a real profit engine at roughly a 46 percent operating margin, while launch roughly broke even. The AI-compute segment, Super Intelligence, is now bigger than Starlink by revenue, but total capital spending ran above the quarter's entire revenue. Shares were already down thirty-eight percent from their June high before the print, and the desk weighs a lockup overhang against a re-rating toward an AI-spender multiple.

One change against the week behind us. Over the past week, this show watched the market sort AI spenders first by earnings, then by forward guidance, and yesterday by how they fund the gap. Today that funding lens runs through most of the tour, from Meta's bonds and Oracle's stock sale to SpaceX's capex running ahead of its revenue.
