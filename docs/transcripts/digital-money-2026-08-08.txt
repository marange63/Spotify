Good morning. It's Saturday, August 8th, and this is your digital money briefing.

Start with the print that moved everything this week. Friday's July jobs report showed the US economy lost 23,000 jobs, against a Bloomberg consensus of plus 80,000, with unemployment ticking down to 4.1 percent. That's a huge miss, and rate futures immediately repriced toward a September cut. Bitcoin and ether both caught a bid on the release. That's the actual news. The three-day, 626-million-dollar run of spot Bitcoin ETF inflows you may have already heard about, most of it into BlackRock's IBIT, happened earlier in the week and was the run-up, not the reaction to Friday's number. Treat it as backdrop, not the story.

So where does Bitcoin actually sit. As of Friday morning it was trading around 64,700 dollars, essentially flat to modestly higher on the day. That's still roughly 49 percent below its October 2025 all-time high of just over 126,000 dollars. There is a real accumulation signal underneath that price: long-term holder supply has grown by something like 380,000 bitcoin over the trailing 30 days, an approximate figure from aggregated on-chain data rather than a single clean source, but directionally consistent with holders pulling coins off exchanges rather than selling into strength. And it doesn't sit in isolation. Exchange reserves have kept declining alongside that accumulation, which tightens the free float. A tighter float cuts both ways. It can amplify an upside squeeze when buyers crowd in, but it just as easily opens an air pocket on the way down when spot liquidity is thin. So the same tightening that reads as bullish accumulation also makes the tape more fragile, not less.

That's a genuine institutional-comfort signal. But it's not what moved the tape this week. What moved the tape was a bad jobs number and a rate-cut bet. A market that needs weak macro data to clear resistance and still trades nearly half off its peak is behaving like a high-beta risk asset tracking equity sentiment, not the uncorrelated hedge the bull case wants it to be. Contrast that with gold, which has largely held onto its safe-haven role this year while Bitcoin has not. That's the distinction to hold onto: real accumulation, but a liquidity-driven rally, not an adoption-driven one.

Now follow that same jobs print one layer down, because it doesn't just help Bitcoin's beta. It's also a direct threat to the stablecoin issuers sitting underneath the whole digital-dollar stack. Circle reported its second quarter this week, and the numbers confirm something Morgan Stanley had only modeled a few days earlier. USDC in circulation ended the quarter at 73.3 billion dollars, up 19 percent year over year — but that growth rate is decelerating sharply, down from 28 percent year-over-year growth just one quarter earlier when USDC stood at 77 billion. That's a sequential contraction of roughly 3.7 billion dollars, about 5 percent, in a single quarter. Circle's own earnings call adds nuance: average circulation during the quarter actually hit an all-time high of 76.5 billion, so the pullback was concentrated right at quarter-end. Still, the direction is now a confirmed fact from Circle's own filings, not an analyst's assumption. Meanwhile USDC's share of the total stablecoin market slipped to 27 percent, down 66 basis points year over year, with Tether continuing to hold the dominant share of the category.

Here's why that intersects directly with Friday's jobs number. Circle's total revenue and reserve income was 701 million dollars in the quarter, and of that, reserve income specifically was 668 million dollars — roughly 95 percent of everything Circle makes, calculated straight from those two reported figures. That reserve income is earned on the Treasury bills backing USDC, and the yield on that pile is already falling: Circle's reserve return rate was 3.48 percent in the quarter, down 66 basis points year over year, which the company itself attributed to the decline in SOFR. So this is not a hypothetical rate-cut risk. It is already showing up in Circle's numbers, before the Federal Reserve has cut again. Layer Friday's weak jobs report and rising rate-cut odds on top of a float that's already shrinking, and you get a double squeeze: less USDC outstanding to earn a yield on, and a shrinking yield to earn on what's left.

And the yield side faces a structural competitor, not just a rate cycle. Tokenized money-market funds pass their Treasury yield straight through to the holder, while USDC by design keeps that yield for Circle. In a world where rates are falling and every basis point matters more, a product that shares the yield with the user is a harder rival to beat than one that keeps it. Circle's answer to all of this is Arc, its own blockchain, whose public mainnet is confirmed for September 16th, built around an agent-payments stack and tokenized real-world-asset support — a genuine attempt to build a second revenue line before the rate cycle bites harder. But right now Arc is a launch date, not a revenue number. So the takeaway here: Circle is, underneath the crypto framing, a levered bet on one spread — reserve yield minus distribution cost — and both sides of that spread are now under pressure at once.

While the two biggest layers of the stack argue over who owns the dollar, the layer underneath both of them just had a genuine failure, and it's worth closing on because it cuts against the "institutionalization equals security" story directly. Over the past week and a half, attackers have drained more than 130 million dollars from users of Coinkite's Coldcard hardware wallets. The root cause traces back to a single line of code from 2021 — a build-configuration error that caused some devices to generate seed phrases using a weak software random-number generator instead of the hardware's own entropy source. That flaw sat undetected for roughly five years before someone exploited it at scale. Coinkite has published and updated an advisory telling affected users to update firmware and migrate to new seed phrases, and the loss total has been moving upward through the week as more affected wallets get identified, so treat 130 million as an as-of-early-August figure, not a final one.

This matters because it's a self-custody failure, not an exchange or custodian failure. The entire "not your keys, not your coins" pitch that underpins cold-storage practice for both retail holders and increasingly institutions assumes the hardware layer is the safe layer. A five-year-old, undetected entropy bug at nine figures of loss says that assumption has its own single point of failure, and it's one that never shows up in ETF flow data or custodian AUM reports. Expect this to sharpen scrutiny of hardware-wallet firmware-audit practices. And it's a plausible tailwind for multi-party-computation and multi-signature custody providers, who can position explicitly against single-device cold storage precisely because their model has no one box holding the whole key.

Put the week together and the pattern is this. A single macro print split the digital-asset stack down the middle: the same jobs miss that revived Bitcoin's bid by reviving rate-cut odds is mechanically compressing the reserve yield that is almost the entirety of Circle's revenue. Real accumulation is happening on the Bitcoin side, and real diversification is being attempted on the Circle side, but neither has actually decoupled from the macro cycle or from operational risk. Institutionalization is real. It is not yet insulation. That's the whole story this week, not the parts of it that just repeat what you've already heard.

That's the briefing for August 8th. Have a good weekend.
