Good morning, this is Cautious Optimism Briefings for Thursday, November 26th, 2026.

On November 20th we flagged that XPENG's chairman had guided its IRON humanoid to mass production at "the end of this year or in January." At Wednesday's AI Day, XPENG put an actual date on it. That's one of three rungs of evidence in physical AI this week. They sit in ascending order of how much they prove. A spec sheet is the bottom rung. A demo is the middle rung. A fielded fleet is the top rung, and this week only the top rung gives us a real answer.

Start with the spec sheet. One sourcing note first. Everything here is XPENG's own presentation, relayed by trade press such as CnEVPost and Humanoids Daily, not a filing. XPENG says IRON enters hardware mass production in January 2027, so the end-of-year option from the chairman's guidance is gone. IRON will then train as a sales assistant in XPENG's domestic retail stores starting in the first quarter, with public release targeted for the second quarter. Notice what that sequencing means. The production line starts before the robot has finished training for its first job. Our read is that the line is being dated ahead of any evidence that the product works. That's the same "capacity, not output" pattern we flagged yesterday on Figure's robot factory and Tesla's Optimus ramp. No volume, unit cost, or price appears in any AI Day coverage this desk checked. One outlet even put the missing price in its headline. A manufacturing-line milestone is not a shipment number, and XPENG hasn't given us one.

The one spec worth carrying is the battery. XPENG says IRON runs a solid-state pack rated above 300 watt-hours per kilogram. It delivers an hour and a half of work on a twenty-minute charge. By our arithmetic, that cycle works out to roughly eighty percent availability. Call it a fifth of the robot's time spent charging, before you even ask whether any given task succeeds. That's fine for a showroom floor. It's a real constraint for a three-shift factory or warehouse. XPENG didn't disclose a full-pack runtime beyond that duty cycle. So take the figure for what it is: desk arithmetic on a partial number.

Now the part of AI Day that actually matters. IRON runs on three of XPENG's own proprietary Turing chips, not Nvidia's Jetson Thor reference stack. A second trade outlet covering the same event, Techzine, reports that these are exactly the same Turing chips and VLA 3.0 models that XPENG uses in its cars. That makes XPENG the sharpest example yet of a pattern this desk has tracked all week. Hyundai Mobis is building Atlas's actuators in-house. Tesla is putting its own AI5 chip into Optimus first. And now XPENG is running its car chips and car models in a humanoid. Each is pulling a critical layer of the robot inside the company. XPENG goes furthest, because the layer it owns is compute and the model. That's exactly the layer Nvidia's reference platform sells.

Here's the second-order point, and it's our inference. A carmaker that shares chips and models across vehicles and robots can spread that development cost over its car volume. The robot doesn't have to pay for its own silicon program. A humanoid pure-play does. So does a Western builder assembling a supply chain from scratch. That's a structural cost edge, and it changes who can afford to build humanoids at scale. It also means the most vertically integrated humanoid builders are exactly the buyers Nvidia's reference platform doesn't capture. The edge has one condition. Car-grade chips and driving models have to transfer to manipulation work, and nothing shown at AI Day proves that yet.

There's also a caveat on the first deployment. This desk raised it when Hyundai's own plants became the first buyer for Atlas. Internal demand is not a market test. The same applies here, because IRON's first customer is XPENG's own stores. And greeting shoppers is a low-payload, low-manipulation job, about as demo-friendly as humanoid work gets. Nothing reported from AI Day speaks to autonomy, teleoperation share, or task reliability. XPENG gave us a date. It did not give us a number.

That's the bottom rung. The middle rung is demos, and this week it's a public fight. Figure's chief executive, Brett Adcock, posted a clip of the Figure 03 robot retrieving an apple after its table was moved. He called it "zero shot and fully autonomous." Per coverage of the exchange, that was his answer to Elon Musk's claim that humanoid demos are generally staged. Musk has promised an "authentic" Optimus demo within a month or two. That's a catalyst, not a result. Figure's one hard number isn't new this week. Its Helix model completed 56 percent of three selected household tasks, up from 9 percent without pretraining on Figure's own human-video dataset. That's self-reported, on tasks Figure chose. Neither company has published intervention or teleoperation data. A demo samples one success. It doesn't sample the long tail.

Which brings us to the top rung, a fleet already in commercial service. On Sunday, this desk asked whether Waymo's fixes would hold in the field. This week brings new evidence on that question, and it points the wrong way. A sourcing note: these facts come from Reuters, as relayed by Insurance Journal, not from the regulators' own documents. Reuters counted eleven incidents of Waymo robotaxis entering closed freeway lanes since October 1st. There's no public trip or mileage denominator, so that count can't be turned into a rate. The sharper detail is the timing. Three of those incidents came on a single day, November 9th, when three Waymo vehicles entered freeway lanes coned off for construction. That was just three days after Waymo had narrowed its own operating scope to reduce exposure to closed freeway lanes. California's DMV has barred Waymo from all state freeways since November 11th. Waymo has since filed recalls with NHTSA to strengthen how its software handles closed freeway lanes. It still reports no collisions or injuries from these events, and that's a real counterweight. But the new information is about whether the fix lasts, not about raw counts. The cheapest fix available to an autonomous fleet is shrinking where it's allowed to drive. Waymo's version of that fix didn't hold for three days.

Here's the second-order read, and again it's our inference. Narrowing the operating envelope is the industry's standard first-line fix. This episode shows that fix isn't self-certifying. Waymo's next answer is a filed software recall. A regulator that watched the scope restriction fail that fast is likely to want a demonstrated incident-free stretch before reinstating freeway access. A filed update alone probably won't do it. And the DMV isn't the only regulator looking. NHTSA has also opened a recall query into Waymo robotaxis passing stopped school buses. That lengthens the timeline for Waymo getting the freeway route class back. It also raises the bar every other operator will face when it asks a state for freeway approval.

Pull the three rungs together, and here's the picture. What's genuinely shifting this week is cost structure, not capability. XPENG runs its robot on the same chips and models as its cars, and that changes who can afford to build humanoids at scale. But a production date is still not evidence that the robot works. On capability, nothing in humanoids inflected this week. Nobody, not Figure, not Tesla, not XPENG, has published reliability or intervention data. And the one piece of hard fielded evidence, Waymo's, points the other way. A fleet with years of commercial service is still being pulled off an entire class of roads over cones. That gap, between a cheaper way to build robots and robots that work unsupervised, is the one every humanoid timeline this month is racing against.

Watch three things from here. First, whether XPENG's January line start ever comes with a volume or a price attached. Second, whether Musk's promised "authentic" Optimus demo survives the scrutiny Figure's clip got. Third, whether California lifts Waymo's freeway bar on a filed fix, or only after a demonstrated clean record.

That's the briefing. Stay cautiously optimistic, and we'll talk tomorrow.
