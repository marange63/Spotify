Good morning, it's Sunday, August 16th, and this is your private credit briefing.

We led Friday with Nvidia's six-firm coalition to mobilize over 500 billion dollars for AI data centers, and came back to it Saturday. Nothing has been drawn yet, those are still non-binding memoranda. And this week the marginal flow in credit actually ran the other direction: out of private markets and back into the banks.

Start with the clawback. Bloomberg's Credit Weekly reports that companies with private debt are refinancing into the syndicated loan market roughly three times more often than the reverse flow. In the second quarter, banks pulled 4.5 billion dollars across nine deals out of private credit, versus just 1.3 billion, three deals, moving the other way. The clearest example is Catalent, which refinanced private loans from Ares and Blue Owl into a 4.1 billion dollar syndicated term loan and cut its borrowing cost by 225 basis points. That's not a rounding error. That's direct evidence the spread premium private lenders have been charging was partly a function of the higher-for-longer rate cycle, not a durable structural toll for disintermediating banks. Commercial and industrial bank loan growth backs this up, running at a 14.2 percent annualized rate last quarter versus just 4.4 percent a year earlier — banks have the balance sheet and the appetite to compete again. And Blackstone Secured Lending Fund cut its net asset value by the most in six years in the second quarter, the clearest mark-to-market evidence yet that this isn't just a pricing story.

Two caveats before you treat this as a regime change. The deal count is tiny — nine versus three — and could easily be noise. And full-year 2025 flows still net toward direct lenders, 40.8 billion in versus 34.1 billion out; the reversal so far is in this year's momentum, not yet the level. But the direction matters, and the borrowers moving first are the good ones. Catalent always had bank-market optionality. So if banks keep winning back the best credits, the natural next question is what's left in the direct lenders' book — and whether it's adversely selected.

That question gets sharper when you look at pricing discipline from the lender side. Lincoln International data cited by the Wall Street Journal shows payment-in-kind provisions on new private credit loans fell to 13.5 percent in the second quarter, down from 25 percent at the end of last year — roughly halved. Lincoln's Brian Garfield frames this as bargaining power shifting back to lenders: PIK is being treated less as a sweetener and more as a warning sign. On its face, that's underwriting discipline improving.

But look at the back book, not just new originations. PitchBook data on the BDC cohort shows aggregate PIK interest income actually ticked up slightly to 8.3 percent of total interest in the second quarter, from 8.2 percent in the first — still near multi-year lows, but not collapsing the way the new-issue number is. And a growing share of that back-book PIK isn't new-issue accommodation at all. A year ago, Lincoln found that 56 percent of loans carrying any PIK had none at underwriting — the PIK was bolted on later. That figure is stale and from a different dataset, so take it as structural color, not a current print. But the shape is the point: most PIK in the book was never an origination sweetener, it was added after the fact to keep a struggling borrower current. Industry sources, including Fitch, now track a category they call "bad PIK" or shadow defaults — loans where lenders convert cash-pay interest to payment-in-kind after issuance to keep a borrower's liquidity afloat, functioning as a proxy for defaults that would otherwise have happened. That shadow-default rate is running at roughly three times its 2021 level. So the honest read is two different populations moving in opposite directions: new deals are getting tighter and more disciplined, while the existing book is where real stress is quietly accumulating, dressed up as a payment-in-kind election rather than a non-accrual.

Now put the deployment side next to the pricing side, because they compound. Ares Management raised more than 36 billion dollars in the second quarter — a record — and ended June with a record 170 billion dollars of dry powder, AUM up 17 percent to roughly 671 billion. That's the number the industry markets. Here's the harder one: private-credit inflows sector-wide are down about 25 percent year to date versus the same period last year, and redemption requests in the retail channel are running hot — 38.1 percent of net asset value at Blue Owl Technology Income, 18.9 percent at Blue Owl Credit Income, 16.8 percent at Apollo Debt Solutions. So you've got record institutional dry powder that can't find deals, because banks are winning the good ones, sitting next to retail vehicles fielding redemption requests they can only partly satisfy from cash flow. That combination is the deployment squeeze: it either compresses forward returns as capital chases fewer good deals, or it pushes BDCs to reach for yield in riskier sponsors or AI-infrastructure lending to defend their numbers. Neither is a strength dressed as one. And notice where the second option leads. Reaching into AI-infrastructure lending to backfill deployment ties the residual book straight back to the concentration risk we flagged the last two days — the same handful of vendors and hyperscalers, now funded partly to make up for the good middle-market credits the banks are taking away.

It isn't a one-way rout, though. Jane Street is reportedly negotiating with investors, including PIMCO, to rework as much as 11 to 15 billion dollars of its debt into a private placement — moving from public-style disclosure into private credit specifically to limit what it has to report to the market, not primarily to save on cost. That's the opposite motive from Catalent. So the flow out of private credit isn't universal; it's motive-dependent. Cost and credit quality are pulling the best sponsor-backed borrowers to banks, while disclosure control and structuring flexibility are pulling large, sophisticated financials like Jane Street the other way. The asset class is being repriced and re-segmented, not simply out-competed.

Last thread, the default read itself. Moody's forecasts U.S. speculative-grade defaults falling to 3.0 percent by October, down from 5.3 percent a year earlier, with Europe falling to 2.4 percent from 3.8. That's the headline. The narrower Morningstar LSTA loan-index trailing default rate, which tracks actual missed payments and bankruptcies rather than distressed exchanges, rose over the same period, from 0.82 percent to 1.44 percent. Those two measures aren't directly comparable in scope, so treat the gap as suggestive rather than proof. But it rhymes with everything else this week: the marketed number keeps improving while the harder, narrower number keeps drifting up.

So where does the cycle actually stand? This is repricing, not yet distress — spreads compressing, PIK tightening at origination, banks re-engaging, none of that is a credit event. The binding risk isn't the headline spread level, it's adverse selection and liquidity mismatch: direct lenders left holding the residual book as banks cherry-pick the best names, and retail vehicles facing redemptions just as spread compression limits what they can earn refinancing what's left. Watch three things from here: whether next quarter's bank-versus-private refinancing flow confirms the reversal or reverts to 2025's norm, whether Blue Owl and Apollo's redemption requests keep climbing, and whether that Moody's-versus-LSTA gap widens further.

That's the briefing. Have a good Sunday.
