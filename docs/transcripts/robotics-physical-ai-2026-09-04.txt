Good morning, and welcome to Cautious Optimism Briefings for September 4th, 2026.

The humanoid bet has quietly stopped being a manufacturing bet. It has become a compute bet. Yesterday's biggest announcement in the sector shows how far that shift has gone.

Figure AI has signed a multi-year partnership with the cloud provider Nscale. The deal covers up to one hundred thousand Nvidia GPUs on the Vera Rubin platform. That is Nvidia's next generation, and it has not shipped yet. Figure's own release puts the initial commitment at three and a half billion dollars of compute, with what the company calls intent to scale to over six billion. First deployment is targeted for the second half of 2027, in Barstow, which is where Nscale will stand the machines up. And Nscale is not simply Figure's vendor here. Nscale is also making a strategic investment in Figure as part of the same deal.

That structure is the news, not the dollar figure. This is a compute-purchase agreement shaped like a frontier language-model lab's chip contract. It is not shaped like a robotics manufacturing contract. And it sits inside a company that has never disclosed a robot-revenue figure.

The circularity matters, so say it plainly. The supplier owns a piece of the buyer. That is the vendor-financing structure the market has been repricing across AI infrastructure all year, now showing up in robotics at scale for the first time.

Now the caveats, because every element of this deal is forward-looking. The six-billion number is an intent to scale, not a signed commitment. The disclosed hard number is three and a half billion. The GPUs deploy in the second half of 2027, so none of this compute is live today. Nscale's exact stake in Figure was not disclosed. And Figure published no unit-shipment target, no robot-revenue number, and no compute-per-robot economics alongside any of it. There is nothing here to divide the money by.

That compute has to be pointed at something. Figure answered that question about ten days ago, when it came out of stealth with a consumer app called Index. Index pays people to upload first-person video of everyday tasks, building a training corpus for robot manipulation. We covered that launch already, so here is just the number that matters today. The app is now ingesting thirty minutes of video every second, and Figure has committed to spend over a billion dollars on data and compute over the next twelve months. The Nscale deal is what makes that pledge legible. The compute half of the billion now has a name, a size, and a delivery date.

One callback is worth making here. Earlier this week this show flagged a fork opened by the startup Skild AI. Skild argued that a single human video, shown in context, can do the work of roughly three hundred eighty teleoperated demonstrations. The implication was that the binding constraint on robot learning is architecture, not data volume. Figure's answer to that argument is not a competing benchmark. It is capital. Figure has escalated the volume side of the bet by roughly an order of magnitude, rather than publish a transfer metric of its own.

Two second-order effects follow from a purchase this size. The first lands on Nvidia's demand book. A robotics buyer pre-committing to a hundred thousand units of the Vera Rubin generation diversifies that book beyond hyperscalers and language-model labs. It also drops Figure into the same power-and-siting queue as the AI data-center build-out. Figure's timeline is now hostage to Nvidia's 2027 schedule and to megawatts in Barstow, not only to its own factory yield.

The second effect lands on everyone else in the field. If training a general-purpose robot brain costs billions of dollars of compute, the humanoid sector bifurcates. On one side sit the players who can raise on frontier-lab terms, plus Tesla and the Chinese cohort, who can self-fund. On the other side sit the smaller Western startups, who partner, get bought, or retreat to task-specific work. The capital bar just moved, and it moved before anyone demonstrated that the compute buys capability.

Now to the company running the opposite theory of where the hard part sits. Tesla has begun Optimus production at its Fremont factory, on the line that used to build the Model S and Model X. Musk called it the hardest product to scale manufacturing that Tesla has ever made. He explicitly declined to forecast the ramp rate. His standing framing, repeated since January, is that early production will be, quote, agonizingly slow, but eventually end up being insanely fast. That is an expectations reset from the party with every incentive to inflate the number, which makes it more informative than a confident timeline would be. Early Optimus units are going into Tesla's own internal training program on its factory floors, not to outside customers. That keeps Tesla off the hook for external unit economics at exactly the moment the rest of the sector is being asked to publish theirs.

It is worth being precise about what Tesla has not said. There is no disclosed Fremont unit count, no weekly output rate, and no yield figure. Production start is a status, not a rate. And the annual capacity numbers circulating for Fremont and for the planned Giga Texas line are line design points, not orders and not deliveries. They should never be spoken in the same breath as a shipment figure.

Some listeners will have watched Tesla's Cybercab event in Austin last night for a fresh Optimus disclosure. It was not one. That event was staged as a robotaxi product launch, with Optimus present as scenery rather than as data. A dedicated search of the coverage turned up no verbatim Tesla figure for Fremont output, yield, units built, or ship date tied to that stage. So the honest thing to say is the plain thing. Tesla put no new Optimus number on the record last night.

So what does this sector actually earn? For a denominator, look to Unitree, the Chinese quadruped and humanoid maker that listed on Shanghai's STAR Market a couple of weeks ago. Set the listing itself aside. The number that matters is Unitree's income statement, the first real one this sector has produced anywhere. Unitree booked one point seven one billion yuan of revenue in 2025, roughly two hundred fifty million dollars. Adjusted net profit was six hundred million yuan, about ninety million dollars. That revenue is mostly quadrupeds and lower-cost units sold into research and entertainment channels. So it is not a clean proxy for humanoid demand, and it is a floor on what the sector earns rather than a ceiling on what it could. But set it against the number that opened this briefing. Figure has committed capital more than ten times the industry's best-documented annual revenue, at a company that has disclosed none of its own.

Let me pull the threads together. For a week now, the biggest number on this beat has been a commitment rather than a delivery, and today's is the largest and the furthest out of them. There is also a live risk to the whole structure, and it is the Skild argument from earlier this week. If a low-data architecture turns out to be directionally right, the asset that loses value is precisely the one Figure has just bought. The differentiator would relocate down the stack, to actuator cost, battery, and manufacturing yield. That favors the Chinese cohort, and Unitree is the evidence: the only real income statement in this sector was earned selling hardware, not compute. That stack also runs on rare-earth permanent magnets, and there the export-control story cuts against the panic. Beijing tightened controls twice within five weeks this summer, yet magnet exports rose in July to five thousand six hundred forty-nine tons, up from four thousand seven hundred thirty in June. The leverage is real; the physical constraint is not yet binding.

Three things would settle any of this. A compute-to-capability figure from Figure, showing the billions translate into better robot performance. A real production rate out of Fremont. Or any humanoid maker beyond Unitree publishing revenue instead of a commitment. Until one of those arrives, treat every headline number in this sector as a promise, and price it accordingly.
