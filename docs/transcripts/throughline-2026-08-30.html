Good morning, this is The Throughline for Sunday, August 30th, 2026. Today's headline: one hawkish speech from Fed Chair Kevin Warsh at Jackson Hole on Friday is still setting prices across markets that otherwise have nothing to do with each other, from the long end of the Treasury curve to gold, bitcoin, and the borrowing cost sitting underneath the entire AI buildout.

Start with the macro backdrop. Strategic Power spent the day on one question. Can Washington talk the thirty-year yield down by itself? Treasury doubled the size of its long-dated bond buybacks. It was also reported to be weighing its roughly nine-hundred-fifty-billion-dollar cash account as extra ammunition. The tape said no twice. The thirty-year finished the buyback announcement higher than it started. Then it climbed again on Warsh's speech, as market-implied odds of a September rate hike jumped from thirty-five percent to fifty-seven percent. The takeaway for that beat is that the long end is being tolerated by the market, not managed by Washington. And that yield is exactly what prices the debt-heavy data-center buildout everyone is counting on.

From the bond market to the asset that has been trading as its mirror. Digital Money took the same speech to the crypto tape. Bitcoin fell about four percent, to a low just under seventy-seven thousand dollars. Gold fell more than three percent the same day. So this was both hard assets repricing on a real-yield shock, not bitcoin failing a test that gold passed. Nearly half a billion dollars of leveraged crypto positions were liquidated in twenty-four hours. Spot bitcoin ETFs ended a nine-session inflow streak with about two hundred million dollars of outflows. Set that against three billion of August inflows and it is a crack, not a collapse. The open question that beat is now tracking is whether bitcoin's gold-like correlation survives a genuinely hawkish month, or whether it was always just a squeeze artifact.

That same repricing runs into the credit market, and that's where the day's structural read begins. Private Credit and the Corporate Credit Cycle found capital arranging itself so that no outside party gets to set a price. CLO managers are increasingly funding their own first-loss equity tranche through captive vehicles, rather than selling it to third parties. That removes the one investor with an arm's-length reason to argue about the mark. It is the same governance shortcut the private equity beat flagged on Friday, in deals where the sponsor controls both sides of the sale. Meanwhile banks are out-competing direct lenders for the best credits. CoreWeave's three-week-old bank loan priced at five hundred fifty over SOFR. And there are thinner reports of borrowers refinancing out of private credit into cheaper bank debt. That would quietly leave the direct-lending book worse, with no default number moving to show it.

The same question, who gets to set the number, runs straight into the compute stack. There, the week's motif was every headline commitment sliding to a later date, except one bill that isn't moving at all. Semiconductors and Compute Infrastructure took apart Marvell's custom silicon agreement with Google, the one headlined at up to a hundred and twenty billion dollars. That number is a warrant vesting ceiling running out to fiscal 2033, not an order backlog. Which is why Marvell fell about eight percent even though the quarter itself beat. The same slippage showed up twice more. Samsung's two-nanometer yield gap cost it Qualcomm's next flagship processor. And Intel's Xeon 7 server platform slipped a full year, into 2027. The one cost that did not slide is HBM4 memory, trending toward roughly double what HBM3E costs per gigabyte. It lands on every one of those delayed roadmaps whenever they finally ship.

Frontier AI Lab Competition read that identical Marvell warrant from the buyer's side, and reached the same conclusion independently. Google is paying in equity optionality because it could not get a firm purchase commitment. That is the third headline capex number this beat has watched shrink on inspection in three days. It also carried the week's rare outside checks on the labs. The payments processor Ramp tracks card spend across seventy thousand businesses. In that data, Anthropic's flagship model has flattened at about eleven percent of what those customers spend on Anthropic's tools. Anthropic's own cheaper model has already passed it. Separately, a federal judge ruled the Pentagon's national-security designation of the company illegal and baseless. Both checks cut the same way. If capability is no longer commanding the price, the contest moves onto cost structure and distribution.

If price is now the competitive variable, Enterprise Technology has the closest thing yet to a real AI attribution number from a software vendor. Workday said AI products drove more than a quarter of new annual contract value. But its subscription growth was fourteen percent last quarter and thirteen point nine percent this one. That is flat across exactly the stretch AI is supposed to have been driving a quarter of new bookings. Set it beside Salesforce's self-defined Agentforce run-rate, which has no denominator, and CrowdStrike's silence on AI attribution altogether. Workday's disclosure is still the best of a weak field.

One thread is worth naming, because it inverts the one this show has run for three days. The complaint all week was that every figure on these beats was published by the party it flattered, with no outside check attached. Today the checks finally arrived, from a payments processor, a federal judge, and a bank syndicate willing to print a spread. And the self-reported numbers all shrank in the same session. A capex ceiling that is not an order. An AI attach rate that has not bent a growth line. A first-loss tranche marked by the manager's own affiliate.
