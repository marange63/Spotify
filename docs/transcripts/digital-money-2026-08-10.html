Cautious Optimism Briefings

Digital Money, Crypto, and Market Structure

Transcript · August 10, 2026

Good morning, it's August 10th, 2026, and this is your briefing on digital money, crypto, and market structure.

Start with Bitcoin, because the tape itself is quiet, and that quiet is the story. US spot Bitcoin ETFs pulled in $853.5 million over five straight days last week, the week of August 3rd through 7th, according to aggregated flow data — the strongest weekly haul since April, with BlackRock's IBIT said to account for roughly 81 percent of it. That's the picture on paper. In practice, Bitcoin sat at $64,895 on August 9th and around $64,883 on August 10th, essentially flat, and still about 49 percent below October's all-time high. This is the second straight week that heavy ETF buying has failed to move price at all. So the puzzle we flagged last week — that ETF flows may no longer be the marginal price-setter — isn't resolving, it's hardening. If institutional buying this size can't move the tape, something else is absorbing the supply, and that something is probably risk appetite, not adoption. Worth watching, but the real movement this week is one layer down, in the plumbing of the dollar-stablecoin system itself.

That brings us to Circle. On its August 7th earnings call, Circle confirmed it has renewed its commercial distribution agreement with Coinbase through 2029, keeping Coinbase as USDC's central partner across retail and institutional platforms. This resolves, at least contractually, the threat that had hung over Circle's stock since late June, when Coinbase joined the Open USD consortium — a rival stablecoin network backed by more than 140 partners including Visa, Stripe, Mastercard, and BlackRock — a move that knocked roughly 17 percent off Circle's shares at the time. Locking Coinbase in for three more years removes that near-term uncertainty. But look at how it was renewed. Circle's own executives described it on the call as renewing "on its existing terms," and Coinbase's CFO, Alesia Haas, told her own earnings call in late July that the contract would "renew on the same terms" once conditions were met. Both sides are telling us the split didn't change. Circle has historically given up the majority of USDC's reserve income to Coinbase as a distribution fee, and nothing here suggests that arrangement got better for Circle. So the headline stability comes at a real cost: Circle bought three more years of certainty by re-signing a revenue-sharing structure the market has already started to reprice as a drag — the same structure that Morgan Stanley's recent bearish call on the stock has been targeting.

Circle tried to change the subject with two other numbers. First, its CFO, Jeremy Fox-Geen, ruled out a dividend outright, telling analysts, "the short answer is no, we don't," arguing that reinvesting in the platform beats paying out cash. Second, Circle more than doubled its 2026 guidance for non-stablecoin reserve revenue, from $150 million to a range of $310 to $330 million. That sounds like real diversification away from reserve-yield dependence — until you look at the breakdown Circle itself gave. Of that $310 to 330 million, about $180 million is one-time revenue from Circle's Arc token pre-sale, recognized against product milestones the company expects to hit this year. Only $130 to $150 million is recurring revenue from Circle's other products. So the guidance raise is roughly 55 to 58 percent one-time accounting, not durable growth — a materially less clean number than "doubled guidance" implies on its own, and one that likely steps down once the Arc milestone recognition tapers next year.

One more wrinkle: Coinbase hasn't walked away from its hedge. Coinbase CEO Brian Armstrong described his company as running "a multi-stablecoin platform" while still maintaining "a great partnership with Circle and USDC." That's not a company giving up Open USD membership — it's a company keeping both options open, harvesting today's USDC economics while building optionality in a rival rail. So the honest read on this week's news is: Circle removed a near-term distribution risk, but not the underlying competitive threat, and paid for that removal by re-committing to economics the market already dislikes, dressed up with a growth number that's half accounting recognition. Call it deferral, not resolution.

Now zoom out, because the same dollar-stablecoin model that's central to Circle's business is winning a much bigger fight abroad, and that raises its own problems. In an August 7th speech, the IMF's First Deputy Managing Director, Dan Katz, warned that local-currency stablecoins can actually accelerate dollarization rather than prevent it. His argument: once a local stablecoin shares blockchain rails with dollar tokens, users can convert frictionlessly through decentralized exchanges and liquidity pools, which moves currency conversion outside the regulated banking system entirely. He pointed to South Africa's rand-backed ZARU token as a live example. USDT alone settled roughly 27 billion rand, about $1.7 billion, on South African exchanges in the year to April 2026, while ZARU's own volume isn't even publicly reported — a sign, though an inferred one, that dollar tokens are simply absorbing the demand a local-currency alternative was supposed to capture. Nearly 99 percent of all stablecoins in existence are dollar-denominated. South Africa's own central bank governor, Lesetja Kganyago, said in late July that stablecoins "must not be allowed to create loopholes for capital controls." So the same USD-token dominance that underwrites Treasury-bill demand at home is, from an emerging-market regulator's chair, a capital-account escape valve. Expect this to show up in how countries outside the US license or restrict stablecoin rails going forward, even as Washington pushes for broader domestic adoption. Distribution dominance, in other words, is becoming a policy problem for everyone except the country whose currency is winning.

Which brings us to the US rulebook that would formalize all of this. Senate Majority Leader John Thune filed a cloture motion on the CLARITY Act on August 8th, which preserves the bill's position for a floor vote in September rather than forcing it to restart from scratch when the Senate returns from recess. That's real, if modest, progress. But Republicans are still roughly seven Democratic votes short of the 60 needed for passage, and disputes over stablecoin-yield provisions and an ethics side-agreement tied to the president's crypto income remain unresolved. A procedural filing is not a vote secured.

Put it together and the thread for today is this: the dollar-stablecoin complex keeps winning on distribution, at home through Circle's renewed Coinbase lock-in and abroad through dollar tokens swamping local alternatives. But the economics underneath that dominance, and the American regulatory framework meant to formalize it, both remain unsettled. Winning distribution isn't the same as winning the economics, and it isn't the same as winning the rules.

That's today's briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
