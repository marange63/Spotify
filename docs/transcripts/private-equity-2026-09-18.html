Private Equity & the Deal Economy

Good morning, it's September 18th, and this is your private equity briefing.

Start with Blackstone, because it's the cleanest instance this week of a pattern worth naming directly. Bloomberg reported yesterday that Blackstone is in conversations with potential buyers to arrange a secondary sale. The transaction would let existing investors in a US fund run by Blackstone Property Partners cash out. That fund carries a net asset value of roughly eleven billion dollars. The detail that matters isn't the size of the fund. It's that Blackstone itself is taking a formal, structuring role in the deal, rather than letting it happen the ordinary way, as an arm's-length trade between one limited partner and another.

Bound this hard, because it isn't done. These are conversations, not a signed transaction. No price has been disclosed. No discount to net asset value has been disclosed. No buyer has been named. And this is a real estate vehicle, not a buyout fund. The mechanism generalizes across private markets. The asset class does not. One more piece of hygiene. The fund sits inside Blackstone's broader Property Partners strategy, which the firm reports at fifty-seven point seven billion dollars across multiple funds. Don't let that headline number stand in for the eleven-billion-dollar fund actually being transacted.

So what does it mean. When a general partner starts arranging the exit for its own fund, instead of an asset sale generating the cash, that is a tacit admission the organic realization isn't coming fast enough. Look at the three roles Blackstone occupies here at once. It is the fiduciary for the limited partners who want out. It is the manager whose own marks the price will be struck against. And it is the firm whose next fundraise depends on that mark holding up. That isn't a scandal. It is a structural conflict, and it is the same conflict embedded in every GP-led continuation vehicle, moved one step closer to the sponsor doing the pricing itself. The number that would settle whether this is genuine liquidity or a quiet markdown is what gets paid against that eleven-billion-dollar NAV. We don't have it. Don't read its absence as evidence there's no discount.

Put it in this week's sequence, because it is the third step in one. On Monday this show covered EQT completing its purchase of Coller Capital, one of the few large independent secondaries buyers with the mandate to price against a sponsor's own marks rather than confirm them. On Tuesday it was CVC closing a ten-billion-dollar secondaries fund. Today it is a sponsor arranging the bid on its own vehicle. The independent price-setter in this market keeps getting smaller, and the sell side keeps moving closer to owning the bid. For a limited partner, that changes the diligence question. It is no longer just what the fund returned. It is who set the price, and what their relationship was to the seller. No standard disclosure answers that today.

Now the channel this flows through, and how much capital is queued to use it. Lazard's own interim report on the 2026 secondary market puts first-half volume at roughly a hundred and twenty-four billion dollars, up about twenty-eight percent year over year. GP-led and LP-led deals came in nearly even within that total, at sixty-one and sixty-three billion. Treat the volume itself as background. This show ran comparable first-half numbers on Monday and Tuesday. The forward-looking piece is capacity. Market reporting puts roughly seventy-seven billion dollars of secondaries dry powder earmarked for second-half GP-led deals alone. That capacity figure reaches us through market summary rather than a named desk's own report, so hold it loosely. The bid for engineered liquidity is deep and growing. That is exactly the environment in which a top-tier manager would choose to run its own process rather than wait for a buyer. Worth remembering that the advisers publishing these totals earn fees on the volume they count.

Now the counter-evidence, because somebody did sell something for cash this month. EQT agreed on September 4th to acquire a majority stake in McGill and Partners, the specialty reinsurance broker, from Warburg Pincus for two billion dollars. Warburg is exiting seven years after backing the company's 2019 launch. McGill has grown to more than six hundred employees across seven countries, on more than two hundred fifty million dollars of revenue. This is a sponsor-to-sponsor sale. Cash for a business, not a continuation vehicle and not a fund-level secondary.

Two disciplines before reading too much into it. No entry multiple was disclosed, so nobody outside Warburg can tell you what a seven-year hold actually returned. Two billion dollars is a confirmed outcome, not a good one. And completion isn't expected until the first half of 2027, roughly nine months out. Signed is not the same as cash in a limited partner's account.

Widen the lens and a pattern shows up. Four insurance-distribution transactions have crossed this desk in six sessions. KKR's seventeen-billion-dollar sale of USI to Aon. Thoma Bravo's take-private of Accelerant. Baldwin, the insurance brokerage, going private. There the buyer was a permanent-capital holding company rather than a fund. And now Warburg's exit to EQT. One narrow sector is clearing while the broader exit market stays clogged. That's a sector bid, not a market thaw. It is what you would expect when buyers want durable, asset-light, non-cyclical cash flows and won't underwrite much else right now. The read-through is narrower than it looks. It tells you what the best asset in the most wanted niche can fetch. It says nothing about the median unsold portfolio company.

So who ends up holding the exposure that isn't clearing. Turn to the retail channel. Perpetual, evergreen private-market fund assets held by individual investors in the US grew from forty-six billion dollars at the end of 2014 to five hundred and five billion at the end of 2024. That is a twenty-seven percent annual rate over the decade, and it is observed data rather than a forecast.

Now the performance side of that growth, from PitchBook's comparison of fifteen large private-equity-focused evergreen funds. Their median 2025 return was just under twelve percent, against twenty-two point three percent for the MSCI All Country World Index. About half.

Be fair to that number before drawing a conclusion from it. Twenty twenty-five was an exceptional year for global public equities. One calendar year measured against a beta-heavy index is not a performance record for an illiquid strategy, and the sample's methodology isn't disclosed, so treat it as directional. But the durable point isn't that evergreens had a weak year. It's that these vehicles price daily or monthly off manager-reported net asset values, on exactly the kind of assets the institutional secondary market is currently routing around and re-pricing. Professional allocators are discounting those marks. The wealth channel keeps buying in at them, with no exit price other than the NAV the manager reports. That is a transfer of valuation risk from the people equipped to question it to the people who aren't.

And notice where the fee lands through all of it. Fee income accrues to the manager on the secondary process, on the continuation vehicle, and on the perpetual retail fund alike, regardless of what any of it clears at for the end investor. That's the wedge. Manager economics can look healthy while the funds underneath stay stuck, which is why the listed managers' shares and their limited partners' cash flows have been telling two different stories all year.

So where does that leave the cycle. Liquidity in private equity is increasingly manufactured by the general partner rather than earned through a sale. And the one number that would tell you whether that is a solution or a markdown, price paid against reported NAV, is the number nobody is disclosing. The nearest catalyst isn't October earnings season. It's whatever price the Blackstone process actually clears at. If it prints near NAV, that is genuine validation of real-estate marks at a top-tier manager, and I would say so plainly. If it prints at a meaningful discount, it is a real-time markdown applied to a real-estate strategy the manager reports at fifty-seven point seven billion dollars. Watch that first. Behind it, watch for any resolution of the Silver Lake-Workday take-private talks, still unconfirmed a month on. Then the third-quarter manager numbers in October.
