Good morning, it's the twenty-seventh of August. Here's what's moving in corporate credit and private markets.

On Sunday, this briefing cited a Fitch number as a genuine data point against the software-blowup story. Private credit default rates in technology software had fallen to one-point-two percent, down from two-point-three. New Federal Reserve data published this month says that same sector roughly doubled its use of deferred interest over roughly the same window. That contradiction is today's story.

The source is a Boston Fed study of business development company filings, published this August. We could not pull the paper itself, so the figures here come from reporting that quotes its language closely. It is built on nearly eight hundred ninety thousand company-quarter loan observations taken from SEC filings. It finds payment-in-kind usage across BDC portfolios climbing from roughly six percent to ten percent of loans since 2022. In software specifically, PIK usage doubled to thirteen percent between the end of 2022 and this past March. But the breadth data matter more than the software number. Construction PIK rose from under five percent to almost twenty. Wholesale trade and transportation both more than doubled. This is not a software story. It's a cross-sectoral drift toward deferring cash interest rather than collecting it.

Here's the argument. A default rate only captures events that get recognized as defaults. PIK lets a borrower stop paying cash interest without triggering one. Fitch's own second-quarter data showed something structurally similar on the other side of the ledger. More than half of that quarter's default events were maturity extensions, not missed payments or bankruptcies. So the deferral instrument has rotated. PIK on the coupon, extension on the maturity date, and the credit stays outstanding either way. Be careful with the juxtaposition. Fitch's index and the Fed's BDC sample cover different borrower populations over different windows, so this is suggestive, not an identity. But a falling default rate in a sector where deferred interest doubled is not evidence of health. It's evidence the measuring stick moved.

There's a second number in the same Boston Fed data that sharpens this. BDC median lending spreads compressed by about one percentage point over the past two years, even as PIK usage and borrower credit metrics were deteriorating. Lenders are being paid less in cash yield to absorb more deferral risk. That's the shape of a market where capital is chasing deal flow, not one where underwriting is tightening to match the risk.

And there's a mechanical consequence that shows up long before any loss is recognized. PIK interest counts as investment income even though no cash arrives. So a rising PIK share inflates reported net investment income while the cash actually collected weakens underneath it. This show ran the aggregate on Tuesday. Median dividend coverage across listed BDCs was point nine nine times in the first quarter, and point eight nine times once you strip PIK out. That's a distribution problem well before it's a credit-loss problem. The payout is the line that breaks first.

Now steelman the calm read, because the same Fed paper quantifies the bank-to-nonbank linkage directly, and on its own terms it's genuinely reassuring. Bank lending commitments to BDCs grew from about ten billion dollars in 2013 to more than fifty billion by 2025, with roughly thirty-five billion actually drawn. But banks hold senior secured claims on those BDCs, and the commitments are under two percent of large banks' Tier 1 capital. That's a small, structurally protected exposure, not a live systemic channel. Two more points for the defence. Median BDC spreads still run four to five percentage points over SOFR, against roughly two points for high-yield term loans, so the absolute compensation is real even after a point of compression. And rising PIK could partly reflect deal terms drifting PIK-friendly across the market regardless of borrower health. The Fed infers cash-flow strain from the breadth of the pattern. Breadth is an inference, not a proof.

Here's why that reassurance is incomplete rather than wrong. BDCs are the part of private credit that files with the SEC. The Fed's own language says rising deferrals, compressing spreads and concentrated exposures likely exist across the less transparent private credit market beyond BDCs, the part it can't see. So ten percent PIK is a floor on market-wide prevalence, not an estimate of it. The visible tail of the market is deferring more and getting paid less for it. There's no reason to assume the invisible majority is doing better.

Follow that transparency question to where the money actually sits. US life insurers held eight hundred seven billion dollars of private illiquid bonds at year-end 2025, up a hundred twenty-two billion in a single year. And AM Best data show the balance sheets absorbing that growth are getting weaker. Roughly a third of annuity reserves now sit at ninety-five companies whose long-term credit rating is lower than it was in 2007. On a weighted basis, reserves have shifted toward companies rated nearly two notches lower. Then layer on the funding side. Federal Home Loan Bank advances to insurers reached a hundred seventy-seven-point-nine billion dollars in 2025, and insurance's share of total FHLB borrowing rose to twenty-six percent from seventeen percent in 2022. The sourcing doesn't establish that those advances are funding private credit specifically rather than ordinary balance-sheet management, so treat that link as contextual. The combination is what matters. A government-sponsored liquidity line is a growing share of the funding stack, at insurers whose average rating is drifting down, while their private illiquid holdings keep growing. That's a slower-moving version of the same recognition problem. Stress there surfaces as a reserve change or a ratings action, not as a price.

Now the other side of it, where new capital is actually being put to work. Eagle Point Credit Management, a roughly fourteen-billion-dollar manager, just closed a one-point-three billion dollar mezzanine tranche inside a sixteen-billion-dollar project financing an Anthropic-anchored data center in Texas. That sixteen billion is the whole package, not the private credit piece. Morgan Stanley leads the senior bank debt, which Google has guaranteed. Private credit takes the layer beneath that, unguaranteed. The facility started as a hundred-fifty-million-dollar target last September and grew roughly nine times over in under a year as Anthropic was confirmed as anchor tenant. No source discloses the spread or coupon on that mezzanine piece, and that absence is the point. The one number that would tell you whether the subordination is being paid for is the one nobody published. What private credit is underwriting here is one lab's compute demand, not the building. And if this bank-senior, private-mezzanine template repeats across the AI capex wave, expect the same spread compression visible in the BDC data to show up here too.

Last, a partial answer to a catalyst this show has flagged all week. Did the non-traded BDC redemption queue roll into the third quarter. The first three NAV BDCs to report show repurchase requests at four-point-six percent of NAV, down from seven-point-nine percent for those same funds in the second quarter. Three funds is not a trend, and the first to report are plausibly the ones with the better news.

So where does the cycle actually stand. The binding risk isn't default frequency. It's recognition timing and disclosure. PIK and maturity extensions are postponing the events that would force a mark. Spreads are compressing rather than compensating for that deferral. And the risk keeps migrating toward balance sheets that disclose more slowly than the BDCs the Fed can actually study, from insurance reserves to unguaranteed AI mezzanine.
