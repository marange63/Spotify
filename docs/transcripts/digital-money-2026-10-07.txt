Good morning. It's October 7th, 2026, and this is your digital money briefing.

Here's the thesis. The institutional layer of digital assets is sitting at breakeven, not expanding. Bitcoin is pinned near the price where its largest holders get their money back. The on-chain dollar stack has stopped growing, even as its usage falls. And the rulebook meant to replace stalled legislation is now being challenged in court.

Start with Bitcoin. It traded at about $86,300 on the morning of October 6th, up modestly from the day before. The on-chain analytics firm Glassnode has flagged that area as a breakeven cluster for long-term holders and ETF buyers alike. Roughly 1.07 million bitcoin was accumulated between $83,000 and $86,000, almost entirely by long-term holders. Glassnode also put the US spot ETF complex's breakeven right at the top of that band. We've tracked this band since late September. What's new is that it isn't just a chart pattern. It's where the largest institutional cohort gets whole.

A word of caution on dating. Glassnode's report was published September 9th, roughly a month before this price point. At that time, corporate-treasury holders broke even near $80,500, just below where bitcoin traded that day. The same report estimated the ETF complex's paper loss at about $3.9 billion, the closest it had been to breakeven since January. Bitcoin has since traded higher, so that loss is likely smaller now, and possibly a small gain. Treat these as month-old structural levels, not live readings.

The ETF flow picture explains why that cohort matters. Combined bitcoin ETF assets are $109.3 billion, about fifteen percent below the mid-January peak. Most of that drawdown is price, not redemptions. Full-year net flows are only around a billion dollars, despite a strong third quarter. In other words, the money that piled in early in the year has spent most of 2026 underwater and is only now getting back to flat. Early October offered a partial bright spot. Funds took in nearly $190 million on October 2nd, led by BlackRock's IBIT, recovering a late-September outflow. We have no verified daily flow data since then. So the ETF bid isn't bleeding, but it isn't building momentum either. It's a cohort nursing a year-long position back to even.

Now the macro trigger. The September jobs report, released October 2nd, showed payrolls up just 29,000 against a consensus of 84,000. That pushed market-implied odds of a Fed hold at the October 28th meeting to roughly 82 percent, from 36 percent a week earlier. The decision hasn't happened, so treat that purely as pricing. And note what actually moved. The market priced out a hike. It did not price in a cut. Sygnum Bank's chief investment officer drew the right boundary: a weak print strengthens the no-hike scenario, but weak is not automatically bullish. Yesterday we also noted that our Capital Markets Radar desk saw the ten-year yield rise in the days after that print. So the dovish relief may be more fragile than the crypto press suggests.

On the mechanics, one popular framing treats the short-liquidation shelf between $82,000 and $86,000 as a ceiling. That gets it backwards. Short-liquidation levels above spot are squeeze fuel for a rally, not resistance holding it down. Bitcoin has already pushed through most of that zone, and briefly above $87,000 on jobs day. So part of that spike may simply have been short sellers getting forced out. That's our inference, not a measured fact.

Put the bull and bear case side by side. The bear case is that three cohorts clustered near breakeven are a wall of potential sellers looking to exit at flat. The bull case is the mirror image. Breakeven holders aren't forced sellers, so there's little distressed supply below spot. And a clean close above $86,000 would put the largest institutional holders back in profit after months underwater. Watch whether bitcoin can hold a daily close above that level. That's the real test of whether this band is a floor or a ceiling.

If the bitcoin bid is sitting at breakeven, the on-chain dollar layer is sitting at a standstill. This answers the question we left open yesterday, when we had no verified early-October total. Total stablecoin market cap is now $295.55 billion, up less than one percent over the trailing thirty days. Tether is about $183 billion of that and USDC about $73 billion, so USDC is roughly a quarter of the market. That's a single snapshot, so it tells us nothing yet about whether USDC is gaining share. But supply isn't collapsing, as some recent commentary claimed.

Usage is a different story. Monthly on-chain transfer volume fell about 23 percent over that same thirty-day window. So the float is steady while the activity running across it shrinks. The cause isn't established. The window spans quarter-end, and lower DeFi churn could explain more of it than weaker payments. Still, supply and usage now tell two different stories. Issuer reserve buying of Treasury bills tracks supply, not usage. A flat float means that channel holds, but it adds no new T-bill demand. Meanwhile the case that stablecoins are taking a growing share of real payment activity gets weaker. So the takeaway is simple: don't let a flat or rising market cap stand in for adoption, because the usage data says otherwise.

The other half of on-chain cash tells a similar story. Tokenized Treasury products now total $15 billion, down about five and a half percent over thirty days. Nothing in hand explains why. The category leader has also changed. Circle's USYC and Ondo's USDY have both overtaken BlackRock's BUIDL, and all three now sit within about $150 million of each other. So this is a snapshot, not a rout. One large redemption could reshuffle the order again.

Still, it complicates the "BlackRock legitimizes the category" narrative. The bull reading is that a multi-issuer market is what maturation looks like. The bear reading is that several issuers are now competing on fees and yield for a pool that isn't growing, which pressures everyone's economics. Either way, Circle is the common thread. It runs the number-two stablecoin and now the largest tokenized Treasury fund. On October 2nd we framed Circle's earnings as float times yield. With the float flat, the short rate does the work, and the market has priced out a hike rather than priced in a cut. For now, that rate path holds.

That slowdown in on-chain activity is the context for Coinbase, the main listed read-through. On October 1st, Coinbase finished migrating its International Exchange derivatives clients onto Deribit, consolidating rather than competing head-on. Five days later, it launched live crypto price prediction markets for US users, with contracts settling in windows as short as fifteen minutes. There's no usage or revenue data for that product yet. The sell side liked what it saw. Wells Fargo initiated coverage with a $200 target, and BofA raised its target to $203 two days later, both per secondary reporting. Those targets are sentiment, not evidence. The real signal is strategic. Revenue tied to throughput weakens faster than revenue tied to float when activity slides, and Coinbase is adding higher-frequency products exactly as on-chain transfers fall.

Last, the rules all of this depends on are themselves contested. The Senate's CLARITY Act failed cloture 49 to 50 back on September 15th, leaving regulators to fill the gap on their own. That agency route has now drawn a legal challenge. On October 2nd, the Independent Community Bankers of America sued the Comptroller of the Currency in federal court in Washington. The suit challenges the national trust-bank chartering framework, including the charter of crypto custodian Protego, on statutory-authority grounds. It's a filed complaint, not a ruling, and there's no sign of operational disruption. The motive is partly a turf fight, community banks defending their franchise against non-depository competitors. But consider the scenario if the challenge succeeded. Crypto custodians would be pushed toward state charters, raising costs and fragmenting the custody layer that ETFs and institutions rely on. Durability risk has moved from Congress to the courts.

Meanwhile, the stablecoin clock keeps ticking. Comments on Treasury's proposed issuer-eligibility rule close October 19th. And with no final rules scheduled, the GENIUS Act takes effect no later than January 18th, 2027.

Put it together. Three cohorts near breakeven, an on-chain dollar stack that stopped growing, and a rulebook now in litigation. Watch for a daily close above $86,000 in bitcoin, the next round of ETF flow data, the October 19th comment deadline, and the October 28th Fed decision, which has not yet happened.
