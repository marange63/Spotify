Semiconductors & Compute Infrastructure

Good morning. It's Sunday, July 26th, 2026, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

Memory has been the pacing signal for AI compute all week, and today the story is that the scarcity is no longer contained to high-bandwidth memory. It has spilled into commodity DRAM and NAND, and that changes who pays for the AI buildout.

Start with the number that carries it. Contract prices for conventional DRAM are projected to rise thirteen to eighteen percent quarter over quarter in the third quarter of 2026, with NAND flash contract prices up another ten to fifteen percent. Those are quoted industry figures for contract pricing, not headline spot-price claims, and they matter because they are the input costs baked into every laptop, phone, and server shipped this fall. Layer on top of that Micron's own disclosure that its HBM supply remains fully allocated for all of calendar 2026, covering both HBM3E and the newer HBM4. Micron is guiding fiscal third-quarter revenue near thirty-three and a half billion dollars and gross margin around eighty-one percent, both tracking ahead of prior expectations. That's guidance, not audited results, but the direction is unambiguous.

So the memory makers are not just sold out on the exotic AI-specific product. They are now pulling capacity away from ordinary DRAM and NAND to feed HBM, and that's tightening the commodity side too. The so-what here is that AI infrastructure demand is starting to look constrained by memory economics as much as by GPU availability. That is the distinction that matters most for anyone modeling the buildout. Compute-constrained demand is gated by how many accelerators a fab can pack out. Economics-constrained demand is gated by whether the total bill-of-materials still pencils once memory prices have moved this far. This week the constraint visibly shifted toward the second kind, and it is now showing up in consumer bill-of-materials, not just hyperscaler capex budgets.

Follow that transmission channel one step further, because it is the part the market tends to underprice. DRAM and NAND are not a rounding error in a PC or a phone. They are one of the largest line items after the main processor. So a fifteen-percent contract-price move lands directly on the margins of the box makers, Dell and HP in PCs, Apple and Samsung's own handset division in phones. Those companies face a familiar choice: eat the cost and compress margin, or pass it through and test how much price the end customer will bear into a soft consumer-electronics cycle. Either way, this is a clean AI-capex-to-consumer transmission channel. The compute boom that started as a data-center story is now quietly taxing the cost structure of every device that ships with memory in it.

There's a subtler second-order effect on the AI-server side too. If commodity memory margins now rival what the makers earn on HBM, the incentive to convert every possible wafer to HBM weakens. That's the uncomfortable possibility underneath the bullish narrative: memory makers could rationally under-invest in HBM capacity relative to what hyperscalers are assuming, precisely because ordinary DRAM has become lucrative enough to keep making. If that happens, the shortage lasts longer than the demand-side models assume, not because demand is stronger but because supply is deliberately slower to convert. Take the sharpest version of that claim with real caution, though. The reporting that SK hynix is deliberately slow-walking HBM4 conversion to harvest elevated DDR5 margins is single-source trade reporting, not something SK hynix has confirmed. Treat it as a plausible incentive the pricing data is consistent with, not as an established fact.

That scarcity is now pulling capital into the tools that build memory, and the equipment makers' own numbers back that up. Applied Materials' chief executive told investors the company now expects its semiconductor-equipment business to grow more than thirty percent in calendar 2026, an upward revision from prior guidance. The industry association SEMI projects worldwide fab-equipment spending will rise eighteen percent to one hundred thirty-three billion dollars in 2026.

Here's the correction worth making explicitly, because the tempting read is wrong. This is not a story of memory equipment overtaking logic equipment. SEMI's own multi-year breakout has Logic and Micro leading total investment at two hundred twenty-eight billion dollars from 2027 through 2029, with Memory second at one hundred seventy-five billion over that same window. Applied Materials itself attributes its growth to leading-edge foundry logic, DRAM, and advanced packaging together, not to memory pulling ahead of logic. So the more defensible read is that capital spending is broadening across the whole equipment order book at once, logic and memory rising together, rather than capital rotating out of logic and into memory. That is arguably a less dramatic story than a reversal, but a more durable one. It means ASML, Applied Materials, Lam Research, KLA, and Tokyo Electron are all seeing order books swell across their full customer base, not just one segment. A broad-based order book is harder to unwind than a single hot segment, which is exactly why it's the more bullish structural signal, even if it makes for a duller headline.

The clearest proof that capital is chasing this scarcity up the supply chain sits with ASML. It booked a record six-point-eight-six-billion-euro order from SK hynix tied to that company's Nasdaq listing, with its EUV backlog now stretching into 2028. That order composition, EUV versus older tools, hasn't been independently confirmed, so hold it as a headline order value rather than a verified EUV-only figure. But the value itself is real, and it shows memory capacity expansion is now a top-of-book priority for the industry's most critical toolmaker. When the company that gates the entire leading edge is taking record orders from a memory maker, the scarcity is being answered with committed capital, not just quoted prices.

Which brings us to the capital-markets bookend. SK hynix completed a US listing on Nasdaq this month, raising twenty-six and a half billion dollars, the largest-ever US market debut by a non-American company. Worth being precise: this is a dual listing, since SK hynix has traded in Seoul since 1996, not a first-time initial public offering. What the listing gives the company is a dollar-denominated capital vehicle, one it can tap to fund HBM4 and HBM4E capacity without leaning entirely on Korean-won financing. It also drops SK hynix onto the same exchange as Micron and, in time, invites direct comparison with Samsung's memory business, which sharpens investor scrutiny of who is actually winning HBM share.

On the day of that listing, chief executive Kwak Noh-jung said the memory industry faces what he called the worst supply year in the industry's history, referring to 2027. Hear that in context. It is a forward-looking statement from a chief executive raising capital and heading into pricing negotiations with Nvidia and other HBM customers. So treat it as guidance and incentive, not as an audited forecast. Still, the fact that SK hynix is raising this much dollar capital right now tells you the company is willing to bet its own balance sheet on the shortage being real and durable.

So where does that leave the skeptical read. What's quoted and near-term, the third-quarter contract-price guidance and Micron's fully allocated HBM book, is solid ground. What's further out, a shortage running through 2027 and demand outstripping supply beyond 2030, is management opinion offered at a moment when talking up scarcity serves the speaker's negotiating position. The gap between those two things is where the risk lives. The first real test of whether the market is pricing durable structural scarcity or a cyclical peak comes fast. SK hynix reports again on July 29th and Samsung follows on July 30th. Those prints will show whether the pricing power behind this week's numbers is actually landing in the ledger, or whether expectations have run ahead of it.
