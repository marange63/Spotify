Good morning, it's Friday, October 9th, and this is your private equity briefing.

The question this week is who actually sets the price when a sponsor sells, because too much recent liquidity has been priced by insiders. That means continuation vehicles, co-sponsors, and counterparties with pre-negotiated purchase rights. This week gives us two genuinely arm's-length answers. But look closely at who the arm's-length buyers are, because that's the real story.

Start with Boots. Sycamore Partners and the Pessina family are selling Boots' UK and Ireland retail business, Boots Opticians, and No7 Beauty Company to Wittington Investments, the Weston family's Canadian holding company. The insurer Fairfax Financial is co-investing. Wittington takes operational control; Fairfax's stake size isn't disclosed. The total purchase consideration is 8.9 billion dollars, and Wittington's own announcement is explicit that this figure includes assumed debt. Sycamore and the Pessina family are keeping two other pieces of the old Boots group, Farmacias Benavides and Alliance Healthcare Deutschland. Completion is expected in the first quarter of 2027, pending regulatory approval. So this is signed, not closed, and nobody has cash yet.

For scale, Sycamore's take-private of Walgreens Boots Alliance last year was valued at up to 23.7 billion dollars including debt, so Boots at 8.9 billion is a meaningful but partial slice. It is not Sycamore recouping the whole buyout with one asset.

And the disclosure stops there. No Boots-specific EBITDA or multiple is disclosed, and there's no use-of-proceeds language either. So we don't know whether this cash repays acquisition debt from last year's buyout or flows out to Sycamore's limited partners as a distribution. Both readings stay live. One is a fast, opportunistic monetization of the best piece of the deal, which shortens the hold on the prize asset. The other is balance-sheet repair inside a still heavily levered take-private, which leaves the remaining stub carrying the risk. The so-what: the buyer here is genuinely independent, family and insurance capital rather than another sponsor or the public market, but a signature is not a check, and we still don't know where the money's going.

If Boots tells you who's buying, Clarion tells you what a strategic will pay that an auction wouldn't. Blackstone ran a formal sale process for its events business, Clarion Events, last year, targeting roughly 2 billion pounds. According to people familiar with the matter, it failed to find a buyer at that valuation and was shelved. Now Informa has agreed to buy Clarion for 2.24 billion pounds, about 3 billion dollars, including debt. That's 11.1 times Clarion's expected 2027 EBITDA. Flag that as a forward multiple, which flatters it against a trailing number. The uplift from the 2025 target isn't perfectly clean either, because we don't know whether that 2-billion-pound target was quoted on the same debt-inclusive basis. And there's a less flattering explanation for the higher price: one buyer with specific synergies, which tells you little about what a financial buyer would pay for a comparable asset. Still, a strategic found a price the auction didn't.

And here's the turn that matters more than the headline number. Informa isn't just writing a check. It's funding the deal partly by selling about 940 million pounds of new equity in a share placing, and it's pausing its own buyback to redirect the cash. So the exit valve for this private equity sale runs straight through a public company's balance sheet. Informa's own shareholders are effectively financing Blackstone's exit, and getting diluted in the process. The so-what: this strategic-buyer channel only works as long as acquirers' own equity currency holds up and their boards are willing to dilute. If public valuations for serial acquirers like Informa weaken, that valve narrows fast.

Put Boots and Clarion together and you get a dispersion story. Our read is that assets with an obvious strategic or permanent-capital owner — a family holding company, an insurer, an events consolidator — can find a real, arm's-length price and get marked against it. Assets without a natural buyer in that lane don't get that benefit. They sit on the shelf, and their marks have to answer to a buyer pool that may simply not be there. That's the clearest lens for the week's two biggest exits. It sharpens, rather than overturns, the case that exit liquidity is concentrating in the assets that already had the most obvious owners.

Now the same squeeze from the other end — the entry price. CVC Capital Partners and Groupe Bruxelles Lambert are trying to take Italian pharma company Recordati private. This week they raised their bid and said the new price will not be raised again, putting the maximum outlay at 10.52 billion euros, or about 53 euros a share by our math. The push came after Recordati's independent directors and activist investor Palliser Capital objected to the original price. Palliser had wanted 60 euros a share, well above where CVC and GBL landed. Rossini, the controlling shareholder, has agreed to tender its 46.82 percent stake. Whether the raised bid clears is still unresolved. The tender hasn't closed, and we don't know whether the independent directors who objected now support this price.

Two readings compete here, and the evidence doesn't let us pick one. Either public-market discipline is reasserting itself against sponsor pricing, forcing CVC to pay up for a take-private the way Informa paid up for an exit. Or Rossini's near-majority commitment gave the sponsors enough certainty that bumping the price once was simply cheaper than fighting on, which would mean the activist's real leverage was thin all along. Either way, the so-what is the same. If strategics are setting exit prices from above and minority holders are pushing entry prices up from below, the spread that sponsor value creation has to bridge is getting squeezed from both ends at once.

So where does the cycle actually stand heading into the weekend? Arm's-length exits are clearing this week, but only where a strategic or permanent-capital buyer with its own reason to own the asset exists. Boots had the Westons and Fairfax; Clarion had Informa. Neither deal is cash yet. Both are signed, not closed, Informa still has to complete its placing, and Recordati isn't even settled. For limited partners, none of this is a distribution yet. And if Sycamore uses the Boots proceeds to pay down buyout debt, its LPs may never see the Boots sale show up in their DPI at all. The real test is what happens to assets with no natural strategic owner waiting in the wings.

That's why Gigamon is worth watching. Elliott and Siris are reportedly exploring a sale of the infrastructure software firm above 2 billion dollars, nearly a decade after Elliott's original 1.6 billion dollar take-private. That's an early, unconfirmed process, sourced to people familiar with the matter rather than the firms themselves. And the headline uplift over nine years says nothing on its own about the actual return once leverage and distributions are counted. But it's the next read on whether a stuck software vintage can clear the way Boots and Clarion just did. Beyond that, watch the Recordati tender result and Boots' regulatory progress toward that first-quarter 2027 close. And when the big managers report third-quarter earnings in the coming weeks, read them for one number: realized carried interest against fee-related earnings. If realizations aren't rising, the exit thaw still isn't reaching LPs as cash.

That's the briefing for October 9th. Have a good weekend.
