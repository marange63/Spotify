Good morning, it's Friday, August 7th, and this is your Cautious Optimism capital markets briefing.

For four straight sessions this show has told you the same thing: Friday's jobs report is the catalyst that tests every leg of this trade. This morning it landed, at 8:30 Eastern. As of this taping the confirmed headline print and the market's immediate reaction aren't yet locked down cleanly enough to hand you a number I'd stand behind, so I'm not going to invent one. What I can give you is the setup you need to interpret it the moment you see it, because that setup is the real story.

Start with the Federal Reserve. On July 29th the FOMC held its target range at 3.50 to 3.75 percent, but the vote was 9 to 3, and the three dissents were not the kind you usually see. Beth Hammack, Neel Kashkari, and Lorie Logan all wanted a quarter-point hike, not a cut. The Fed's own statement said inflation remains elevated relative to its 2 percent goal, in part reflecting supply shocks in certain sectors, including energy. That's a Fed worried about inflation at the exact moment three of its own members are demanding tighter policy. Now set that against the labor market. ADP's July report, out August 5th, showed private payrolls up just 44,000, badly missing the 75,000 consensus and the weakest print of the year, a sharp step down from June's revised 95,000. Wall Street's own forecasts for this morning's official number were already scattered before the tape even printed: the Dow Jones consensus centered on roughly 83,000 payrolls with unemployment steady at 4.2 percent, but Bank of America was looking for just 80,000, and Vanguard was down at 18,000. That's not a market with a shared view. That's a market genuinely unsure whether the labor side is cracking.

Here's why that dispersion matters more than any single forecast. A Fed that just produced a rare hawkish dissent over energy-driven inflation is now facing a jobs picture that even sophisticated desks can't agree on. If the print comes in soft, alongside the sticky detail buried inside that same ADP report -- job-switcher pay growth ran 7 percent year-over-year, the fastest since last August -- you don't get a clean dovish signal. You get a stagflationary bind: growth cooling while a piece of the inflation picture stays hot. That combination re-prices term premium higher even as growth expectations fall, which is bad for duration and risk assets at the same time, not one or the other. Don't let a weak headline number talk you into an automatic rate-cut trade this morning. Read the wage-growth internals first.

Now, the swing variable that could resolve part of that Fed tension sits eight time zones away, in the Strait of Hormuz. Iran and Oman this week reached what Tehran's deputy foreign minister called an agreement in principle: a 60-day, potentially extendable framework where inbound tankers move through Iranian waters and outbound ships use Omani waters with Tehran's approval. Roughly a quarter of global seaborne oil trade transits that strait, so this is not a regional footnote. The market reaction has been genuinely volatile. The S&P 500 hit a fresh record on August 3rd, up 1.79 percent to 7,736, on hopes the strait would fully reopen. Three days later, Iran said the deal won't fully reopen the strait after all, and Brent crude surged 3.83 percent to $82.49 in a single session. Here's the part that should catch your attention: the S&P barely moved, down just 0.18 percent, and the VIX actually fell, dropping over 4 percent to 15.15. Gold, meanwhile, pushed to a seven-week high near $4,286 an ounce.

Now watch the bond market inside that same session, because it tells you something equities didn't. On the day oil jumped almost 4 percent and gold ran to a seven-week high, the 10-year Treasury yield didn't fall the way a clean safe-haven scramble would demand. It rose, about 5 basis points, to 4.67 percent. So the biggest, most liquid market on the planet met a supply-shock oil move by pushing yields up, not down. That is the term-premium reflex, not the flight-to-quality reflex, and it fits the stagflation read from the jobs setup: the marginal worry priced into duration this week is sticky inflation, not slowing growth.

So equity investors are treating a real supply-shock geopolitical event as an oil-and-gold story, not a broad risk-off trigger. That's either the correct read, because the framework genuinely caps the downside even if implementation is messy, or it's complacency, because a deal that Iran itself says won't fully reopen the strait still carries real collapse risk -- it needs sign-off from shipping insurers and U.S. sanctions authorities, and the prior June ceasefire fell apart within weeks. The reason this connects directly back to the Fed: a durable Hormuz de-escalation would be disinflationary for energy, and it would remove the specific supply-shock argument the Fed itself cited in July. Watch this as a live input into the September policy debate, not a side story.

That brings us to the layer with the least room for error: credit. High-yield spreads were quoted at 275 basis points as of August 5th, described by credit-data provider Convex as sitting in the richest decile of history, against a long-run median near 450 basis points. Translate that: credit is pricing close to zero probability of a growth shock, at the exact moment a jobs report just landed and a war-risk oil shock is still live. With only 275 basis points of cushion, the convexity math is asymmetric. A weak print or a Hormuz-deal collapse has far more room to blow spreads wider than spreads have room to tighten further from here. Credit is the market with the least insurance against being wrong this week.

One more data point for that same complacency picture, on the crypto side. Spot Bitcoin ETFs pulled in $626 million in net inflows over the first three trading days of August, a sharp reversal from July, which saw persistent bleeding, including $265 million out the door on the final session alone. Both Bitcoin and Ethereum opened at their highest levels of the week on August 6th, and the reporting tied those gains to the same two catalysts moving everything else -- the Hormuz negotiations and that soft ADP print. Keep the level in perspective, though: Bitcoin was still trading in the mid-60-thousands, roughly 43 percent below its October 2025 record above 126,000, so this is a bounce inside a long drawdown, not a new regime. And notice the tell in how it moved. Bitcoin traded this week like a liquidity-and-rate beta, in the same direction as equities and gold on the same news, not like an independent hedge against this specific energy-inflation risk. Gold is doing that hedging job right now. Bitcoin is riding the same wave as stocks.

Put it together and you get a market where nearly every asset class is priced for calm heading into a morning that was supposed to resolve the calm one way or the other. Tight credit, a pinned VIX, record equities, and a crypto complex flipping back to inflows, all just ahead of a jobs report with genuinely disputed forecasts and a Fed that's already split over what a soft number would even mean. Go find this morning's actual payrolls print before you trade anything today, and when you do, check the wage internals and the revisions, not just the headline. That's where this story actually resolves.

That's your capital markets radar for August 7th. Trade safe, and I'll see you tomorrow.
