Good morning, and welcome to Cautious Optimism Briefings for Tuesday, October 6th, 2026.

Today is two deals, and both make the same bet. Durable value in enterprise software sits in the system of record that AI and agents write into. In both cases, the bull case rests on the hardest number to deliver.

Our lead is Schneider Electric's agreement to buy PTC, announced on October 5. The terms come from the deal announcement that PTC filed with the SEC as an exhibit to an 8-K. PTC makes computer-aided design and product lifecycle software, the digital thread that links engineering decisions to the physical product. PTC shareholders receive 205 dollars a share in cash. That implies an enterprise value of 23.7 billion dollars.

The premium is 42.3 percent to PTC's last closing price. That is a large premium for a mature software asset. But the multiple matters more. Schneider prices the deal at 21 times PTC's 2027 estimated adjusted EBITA. With full run-rate synergies, that falls to 13 times. So the price only looks sensible if the synergies arrive.

Let's look at those synergies. Schneider expects 250 million euros of cost synergies within three years. It also expects about 250 million euros of one-time implementation costs to get there. The cost savings are real, but modest against a deal of this size. The larger and less certain piece is 800 million euros of revenue synergies, from cross-selling software into complementary customer bases. The cost line is the part Schneider controls. The revenue line depends on customers. So this is a cross-selling bet more than a cost deal. Revenue synergies are the category acquirers most often fail to deliver. So the first question for this deal is not what PTC is worth today. It is whether Schneider's hardware customers will buy PTC's software at scale.

The financing is the second pressure point. Press reports put the equity at 5 to 6 billion euros, raised through an accelerated bookbuild, and senior debt at 16 to 17 billion euros across several currencies. That is roughly three euros of debt for every one of equity. The primary filing does not restate those financing figures, so treat them as press-reported. Against that, Schneider says it expects to retain its Category A credit ratings. That is the company's own expectation, not a rating agency's view. No agency has yet spoken either way. So the leverage is a stated risk, not a proven strain, until S&P or Moody's weighs in.

Then there is time. Closing is anticipated by the third quarter of 2027. The deal requires approval from a majority of PTC's outstanding shares, plus regulatory clearance. That leaves roughly a year of limbo. Rivals such as Siemens and Dassault Systèmes can use that year to work on PTC's customer base, and PTC's own sales teams have a year of uncertainty to manage. Long closes also give customers a reason to delay renewals until they see the combined roadmap. That is our inference, not something either company has said.

Now the strategic read. Schneider says the deal lifts its Software and Services business to an estimated 24 percent of group revenue on a pro forma basis. So software becomes a stated share of a hardware-led group, which is a structural change in how Schneider presents itself. Our read is that AI-era value accrues to the systems of record that touch physical operations. The competing reading is more defensive. Schneider may be buying a moat against AI-native design tools, not a growth engine. The announcement guides PTC's revenue and ARR to grow about 10 percent a year through 2029. A buyer paying 21 times forward adjusted EBITA for growth of that size is betting on synergies, not on the standalone trajectory. The first sign the premium was overpaid would be synergy slippage, and that will show up well before the margins do.

The bigger signal is who the buyer is. Schneider is not a software company or a financial sponsor. It is an operational technology incumbent buying the design and lifecycle layer. That fits the wider software deal market. Private equity firms say software businesses are hard to sell right now, and exits are reviving mainly where a strategic buyer wants the asset. In private credit, redemption requests are rising at a technology-focused lending fund, as investors price software risk before the fund's valuations catch up. A strategic buyer paying cash for a software asset tied to physical operations fits that pattern. The takeaway is that software is clearing when someone with an operating reason to own it shows up, while the financial buyers sit on the sidelines.

PTC is the system of record for physical products. One layer down, the system of record for software is the database an agent creates. That brings us to Supabase.

Supabase, the open-source Postgres development platform, announced 150 million dollars of new funding on October 2. GIC led the round, with CapitalG, IronArc and SquarePeg participating. The company also agreed to acquire Turso, a database infrastructure company. Supabase says it adds more than 1 million users and 4 million databases a month. It also says about 70 percent of new databases are now created by agents or AI-driven tools. Those are company-stated figures, relayed by the press. The company has not said how it defines a user or a database, so read the numbers as its own framing.

Neither the Turso purchase price nor the round valuation was disclosed. So we do not know what Supabase paid for Turso, or what investors think Supabase is worth. Without either number, the round tells us about investor appetite, not price discipline.

MongoDB, Databricks, Snowflake and the Postgres platforms are all competing to be the database an agent reaches for first. Supabase now puts a vendor-stated number on that contest. The prize is being the default that coding agents provision without a human making the choice.

The skeptical read is about what a creation actually is. A database created by an agent may be ephemeral. It may be free-tier scaffolding, or a throwaway test environment that nobody ever pays for. So a 70 percent share of creations tells us about agent behavior. It says nothing about paid usage, retention, or revenue. Creation counts are not active databases, and they are not paying databases. That is the same gap between user counts and active engagement that we apply to every enterprise platform.

Turso is the more revealing move. Turso is built to run very large numbers of isolated, low-cost databases, in public cloud and in bring-your-own-cloud environments. If databases are being created at 4 million a month, mostly by agents, the cost per database becomes the economics that matter. Buying that capability is a unit-cost move, not a feature grab. That points toward pricing that meters per database or per tenant, rather than per instance, in managed Postgres and serverless database products. It also suggests that hyperscaler managed databases, such as Aurora, Cloud SQL and Azure Database, may not have been built around a provisioning pattern this granular. That is an inference, not a reported finding. Put simply, the managed-database price list is the next place to watch for evidence that agents are changing the economics of data infrastructure.

So here is the close. In both deals, the asset is the system of record. The unproven number is monetization. At Schneider, it is the 800 million euros of cross-sell revenue. At Supabase, it is paying, retained databases. Two markers to watch. The first is the PTC shareholder vote and the regulatory path toward a close by the third quarter of 2027. The second is whether Supabase discloses paid or retained database metrics, which would finally tell us whether agent-created databases are a revenue line or only a traffic line.

That's our briefing for today. Thanks for listening.
