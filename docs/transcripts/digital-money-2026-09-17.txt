Good morning, it's Thursday, September 17th, and this is your digital money briefing.

Let's open on the decision this program refused to call for three straight days. The Federal Reserve raised the federal funds target range by a quarter point yesterday afternoon, to 3.75 to 4 percent. That is the first hike since 2023, and the vote was unanimous, 12 to nothing. It is also the exact outcome prediction markets had been pricing at roughly 88 percent odds going into the meeting, the number this show quoted on Tuesday.

Here's the problem. On Monday and Tuesday this program argued that an implied hike in the mid-to-high eighties meant most of the damage was already in the price, and that the real asymmetric surprise sat on the low-probability side, a hold. That call was wrong. Bitcoin printed roughly seventy-five thousand dollars after the decision, its lowest level since August 21st. A second round of leveraged liquidations, on the order of a hundred and forty million dollars, hit the market anyway. Handle that liquidation figure as exchange-aggregator data rather than venue disclosure. And the sourcing can't cleanly separate how much of that second wave was the Fed versus a continuation of Tuesday's deleveraging after the CLARITY Act died in the Senate. Treat the two catalysts as compounding, not as fully separable. What isn't in doubt is that the hike landed as priced and the market fell regardless.

So what actually repriced? The path, not the level. The Fed's own September projections put a number on it. The median federal funds rate projection is 4.1 percent for the end of 2026, and 4.1 percent again for the end of 2027. Both sit above the range just delivered. Inside the 2026 distribution, twelve participants cluster a full quarter-point above where policy now sits, and four sit higher still. So a majority of the committee is signaling at least one more hike this year, on top of the one that just landed.

Underneath that, the ten-year Treasury yield pushed above 5 percent. Be careful with the superlative there. This week's sourcing dates it as the first break above 5 since November 2023, while a separate read the same week put the intraday touch fractionally higher and called it the highest since 2007. Take the direction, not the record. The point is that the real discount rate repriced even though the policy rate matched consensus exactly. A hike that's priced only means the number is priced. The trajectory behind it is a separate bet, and that's the bet the market lost.

That distinction matters more than it sounds, because the same decision hits this sector in opposite directions on two different parts of the curve. A higher front end raises reserve income per dollar of stablecoin float. That is the argument this show made yesterday for why a hike helps Circle and Coinbase rather than hurting them. A higher long real yield does something else entirely. It raises the opportunity cost of holding an asset that pays you nothing, which is Bitcoin. Same decision, opposite signs, different tenors. It also pulls dollars around inside the stack, from the non-yielding wrapper toward tokenized Treasury and on-chain money-market products that now pay more.

As for whether the tape agreed with that reserve-income claim, I'm calling that test open. There is no clean, dated post-FOMC close for Coinbase or Circle that separates the rate decision from Tuesday's legislative selloff. And the sourcing on Tuesday itself conflicts. The figures this show used yesterday, declines of roughly ten and eleven and a half percent, come from one tracker. Another puts the same session nearer seven and eight. Rather than guess, I'll wait for a clean print.

What isn't ambiguous is the flow picture. US spot Bitcoin ETFs saw about four hundred and fifty million dollars of net redemptions on the CLARITY vote session, and roughly two hundred and ninety-six million more on the FOMC session itself, led again by BlackRock's IBIT. That's about seven hundred and forty-six million pulled over two days, on aggregator data rather than issuer disclosure. For scale, this show reported September's month-to-date net inflow at plus four hundred and sixty-seven million on Tuesday. Two sessions erased it. September's ETF demand signal is now negative, not merely thinner.

Then there's what the hike costs Bitcoin's own story. Late in August, Bitcoin's correlation with gold hit its highest level since 2020, while its correlation with the S&P 500 collapsed toward zero. The precise coefficient is genuinely disputed across providers, so I won't quote one. The direction is the finding. The digital-gold case briefly looked like data rather than marketing. But it was built on an easing backdrop of falling real yields and a softer dollar, and the Fed just delivered the opposite. Gold itself has not been behaving like a hedge lately either, which means Bitcoin's new correlation is a correlation to an asset currently failing at its job. Glassnode's analysts flagged the fragility before the hike even landed. These decorrelations from equities during bond selloffs have historically been short-lived. So watch for reversion. If it comes, the levered-Nasdaq-proxy framing returns, and every allocator who bought Bitcoin as a diversifier owns a mandate written on a correlation that didn't hold.

Now to the part of this stack that needed neither a Senate vote nor a Fed decision. Circle put Arc, its purpose-built settlement layer, into public mainnet yesterday. This one is single-sourced to The Block, so weight it accordingly. The founding validator set runs to eleven institutions and includes BlackRock, Visa, Mastercard, DTCC and ICE. That is the card networks and the US clearing utility sitting on the infrastructure itself rather than building on top of it. Circle also minted the full ten billion ARC token supply this week, described as a technical milestone rather than a step toward public trading. Fold in the roughly four-hundred-million-dollar acquisition of Tazapay, a cross-border payments network, agreed earlier this month. Settlement rails plus last-mile payment reach inside the same month is one strategy, not two headlines. Circle is trying to capture the settlement economics that today accrue to Ethereum, Tron and the card networks.

Here's the skeptical read. Validator logos are not routed volume. Eleven institutions onboarding in phases is an announcement. DTCC's plan to tokenize DTC-custodied assets on Arc doesn't begin until the second half of 2027, which is long enough for the commitment to quietly change shape. The testnet cleared more than seven hundred million transactions, but a testnet number carries no economic weight behind it. And an issuer holding the entire token supply while leaving tradability and governance explicitly unresolved is an option on future value capture, not evidence of a distributed network. Sit with the irony too. A validator set built from BlackRock, DTCC, ICE, Visa and Mastercard is about as far from censorship-resistant neutral infrastructure as you can get.

The counterweight is Coinbase, which took the other route out of the same vacuum. On September 1st it filed with the SEC to bring twenty-four-seven single-stock perpetual futures onshore, structured as security futures under a dual-agency framework. That is a filing, not a product. There's no launch date, no leverage cap, no margin rules and no named underlyings, and CFTC approval is still outstanding. Goldman Sachs lifted its Coinbase price target to two hundred and nineteen dollars in early-to-mid September, its second increase in a matter of weeks, citing derivatives, prediction markets, tokenized equities and what it calls agentic finance. But the CLARITY Act's failure is exactly what makes that filing harder to bank. The statute that would have told the SEC and the CFTC how to divide this turf just died. A price target built on multi-agency cooperation is now a bet on agency discretion, and discretion can be reversed by an administration without a floor vote. There's a substitution question underneath it as well. Onshore equity perpetuals would mostly relocate leveraged speculation that already exists offshore rather than create genuinely new volume.

So here's the spine. The rate the market priced moved almost nothing. The path it hadn't priced, and the real yield underneath it, moved everything. And the sector's answer to a paralyzed Washington isn't to wait for clarity. It's to build around it, whether that means Circle laying its own settlement rails or Coinbase filing for a product anyway and hoping two agencies find a way to agree.

That's the briefing for September 17th. Talk tomorrow.
