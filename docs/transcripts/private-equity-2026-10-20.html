Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Equity & the Deal Economy

Transcript · October 20, 2026

Good morning. It's October 20th, 2026, and this is your private equity briefing.

All last week this show counted capital going in: take-privates signing, and continuation vehicles rolling sponsors' best assets into vehicles they still control. Today's question is different. How do sponsors produce cash for their limited partners when they still can't sell anything?

The clearest answer this week is the dividend recap, and Moody's has put a number on how much room is left to run it. As reported by Bloomberg and CNBC, Moody's estimates that private equity-backed companies in Europe, the Middle East and Africa could take on roughly forty-one billion euros of extra debt. That's about forty-six billion dollars, and it's the distance before their leverage reaches levels that risk a downgrade. Treat it plainly as a capacity estimate, not issuance. It's what could be borrowed, not what has been.

The mechanism matters more than the headroom figure, so here's our read of it. The portfolio company borrows against itself, and the new debt funds a cash payment out to the fund's limited partners. On paper, that payment counts as a distribution. It lifts DPI, and it lifts IRR. But the sponsor's equity stake in the company hasn't moved. No risk capital has actually left the fund. What's changed is that the company now carries more leverage than before. So the LP gets cash and the optics improve, while the ownership risk the fund carries is unchanged or worse.

Now the honest complication. Moody's and the coverage around it say sponsors are increasingly leaning on recaps as the exit window stays shut. The hard volume data doesn't support that as a 2026 trend. PitchBook LCD tracks recaps financed in the US broadly syndicated loan market, by far the biggest funding source for these deals. Those totaled about twenty-eight point seven billion dollars in the year through September 2nd. That's down thirty-nine percent from forty-seven point four billion over the same stretch of 2025. So US recap issuance is cooling this year, not accelerating. Activity did pick up again over the summer. And full-year 2025 was the second-highest year for recaps on record, so the practice is cooling from an elevated level.

What Moody's actually points to as trend evidence isn't rising recap volume. It's falling distributions. Moody's cites PitchBook data showing private equity distributions fell from more than thirty percent of starting net asset value in 2022 to about twelve percent now. That drought creates the incentive to recap. It doesn't prove the recap response is growing. The accurate framing is a persistent tool under steady pressure, not a surge.

Moody's own read is also more benign than a pure stress story. Its analyst Mayank Mallik says most recent recap deals involved companies that beat their original business plans. The new debt generally brought leverage back to its starting level, without materially hurting exit prospects. So the practice reads less like panic. It reads more like disciplined sponsors taking cash off the table on winners while the sale market stays unattractive.

But there's a selection problem underneath that benign reading, and it's the one worth sitting with. Moody's says recaps are limited to the strongest companies with the most capacity for new debt. Those are the same companies that would otherwise be first in line for a sale or an IPO. So the recap substitutes for the exit rather than preparing one. The median portfolio company, without the earnings growth to carry new debt, has no such lever. Aggregate DPI, lifted by a handful of strong assets, can mask real weakness at the median fund.

Put that against how long sponsors are now holding. Bain's 2026 Global Private Equity Report puts average buyout holding periods at exit around seven years. That's up from five to six years across 2010 to 2021. Nearly forty percent of companies are now held more than five years, against twenty-nine percent in 2019. Recaps don't shorten those holds. They make them easier to tolerate.

There's also a cost waiting at the eventual exit. A company that's already been recapped carries more debt than it did going in. So the equity value left for the next buyer or IPO investor is smaller. That raises the bar for a successful realization, just as last week's full-premium entries did. And one watch item on deal feasibility: this lever only works while lenders keep extending against portfolio companies. If that window narrows, the most accessible distribution tool closes with it. The credit side of that belongs to the private-credit briefing. The takeaway here is simple. Recaps buy DPI optics with leverage. They don't buy liquidity with a sale.

Secondaries pricing tells the same best-asset story. Single-asset continuation vehicles, where a sponsor rolls one trophy holding into a new vehicle it still controls, priced at an average discount of just two point nine percent to NAV in the first half. That's according to GA Group. Buyout LP interests sold on the secondary market, the genuinely arm's-length trade, priced at an average of ninety-one percent of NAV, per Hamilton Lane. That's a nine percent discount.

Those figures come from different providers covering different populations. Treat them as two reads, not one spread calculated off a single dataset. But the shape fits the recap story. Sponsors get near-full pricing when they sell their own best assets into vehicles they still control. Where real third parties set the clearing price, the discount is closer to nine percent. Adverse selection is part of that gap, since the best assets go into single-asset deals. And an LP-stake discount also reflects fund age, unfunded commitments, and how urgently the seller needs cash, not just mark quality.

So calibrate it honestly. The gap is real, and it's moderate. A nine percent average discount says marks run somewhat rich. It doesn't say they're fiction. Anyone pricing private equity NAVs as broadly broken is over-discounting on this evidence. Anyone treating near-par continuation-vehicle pricing as proof the marks are right is complacent. The honest price signal sits in the less-advertised trade, about nine percent under the mark.

One data point on the deal side before we pull this together. Mubadala Capital's take-private of Clear Channel Outdoor, valued at approximately six point two billion dollars, closed on October 14th after regulatory and stockholder approval. After a week of deals that had only signed, it's the first sponsor-style take-private in this stretch to go all the way through. But it's capital going in. It turned into cash for Clear Channel's public holders, not for any private equity limited partner.

So where does the cycle stand? Distribution is being manufactured through engineered channels rather than earned through arm's-length sales. Last week it was Bumble's take-private. There, Blackstone affiliates and the founder supplied the shareholder approval themselves, by written consent, with no public vote. Then it was continuation vehicles anchored inside a sponsor's orbit. Now it's debt-funded dividends, a tool that's persistent rather than surging. The biggest unresolved risk is that DPI optics keep improving while real realizations don't. For limited partners, reported performance can look healthier than their actual exposure to the underlying companies. The logjam isn't being cleared. It's being deferred, while holds run past the five-to-six-year norm.

The next hard test comes Thursday, when Blackstone reports third-quarter results before the open. Nothing is reported yet. Zacks consensus earnings per share has been revised down to a dollar thirty-one. That would be a decline of about fourteen percent year over year. It fits this show's "fees up, carry flat" read, but it's a sell-side estimate, not evidence. So don't watch whether Blackstone beats that number. Watch where its realization revenue came from. Did it come from third-party sales, or from more of the same engineered channels? Blue Owl follows on October 29th, then Apollo and KKR in early November. That's where this read either gets confirmed or falsified.
