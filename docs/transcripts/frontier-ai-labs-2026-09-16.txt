Good morning, it's Wednesday, September 16th, 2026, and this is your Frontier AI Labs briefing.

On Monday this show said the tri-lab safety story would only earn another segment once one of the three US labs put something on the record. On Tuesday, one did. OpenAI's global policy chief, Chris Lehane, told reporters at a Washington briefing that OpenAI has been working with Anthropic and Google DeepMind on AI safety for weeks. Weeks, meaning before Dario Amodei's "pace the frontier" essay landed on September 12th. So the essay wasn't the trigger. It was the public rollout of an arrangement already underway.

What Lehane actually confirmed is narrower than it sounds, and that's the story. He said OpenAI doesn't expect to need an antitrust waiver to coordinate with its two biggest rivals on safety. He reached for an analogy. Airlines are allowed to share safety information under existing antitrust law, so labs should be too. His own words were, quote, "it's better to try to work together to prioritize safety." That's a legal opinion, not a technical standard. And a firm that volunteers that its cooperation needs no waiver is telling you something implicitly. It understands this arrangement as coordination among competitors, the kind of thing that normally does require a lawyer's sign-off.

One correction to how this has been framed elsewhere, including in this show's own working assumption. The antitrust waiver request traces to Amodei, not to Sam Altman. Reporting indicates it was Anthropic's chief executive who asked for a narrow waiver so rival labs could discuss common safety measures. OpenAI's own outreach to Congress around September 11th was a corporate move, seeking guidance on Sherman Act exposure. It was not a quoted personal statement from Altman, and no clean verbatim Altman quote on antitrust exists in the available reporting. By September 15th it is Lehane, not Altman, on record saying no waiver is needed. So keep the roles straight. Anthropic is asking permission. OpenAI is saying it doesn't need any.

And there's a third voice still missing from this room. No Google DeepMind executive or spokesperson has confirmed participation in these talks in Google's own words. The only Google-side signal is chief executive Demis Hassabis publicly supporting Amodei's essay on social media. That is support for an idea, not confirmation of a joint working arrangement. Three days running now, Google has been described as aligned by other parties and never quoted directly. The silence is itself the data point. It tells you who expects to be bound by whatever comes out of this, and who doesn't. No charter, no membership list and no published standard exists from any of the three labs as of today. Anthropic's embedded-evaluator pledge remains the only concrete commitment on the table.

Bank the takeaway before we move on. If this arrangement ever hardens, what it hardens into is third-party evaluator access. That is an artifact only a lab with offices, employees and logs can produce. An open-weight lab cannot manufacture it at any price, because there is nobody to embed. So the regime raises the cost of being a credible challenger while doing nothing about the channel that actually sets prices. That also tells you where binding regulation eventually lands. Not on model release, which only reaches the firms that already publish, but on deployment and procurement — what a buyer is allowed to run, and where.

Which raises the obvious question. Who is setting the terms of competition outside that room? The answer this week is price.

DeepSeek's V4.1-Flash, released September 10th, got its first quote-confirmed independent comparison against a US flagship this week. The evaluator OpenDesign found the model reached 98 percent of GPT-6 Astra's quality score on everyday design requests, at 1.4 percent of Astra's cost. That is the evidence this show said didn't exist on Monday. It has arrived, and it is thin. One evaluator, one task category. Design work is close to the easiest place for a cheap model to reach parity with a frontier one, and nobody has replicated it. The gap narrowed. It did not close. And six days after release there is still no data on US enterprise adoption or hyperscaler hosting of V4.1-Flash. The price claim is running well ahead of any distribution evidence.

Astra is the denominator in that comparison, and it's worth a beat on why. OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra, shipped September 3rd, is the first model to cross OpenAI's own "Critical" threshold for cybersecurity capability under its Preparedness Framework. It went from 78.5 percent on ExploitBench, the exploit-generation benchmark, to a perfect 100. That perfect score cuts two ways. Either the model got that much better at writing exploits, or the test is exhausted. Its abstract-reasoning headline, 99.9 percent on ARC-AGI-3, comes from OpenAI's own provider-adapter harness, and OpenAI's own documentation says a plain stateless API call should not expect that. All of which makes "98 percent of Astra's quality" a soft denominator in both directions. Now note the timing, carefully rather than over-reading it. Astra tripped that Critical rating nine days before the essay. If the tri-lab talks really have been running for weeks, Astra did not start them. But a flagship crossing its own maker's danger threshold is the most plausible reason those talks accelerated into public view this month.

The commoditization read follows, and it's the part that prices assets. None of the three US labs is going to answer a one-percent-of-cost competitor with flagship price cuts, because the flagship margin is what funds the training run. What they defend instead is the cheap mid-tier and the product surface wrapped around it. So the winner-take-most case survives at the frontier-reasoning tier and erodes underneath it. For any workload that doesn't need frontier reasoning — and design requests are exactly that kind of workload — the base model keeps sliding toward commoditized infrastructure, with the durable value sitting in post-training and distribution rather than in the weights.

Put the two stories together and you get today's thesis. A release-side pact among three US labs cannot reach the party actually setting price per unit of quality, and that party is a Chinese open-weight lab that would never sign it.

Now the other constraint nobody at that safety table controls. Power. Elon Musk told SpaceX employees that xAI's data center capacity will grow roughly sevenfold, to ten gigawatts by late 2027, which he projects could bring in three hundred to five hundred billion dollars a year in revenue. Musk endorsed Amodei's pacing call on September 12th with two words: "Dario is right." Three days later, reporting had him living onsite at the Memphis data center campus, in an Airstream trailer, personally driving the buildout. Treat the capacity and revenue figures as exactly what they are. These are Musk's own projections relayed to employees, not audited or filed numbers, from someone with a long record of missed capacity timelines.

The tangible cost of that speed already showed up. On September 3rd, Grok went dark across X and its mobile apps, in an outage the company itself attributed to its own hardware. And on the model side xAI has still published nothing: no model card, no benchmarks, no context window and no pricing for the Grok 4.7 release Musk targeted for September 12th. So this week's xAI disclosure is entirely power, capex and its chief executive's sleeping arrangements.

Two things follow. First, endorsing a slowdown costs nothing while you are pouring concrete, which is worth remembering the next time a lab principal signs a pacing statement. Second, ten gigawatts of incremental demand, even partly real, is an additional call on turbines, transformers and regional grid interconnects on top of hyperscaler capex already committed. That is the actual pacing mechanism in this industry. Not a voluntary standard, but queue position and hardware reliability, and xAI just demonstrated both sides of it in the same fortnight.

What would change this read: a published charter or membership list from the safety talks, a formal antitrust position from the Justice Department or the FTC rather than a company's own comfort level, a provider-neutral evaluation of DeepSeek against Astra on reasoning rather than design, or independent confirmation of the interconnect agreements behind xAI's ten-gigawatt claim. Until then, the labs are negotiating procedure among themselves while price and power get set outside the room.

That's the briefing for September 16th. Talk to you tomorrow.
