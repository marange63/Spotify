Cautious Optimism Briefings

AI in Biopharma R&D

Transcript · October 19, 2026

Good morning, it's Monday, October 19th, 2026. Today's thread: the first maturing clinical proof point from a public AI-discovery platform arrived last week, and it's worth being precise about what it proves and what it doesn't.

On Thursday we covered Recursion Pharmaceuticals' $200 million raise and Sanofi's option exercise as capital-markets news. What we skipped was the clinical data that came out the same morning. That data is the more important story. Recursion's updated results from its TUPELO trial of REC-4881 in familial adenomatous polyposis, or FAP, show 87% of patients with a reduction in total gastrointestinal polyp burden after 12 weeks of treatment. The median reduction was 47%.

The durability read is the part that matters most. Twelve weeks after dosing stopped, 85% of patients were still below their baseline total polyp burden. In the upper GI tract specifically, 92% of patients improved on drug, and two of them saw reductions of 98% or more. That's an effect in patients that persists off therapy. It is not a benchmark score.

Two things temper that headline. First, the comparison to the December 2025 readout isn't apples to apples. That earlier 43% median came from just twelve efficacy-evaluable patients in an earlier cohort. Today's 47% comes from a broader population that now includes adults from age 18. Treat it as a bigger dataset, not a drug that got better.

Second, look at the trial design. TUPELO is open-label, and no control arm has been disclosed. The efficacy read rests on sixteen evaluable patients, out of twenty-six evaluable for safety. Grade 3 adverse events occurred in four patients, and four discontinued because of adverse events. Recursion says it anticipates a registrational trial in the second half of 2027, pending FDA discussions. That's a plan, not a commitment. And it marks the distance still left between this dataset and an approval.

Now the part that needs the most care: what this result says about the AI. Recursion's own 8-K describes REC-4881 as working "through a mechanism identified by its AI platform." But REC-4881 is not a new molecule. Independent reporting identifies it as Takeda's TAK-733, a MEK1/2 inhibitor Takeda made around 2006. Recursion in-licensed it in 2020. According to that same reporting, the platform's contribution was screening existing compounds in APC-deficient cell models and flagging this known inhibitor as a phenotypic hit for a new indication.

That's indication selection, not drug discovery. It's a genuinely useful capability. Finding new uses for old, de-risked chemistry is a real lever on cost and timelines. But it is not evidence that machine-learning models can find new biology. Keep those two claims separate whenever you hear "AI-discovered" attached to this drug. The read-through for AI-discovery equities follows directly. If this is the sector's most mature clinical evidence, investors should value platforms on clinical execution and indication selection, not on claims of originating new chemistry. And in-licensing shelved compounds for repurposing looks like the nearer-term route to value.

The capital markets, notably, rewarded the clinical data rather than the platform story. Recursion raised roughly $200 million the same day, oversubscribed. The company says that funds operations into 2029. Sanofi's option exercise is the more instructive number, though. It triggered a $10 million milestone and brought total upfront and milestone payments under that collaboration to $144 million to date. All week we've discussed AI-platform deals in terms of their headline biobucks. This is what one actually transfers in cash, years in: a steady drip, paid as candidates advance. And the Sanofi candidate isn't in the clinic yet, so the $10 million is an option payment, not validation. So what: both investors and pharma partners are paying for clinical derisking of specific assets, in increments, not for a platform's promise to originate biology.

Which raises the harder question. If the clinic's first real proof point rests on known biology, can the models predict new biology yet? On the task that matters most, the best available evidence says no. The standing reference is a 2025 Nature Methods study by Ahlmann-Eltze, Huber, and Anders. It tested five foundation models plus two other deep networks against simple linear baselines. The task was predicting how cells respond to single and double gene perturbations. None beat the simple baselines.

The double-perturbation result is the sharpest. There, every model had substantially higher error than a basic additive baseline. And pretraining on a large single-cell atlas gave only a small benefit over random embeddings. The paper isn't new, but commentary revisiting it as recently as October 7th finds it still unrebutted. It's also narrowly scoped. It covers perturbation-response prediction only. It doesn't indict structure prediction or fine-tuned cell-type annotation.

There's a competing reading worth holding. Perhaps perturbation data is simply too sparse and noisy for any architecture to beat an additive model. That would make the binding constraint data, not model design. But that's still bad news for near-term in-silico target discovery. Either way, the most heavily marketed layer of the AI-biology stack hasn't yet beaten basic statistics on its own flagship task.

And yet that's exactly where infrastructure money keeps flowing. Danaher announced a collaboration with NVIDIA on October 14th. We named that deal in one line on Friday, without the scope. Per secondary reporting, initial work uses NVIDIA's DGX Spark hardware, the RAPIDS software stack, and BioNeMo Open Models. No dollar value has been disclosed. For scale, set it against the benchmark already on the books. Back in January, Eli Lilly and NVIDIA committed to jointly invest up to $1 billion over five years in infrastructure and research. That co-innovation lab is built on BioNeMo and NVIDIA's Vera Rubin architecture.

Buying infrastructure isn't the same as validating models. Some of this tooling will serve imaging and process data, where the perturbation critique doesn't apply. But my inference is this. Demand for general-purpose biology-model compute is running ahead of proof that the hardest predictive tasks are solved. For NVIDIA and the tools vendors, that leaves this spend exposed to a stretch of underdelivery if prospective validation keeps lagging.

Where measurable value sits today is narrower. Eli Lilly's TuneLab platform has opened insitro-built models for predicting small-molecule pharmacokinetics to qualifying outside biotechs. They're delivered through federated learning. Biotechs get predictions from models trained on Lilly's internal data, and Lilly never hands over the raw data itself. Two caveats. No performance metrics have been disclosed, so there's no evidence yet that these models beat what a well-resourced biotech builds in-house. And the public material doesn't say whether Lilly or insitro controls the trained weights. Call that an open contractual question.

Still, the template is notable. A sponsor monetizes proprietary data by exposing a model instead of the dataset. My inference is that this protects the data moat while still creating value for partners. It also echoes Thursday's open question about insitro's AstraZeneca deal: who keeps the data and models insitro builds on someone else's biology? Here, insitro builds the model and Lilly keeps the data. Same company, same structural tension, different partner.

Pulling the threads together. AI is demonstrably helping with indication selection, and plausibly with narrow predictions trained on proprietary data, like PK. If shared PK models work as intended, they should modestly cut attrition from poor drug properties early in development. That moves clinical success rates at the margin, not structurally. What AI hasn't shown is that it can predict novel biology well enough to replace wet-lab discovery. Until AI-picked biology clears Phase 2, efficacy failure remains largely untouched. So expect R&D spend to keep reallocating toward data and compute rather than shrinking.

Three things to watch. Do Recursion's FDA conversations produce an agreed registrational design for 2027? Does any biology foundation model post a genuine prospective win over a linear baseline? And does TuneLab ever disclose real performance numbers for its PK models?
