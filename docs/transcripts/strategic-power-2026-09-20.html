Good morning, and welcome to Strategic Power for Sunday, September 20th, 2026.

The sharpest development this week is not a strike or a summit. It is an American president asking an ally to stop hitting an enemy's diesel refineries, because of what diesel costs at home.

The sequence matters. On Friday, September 11th, US diesel passed six dollars a gallon on average, a record, according to NBC News. Three days later President Trump said of Ukraine's president, quote, "He has to stop knocking out diesel fuel in Russia." He then posted that Ukraine had agreed not to hit Russian energy targets, and that Russia had agreed likewise.

There was no such agreement. Officials involved in the negotiations confirmed that no formal deal and no credible Russian guarantees had been secured when Trump spoke. Ukraine said it hit the Syzran refinery overnight. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said Russia had not stopped striking Ukrainian energy. That is a Kremlin statement, not an independent finding, but the strikes themselves are not in dispute.

So the truce is noise. The constraint behind it is the signal. Washington is weighing its own pump prices against the most effective economic weapon Ukraine has against Russia's war machine.

Now the skeptical part, because the causal chain is weaker than the politics implies. The United States buys essentially no Russian diesel. Any effect on American prices runs through global refining margins, not a direct supply line. And diesel is short from two directions at once.

The first is the Gulf. The International Energy Agency says Gulf net diesel exports averaged 390 thousand barrels a day in August. That is just over a quarter of pre-war levels, with Hormuz still severely constrained. The second is Russia. Time reports Russian refinery runs hit a 24-year low, and the IEA says a near-halt in Russian product exports has compounded the Gulf losses.

Here is the part Washington's framing skips. Russia's own government extended a ban on diesel, marine fuel and gas oil exports by direct producers on August 29th. It runs through September 30th and is renewed month by month. So some of the Russian export shortfall is Moscow's choice, not Kyiv's damage. Nobody has publicly split the record American price between the Gulf outage and the Ukrainian campaign. That is the central weakness in the argument being made to Kyiv.

The second-order effects cut both ways. If Kyiv yields, Russian refining recovers and margins ease, but Ukraine gives up its main pressure tool. And Moscow's own export ban could keep those barrels off the market anyway. If Kyiv refuses, friction with Washington rises just as gasoline becomes a midterm issue. Zelenskyy has conditioned any pause on Russian reciprocity, so I would not assume either outcome. The so-what is simple. American pump politics now shape an ally's war plan.

That leads to a correction of something we have said all week. We credited commercial workarounds, like the ship-to-ship transfers at Sohar in Oman, for keeping Brent from running away. Brent is sitting around 103 to 105 dollars. The IEA's September report points to a bigger reason. It now forecasts world oil demand falling 2.5 million barrels a day in 2026. That is 940 thousand barrels a day steeper than last month's forecast, because the US-Iran impasse delays any normalisation of flows.

In other words, the price is contained because consumption left, not because supply returned. Demand destruction is not a stabiliser. It is the shock being absorbed by consumers instead of by inventories, and it leaves nothing extra for the next disruption.

The official forecasts sit oddly against this. The EIA still expects Brent to average around 90 dollars in the second half of the year, well below the tape. Yet it assumes Middle East flows stay constrained through the fourth quarter. The forecast is lagging the market.

There is also less cushion than there was. The US Strategic Petroleum Reserve held 285.4 million barrels as of September 4th, its lowest level since 1982. That is the first of three buffers being drawn down. An eventual refill program would become a standing bid under crude, not a ceiling on it.

The second buffer is military, and it comes from a Congressional Budget Office analysis released September 15th. The CBO estimates the war with Iran has cost about 38 billion dollars in combat operations through August 1st. It also estimates the conflict has consumed between one-half and two-thirds of the US inventory of certain missile-defense interceptors since June 2025.

Replacing expended munitions would cost 21.7 billion dollars, and 13.1 billion of that is interceptors. Then comes the line that matters. Rebuilding those stocks could take at least five years, even if production rates increase.

Read that against the money. An estimate is not a rocket motor. Funding can be found in a single budget cycle, but the production line sets the clock. Munitions, not platforms, are now the binding constraint on American power projection, and that includes any Pacific contingency.

The bullish defense-prime reading is the lazy one. A five-year rebuild turns money into backlog, not near-term deliveries. That is a revenue-timing story, not a capacity story. And the CBO says each extra month of low-intensity fighting costs only two to three billion dollars. That is small next to a 38 billion dollar total, which is exactly why the war has no fiscal forcing function to end it. It is also fiscal supply arriving into a ten-year yield near five percent.

That brings us to the third buffer, which is monetary room. The Fed raised rates on September 16th to 3.75 to 4 percent, by a vote of 12 to 0. The ten-year yield sat just above 5 percent afterward. You have heard that from us. The new fact is the Bank of Japan.

On Friday the BoJ raised its policy rate to 1.25 percent, the highest in 31 years. It cited energy prices, global supply pressures and inflation above its 2 percent target. The dollar had already reached 156.30 yen after the Fed. So the same Gulf shock is forcing tightening on both sides of the Pacific, while war spending adds to bond supply.

Two consequences follow. A five percent ten-year raises the hurdle rate for utilities, independent power producers, infrastructure funds and AI data-center projects. Power gets harder to finance just when the grid needs more of it. And a weak yen alongside a rising Japanese policy rate raises the risk of a carry unwind and of Japanese selling of Treasuries. That would carry a Gulf energy shock into the US long end by way of Tokyo. That is a risk, not a forecast.

Now the hard-money angle, because this is the setup where you would expect it to work. It has not. Gold spiked to about 4,366 dollars on the Fed day and fell back to around 4,240 after the decision. Bitcoin held in the mid-to-high 70,000s through a bond selloff and the Senate's failure to advance the Clarity Act, which drew just 49 supporters against the 60 needed. The market is trading real rates, not debasement.

Which brings us to leverage. Treasury Secretary Bessent and Trade Representative Greer are due to meet Vice Premier He Lifeng in New York on Sunday. President Trump is due to meet Xi Jinping in Washington on September 24th. The tariff truce, which caps second-term tariffs at about 20 percent, expires November 10th. Neither meeting has happened as I record this, so there is no outcome to report. Beijing has also not clearly confirmed Xi's attendance.

Two signals stand out before the talks. A senior US official said China's rare earth performance, quote, "has not been up to par." Complaining publicly about a counterparty days before sitting down suggests weak enforcement options, not strong ones. Separately, on September 8th the NSA, CISA and the FBI issued an advisory saying China-based AI companies had extracted billions of tokens across millions of requests from US frontier models. Pairing a security accusation with a summit suggests the agencies and the negotiators are not running the same play. And a proposed 14 billion dollar arms package for Taiwan is reported to be still pending.

So here is the through-line. Washington arrives with a drawn-down strategic reserve, drawn-down interceptor stocks and little monetary room. The one lever it is complaining about publicly is Beijing's compliance, not its own capacity. Power is shifting less because of announcements than because physical buffers are running down.

Two things to watch. Whether Kyiv actually pauses refinery strikes, and whether the November 10th deadline gets extended.
