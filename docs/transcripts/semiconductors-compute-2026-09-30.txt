Good morning, it's Wednesday, September 30th. Samsung shows up on both sides of the chip stack this week, and in both places it's being selective about where it competes.

Here's why Samsung leads today. It's the one supplier that could, in principle, loosen the leading edge in both logic and memory. Monday brought a fresh read on its foundry execution. And Micron's results after the close today are the first hard test of the memory pricing Samsung has been reported to be locking in.

Start with the foundry side. On Monday, the Korean outlet ChosunBiz reported, citing industry insiders, that Samsung's 2-nanometer yield has climbed further. It moved from a widely recognized level of around 55 percent to nearly 60 percent. The same reporting puts that yield at about 30 percent in mid-2025. That's roughly a doubling in about fifteen months. It's a real manufacturing trajectory, not a one-quarter blip. But it's a single secondhand report, and it should be weighed that way.

Here's why it matters for a thread we've been carrying. Last week, we reported Samsung holding out on 2-nanometer pricing rather than discounting for Qualcomm's volume. At its Snapdragon Summit, Qualcomm then confirmed both new flagships on TSMC's 2-nanometer process. It said it was taking a deep look at Samsung for the next generation. Today's yield report tests what sits underneath that standoff. Is Samsung's refusal to cut price backed by real manufacturing progress, or is it just stubbornness?

Now a problem with our own record, and it's worth being plain about it. Last week, this show relayed Korean press, via TrendForce, putting Samsung's 2-nanometer yield above 70 percent. Now a different Korean outlet puts it near 60. And TrendForce itself reported an estimated 55 to 60 percent back in November of last year. Those three numbers can't all sit on one smoothly improving curve. The likeliest explanation is that they describe different dies, different test vehicles, or different measurement points. None of the reporting says which product today's 60 percent refers to. So treat Samsung's 2-nanometer yield as a band, not a point. The direction is credible. The level is not settled.

One more thing that band tells you. If last November's estimate was right, most of the gain came during 2025, and progress at the top of the curve has been slow this year. That's my reading of three secondhand reports, not a disclosure. And there's a missing benchmark. No verified, like-for-like figure for TSMC's 2-nanometer yield is available to set against Samsung's. So nobody can honestly tell you how wide the gap to TSMC is today.

Samsung's next move adds a second layer. With yields reportedly stabilizing, its foundry unit has shifted engineering focus away from chasing yield. The new priorities are power consumption and thermal management. It's also accelerating SF2P, its second-generation 2-nanometer node, with mass production targeted for this year. There are two ways to read that pivot. One is confidence: yield is good enough, so move on to differentiation. The other is a quiet repositioning. On that reading, Samsung concedes the highest-power AI accelerator sockets to TSMC and aims SF2P at mobile and edge designs. The evidence doesn't yet decide between them.

Keep the competitive dimensions separate. This is a manufacturing-execution story, sourced to unnamed insiders in trade press rather than Samsung data. No new marquee high-volume 2-nanometer customer has been confirmed on the back of it this week. And "mass production this year" is a target Samsung set for itself, not delivered volume. So land it here. Better yield makes Samsung a more credible second source at the leading edge. It does not make Samsung the price-breaker that would pressure TSMC.

Samsung's memory business is being selective too, but in a different way. TrendForce first reported this about four weeks ago, relaying unnamed sources. It's newly relevant because Micron's print today will test the pricing it implies. According to that report, Samsung's memory division has allocated about 70 percent of its production capacity through 2031 to long-term agreements. The named customers include Nvidia, Microsoft, and Google. Two qualifiers matter. That figure covers the memory division broadly, not HBM specifically. And it's secondary reporting, not Samsung guidance.

The horizon is what stands out. Micron's HBM is booked through 2027. SK hynix says shortages could run past 2030. Samsung's reported lock runs to 2031, further than anything this show has covered. And the price backdrop is the one we ran last week. Spot HBM3E has been trading at roughly four to five times the long-term contract price. So Samsung is choosing to keep most of its output inside the contract price, rather than let it chase that spot premium.

That cuts against the tidy structural-shortage story the rest of the industry is selling. A supplier truly convinced that scarcity is permanent might hold more capacity back for spot. Locking 70 percent of output for five years looks at least as much like a hedge against the cycle turning as a bet on endless scarcity. But that reading hinges on a term the reporting doesn't disclose. Do these agreements fix price, or only guarantee volume, with price reopened periodically? If it's the second, Samsung gives up little, and this is about allocation, not conviction. The evidence doesn't settle which.

The second-order effect is clearer than the motive. If most of Samsung's memory output is committed to the largest buyers through 2031, everyone else competes for what's left. That means enterprise server makers, PC makers and smaller AI labs, paying something closer to spot. It pushes more of the AI buildout toward the hyperscalers who can sign five-year contracts. So the so-what is that access, not just price, is becoming the dividing line in memory.

Samsung's selectivity shows up in HBM too. According to Benzinga's reporting, Samsung has skipped 16-layer HBM4. That sets up a clean competitive map across the three memory makers. The same reporting has SK hynix at volume on 16-layer HBM4 for Nvidia's Rubin platform. Micron is the more interesting case. Micron is shipping HBM4 in volume, but apparently at a lower stack height. At 16 layers, it's still sampling. Its fiscal third-quarter release said HBM4 is in high-volume shipments for its lead customer's platform, while Benzinga has its 16-layer parts at samples. The stack-height reconciliation is my inference. Micron's release names no stack height. So the map reads like this. SK hynix keeps the technology lead, Micron is shipping but trailing at the top of the stack, and Samsung has opted out of that race for now.

That brings us to the one scheduled catalyst this week. Micron reports fiscal fourth-quarter results after the close today, and nothing about that print is known yet. Two things will matter most when it lands. The first is how much of the revenue mix HBM4 now carries. The second is any language on contract structure and pricing direction. That would be the first hard test of whether memory pricing is peaking even while physical shortages persist.

Before I close, a capital-markets counterweight. Chip stocks whipsawed this week on rates, not fundamentals. Arm, Qualcomm and Marvell sold off sharply on Monday, then bounced on Tuesday. Arm led the rebound, up about 5 percent in morning trading. The aggregator 247 Wall Street attributed the round trip to rate expectations turning against long-duration growth stocks. That's the outlet's inference, and no company-specific news explained either move. A two-day round trip is noise unless it persists. The so-what is that the physical supply chain stays as tight as this show has documented all week, while the equities trade on the bond market. Those are two different clocks.

So here's the scorecard. Samsung gains on manufacturing execution, though its yield level is contested and has no TSMC benchmark beside it. In foundry, it's holding price rather than buying Qualcomm's volume. In memory, it's doing something different: trading spot upside for locked-in volume, which looks as much like a cycle hedge as pricing power. It has no confirmed new foundry customer, and it has left the top of the HBM stack to SK hynix. The losers are the fabless designers hoping for a cheaper leading edge, and the memory buyers without long-term agreements, who are left competing for what isn't locked up. The next fundamental anchor is Micron's print after the close today.

That's the briefing for Wednesday, September 30th. Take care.
