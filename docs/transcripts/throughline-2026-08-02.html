Good morning, this is The Throughline for Sunday, August 2nd, 2026. The single most important development across today's briefings: the AI buildout's demand case just got fresh confirmation from Amazon and Lam Research, at the exact moment the cost of financing that same buildout started rising sharply, in credit markets, in a widening Gulf war, and in a Fed that won't say which way it leans next.

Start with the macro backdrop. Strategic Power leads with the US-Iran war now closing on two chokepoints at once, the Strait of Hormuz and the Bab al-Mandeb corridor, with Saudi Arabia standing up a 14-country naval coalition that has a real command structure but no confirmed start date. Brent swung from $95 to $87 a barrel on the war-versus-diplomacy whiplash, and on August 2nd President Trump said he's canceling a planned strike and tied a prospective deal to reopening Hormuz — reported, not yet confirmed. That same energy shock is already sitting inside the Fed's language, which cited the Middle East conflict directly when three regional presidents dissented in favor of a hike on July 29th, and it's colliding with PJM's proposal to backstop a 6.8 gigawatt power shortfall — power access, not chips, may be the binding constraint on AI buildout now.

That same hawkish, rate-uncertain backdrop runs straight into Capital Markets Radar, where high-yield spreads sit near historic tights even as private-credit defaults just hit a record 6 percent. The gap is already showing up in AI financing specifically: CoreWeave had to sweeten a $2.6 billion loan tied to its Anthropic buildout twice, widening the spread by up to 125 basis points. So the cost of capital funding the AI compute build is rising at the margin, even as equity investors keep paying up for the same story. Digital Money adds a plumbing wrinkle to that same rate story: the stablecoin float actually shrank for the first time since 2023, as yield-bearing tokens like Ethena's USDe lost a quarter of their value once returns fell below the risk-free rate on offer elsewhere on-chain — a reminder that a chunk of that "sticky" on-chain money was always mercenary, chasing yield rather than parked as infrastructure.

Private Credit and the Corporate Credit Cycle sharpens that same financing-cost story from inside the industry itself. Institutional and insurance money is compounding at record rates — Ares, KKR, and Blackstone all posted record credit fundraising or AUM this quarter — while the retail wealth channel is wobbling, with Blue Owl's fundraising nearly halved and Blackstone's own BCRED fund fulfilling only about half of investor redemption requests against its cap. Managers are calling the pressure eased; the fund-level mechanics say otherwise.

Turning to the AI and compute stack, where the demand side of this week's argument got settled. Semiconductors and Compute Infrastructure has Lam Research delivering the clearest hard evidence yet that the AI capex story is structural, not a bubble: record revenue, a wafer-fab-equipment forecast raised twice in a year to the low $150 billions, and an advanced-packaging growth guide lifted to more than 70 percent. That's the equipment layer confirming, from the supply side, what chip equities spent late July second-guessing.

Frontier AI Lab Competition shows the same compute logic playing out between labs directly: SemiAnalysis reports Meta will out-total both OpenAI and Anthropic on raw compute by year-end, even as its shipped model still lags Chinese rivals — a bet that the race gets decided on infrastructure later, not the leaderboard now. Meanwhile a confirmed DeepSeek-powered cyberattack campaign, blocked by OpenAI's own safeguards but not by DeepSeek's open framework, and a missed federal deadline to define which models even count as "covered," show the safety and governance axis of this race is just as live as the compute one.

AI Products and Applications carries that authority question onto the desktop: Perplexity's new Windows agent reads and edits your real files and documents, but it still pauses for permission before anything risky — a tacit admission that reliability isn't solved. Microsoft's own autonomous cybersecurity agents, by contrast, are already acting on production systems with no human sign-off. Enterprise Technology closes that loop with the clearest revenue proof yet that the compute build is converting to cash: AWS grew 37 percent to $42.2 billion with 39 percent operating margins, a $496 billion backlog, and Andy Jassy saying the company won't have enough capacity to meet demand through 2027. Amazon is also raising its own capex guidance specifically because memory got more expensive — the same input-cost pressure Lam's numbers are chasing from the other direction.

Last, the applied frontier. Robotics and Physical AI has Google DeepMind's Gemini Robotics 2 showing a single model can drive a humanoid's whole body and hand off subtasks to a different robot entirely — evidence the robot brain and the robot body are separable businesses, favoring platform suppliers over vertically integrated makers. The body side, meanwhile, is getting priced in real time: Unitree opens IPO book-building August 5th on a majority-humanoid revenue base, but its margins are already compressing on rising rare-earth costs, and Boston Dynamics' new owner Hyundai faces a labor strike demanding no Atlas robot enters a production line without union consent.

Here's the thread worth naming. This show spent Thursday and Friday asking whether AI capex converts to cash, and Amazon, Microsoft, and now Lam Research have largely answered yes. Today's twist is that the answer arrived in the same week the price of financing that build started climbing everywhere at once — in private credit spreads, in a war-driven oil shock feeding a divided Fed, and in a stablecoin float shrinking because on-chain yield no longer clears the risk-free rate. The demand case is closing. The cost-of-capital case is just opening.

That's The Throughline. Have a good Sunday.
