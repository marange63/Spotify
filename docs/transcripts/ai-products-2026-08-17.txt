Good morning. It's Monday, August 17th, and here's what's genuinely new in AI products.

We flagged Gemini 3.7 Flash on Saturday when Google had shipped the model but hidden the price. That number has now landed, and it changes the calculus. Google's introductory pricing is 75 cents per million input tokens and $3.75 per million output tokens, available through the end of this year. That's half of what Gemini 3.6 Flash cost at launch, and it undercuts both Claude Sonnet 5, at $2 and $10, and GPT-5.6 Terra, at $2 and $12. Google also reports coding and agentic gains over its own predecessor: its FrontierCode benchmark rose from 34.4 percent to 43.6 percent, and its AutomationBench score for multi-step agent tasks jumped from 17 percent to 30.4 percent. Within the same week, GitHub added the model to Copilot's picker across every paid tier, from VS Code to the Copilot CLI.

Here's the catch worth naming before you take the capability claim at face value. Those coding and agentic benchmarks are Google's own numbers; no independent lab has reproduced them yet. The one broad, third-party composite that does exist, Artificial Analysis's Intelligence Index, doesn't show a clean win at all. It scores Gemini 3.7 Flash essentially tied with Claude Sonnet 5 and just behind GPT-5.6 Terra. So the picture splits: the price cut is a published, checkable fact, but the capability edge Google is marketing is thinner than the launch materials suggest, and on independent measures it's a wash, not a lead. Independent testing also found a real latency tradeoff Google doesn't emphasize: at high reasoning effort, median time to first token runs near ten seconds, well above the roughly three-second median for comparable models, though it drops under a second at low effort. And the lone real-world reliability report circulating, a developer's claim of a 35 percent cost saving and fewer tool errors versus 3.6 Flash, was published on Google's own site as a customer testimonial, not independently reported. There's also no data yet, from GitHub or anyone else, on whether developers are actually switching their default model inside Copilot. So the honest version of this story is: the price advantage is real and time-boxed to this year, the distribution is real and immediate, but the capability claim is contested, and default availability is not the same as developers actually choosing it.

That's still consequential, because cost and distribution move faster than proof of quality. Even a model that's merely tied with Sonnet 5 on independent measures, priced at a fraction of the cost and embedded in the dominant IDE copilot within hours of release, pressures the market. And there's a budget consequence underneath it. If a model that's competitive on quality costs a fraction of the flagship, enterprise dev teams have every reason to route their high-volume, routine engineering work — the repetitive agentic loops — to the cheap model, and reserve the expensive reasoning models for the genuinely hard problems. That splits the coding-model market into a cheap high-volume tier and a premium reasoning tier, and it's the cheap tier Google just tried to anchor. Expect Anthropic and OpenAI to respond on price, or bundle their own cheaper fast models into IDE integrations, because the mid-tier coding-agent layer is commoditizing regardless of who actually wins the benchmark argument.

Now the other side of that same question: what happens once a capability gets cheap to produce. Google is giving coding capability away. Microsoft is doing the opposite. Starting tomorrow, August 18th, Microsoft is retiring free Copilot Deep Research entirely. Its replacement, called Researcher, is more capable but sits only behind Microsoft 365 Premium at $19.99 a month. Reports you generated for free are kept, but you can only reopen them if you pay. Core Copilot chat stays free, so this isn't a blanket paywall, but it does pull the flagship research capability out of the no-cost tier at the exact moment Google is cutting prices and OpenAI has spent the past two weeks lifting usage limits. That's a live divergence in how vendors are choosing to monetize AI capability once it stops being expensive to run. The deeper question is whether distribution can substitute for price. Microsoft's whole advantage here is that Copilot ships inside Windows and Office, in front of users who never chose it. If those users won't pay twenty dollars a month for cited research when a good-enough version is one browser tab away at Perplexity, Gemini, or ChatGPT, then default placement isn't worth much the moment the feature costs money. If they will pay, Microsoft has just shown it can charge for the expensive end of Copilot without shedding users. Either way the answer reprices the embedded-assistant thesis, and we'll read it in retention data over the coming weeks, not in the launch.

Price and distribution aren't actually what decides whether agentic AI sticks inside a regulated enterprise, though. Governance is. Anthropic extended its Compliance API, previously limited to Claude chat, to also cover Claude Cowork and Claude Code sessions as of last week. That means compliance teams can now pull unified transcripts, tool calls, and metadata for coding-agent and desktop-agent sessions through the same audit interface they already use for chat, without a separate logging integration per product. That sounds like plumbing, but it removes a genuine deployment blocker. Banks, hospitals, and defense contractors have generally not been holding back on agentic tools because the models weren't good enough. They've been holding back because there was no audit trail to satisfy a compliance officer before letting an agent touch files or write code autonomously. Folding that into one API is the kind of unglamorous infrastructure work that actually converts a capable model into a durable enterprise deployment. It's currently a beta limited to Claude Enterprise customers, with no adoption numbers disclosed yet, and coverage still excludes Claude Code on the web and sessions run through Bedrock, Vertex AI, or Microsoft Foundry. And there's a competitive tell here. Whoever owns the audit surface tends to own the regulated-industry account, because a compliance officer signs off once, on one logging interface, not once per tool. So expect ChatGPT Enterprise and Microsoft Copilot to need a comparable unified audit surface if they want the same banks, hospitals, and agencies. Matching the model was never the hard part in these accounts; matching the paperwork is.

One more item, briefly, because it complicates the picture in a different direction. Z.ai released an open model called GLM-5.3 last Friday, built with strong cybersecurity scores, 84.5 percent on the CyberGym vulnerability-discovery benchmark, ahead of comparable frontier models, and 54.4 percent on ExploitBench, more than double its predecessor. In an early demonstration, the model was pointed at Cursor's own code editor and reportedly surfaced a serious file-write vulnerability, disclosed privately and not yet publicly patched. Treat that as reported, not confirmed. There's no CVE, no vendor advisory, and the severity comes from one security researcher's characterization. But it's a useful reminder that the same cheap code-understanding capability driving this whole price war cuts both ways. It's now also a tool for probing the AI coding tools themselves. And it puts a particular question over the AI-native IDEs. Tools like Cursor grew fast on developer experience, less on hardened application architecture, and they're now high-value targets precisely because so much code flows through them. Expect AI-assisted security auditing to start becoming a standard pre-ship step for the coding tools themselves.

Put together, the frontier of AI products this week isn't really about which model wins a benchmark. It's about what happens once coding capability gets cheap and instantly distributed: vendors splitting on whether to give it away or charge for it, and enterprises deciding based on audit trails, not sticker price, whether to actually deploy it.
