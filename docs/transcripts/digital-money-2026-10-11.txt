Good morning, and welcome to Cautious Optimism Briefings. It's Sunday, October 11th, 2026.

A year ago this weekend, Bitcoin had its worst afternoon on record. Last October, days after a record high, Bitcoin fell from about $122,000 to $105,000, most of it within minutes. Roughly $19 billion in leveraged positions were liquidated across crypto markets. That anniversary arrived this week with a question attached. Has anything about the market's leverage structure actually changed? This week answered it with a live test case, in miniature.

Start with the build-up, because it got less attention than the drop. In the two days to October 2nd, Bitcoin futures open interest rose about 4.3 percent, to roughly 653,000 BTC, according to CoinGlass data cited by CoinDesk. Over the same stretch, the perpetual funding rate roughly tripled, to about 10 percent. That is a crowded long book building fast, and it built off a depressed base. The point to bank is the speed. A re-leveraging that quick is the precondition for a forced unwind, and that is exactly what followed.

Here is the unwind. On Wednesday, Bitcoin fell under $84,000. About $360 million of long positions were liquidated in roughly ten minutes, according to a report citing WatcherGuru. The same day, US spot Bitcoin ETFs posted $487.1 million of net outflows, the largest since late June, per SoSoValue. Outflows ran for a second session before the pressure eased.

Now the watch item we set on Friday. We said the test was whether a third outflow day followed. It didn't. On Friday itself, the spot ETFs took in $21.13 million, almost all of it through BlackRock's IBIT, again per SoSoValue. So the streak stopped at two days. But keep that inflow in proportion. September brought in about $2.65 billion for the month. A single day of twenty-odd million is a rounding error against that pace. Selling stopped. Conviction buying has not come back.

Here's why that matters for the institutionalization story. The ETF wrapper is supposed to be the evidence that this asset class has grown up. The claim is that flows are sticky, patient, and allocator-driven. What we just watched is flows moving in step with a leverage cycle that built and broke inside a single week. The biggest outflow landed on the same day the long book got flushed. That is not a structure damping reflexivity. It looks like a structure riding it.

The fair counter deserves its full weight. Two days of outflows followed by a small inflow is also what ordinary de-risking looks like near a cost basis. Nobody promised a daily floor. There is a third explanation too: a basis-trade unwind, where funds sell the ETF as they close the futures leg. We can't measure that this week, because we don't have CME basis data for the window. So treat it as a live possibility with no numbers behind it. Even the on-chain read is contested, with CryptoQuant and Santiment publishing conflicting interpretations of the pullback. The takeaway for allocators is plain. Right now, the ETF wrapper inherits perpetual-market volatility rather than insulating against it.

Back to the anniversary, because it is the right yardstick for sizing all this. TokenPost measured total crypto liquidations of $91.63 million in the day running into Saturday morning, the anniversary itself. Some aggregators ran headlines implying another $19 billion day landed on that date. That's wrong. They conflated last year's figure with this year's calendar. By any measure, this week's flush was one to two orders of magnitude smaller than last year's. And one standing caveat from earlier in the week still applies. Liquidation tallies understate reality, because Binance's reporting is incomplete.

So is the system safer? One risk strategist, Connors of Risk Dimensions, was blunt in CoinDesk's anniversary retrospective. Quote, "The levered products have not gone away." The retrospective's argument is that last year's mechanism is still in place. That means heavy reliance on perpetual futures, exchanges with every incentive to keep offering them, and price discovery led by derivatives. Connors also says the four-year halving cycle "has changed," and that it gives less signal than it used to. That cuts against anyone trying to time this drawdown off the calendar. Remember, that is one practitioner's judgment, not a measured finding.

The behavioural evidence is what gives his view weight. The bull case says this week's shock was absorbed, and the liquidation numbers support that for this particular flush. But there's an asterisk. Open interest rebuilt from an eleven-month low this time. As we noted Thursday, it was still well short of this year's record near 800,000 BTC. A smaller flush from a lighter book tells you the book was lighter. It tells you nothing about tail behaviour once positioning climbs back toward those highs. And we can't compare today's open interest cleanly with the level before last year's crash, because the available figures use different scopes. So the honest conclusion is narrow. Smaller doesn't mean structurally different. It may just mean we caught this cycle earlier.

So who could actually change that structure? On paper, two US regulators are trying. Start with the CFTC. It approved an advance notice of proposed rulemaking on October 5th. The notice covers a retail crypto commodity framework, and a federal option for crypto exchanges called Regulation CAM. But as of midweek, it still hadn't been published in the Federal Register. That means its 60-day comment clock hasn't started. This past Tuesday, we could only say the rule sat at White House review. On Thursday it had names but no text. Now it is approved but unpublished. Each step is real, and each is slower than the headlines imply.

The design matters more than the timing. CFTC Chairman Michael Selig described Regulation CAM as, quote, "a purpose-fit option for crypto-asset exchanges that wish to operate under a single federal market-regulatory scheme." Note the word option. Exchanges elect in. Perpetuals are a revenue line for the venues that host the leverage. Those venues have no obvious reason to volunteer for a federal regime. The bull read is that a federal venue regime could onshore activity and improve visibility. That is plausible, but we have no evidence on likely uptake. The more probable outcome echoes what Coinbase did with its new clearinghouse, which we covered Thursday. Regulated perimeters get built around the lowest-risk business first. So this rulemaking is likelier to onshore low-leverage retail activity than to constrain the leverage behind this week's cascade.

The SEC is on a different, adjacent track. Its Regulation Crypto Assets proposal offers issuers a one-time exemption for raises up to $5 million over four years. It adds a separate exemption of up to $75 million a year, plus an investment-contract safe harbor for qualifying tokens. Comments are due October 20th. That is an issuance framework. It matters for token launches, and the comment letters will be a read-through for issuer and exchange equities. But it says nothing about derivatives margin or exchange leverage. Meanwhile, the broader CLARITY Act remains stalled after failing cloture by a single vote in mid-September. In that vacuum, US crypto rules are being built agency by agency. No statute is setting a floor on leverage.

Put it together. The leverage cycle behind last year's $19 billion crash is still structurally intact. This time it rebuilt and unwound inside a single week, at far smaller scale. ETF flows aren't countering that cycle. They appear to be riding it, and the end of the selling is not the return of the buying. And the regulatory vehicles now in motion, the CFTC's elective exchange option and the SEC's issuance exemptions, aren't built to reach the leverage at all.

Watch three things into mid-month. First, whether open interest and funding rebuild again off this flush. Second, whether ETF flows turn genuinely positive rather than just flat. Third, whether the CFTC's notice clears the Federal Register and starts its clock. September CPI lands October 14th, and the Fed decides on October 28th. Neither has happened yet, so treat any rate-driven story for this week's price action as unconfirmed.
