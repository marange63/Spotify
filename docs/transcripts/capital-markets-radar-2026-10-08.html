Capital Markets Cross-Asset Radar

Transcript · October 8, 2026

Good morning. It's Thursday, October 8th, 2026, and this is Capital Markets Radar.

Start with Wednesday's session, because it said something the headline didn't. Oil and yields both rebounded. The S&P 500 slipped just 0.22 percent, a rounding error a day after its record close. The Russell 2000 fell 1.31 percent, roughly six times as hard. On Tuesday this desk argued that a front-end rate move hits floating-rate, short-funded balance sheets first. Small caps are where a lot of those balance sheets live. Wednesday is the first session where that mechanism showed up in prices. For a week we've asked why stocks keep ignoring a hiking Fed and multi-decade-high long yields. Today the question flips. If the rate shock isn't landing in the cap-weighted index, where is it landing? The answer is credit, the dollar, and the hedges that are supposed to protect you from exactly this kind of move.

Begin with credit, because this is the first time this desk can put a spread number on it. One source citing FRED's ICE BofA series has high-yield option-adjusted spreads at 2.60 percentage points in late August. That's among the tightest readings since 2007. FRED's own daily prints are the firmer anchor. They show the spread at 3.24 on October 1st, more than half a point wider in about a month. That window straddles last month's hike. By Monday it had eased back to 3.12.

Two readings fit those numbers, and both deserve weight. The complacency reading rests on the level. A spread just over three points is nowhere near a stress regime. The direction reading says the opposite. Credit started repricing the hike weeks before equities blinked. Investment grade tells the same story more quietly, at 86 basis points against 76 a year ago. Then there's the retrace. The pullback from 3.24 to 3.12 shows buyers fading the widening, so this is a wobble that got bought, not a break. One honest gap: we have no default, leveraged-loan, or new-issue data behind the move. A hike that raises funding costs is the obvious candidate, but the cause is inference.

Here's why the direction matters more than usual. A widening that starts from the tights leaves a thin cushion if a single credit event hits. Our Private Credit briefing has been tracking where that event could come from. Fed examiners have been running on-site reviews of private-credit lending at JPMorgan, Wells Fargo, and Morgan Stanley, among others. And redemption stress in direct-lending funds is sorting toward software borrowers rather than spreading across the complex. Linking that private stress to public spreads is our inference, not something the data shows yet. So what: public credit has been quietly pricing this Fed for a month. The S&P has not.

If credit is feeling the hike, the cleanest cross-check is the currency. On Tuesday this desk named the dollar as the tie-breaker. A firm dollar would fit a policy-path story. A soft one would point to term premium. Trading Economics now has the dollar index up 3.61 percent over the past month. That's a firm dollar. It also tightens funding for everyone borrowing dollars offshore. Hold the policy-path thought, though, because the Fed's minutes are about to complicate it.

Non-yielding hedges are the clean casualty. Earlier this week, this desk's arithmetic from Treasury's tables showed breakevens flat while nominal yields climbed. That makes this a real-rate move, though we have no fresh real-yield print for Wednesday. Gold starts October under $4,400 an ounce with the ten-year above 5 percent, per Canadian Mining Report. That's a steep retreat from January's all-time high near $5,595. Bitcoin marked the anniversary of its record this week about 32 percent below that peak. It got there even after posting its first winning quarter in a year. Both assets are sold as hedges against debasement and low real rates. Right now they're getting the opposite.

The caveat cuts both ways. This is one month of dollar strength into a hiking cycle. It's cyclical, not settled. A soft CPI print or a de-escalation in the Gulf would unwind dollar longs fast. That would likely lift gold and bitcoin together. So what: the hedges aren't failing because inflation fear has faded. They're failing because the shock is in real rates and the dollar.

The one input that could turn this into an inflation shock is energy. Saudi Arabia says its East-West pipeline is back to 5.8 million barrels a day. That route had been carrying Saudi crude around an effectively closed Strait of Hormuz. September's drone strikes forced it to shut. Flows are now restored. Yet the EIA's October outlook raised its fourth-quarter Brent forecast to $105 a barrel, citing the attacks and falling global inventories. That's the paradox worth banking. The premium is pricing how fragile the route is, not barrels actually lost. And the fragility is still live. A single AFP source says a fresh strike on a pumping station briefly halted flows again. Saudi officials and Bloomberg's sources say the line kept running. Higher energy costs are also a margin drag outside the energy sector, an offset to the earnings story equities are leaning on. So what: breakevens have stayed flat through this whole selloff, and oil is the likeliest thing to finally move them.

That brings us to the Fed, and here the desk owes you a correction, not a victory lap. Start with the market's read going into Wednesday afternoon's minutes. CME FedWatch had October-hike odds at 73 percent in late September. After the weak September jobs report, CNBC reported those odds had faded below 20 percent, even as long yields kept climbing. That divergence is the over-read this desk has flagged all week. We don't have a verified reading taken after the minutes, so we won't give you one.

The minutes themselves cut against that complacency. Most participants assessed that another increase would likely be appropriate. Timing was left to incoming data rather than a fixed schedule. And a number of participants saw a higher path as necessary based on their own modal outlooks. That is not a Committee looking for an exit.

Now the correction. Over the last two episodes, this desk leaned toward reading the selloff as a policy-path shock rather than term premium. The dollar's move seemed to settle it. The minutes complicate that directly. They put the intermeeting rise in nominal yields at around 35 basis points across the two-to-ten-year segment. A few participants discussed what was driving longer-term yields, and the minutes cite higher term premiums as part of the picture. So the Fed's own account doesn't treat this as a clean policy-path move, whatever the dollar says. Treat the debate as open. The Committee also judged financial conditions still supportive of growth despite the yield rise. That tells you it isn't alarmed yet.

One housekeeping point on the level. Treasury's closing curve has the ten-year at 5.28 percent on Wednesday. That's a basis point above Tuesday's close and still below Monday's 5.31. So yields rebounded, but they didn't make a new closing high. The multi-decade-high headline still belongs to Monday's intraday print.

Put it together, and the over-read and the under-read are mirror images. The over-read is the collapse in October-hike odds, which long yields simply ignored. The under-read is a month of credit widening and dollar strength that the cap-weighted index hasn't acknowledged. Small caps on Wednesday were the first sign that gap is closing from below.

The tests are dated, and none has happened yet. Bank earnings open October 13th with JPMorgan, Goldman Sachs, Wells Fargo, and Citigroup. Bank of America and Morgan Stanley follow the next day. Credit costs there are the first live read on whether this month's spread widening shows up in provisions. September CPI lands on the 14th, the first chance for the oil premium to reach the data and move breakevens. The FOMC decides on October 28th. Assume none of the outcomes.

That's Capital Markets Radar for October 8th. Trade carefully out there.
