Good morning, it's Saturday, August 8th, and this is Capital Markets Radar.

We've said all week that Friday's jobs report was the test for this entire trade, and now we know how it broke. Nonfarm payrolls fell 23,000 in July, against consensus for a gain of roughly 83,000, and the Bureau of Labor Statistics revised May and June down by a combined 103,000 jobs on top of that. This is a genuine surprise, not noise. The Federal Reserve, under Chair Kevin Warsh, had just held rates at 3.50 to 3.75 percent on July 29 for a fifth straight meeting, and three FOMC members dissented in favor of a hike, citing persistent inflation. That was the hawkish frame going into Friday. The payrolls miss flipped it. September hike odds fell from 58 percent to 42 percent on the day. The unemployment rate itself held at 4.1 percent, so be precise about what broke here: this is payrolls momentum rolling over, not yet a broad crack in the labor market. Keep that distinction, because it will matter for how hard the Fed leans into any easing signal.

The market's reaction to all this is where it gets interesting, and where I think a lot of positioning is about to get tested. The 10-year Treasury yield fell 7 basis points to around 4.6 percent, which is a clean rates-relief move. At the same time the S&P 500 closed at a record 7,757, up six tenths of a percent, and the Nasdaq jumped 1.3 percent. And gold, which usually rallies on fear rather than relief, jumped 2.6 percent to 4,350 dollars an ounce. Think about what each of those three moves is actually pricing. Falling yields and record equities together are a soft-landing, rate-relief trade. A 2.6 percent gold spike on the same day is a recession, safe-haven trade. Those two stories cannot both be right. The market is pricing "the Fed eases and everything's fine" and "something is genuinely wrong with growth" simultaneously, in the same session, and that kind of internal inconsistency doesn't sit quietly for long. It resolves, usually abruptly, in one direction or the other. Don't let the record equity close talk you into reading this as a clean dovish all-clear.

Take the benign read first, because the near-term mechanics genuinely support it. When policy-rate-hike odds fall and real yields come down, the assets that benefit most are the longest-duration ones, and that is exactly what showed up in the tape. The Nasdaq's 1.3 percent gain against the S&P's six tenths is not a rounding difference. It's the same rate-relief impulse flowing hardest into the growth and tech names whose valuations lean most on distant cash flows. Lower discount rates lift long-duration equity first, and Friday's leadership fits that to the letter. The same move eases conditions offshore. A softer dollar, pushed down by those falling US yields, loosens global dollar-funding at the margin, and that is a quiet tailwind for emerging-market assets and for commodity-linked trades that borrow in dollars. So the soft-landing story is not empty. It has real mechanical support in exactly the places that rallied, which is precisely why it's seductive.

That's the bull case, and it's coherent on its own terms. So where does the story get tested first if it breaks the other way, toward recession rather than soft landing? Credit is the layer with the least room to absorb it. High-yield spreads have sat near the richest decile of their twenty-five-year history all week, roughly 280 basis points over Treasuries against a long-run median closer to 450. We've flagged that setup every day this week as priced for perfection. The news today isn't the spread level itself, it's that the trigger we were waiting for just arrived. If the payrolls miss deepens into an actual growth scare rather than a one-month stumble, high yield has vastly more room to widen than it ever had to compress further, and that asymmetry is now live rather than theoretical. Watch spreads, not just yields, as the tell on whether this is a growth scare or a Fed-relief rally.

Now to the other side of the dollar story, because the same US-rate move that's pulling Treasury yields down is tangled up with a separate, and frankly more dramatic, policy event out of Tokyo. Japan carried out a record single-day currency intervention worth roughly 8.45 trillion yen, followed a day later by a further 5.33 trillion yen operation, in what's being described as coordination with Washington. That's the largest single-day defense of the yen on record, following four-decade lows driven by high energy costs, fiscal concerns, and the persistent gap between US and Japanese rates. And despite that scale, the yen still weakened back past 158 to the dollar by Friday, giving back part of the intervention's gains. That's the real signal here: record-size intervention could not hold the line. When policy that big can't stop the drift, it tells you the rate-differential and fiscal drivers are stronger than the intervention itself, and it keeps a Bank of Japan policy response, or an even larger follow-up operation, on the table as a live risk. It also keeps carry-trade unwind risk alive as a tail scenario for global risk assets, because Japan remains one of the largest foreign holders of Treasuries, and any disorderly move in Japanese government bonds ripples straight into global duration markets.

One caveat worth naming on the dollar. The dollar index touching a seven-week low around this period is being pushed lower by both the intervention and the separate US-yield decline at the same time, so don't read the whole move in the index as a Japan story. Some of it is simply the Fed repricing we just walked through, and that overlap is exactly why the easier dollar-funding tailwind and the yen drama are hard to cleanly separate right now.

Let me close with one structural item that's lower-drama but worth banking. BlackRock launched two new tokenized money-market products this week, both structured to qualify as eligible reserve assets for stablecoin issuers under the GENIUS Act. BlackRock's CFO said on the announcement that the firm already manages 60 billion dollars of reserves for Circle alone and expects, in her words, lots of growth ahead in stablecoin. This isn't a market-moving headline today, it's plumbing. But it matters because it formalizes tokenized cash and stablecoin-reserve management as a distinct product line at the largest asset manager in the world, and that steadily grows structural demand for short-dated Treasuries and repo collateral at the front end of the curve. It's a slow bid, not a fast one, but it's a real one, and it sits underneath every one of the rate moves we just discussed.

So here's where that leaves the week. The Fed's debate flipped from hike risk to hold-or-cut on one data point, credit's asymmetry warning just went from hypothetical to live, and Japan's record intervention couldn't hold the yen. The next catalyst to watch is the preliminary annual benchmark revision to the payrolls data, expected toward the end of August, which will either confirm this jobs miss as the start of a real trend or mark it as a one-off. Until that lands, resist the urge to over-trade a single print, however loud Friday's reaction was.
