Good morning, it's September 15th, 2026, and this is your private equity briefing.

Let's start with the first-half secondaries number, now that a named research desk has finally published one. Evercore's H1 2026 Secondary Market Review puts total first-half secondaries volume at 121 billion dollars, up 19 percent year over year. GP-led transactions ran to 65 billion of that. Single-asset continuation vehicles alone accounted for 34 billion, up 88 percent year over year, and now 53 percent of all GP-led volume. That is a real upgrade over the aggregator figures and interested-party press releases this show declined to use last week.

Now the complication. Evercore puts full-year 2025 secondaries volume at a record 226 billion dollars. Three days ago this show used Jefferies' figure for that same completed calendar year, and Jefferies said 240 billion. Two named advisers, both of whom earn fees on the transactions they're counting, are roughly 14 billion dollars apart on a year that finished nine months ago. Neither house publishes how it counts, and nothing on the record explains the gap as a difference in scope rather than measurement. The single-asset breakdown reaches us through a summary of Evercore's report rather than the report itself.

So take the honest version. What we actually have is a range, authored by intermediaries. That matters more here than in most markets, because the whole stated function of the secondaries market is to set an outside price on other people's marks. The market whose job is to price the marks is itself measured by the people who profit from the transactions. Keep it in mind for everything that follows, because every valuation in this briefing is reported by someone with an interest in the level.

The mix shift inside that number is the real signal. Single-asset continuation vehicles growing 88 percent year over year, to more than half of all GP-led volume, tells you sponsors are increasingly choosing to re-underwrite and hold their best assets rather than sell them into a still-weak strategic and IPO market. That's a rational response to depressed exit multiples. It's also a way of pushing the liquidity problem onto secondaries buyers, while letting the selling limited partners who roll into the new vehicle count it as something close to a realization. Most of their capital never actually left the deal.

So if that's how much inventory is moving through the secondaries channel, the next question is how much fresh capital is lining up to buy it. CVC Secondary Partners closed its sixth global secondaries fund on September 3rd at 10 billion dollars. Trace the arc. Its 2019 vintage closed at 2.7 billion, its 2023 vintage at 5.8 billion, and now 10 billion. That's a franchise nearly quadrupling in seven years. Roughly half the limited partners in the new fund were new to CVC's secondaries franchise entirely. That's fresh money entering secondaries, not existing secondaries investors upsizing, arriving at exactly the moment when pricing discipline matters most. Two disciplines on that. A final close is capital committed, not capital called or deployed. And a 10 billion dollar fund cannot buy the same mid-market deals a 5.8 billion dollar fund did, so the pricing record of the predecessor tells you less than it looks like it does.

Set that fundraising pace against the primary buyout side of the business. On Sunday this show covered Apollo's Fund XI, its flagship buyout vehicle, launched in February against a target of 22 to 25 billion dollars. As of early August it had drawn about 12 billion, roughly half, six months into marketing, with the firm publicly floating a close slipping into 2027. The contrast is the point. The same limited-partner base that's slow-walking commitments to traditional buyout re-ups is racing to fund the vehicle that buys those funds' assets at a discount. That's not a vote of confidence in the asset class. It's allocators choosing to sit on the buy side of their own industry's liquidity problem rather than wait for it to resolve.

Now, what has any of this actually returned in cash. This is where the vintage-by-vintage distribution data lands hardest. Median private equity fund DPI — cash actually returned divided by cash actually called — sits at 0.48 times for 2019 vintage funds, 0.30 times for 2020, 0.16 times for 2021, and just 0.09 times for the 2022 vintage. Four years into their lives, funds raised in that frothy 2022 window have returned roughly nine cents on the dollar in cash. Two qualifications. These figures come from a benchmark summary whose underlying data provider isn't disclosed, so treat the decimals as directional. And some of that slope is mechanical. Younger funds are always going to show less realized cash than older ones. So the honest read isn't the raw level. It's that each successive vintage is running further behind where its predecessor stood at the same age.

The direction is unambiguous, and it supplies the mechanism this show has been asserting all week without a hard number behind it. Limited partners are no longer taking a fund's reported multiple at face value. They're conditioning re-ups on cash actually returned. That explains the Apollo delay better than brand or market conditions do. And it means what you're watching across the secondaries data and the DPI data together is a liquidity machine scaling in size far faster than it's scaling in cash paid out.

That gap between reported value and cash received shows up cleanly in a single allocator's own disclosure. CalPERS, the largest public pension in the country, reported a preliminary 17.0 percent net return for its private equity asset class for the fiscal year ended June 30th. That sat inside a 14.8 percent total fund return, which beat both its 6.8 percent assumed rate and the prior year's 11.6 percent. It's a strong number, and it follows the multi-year rotation away from concentrated large-buyout exposure we covered on Sunday. But hold that 17 percent next to the 0.09 times DPI on 2022-vintage funds. Both are true statements about the same asset class in the same period. A reported annual return is a change in carrying value plus whatever got realized. It is not cash distributed to the pension. And remember how CalPERS did much of that rotating — by selling into the secondaries market, which means part of that return was priced by the very channel we just showed is measured by conflicted intermediaries. The figures are also preliminary and unaudited, which matters more than usual for an asset class where marks are known to lag.

One closing thread on where all of this is headed. Blackstone, Vanguard and Wellington have launched a joint interval fund built for retail and mass-affluent portfolios through the RIA channel, blending public equities, fixed income and Blackstone's private-markets platform. It allows quarterly redemptions of only 5 to 25 percent of net asset value. That's a July launch, so it's background rather than news. But the structure is the story. A retail holder's exit price is whatever NAV the manager reports, and the gate binds hardest in precisely the environment where that NAV is most likely stale. It's the direction this business is heading — retail capital brought in to hold exposure to the same reported values that professional limited partners are now discounting.

So where does the cycle actually stand. Record secondaries volume and record secondaries fundraising are not evidence that limited partners are getting paid. They're evidence of how much unsold inventory needs re-pricing, and how much fresh capital wants to be the one doing the pricing. The binding constraint isn't headline assets under management. It's the exit window and the credibility of the marks behind it. Watch three things. Late-October third-quarter earnings from Blackstone, KKR, Apollo, Carlyle and Ares — not for fee-related earnings, which have stayed healthy throughout, but for realization pace. Whether a third named provider publishes a first-half number that brackets Evercore or contradicts it. And whether CalPERS' audited figures hold that preliminary 17 percent once they're finalized.
