Good morning, it's Thursday, October 1st, and this is your private equity briefing.

Start with the deal that best captures where this cycle actually stands. On September 28th, AAR Corporation agreed to buy a controlling 65 percent of Bain Capital-backed MRO Holdings, an aircraft maintenance business. AAR is paying roughly 1.8 billion dollars of equity value for that stake, which implies a 4.0 billion dollar enterprise value for the whole company.

The announced multiple is 10.7 times MRO's forecast 2026 adjusted EBITDA. But read the fine print in AAR's filing. That multiple counts 75 million dollars of run-rate cost synergies AAR expects to capture. Strip those out and set the 4.0 billion dollar enterprise value against the 285 million dollars of forecast EBITDA, and you get about 14 times. That's my arithmetic on two disclosed numbers, not a figure AAR stated. The point is simple: a strategic buyer is paying for synergies only it can capture. That price is a control premium, not a clean read on what a financial sponsor would pay.

The financing tells you who is taking the risk. AAR is raising about 2.1 billion dollars of new debt to fund the purchase. That's roughly half of MRO's 4.0 billion dollar enterprise value, and it sits on AAR's balance sheet. AAR is also issuing roughly 780 million dollars of its own stock to MRO's existing owners. So the risk splits two ways. AAR's balance sheet takes the leverage risk. MRO's sellers take equity risk in AAR stock, so part of what they're paid rides on how AAR performs after the deal. Then there's the part that doesn't move at all: the other 35 percent, and Bain.

Bain Capital describes itself as a significant minority shareholder in MRO, and it is not fully cashing out. In its own words, it looks forward to "participating in continued value creation as shareholders." AAR's filing makes clear the remaining 35 percent does not convert into AAR stock at closing. AAR only holds an option to buy it later. Five percent is exercisable any time within six years. The other thirty percent comes in three equal tranches, on the second, third, and fourth anniversaries.

That's a critical distinction. The retained stake stays private, unlisted MRO equity for years. It is not a public-market mark in AAR shares. And the pace of that eventual liquidity is set by AAR, the buyer, not by Bain. This is a buyer-paced wind-down, not an exit on the seller's timeline. The deal hasn't closed yet either. AAR expects completion in its fiscal third quarter, which ends in February 2027, pending regulatory approvals.

So what should an allocator take from this? Even the money that moves at closing isn't all cash. Part of the consideration is AAR stock, and the selling owners are subject to customary lockups on those shares. Neither AAR nor Bain discloses how Bain's own proceeds split between cash and stock. Bain's entry price and stake size are undisclosed too, so no multiple on invested capital can be calculated. Only the cash that actually reaches Bain's limited partners counts toward distributions to paid-in capital. The rest stays a mark, whether in locked-up AAR shares or residual MRO equity. Treat this as liquidity arriving in a slice, not an exit.

It's worth placing this against Monday's briefing. There are two familiar exit shapes. One is a clean sale to an outside buyer. The other is a continuation vehicle priced near the seller's own marks. MRO sits between them. An outside strategic buyer sets the price for control. That's a genuine independent print, the kind this show has called rare. But the seller stays in anyway. So the price is set at arm's length, while the structure looks more like a continuation vehicle than a clean sale.

Why would a sponsor roll a stake instead of taking a full exit? Bain and Company, the consultancy, which is a separate firm from Bain Capital, offers the standing logic. Its 2026 Global Private Equity Report frames it like this. A decade ago, a buyout needed roughly 5 percent annual EBITDA growth to deliver a 2.5 times return. Today it needs closer to 10 to 12 percent. Leverage and multiple expansion can't carry a deal anymore. Rolling a stake alongside a strategic buyer, with its own platform and its own synergy case, is a way to buy growth someone else has to deliver. You no longer have to bet that your own fund can manufacture it.

That growth bar is the lens for the week's other marquee deal, where two big sponsors walked away. Three sponsors chased the UK industrial group Bodycote this year, and two of them left. Apollo retreated in June from its 1.52 billion pound bid. Then on Monday, September 28th, CVC Advisers said it does not plan to make an offer. That clears the way for US-based Veritas Capital's recommended takeover, valued at 1.85 billion pounds, or 2.45 billion dollars, including debt. CVC's statement falls under Rule 2.8 of the UK Takeover Code. That rule generally bars it from bidding again for six months, absent a competing offer or a material change in circumstances. The path is cleared, but the deal has not completed.

Bodycote also breaks a pattern this show has been tracking on the entry side. Lately sponsors have been settling for clubs and minority stakes, like Advent's 37 percent of InPost. Bodycote is the opposite: a contested, single-sponsor, full-control take-private. And the notable behavior isn't the winner. It's the two large sponsors who walked away.

Those walk-aways can be read two ways. It could be price discipline. Neither Apollo nor CVC would chase the asset to a return below its hurdle. Or the hurdle and financing math simply wouldn't clear at the level Veritas reached. That would be a more worrying signal about large-cap buyout feasibility. We can't separate the two. Without a disclosed multiple, or a premium to Bodycote's undisturbed share price, there's no honest way to call Veritas's price rich or cheap.

What is clear is that this is a deployment event, not a distribution. Bodycote's sellers are public shareholders, not a sponsor's limited partners. Nothing here returns cash to any LP. It puts dry powder to work and adds one more company to the inventory that will eventually need its own exit. And it adds that company to a system where exit channels are already narrow. Entry this week is adding to tomorrow's logjam.

One more thread, strictly on financing feasibility. Korea's Financial News reports on a September 30th report from KKR's credit division. We couldn't check the original KKR document, so treat the details as secondhand. The report describes "an unusually concentrated investment cycle" in technology borrowing for AI infrastructure. It says technology companies plan to invest nearly 8 trillion dollars in AI infrastructure by 2030. And it says about 20 percent of the investment-grade bond index could be exposed to AI-related risk. Christopher Sheldon, co-head of KKR's credit division, said there hasn't been enough discussion of the volatility if AI growth slows. He called it "a multi-trillion-dollar discussion."

Now the read-through, which is this show's inference, not KKR's claim. The credit markets financing the AI buildout are the same ones that must fund debt legs like AAR's 2.1 billion dollar package for MRO, or the next contested take-private. If AI borrowing keeps absorbing capacity, buyout financing competes harder for what's left. One caution on the messenger: KKR runs a large private-credit franchise of its own. It benefits commercially if public credit capacity looks constrained. So the warning is worth hearing, but not taking at face value.

Pull it together, and here's what this week's evidence says about the cycle. The one real exit cleared in a slice, on the buyer's clock, and on a bet about operating growth rather than financial engineering. One deal isn't a trend. But it is the shape to watch for. Bodycote shows two large sponsors disciplined enough, or constrained enough, to walk rather than chase. And no industry-wide distribution data moved this week, so nothing here says the logjam is easing. The binding risk is still the exit-and-distribution logjam. Partial realizations like MRO's make it harder to tell what actually counts as cash returned to LPs.

Watch three things to confirm or refute that read. First, whether the MRO deal closes in AAR's fiscal third quarter, and on what final financing terms. Second, whether Bodycote moves from a cleared path to a completed deal. Third, the alternative managers' third-quarter earnings in mid-to-late October, where fee-related earnings against realized carry will show whether slice-by-slice liquidity is spreading beyond one deal.
