Good morning. It's Thursday, October 29th, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

All week this show has argued that enterprise AI mostly redistributes existing IT spending rather than adding to it. The evidence was bundled seats at ServiceNow and Okta, a margin story at SAP, and consulting-mix shifts at IBM. Last night's earnings broke that thesis, but only at one layer of the stack. At the infrastructure layer, this looks like genuinely new money. At the application layer, it still looks like redistribution. The prints from Microsoft, Alphabet, and ServiceNow let us draw that line cleanly.

Start with Microsoft, where the headline number is a gap, not a growth rate. Per the company's earnings release, commercial remaining performance obligation rose 73 percent year over year, to 677 billion dollars. Microsoft Cloud revenue, the recognized number, grew 30 percent. So contracted backlog is compounding more than twice as fast as Microsoft can turn it into revenue. Azure itself grew 48 percent. Demand being signed today is running well ahead of demand being delivered today. At the infrastructure layer, that is net-new commitment, not a reshuffled budget.

One caution before going further. Backlog is not revenue, and Microsoft gives no breakdown of who signed it. Earlier this week, this show noted that Oracle's backlog rests on a few giant AI buyers. Microsoft's could look the same. So don't read Microsoft's backlog as proof of a broad customer base.

That demand has a price. Microsoft's additions to property and equipment, its own capex line, were 39.4 billion dollars for the quarter. A year earlier they were 19.4 billion, so spending roughly doubled. Guidance and free cash flow aren't confirmed yet, so I'll leave them out. On Tuesday this show covered Microsoft's voluntary buyout offer, which explicitly excluded data-center and sales roles. Put the two together. Microsoft is doubling what it spends on physical infrastructure while offering exits almost everywhere else. That's one capital-allocation decision, told in two chapters.

On adoption, Microsoft said customers with fifty thousand or more Copilot seats rose eightfold year over year. That points to enterprise-wide deployment inside large accounts, not pilot-stage dabbling. But it's a multiple off an undisclosed base. And no total paid-seat count was restated in what's available this quarter. So this measures concentration in big accounts, not breadth across the customer base. It's an enterprise-sales motion, not a self-serve one. The so-what at Microsoft: the money is new, it's contracted, and it's expensive. And it's landing disproportionately on the biggest buyers.

Google's print says the same thing about infrastructure, and it makes the capacity problem explicit. Google Cloud revenue more than doubled, up 103 percent year over year. Cloud operating margin rose to 36.2 percent, from 23.7 percent a year earlier. That margin expansion is the most important number in the print. It says the AI infrastructure build is converting into profitable revenue, not subsidized growth. One caveat on the top line. Google says growth was driven partly by TPU system sales. So some of that 103 percent is lumpier hardware revenue, not pure recurring consumption.

Google's cloud backlog reached 561 billion dollars. Management expects a bit more than 55 percent of it to turn into revenue within twenty-four months. That's management's expectation, not contracted timing. But it's an unusually specific one.

Now the part that matters most for the overbuild debate. Management said Google is supply-constrained on AI compute. It began using third-party compute capacity at the end of the third quarter, and plans to ramp that further in the fourth. That's a rare, concrete admission that capacity, not demand, is the binding constraint right now. It's the strongest available rebuttal to the idea that this capex cycle is speculative overbuild. But it speaks to near-term scarcity, not to long-run returns. And there's an inference worth stating plainly. If backlog conversion is gated by capacity, near-term cloud growth is set by how fast data centers come online. Capex timing becomes the revenue forecast.

This also connects to yesterday's Throughline, which reported OpenAI renting or buying Google TPU capacity. Google is now selling TPU systems to others while renting compute from others for itself. Scarcity is turning compute into a traded good among rivals. That blurs the line between supplier and competitor.

The bill for all of this now sits on Google's own cash flow statement. Capital expenditures were 51.4 billion dollars for the quarter, and free cash flow was negative 9.1 billion. Management expects capex to rise significantly again in 2027. For weeks, the financing strain in this cycle showed up at one remove, in supplier bonds and lender backstops. Now it's on a hyperscaler's own books. And Google's 561 billion dollar backlog is, in effect, the collateral the market is accepting for that spend.

One more Alphabet item, kept in its place. GAAP earnings per share fell from a year ago. The cause was an 8.2 billion dollar net non-operating expense, which management on the call tied to Alphabet's SpaceX equity stake. Keep that separate from the operating story. The margin expansion is the signal, and the earnings drop sits outside operations. The so-what for Google: demand is capacity-gated, and the cost of chasing it is now visible in its own free cash flow.

Every dollar of that backlog eventually runs through an application someone actually uses. So up the stack, to ServiceNow. This desk had been waiting on ServiceNow for a hard number on AI revenue, and it delivered one. AI net-new annual contract value grew 65 percent year over year. Management raised its full-year AI contract-value target to 1.7 billion dollars, up from an original goal of 1 billion. And year-to-date agentic consumption in production, not pilots, is up more than thirteen times from a year earlier.

Two cautions on those numbers. Contract value is a bookings metric, not recognized revenue. And the thirteen-times consumption figure has no disclosed base, so treat the multiple as directional. Still, ServiceNow's margin answers a worry this desk raised last week. Non-GAAP operating margin came in at 32.5 percent, 150 basis points above guidance. That pushes back on the fear that giving AI away inside existing subscriptions would squeeze margins, at least here, this quarter.

Now the context. ServiceNow's subscription business runs at roughly 4 billion dollars a quarter, growing about 20 percent. AI is a fast-growing slice of that business, not a separate new one. So at ServiceNow, AI is finally measurable, but it looks like a bigger share of an existing pie. That's the redistribution pattern this desk has tracked all week, in contrast to the hyperscalers below it. My inference from that split: the incremental enterprise AI dollar is accruing to hyperscalers and model vendors more than to SaaS seat-holders.

The model vendors sit between those two layers, and one filing this week shows where their money concentrates. Anthropic has filed its S-1 to list on the Nasdaq under the ticker ANT. Standing disclosure: Anthropic makes the model behind this show. These figures come to us secondhand, through summaries of the filing rather than the raw document, so hold them loosely. Net revenue retention was about 900 percent as of the end of September. And about 250 customers each generate 10 million dollars or more in trailing revenue.

Read that retention figure carefully. Nine hundred percent means last year's customers now spend about nine times as much. It's exactly what a hyper-growth year off a small base produces mechanically, and it will compress as the base matures. The customer tiers echo Microsoft's concentration point. Enterprise AI revenue keeps pooling in a small number of very large accounts. My inference: that leaves model vendors exposed to a handful of procurement decisions. Wednesday's briefing showed what one of those looks like. Rippling shifted much of its coding workload from Anthropic toward OpenAI on cost. A trailing retention figure, struck on a single date, wouldn't yet capture a move like that.

So here's where that leaves the stack. At the infrastructure layer, Microsoft and Google show genuine net-new demand. It's running ahead of capacity, and it's landing as real cost on real cash flow statements. At the application layer, ServiceNow shows AI finally becoming measurable, but mostly as a growing slice of an existing business. The redistribution thesis survives only at the SaaS application layer. Hyperscalers and model vendors are taking the new money, concentrated in a few huge accounts. Watch three things from here. First, Amazon's AWS print, for whether a third hyperscaler confirms the backlog pattern. Second, Palantir's earnings on November 2nd. Third, whether Anthropic's roadshow says how concentrated that ten-million-dollar tier really is.

That's today's briefing. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
