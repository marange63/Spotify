Good morning. It's August 8th, and today we're pulling back from last week's noise — the earnings prints, the EA close — to look at what the allocators and the liquidity plumbing are actually doing, because that's where the cycle's real tension sits.

Start with the biggest limited partner in the business. CalPERS — the largest U.S. public pension — has formally moved to a new Total Portfolio Approach, effective July 1st, and reporting attributes to that shift a private-equity target increase from 13 percent of the fund to 17 percent, with total private-markets exposure rising from 33 percent to 40 percent. That reporting comes from a secondary aggregator rather than a CalPERS filing stating the percentages outright, so treat the exact figures as attributed rather than primary-confirmed. What we can confirm directly from CalPERS's own press release is the structural piece: the board voted on this November 17th, and starting this July the Total Portfolio Approach replaces the old strategic asset allocation model entirely.

Here's the callback worth making explicit. This show has spent the better part of a week documenting a frozen exit window and a distribution problem that hasn't gone away. The largest allocator in the country's answer to that is not to pull back — it's to raise its target. That's a live test of conviction over liquidity risk, and right now conviction is winning.

Two things temper that. First, a target is not deployed capital. Raising an allocation ceiling sustains fundraising demand on paper, but it says nothing about cash actually going to work, and it locks in more exposure to the same liquidity mismatch if exits don't reopen. Second, on the benchmarking question — I want to correct something here rather than repeat an assumption. The natural read is that CalPERS softened its private-equity yardstick to some public-market-return-plus-premium comparison, which would flatter underperformance if the premium assumption runs generous. That is not what CalPERS actually did. Its own release describes a single whole-fund reference portfolio, split 75 percent equities and 25 percent bonds, replacing the eleven separate asset-class benchmarks it used before — not a private-equity-specific premium. That's arguably a bigger change, not a smaller one. Private-equity performance at CalPERS will now be judged against a blended, all-public-market yardstick with no private-markets comparator in it at all. So the risk isn't a flattering PE-specific hurdle. It's that PE-specific benchmarking effectively disappears into the whole-fund number.

So the biggest LP is committing more into an asset class it will now measure less precisely. That raises the obvious next question. If that committed capital isn't coming back through traditional exits, where does the cash actually come from?

The answer is the secondaries market, and it just posted a record. Evercore's data shows more than 120 billion dollars of total secondaries volume in the first half of this year alone — a record first half, up sharply from the prior year. Of that, GP-led deals accounted for 65 billion dollars, meaning sponsor-led transactions — where a manager moves an asset into a new vehicle it also controls — now make up more than half of all secondary volume. For context, all of 2025, itself a record year, totaled 226 billion dollars. This year's first half alone is already more than half that full-year number.

That's the mechanism. When the traditional door — an IPO or a strategic sale to a third party — stays shut, managers are increasingly generating liquidity by selling assets to vehicles they themselves sponsor and often still control. And by most accounts these continuation vehicles are pricing close to net asset value, which cuts two ways. It's genuine evidence that marks on the assets actually being transacted aren't wildly disconnected from reality. But it's also selective. Those are the trophy assets a GP chooses to roll forward, priced in a negotiation where the sponsor is effectively on both sides of the table, dealing with a small, repeat set of secondaries buyers. That is not the same as independent third-party price discovery across the broader unrealized portfolio. And as GP-leds become the de facto exit route rather than a niche tool, the definition of a "distribution" gets muddier — capital increasingly rotates between sponsor-controlled vehicles instead of genuinely leaving the system and landing back in an LP's account. Watch the gap between the headline distribution figures managers tout and the share of that liquidity that's actually third-party cash-out.

That same two-speed dynamic shows up one level down, at the manager level itself. Petershill — the Goldman Sachs-sponsored GP-stakes vehicle that buys minority interests in private-equity firms — announced it will delist from the London Stock Exchange and return more than 900 million dollars to its minority shareholders. That's a listed vehicle concluding public markets no longer value it properly, in a business that's supposed to be a diversified, fee-annuity bet on the industry's largest managers. Meanwhile Blue Owl keeps scaling in the same GP-stakes niche, continuing to acquire positions in large managers. The exact timing of Petershill's announcement relative to this week isn't independently confirmed, so take it as recent rather than pinned to a specific date. But directionally it fits the pattern: capital and credibility concentrating in the largest platforms, while smaller or listed vehicles in the same business can't sustain their public valuations.

Pull the threads together. The private-equity cycle isn't resolving its core problem so much as financializing around it. The exit and distribution logjam that's defined this show's coverage all week is still the binding constraint — cash back to LPs, not paper marks, remains the scarce resource. What's changed at the margin isn't that the logjam broke. It's that the industry's two biggest levers, LP allocation and secondaries plumbing, are both leaning further into the illiquidity rather than away from it. CalPERS is raising its target into a weaker measuring stick. The secondaries market is setting records by having managers trade assets among themselves rather than sell them to the outside world.

The near-term catalyst that would actually confirm or refute this read isn't another allocation headline or another record secondaries print. It's a genuine third-party exit — an IPO that clears or a strategic sale that isn't sponsor-to-sponsor, large enough to move realized distributions-to-paid-in for real. Until that shows up, treat this as a cycle where conviction is outrunning cash.
