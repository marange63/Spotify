Good morning, this is The Throughline for Sunday, September 13th, 2026. Today's headline: the most consequential move ahead of the Xi-Trump summit was never announced by anyone, on either side of the Pacific, which is exactly why nobody can negotiate against it.

Start with the macro backdrop. Strategic Power reports that a handful of Chinese rare-earth suppliers have quietly stopped shipping to US companies since early August, after Beijing sanctioned the American due-diligence group Western buyers use to vet those suppliers. No license was denied and no rule was invoked, so there is nothing for Washington to appeal and nothing for Xi to instruct anyone to reverse at the summit. The same briefing finds Washington's own export regime just as dependent on private choices, since Taiwan still hasn't closed the re-export loophole that lets blacklisted servers reach China.

Capital Markets Cross-Asset Radar has the counterweight to a week of hawkish pricing. Crude slid back to a hundred dollars a barrel on Friday, with Gulf and Iranian diplomats due to discuss Hormuz shipping on Monday, even as the IEA forecasts the sharpest annual contraction in oil demand since Covid. Odds of a Federal Reserve hike at Wednesday's meeting still sit near ninety percent, built mostly on a gasoline-driven inflation print. Gold barely moved on that print, which says the real-yield channel is beating the safe-haven channel for now.

That rate path is what the credit briefings are waiting on. Private Credit and the Corporate Credit Cycle has direct lenders charging more, 502 basis points on the latest quarterly wrap, up from 475 in the first quarter, while losing their best borrowers to banks refinancing them out. That is a lender repricing a shrinking book it knows is riskier, not one competing for volume. The genuinely independent data point came from Blue Owl, which sold a third of one fund's book to outside pension and insurance buyers at 99.7 percent of par. That is real evidence against the reflexive case that these marks are fiction.

Private Equity and the Deal Economy has NAV lending quietly changing purpose. It used to fund investor distributions, which regulators were already policing. Now it mainly funds new acquisitions and add-ons, which show up nowhere until the portfolio finally sells, and Apollo's record flagship fund has drawn only about half its target six months into marketing.

Into the AI and compute stack, where this week's evidence kept forcing the same question. Is this number self-reported, or checked by someone with no stake in it? Semiconductors and Compute Infrastructure has DeepSeek's new memory-compression technique knocking three percent off SK hynix and Samsung shares, on a claim that inference could get structurally lighter on memory. Micron's HBM is sold out through 2027, which bounds that risk to 2028 at the earliest. And the compression figures are DeepSeek's own, about DeepSeek's own product, so they take the same discount we apply to any vendor's bullish number.

Frontier AI Lab Competition has Anthropic's own threat report, putting hard numbers on nearly two hundred million distillation exchanges run by Chinese labs through the ordinary paid API. Detection lagged the extraction by months, and the report is self-published weeks before Anthropic sells stock. The sharper twist is Cognition's SWE-2, built on a Chinese open-weight model, now undercutting Claude Code on coding cost, so the threat has jumped from foreign labs to a domestic challenger.

AI Products and Applications has OpenAI shipping ChatGPT for Financial Services, aimed squarely at Bloomberg and FactSet workflows, but with no published pricing and only two named design partners. Harvey answered with a 550 million dollar raise and its own open-weight model. It also claims something no frontier lab can match with a benchmark, 80 percent of the Am Law 100, though that penetration figure is Harvey's own.

Enterprise Technology has Oracle guiding to a capital plan roughly the size of its own revenue guidance, partly funded by customer prepayments. It also has Salesforce paying 3.6 billion dollars for Fin, then running it as a hedge against its own flagship agent product rather than merging it in.

Into the applied frontier, where the same question shows up on a longer clock. Robotics and Physical AI has Agility Robotics' SEC filing, disclosing 1.8 million dollars of revenue against a 140 million dollar operating loss and a going-concern warning. Unitree, the one humanoid maker with a real market price, is down nearly 40 percent from its listing pop and still trades at 125 times revenue.

Space and the Orbital Economy has SpaceX's FCC filing committing Starship, for the first time, to an actual orbital insertion carrying next-generation Starlink satellites. AST SpaceMobile has real hardware in orbit, but its full-year guidance needs a second-half revenue step-change that nothing disclosed so far explains.

AI in Biopharma R&D closes the tour with Owkin's third major pharma license in four months, this one with Boehringer Ingelheim. That is a real pattern of enterprise spending, with no financial terms disclosed on any of the three deals.

One thread is worth naming plainly. Yesterday this program flagged a fault line running through nine unrelated markets at once: self-reported numbers stay convenient, and independently checked ones don't. Today pushes that one step further. Rare-earth suppliers refusing shipments, a re-export loophole nobody closes, those aren't even self-reported. Nobody claims authorship at all, and an action with no author is a harder thing to price than a biased number.
