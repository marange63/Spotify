Good morning. It's Monday, August 31st, and this is your private equity briefing.

This show has spent the past week showing you private-equity assets priced by whoever controls the sale. Inside the fund, through continuation vehicles. At the allocator, when a pension declines to roll. And in the pricing distribution itself, where the sponsor's chosen asset clears at par and everything else clears below it. So let's start today with the one live deal where nobody controls the sale yet.

Silver Lake has been in talks to take Workday private since Reuters broke the story on August 13th. The tape reacted with Workday's best single trading day in a decade, and trading was briefly halted. The move implied a value in the mid-forty-billion-dollar range, and I want to be careful with that figure. It is market-implied, not a term-sheet number, and different outlets have anchored it differently depending on whether they mean pre-news market capitalization, enterprise value, or post-premium math. Nothing has confirmed or killed those talks since roughly August 17th. No offer, no price, no financing structure, no named co-investor. That silence held through Workday's fiscal second-quarter results on August 27th, where the company declined to address the speculation, as companies in active deal talks typically do.

But that same release gave us the first new fact in nearly two weeks, and it came from the target rather than the sponsor. Workday's board authorized an open-ended repurchase of up to an additional four billion dollars of stock. Now set that against the balance sheet. Workday's cash, cash equivalents and marketable securities totaled three point four billion dollars as of July 31st. The authorization is larger than the entire liquid balance sheet behind it.

That gap is the interesting part, because it dictates the shape of the thing. A four-billion-dollar authorization against three point four billion of liquid assets cannot be executed as a single large tender without new financing. It has to be funded out of ongoing free cash flow, quarter by quarter. So whatever this is, it is a slow instrument. It stretches out over a year or more rather than resolving anything in one move.

Read one way, that is a board doing two useful things at once while a sponsor circles. Buying stock supports the reference price a takeover premium would be struck against. And spending down cash removes balance-sheet liquidity a buyer would otherwise have inherited and used. Read the other way, it is ordinary capital-return policy on a stock that derated this year, and the timing is coincidence dressed up by deal chatter. Workday did not characterize it either way. I am not going to assert intent the company has not stated, and neither should anyone else quoting this.

What survives regardless of intent is the mechanism, and that is the part that matters. If a target board can lean on its own cash flow to hold the price up, then the binding constraint on a forty-billion-dollar-plus software take-private is the target's balance sheet and its shareholders, not the sponsor's ability to raise debt. That inverts what this beat took away from Electronic Arts' fifty-five-billion-dollar buyout closing on August 4th, where a single bank committed twenty billion dollars of debt for one transaction. The conclusion we drew on Wednesday was that financing capacity at the top of the market is not the constraint, and that exit capacity below it is. Workday tests a third possibility. Financing capacity may be irrelevant if the seller simply will not clear.

There is a read-across if these talks die quietly, and it is worth pricing now rather than after the fact. A collapse would not be read as a financing failure. It would be read as sponsors declining to underwrite AI-disruption risk in legacy enterprise software at this size, at any plausible premium. That pushes the sponsor bid down-market, toward the few-billion-dollar and sub-billion-dollar software take-privates this beat has tracked all month, where the diligence problem is smaller and the equity check is coverable by one fund. The megadeal tier would stay open for infrastructure and sovereign-backed consortia and closed for growth-challenged software. That is a narrower exit window than the headlines imply, not a wider one.

Now, if Workday is this week's exception, here is the rule getting institutionalized elsewhere. EQT agreed back in January to buy the secondaries firm Coller Capital, and that deal is sitting inside its own stated closing window right now. EQT guided to mid-to-late third quarter. No confirmation of a close has surfaced, so treat it as pending at its deadline rather than done. The terms are three point two billion dollars upfront, on a cash- and debt-free basis, plus up to five hundred million more tied to performance.

Think about what EQT is actually buying there. Coller does not manufacture new liquidity. It prices continuation vehicles, the structure this show noted last week had grown to a record share of secondaries volume. So EQT is paying billions for a management-fee annuity attached to the liquidity drought itself. That is a revealing thing to pay for. The only way a check that size amortizes is if continuation vehicles remain the industry's default exit route for years. The purchase price is therefore a forecast, and the forecast is that the logjam persists long enough to be a business rather than a phase.

The obvious objection is conflict of interest. A sponsor would own the shop that prices continuation vehicles, potentially including vehicles holding that same sponsor's assets. That objection is fair but it is not new architecture. Blackstone has owned Strategic Partners for years, and Ares and Blue Owl sit in comparable positions, with information walls and limited-partner advisory-committee consent as the standard check. This is consolidation of an existing model, not the invention of an abuse.

The sharper point is economic rather than ethical. Whoever owns the pricing venue increasingly sets the observable price for the whole asset class. Private equity has no exchange. Its nearest thing to a public quote is what a secondaries buyer will pay. Concentrate that bid into a handful of multi-strategy platforms, and the number of genuinely independent bidders on any given continuation vehicle falls. Those same platforms are sellers elsewhere in the market. So the reference price converges toward a small group with positions on both sides of it. That is a quieter problem than self-dealing, and a more durable one.

Follow that same sponsor-set mark one step down the ownership chain, because it is now being sold to a holder base with none of an institutional limited partner's protections. Semi-liquid, evergreen private-markets vehicles are approaching roughly five hundred billion dollars in assets, having grown more than thirty percent over the twelve months to last September, with a record number of launches in 2025 and more already this year. Two caveats before anyone leans on that. It spans private credit, real estate and infrastructure alongside private equity, and the equity-only slice is not broken out, so do not use it as a private-equity number. And the growth rate runs to last September, which makes it a trend read rather than a current print.

The structural point holds across all of those asset classes anyway. These are open-ended vehicles with periodic redemption windows, and the price at which an investor redeems is a net asset value the manager itself calculates. I would resist the reflexive liquidity-mismatch scare here. Periodic gating is a disclosed design feature, not a hidden flaw, and rapid growth may simply be distribution working. The narrower and genuinely unresolved question is the one this beat has been circling all week. The retail investor's exit price is the same mark the institutional side spent the week arguing about, except here there is no advisory committee, no side letter and no negotiated consent right to push back with.

That changes where stress would first become visible. On the institutional side, a disputed mark surfaces slowly, in an advisory committee or a fundraising conversation. In an evergreen vehicle, it surfaces as a redemption queue. Record launches into a market that cannot clear the inventory it already owns are pushing the eventual adjustment onto the holders least equipped to contest it, and onto a mechanism that is visible to everyone at once.

So where does the cycle actually stand. The exit-and-distribution logjam is still the binding constraint, and nothing this week loosened it. Workday is the rare case testing whether an arm's-length market can still set the price at all, and the answer so far is that the seller is bidding against the buyer. Watch three things from here. Whether the Workday talks produce a signed deal or go quiet for good. Whether EQT confirms the Coller close inside its own stated window, or lets it slip. And how September quarter-end marks look when they land in late-October earnings, which is where a stale mark finally has to show itself.

That's the briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
