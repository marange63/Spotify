Cautious Optimism Briefings

AI in Biopharma R&D

Transcript · September 3, 2026

Good morning. It's September 3rd, and this is your briefing on AI in biopharma R&D.

This beat has spent a full week landing on the same conclusion. Tempus selling access to its clinical records on the 28th. Roche buying Flatiron and TriNetX outright on the 31st. Genentech optioning a target out of Recursion's image library on the 1st. Every time, leverage sat with whoever owns the data, not whoever runs the model. Today the question is what the model layer itself is doing about that, and who is allowed to look inside it. The answer, from four items across four companies, is that the model layer is converging on the same instinct as the data layer. Generate something proprietary, then control who gets to touch it. But the clean version of that story, in which nobody releases anything, turns out to be wrong in an instructive way.

Start with Xaira Therapeutics, the well-funded and famously quiet AI drug-discovery startup. In March it put out its first model, X-Cell, as a bioRxiv preprint that has not been peer reviewed. The model runs 4.9 billion parameters, trained on X-Atlas Pisces, a proprietary Perturb-seq atlas of 25.6 million cells. The company describes that atlas as the largest genome-wide CRISPRi dataset ever reported. The headline claim is about perturbation prediction, meaning forecasting how a cell's gene expression shifts when you knock out a given gene. Xaira says that prediction follows a power law as you scale the model, with an exponent that GEN News describes as matching large language models. If that holds, it's the first real scaling-law result in virtual-cell biology, not just a bigger model with a better demo. Nobody outside the company has benchmarked it, because it is Xaira's model on Xaira's data, scored by Xaira.

Here's the catch, and it's a catch this beat almost got wrong. The instinct is to say Xaira is sealing the vault. Keep the model, keep the data, keep the moat. That's not quite what's happening. Xaira's own GitHub page for X-Cell states, verbatim, that weights and inference code are "coming soon." Not withheld. Delayed. And a subset of the underlying dataset is already posted publicly, on Hugging Face, under a non-commercial share-alike license. So this isn't a sealed vault. It's closure by delay and closure by license. You can look, you just can't build a competing commercial product on what you see. What Xaira is protecting isn't secrecy for its own sake. It's commercial exclusivity over an asset it's still willing to show off.

That forces an explicit update to this beat's standing read. For five sessions I've drawn a line between owning the data and running the model, and given all the leverage to the data side. If perturbation prediction really scales with data on a power law, that line partly dissolves. The weights become the dataset in compressed, transferable form, and closing the weights is the same defensive act as closing the dataset. The two are one asset, which is why the release calendar for both runs on the same clock.

The cost-curve question is the one that actually matters for capital allocation, and here the honest answer is that it can't yet be quantified. Language-model scaling is bought with compute, which gets cheaper every year. Virtual-cell scaling is bought with Perturb-seq. That means wet-lab labor, reagents, and cell culture at industrial scale, none of which sits on anything like a Moore's-law curve. Xaira has not disclosed what a cell of that atlas costs to generate, and neither has anyone else building one, so the single number that would settle whether this scaling law is affordable simply isn't public. There's a second problem with the analogy. A scaling exponent measured on training loss is a very different animal from one measured on held-out generalization, and those two curves can diverge sharply. What has been published about X-Cell so far doesn't say which of those two curves produced the headline number. So take the scaling claim as real and interesting, and take the implied cost efficiency as unproven. No wet-lab confirmation of any X-Cell-derived prediction has been reported either. This is a loss-curve result, not a druggable-target result, and the gap between those two things is exactly where this field keeps overselling itself.

Now the disclosure pattern gets cleaner, and less generous, at Isomorphic Labs. Alphabet's drug-design unit published a technical report on its Isomorphic Drug Design Engine. It claims a 50 percent success rate in the toughest bin of the Runs N' Poses benchmark, a test built specifically for binding pockets the model has never seen. For scale, AlphaFold 3 scores 23.3 percent on the hardest protein-ligand structure benchmarks. So Isomorphic is claiming roughly double the public frontier on exactly the cases that matter for novel chemistry. Unlike Xaira, Isomorphic isn't promising anything is coming. It published the score and kept the architecture and training methodology dark, and no third party has reproduced the delta. That's the purer version of the disclosure-asymmetry trade. You get the number that sells access, not the detail that would let you audit it. And the number keeps climbing while the clinical calendar keeps slipping. Isomorphic's first AI-designed cancer candidate was supposed to reach Phase 1 by the end of 2025. As of this year's guidance from chief executive Demis Hassabis, that's now end of 2026, and there is still no confirmed trial registration. A benchmark that doubles and a first-in-human date that slips a year are the same company's design capability and translation capability moving in opposite directions. Buyers of this technology are being asked to price a capability they cannot independently verify, against a clinical timeline that keeps moving.

The counter-move belongs to Pfizer, and it's the incumbent's move rather than the startup's. Pfizer partnered with Boltz, the open-source team whose Boltz-1 is described as the first fully commercially accessible open model to reach AlphaFold-3-level accuracy. The plan is to fine-tune those open weights on Pfizer's own historical assay data, with Boltz scientists embedded inside Pfizer's discovery teams. That converges with the lead rather than contradicting it. If a credible open baseline exists, the differentiator stops being the architecture and becomes the proprietary corpus behind it. Pfizer doesn't need to build or buy a closed engine, because it already owns decades of internal data no startup can replicate. The moat moves from the model to the archive, which caps what a closed-model vendor can charge for architecture alone. Hold the obvious limit on it, though. Financial terms weren't disclosed beyond Boltz's separate 28-million-dollar seed round, and no program, cycle time, or hit rate has been attached to the collaboration. This is a signed partnership, not a result.

Sanofi is running the same play one layer down, in operations rather than discovery. It expanded its Owkin partnership into a five-year license for K Pro, Owkin's AI scientist, to build proprietary agentic systems embedded across its own R&D workflows. No deal value and no productivity baseline were disclosed, and nothing was ever published from the 2021 relationship that preceded it, so treat it as a direction rather than a result. But the build-versus-buy answer keeps coming back the same way at both companies. Build it on data you already own, and keep it.

Pull it together. None of this touches Phase II. That remains the standing gap on this beat, and nothing today changes it. What's new is the shape of the argument, not the stage of the pipeline. For the AI-discovery equities, valuation should be tracking the scale and exclusivity of proprietary wet-lab data generation, not benchmark scores. That raises the capital intensity of staying competitive, and it favors balance sheets like Isomorphic's and Xaira's over thinly funded venture platforms. For closed-model vendors, a credible open baseline that a top-five pharma is willing to build on compresses what they can charge for architecture alone, pushing them toward selling exclusivity and co-development instead of access. For tools and instruments vendors, a data-scaling moat is a standing purchase order, because industrial-scale Perturb-seq shows up in consumables and instrument demand years before it shows up in anyone's pipeline. And on R&D spend the standing read holds, with one new wrinkle. This is still reallocation toward earlier and cheaper decisions rather than reduction, but the spend now moves out of external licensing and into internal, capex-like screening infrastructure. The falsification test going forward is simple. An independently replicated benchmark, a released dataset with no strings attached, or a dosed patient. Until one of those arrives, the field is still scoring itself.
