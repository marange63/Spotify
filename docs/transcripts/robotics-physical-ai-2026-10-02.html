Cautious Optimism Briefings

Robotics & Physical AI

Transcript · October 2, 2026

Good morning. It's October 2nd, 2026, and this week the constraints that showed up in physical AI were memory chips, magnets, and capital, not model capability.

Start with Tesla, because yesterday brought something this beat hasn't had in a month: a statement from the company itself. Elon Musk posted that Tesla cut the memory on its AI5 chip in half, to 72 gigabytes. On AI6 it cut memory by a third, to 144 gigabytes. In his words, this was "the only way to get enough volume for Optimus production," and it "greatly reduces cost." Read that sentence carefully. It's an admission that memory supply and cost, not demand and not robot capability, were gating the Optimus build plan. It is not evidence that the volume itself exists.

This connects to the story our chip coverage has run all week. The biggest buyers have been locking up memory supply through long-term commitments and contracted capacity. Today that squeeze shows up for the first time as a design change inside a robot. The products differ. Tesla's chips use low-power DRAM, not the high-bandwidth memory stacked beside data-center GPUs. But the root cause is the same: DRAM is tight. And if Tesla, with custom silicon and enormous purchasing scale, has to cut memory to get volume, consider the humanoid makers buying merchant chips. They take memory pricing as given, with far less leverage. That last point is this desk's inference, not reporting.

Musk says the cut will have, quote, "a negligible effect on Optimus performance." Treat that as his claim. Nobody outside Tesla has benchmarked it. There's also a model-layer implication, and this is inference too. Whatever robot policy runs on the device now has to fit in half the memory AI5 was designed around. That likely means a smaller on-board model, or more reliance on off-board compute. Either way, memory economics are now shaping which brain actually ships on the body. That's the reverse of the usual story, where the model dictates the hardware.

Now set this against the Optimus numbers this desk has carried for a month. Every one of them came from supply-chain or secondary reporting, not from Tesla. The latest had parts on order for fifteen thousand units and a ramp toward twenty-five hundred a week by year-end. The first thing Tesla has said in its own words is about what it can't get enough of, not what it's shipping.

The physical commitments are real. Tesla stripped its fourteen-year-old Model S and X line at Fremont in 46 days to make room for Optimus. It's targeting a million units a year there, and ten million at a new Texas plant. But those are nameplate targets, not run-rates, and Tesla has disclosed no production figure. So what: the binding constraint on Optimus this week isn't a robot that can't do the job. It's a chip supply chain that can't yet feed the build.

Memory is the brain's input. The body has its own input problem. China shipped only 512 metric tons of rare-earth permanent magnets to the US in August. That's a 21 percent drop in a single month, according to the trade outlet Discovery Alert, which reads it as Beijing using export licensing as a pressure tool. One month is a noisy signal, so hold that reading loosely. The timing still matters, because the US-China trade truce expires November 10th. Magnets containing dysprosium and terbium require a Chinese export license. Humanoid and industrial-robot actuators depend on exactly these magnets. No robot maker has reported a delay or cost hit from this tightening. The link is this desk's structural inference, not an observed impact. So what: whether it's the brain's memory or the body's magnets, the humanoid supply chain doesn't control its own bottlenecks.

Inputs are one constraint. Capital is the other. You'll recall the three-part listing test Beijing's securities regulator is reportedly applying to humanoid IPOs: sustainable revenue, narrowing losses, and owned core technology. We covered it in depth this week, so just the callback. The new data point is on the private side. Trade-press figures put China's embodied-AI investment at 47 billion yuan in the second quarter. That's more than double the first quarter, and six times the year-earlier level. One caution: a surge in the quarter before the listing bar tightened may be money racing the IPO window, not conviction about demand. Either way, private money is flooding in just as the public exit narrows. And the first Western humanoid heading to public markets has just walked into the same test.

That's Agility Robotics. On September 30th it unveiled its Digit 5 humanoid. It's going public through a SPAC merger with Churchill Capital Corp the Eleventh, at a $2.5 billion valuation. Agility says it has more than $300 million in multi-year customer orders.

Start with the arithmetic. That order is a single three-year robots-as-a-service contract for a thousand robots. Divide it out and you get roughly $100,000 per robot per year. That's this desk's arithmetic on a "more than" headline number, not a disclosed price. But it's the unit-economics anchor to watch against the labor cost a Digit actually displaces.

Now the discounts, and the merger filing makes them steeper than they first look. Agility's S-4 registration statement, as summarized by an independent analyst, says the customer is also a related party. That's a bigger problem than ordinary customer concentration. The same filing shows that most of Agility's 2025 revenue also came from related parties: $1.13 million out of $1.78 million. So both the headline order and the existing revenue base lean on connected counterparties, not independent market demand. We don't know who the customer is, or whether it's the same party behind last year's sales. There's a second wrinkle. Agility will issue that customer 453 warrants for every robot it sources, about 453,000 if it takes all thousand. The customer gets equity for buying. That's a cost against the headline order, not a pure win.

Then losses. The filing shows a $140 million operating loss on that $1.78 million of sales. Even before overhead, Agility's direct costs ran about two and a half times its revenue. Management also flags substantial doubt about the company's ability to continue as a going concern. And the analyst's read of the filing has operating spending rising year over year, not falling. So run Agility through Beijing's test. It owns its technology. But on revenue quality and on narrowing losses, it doesn't clear the bar. A SPAC lets it reach public markets anyway.

Two more discounts. Digit 5 doesn't reach early-access customers until the first half of 2027, so this order is for a robot no customer has run in production. And the SPAC's roughly $620 million raise is about $420 million of trust cash plus a $200 million PIPE led by Foxconn. The trust figure assumes no redemptions, and it can shrink before the vote. The deal still needs Churchill shareholder approval and SEC review. So what: Agility is the order-book-versus-revenue distinction at its sharpest. Until deployment counts show up, discount headline humanoid order values across the peer set, and ask who the customer is.

Boston Dynamics offers the counterpoint, briefly. On September 21st it opened a training center at Hyundai's Georgia Metaplant. That's a step toward Hyundai's plan for roughly twenty-five thousand Atlas units, which we've covered before. Boston Dynamics' customer is its owner, Hyundai, so it sidesteps the procurement fight Agility faces. But Euronews reported this week that Boston Dynamics is unlikely to pursue a stock listing next year. The reason given: Atlas hasn't been deployed at scale, and the company remains unprofitable. And a training center is a testbed, not a production deployment. So there are two Western humanoid makers on two capital paths. Agility is going public now on a related-party order book. Boston Dynamics has a captive customer and no near-term listing. Neither has yet shown deployment-scale, arm's-length revenue.

Pulling it together. What's genuinely inflecting is industrial commitment to building capacity: Fremont repurposed, a Hyundai training center open, an Agility order signed, however it's structured. What's still unproven is paid humanoid work at scale, for a customer with no other reason to buy. This week's binding constraints weren't model capability. They were memory, magnets, and the quality of capital. Watch three things next. First, Tesla's October earnings call, the first chance for a primary Optimus output number and any memory-cost commentary. Second, the November 10th rare-earth truce deadline. Third, the Churchill shareholder vote on Agility, and how heavy the redemptions run.

That's the briefing. Have a good day.
