Good morning, and welcome to the Semiconductors and Compute briefing for Monday, September 21st, 2026.

There are ten High-NA EUV systems operating at four chipmakers worldwide, with three more in shipment or installation. That is a smaller number than the narrative around it. Twice in the past week this briefing said the High-NA story could not be written as a number, because nobody had put a tool count on the record. Now someone has.

Two details sharpen it. Fleet availability reached 84 percent in July, against a target of 90 percent by the fourth quarter. And the report puts the price at roughly 380 to 400 million dollars per unit. All three of those figures come from a single secondary outlet, TechTimes. They do not come from ASML. Treat the count as reported, not confirmed.

The dates matter more than the count. Samsung is targeting High-NA for high-volume DRAM by 2028. TSMC has committed to volume logic production starting in 2030. Those are different products on different clocks, so do not score them against each other. The same report says TSMC, ASML, Samsung and Intel formed a 12-inch photomask initiative on September 8th, designed to eliminate the reticle stitching penalty by 2033.

So what does that add up to? Lithography is not the variable that decides who ships accelerators in 2027. The tool count does not crown Intel for being early. It also does not mark TSMC as late. TSMC choosing 2030 is a statement about its own density economics. This reframes the race. It does not settle it.

There is a second-order effect for equipment. ASML's High-NA order book looks memory-led in 2028 and TSMC-led only in 2030. That is a very different revenue curve from the one implied by the phrase four chipmakers are using it. And a stitching penalty that lasts until 2033 caps single-die area for every High-NA product shipping this decade. That pushes accelerator scaling further onto chiplets and advanced packaging.

If lithography is not the axis, the next question is yield. Here the evidence moved this week. Korean press, relayed by TrendForce, reports that Samsung's 2nm gate-all-around yield has climbed to over 70 percent. It was below 50 percent at the start of 2026. Say this plainly. It is not confirmed by Samsung. And a foundry negotiating price with a customer has an obvious interest in a high yield number circulating.

Intel is the other half of this. Its chief financial officer told Deutsche Bank's conference that 14A defect density is tracking better than Intel's own target curve. He called it the fastest pace of defect reduction since 22nm. Intel plans to release the 14A 0.9 process design kit in October, with risk production reported for the second half of 2027. He also said customer discussions are shifting from reviewing technical data toward questions about available capacity and supply. That is the oldest tell in foundry sales. It has no named customer and no order attached.

Still, the honest read is that on capability, a non-TSMC leading edge is more credible than it was in January. But capability does not answer the question that matters to a buyer. What does it cost?

That brings us to the evidence that is the heart of this briefing. TrendForce reports that Qualcomm's application processor on Samsung's 2nm could be pushed back to next year. The cause is prolonged price negotiations. The reason Samsung gives is telling. After securing major customers such as Tesla and Broadcom, it is in a stronger position to evaluate contract terms more selectively. A separate TrendForce line says Samsung's first Taylor fab is reportedly already fully booked for 2nm before operations begin. That is a booking claim, with no wafer count or utilization figure behind it.

Read that together. A customer is willing to slip a flagship chip into 2027 rather than accept Samsung's terms. A foundry with a pre-sold line has no need to discount to fill it. That is a second source pricing like an incumbent.

TSMC's own pricing points the same way. A Nikkei Asia report from July, relayed by TradingKey, says TSMC plans a 5 to 10 percent wafer price increase in 2027, covering both advanced and mature processes. It adds a further 10 to 15 percent surcharge for high-performance-computing orders that exceed agreed quantities. This is a standing July report, not new this week, and it is not a TSMC announcement. But look at its shape. TSMC's marginal price rises fastest for the biggest accelerator buyers, exactly where they exceed their allocation. I have no verifiable per-wafer price for 2nm against 3nm, so I will not quantify the gap.

So the so-what is this. Diversification buys supply-chain resilience. It buys no cost relief. A second source that will not discount is a duopoly forming, and the party that absorbs it is the fabless customer's gross margin.

Now apply a standing test to the week's customer datapoints. The test is announcement versus commitment. OpenAI said it is working with Samsung Foundry on joint production and research. Neither company disclosed whether Samsung Foundry has a signed production contract. So no node, volume or date can be asserted. Intel has capacity conversations and no named customer. And there is a correction to our own record.

On the 19th, this briefing ran the reported talks between SK hynix and Intel on making memory at Intel's Ohio site. We said that if real, they would give Intel Foundry its first credible anchor tenant that is not a logic customer. On September 18th, SK hynix said, quote, "No plan is confirmed with Intel." That is not a denial that talks occurred. Korean companies routinely use exactly that formulation while negotiating. But it removes the headline. This beat has kept scoring American onshoring items as directionally real and numerically empty. This is the first one to go to zero. It also means Intel's second-source credibility now rests on 14A, not on a memory tenant.

Which leads to the last move. You can qualify a second fab. You cannot qualify a second jurisdiction. Diversifying supply is not the same as diversifying permission.

On that front, the enforcement frontier is moving from where chips are sold to where compute is rented. Commerce is reportedly preparing a rule that would make it illegal to rent Nvidia GPU computing time to Chinese AI companies through servers in Thailand and Singapore. It may share the draft with industry trade groups as early as September. Two cautions. This is reported and drafted, not published. There is no Federal Register text. And export-control lawyers say Commerce almost certainly lacks the statutory authority to enforce it. Senator McCormick makes the same point in support of legislation. His argument is that under current law bad actors can train models on advanced chips under United States jurisdiction, and the Bureau of Industry and Security has no authority to require a license.

The consensus reads a rule like this as a tightening. I would read it two other ways. A rule Commerce cannot lawfully enforce, floated days before a leaders' meeting, looks more like leverage than policy. And the private regime may already bind harder. Nvidia deployed field compliance teams to physically inspect data centers across Southeast Asia. It also cut its Asian buyer whitelist by more than half. That constrains channel volume more than an unenforceable rental ban would. And it was imposed by the vendor with the most to lose.

There is a second-order effect on the other side of the line. If the rule ever survives the authority problem, it pushes Chinese frontier training further onto domestic accelerators. Those accelerators are already constrained by memory supply. Chinese compute would then be rationed by two stacked American controls, one on memory and one on rented capacity, rather than by SMIC wafer output.

Now the asymmetry on this side. Nvidia guided its current quarter to 108 billion dollars, against 96.2 billion dollars in the quarter that ended in July. It said it is not assuming any data center compute revenue from China in that outlook. So the drafted rule threatens a number already set at zero. A summit outcome that traded access for rare earths would be pure upside that is not in the guide. The Trump and Xi meeting at the White House is expected later this month. Nothing from it has happened yet.

To restate the thesis. A credible second source at the leading edge is arriving. This week's evidence shows it behaving as a price-setter, not a price-breaker. Two tests will tell us if that holds. First, whether Intel names an external 14A customer, with the October design kit as the first dated checkpoint. Second, whether Samsung's 2nm price stance survives when the Qualcomm negotiation resolves. Micron reports its fiscal fourth quarter on September 30th. That is the next scheduled test on the memory side.

That is the briefing for today. Thanks for listening.
