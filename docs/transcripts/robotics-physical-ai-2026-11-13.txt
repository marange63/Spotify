Good morning. It's Friday, November 13th, and this is your robotics and physical AI briefing.

The headline this week isn't a demo. It's a production forecast, and that distinction matters. On Thursday, November 12th, J.P. Morgan published a note on US humanoid robot production. According to Reuters coverage of the note, carried by Investing.com and other outlets, US output will hit 300,000 to 400,000 units by 2030. It rises to about 1.5 million units by 2035. One sourcing note: we're reading the wire coverage, not the report itself, so treat the bank's methodology as a black box.

Even taken at face value, notice what kind of count this is. It counts robots built. It does not count robots sold, robots deployed, or robots doing reliable paid work. All week, this desk has been grading the counts this sector offers. On Tuesday it was store-trial pass rates. On Wednesday it was tasks completed at least once. Yesterday it was robotaxi registrations. A five-year production forecast belongs on the same ladder, on the lowest rung. It says nothing about who's buying, at what price, or with what payback.

Now look at who is supposed to build those units. J.P. Morgan says most US manufacturers outside Tesla are targeting 10,000 to 30,000 units annually. Note the verb. That's a target, not current output. Here's our inference from that range. Even several non-Tesla makers at the top of it would leave the 2030 path well short of 400,000. So the bulk of the bull case has to come from one company. Tesla's Optimus program has repeatedly slipped its own production timelines. That's a general pattern, not a figure from this note. But it means the upper end of the bank's range is effectively a Tesla execution bet, presented as an industry forecast.

So if the volume depends on one company, the next question is what stops everyone else. J.P. Morgan names four binding bottlenecks: hands, actuators, the brain, and production capacity. Now notice what's missing from that list. Demand. Price. Payback period. Who is buying hundreds of thousands of humanoids a year, and why? A list made only of supply-side constraints implicitly treats demand as solved. That's the bull framing, not a neutral one. And the industry's own behavior cuts against it. Last Saturday we covered vendors drifting toward renting robots rather than selling them. Our read is that you rent when buyers won't yet underwrite the payback themselves.

Take the hardware bottlenecks first. Yesterday this desk flagged that China's heavy rare-earth licensing was never touched by Beijing's suspension renewal. That's the regime governing actuator-grade magnets. Hands and actuators are exactly where that chokepoint bites. If they're the gating factor, value plausibly accrues to the component layer underneath the robot brands. That means motors, strain-wave gears, and tactile sensors. J.P. Morgan expects the market for key parts to approach $30 billion by 2035. That covers actuators, motors, and vision systems. Be precise about what that number is. The coverage doesn't say whether it's US or global. It gives no per-unit cost assumption. So don't divide it by the unit forecast to get a price per robot. Read it only as a pool size.

There are two readings of that pool. The bull case is a supplier trade: own the motor and actuator vendors, not the integrators. The bear case is that component manufacturing is where Chinese supply chains already set the cost curve, starting with the magnets. That compresses margins for anyone building those parts domestically. Either way, the so-what is the same. If J.P. Morgan is right about the bottlenecks, the investable question sits in the component layer, not the logo on the robot.

That leads to the strategic comparison. China has its own number, and it's for this year, not 2030. According to the trade outlet Gasgoo, a deputy director at China's industry ministry told a July press conference that annual humanoid production is on track to top 100,000 units this year. Two caveats. First, that's a projection made in July, and it counts production, not shipments. Second, it doesn't break out capability class. It likely blends education, research, and entertainment units with general-purpose industrial humanoids. AgiBot, one of China's leading humanoid makers, is the company whose store trial we covered Tuesday, where not every unit passed. So the raw comparison flatters China on volume, the dimension that matters least. Still, on unit scale, the US 2030 path reads as a catch-up curve, not a lead.

Washington's counter-lever sits in the same Reuters coverage. It reports a Trump administration finding that imported robotics and industrial machinery pose national security risks. Per the coverage, that finding could lead to tariffs or other measures to boost domestic production. Read that carefully. The coverage describes a finding. It does not describe tariffs. Some legal and trade trackers suggest the process has already moved further, toward directed trade negotiations. We couldn't confirm that against a primary document, so don't treat it as settled. But don't treat the tariff risk as remote either. Here's the second-order effect, and it's our inference. If tariffs do come, US humanoid builders that depend on Chinese actuators and motors would see input costs rise. That pushes reshoring of the component layer specifically. So the trade lever and the bottleneck list point at the same place: the parts, not the robot brands.

Three of the four bottlenecks are physical. The fourth is the brain, and that's where the money disagrees with the bank. Physical Intelligence, the vision-language-action foundation model startup, is in talks to raise again. Bloomberg reported those talks in late October, so this isn't this week's news. The round hadn't closed as of the latest coverage. Existing backers Lux Capital and Avenir are in talks to lead. The company has no revenue, according to Bloomberg. The last round we can confirm was a $600 million Series B in November 2025. CapitalG led it, at a $5.6 billion post-money valuation. Reporting points to a sharply higher mark this time, though we can't source the exact figure. If it closes that way, that's a markup on no revenue.

Now set that against Nvidia. As we reported yesterday, secondary conference coverage says Nvidia's GR00T 2 weights will be released as open source in December. License terms are still unpublished. If a credible general-purpose robot brain becomes a free download, a pre-revenue brain lab's valuation has to rest on something else. That means proprietary data, deployed fleets, or talent. The bull read is that investors see the brain as the layer that eventually captures the most value, whatever gets open-sourced underneath it. The bear read is that this valuation is running ahead of the hardware cycle J.P. Morgan describes. Open weights could compress it fast. Neither read is resolved yet. There's a second-order effect here too, and again it's our inference. If the brain commoditizes while hardware stays supply-constrained, the margin pool in humanoids migrates toward whoever controls components and factory capacity. On today's map, that tilts toward Chinese supply chains. So the robot brain is the one layer private markets price as scarce, and the one most at risk of becoming free.

Pulling the threads together. What's inflecting this week is the frame. A major bank has put a quantified production path and an explicit bottleneck list around the humanoid capex story. It's the first sell-side unit forecast this desk has covered, and a step up from demo coverage. What's still a demo is everything downstream of production: reliable, paid deployment at volume. The biggest open question is the one the note leaves out. Who buys hundreds of thousands of humanoids a year, and at what payback? Watch three catalysts. First, whether the Physical Intelligence round closes, and at what mark. Second, whether Nvidia's GR00T 2 weights ship in December, and under what license. Third, whether the national-security finding on imported robotics turns into actual tariffs or other measures.

That's the briefing. Have a good weekend, and I'll be back with more next week.
