Space & the Orbital Economy

Transcript · October 12, 2026

Good morning, it's Monday, October 12th, and this is your space and orbital economy briefing.

Here's today's thread. SpaceX is buying up scarce inputs, first spectrum and now possibly compute, and last week the market marked down nearly everyone standing near it. But the sharpest piece of that story, what it means for AST SpaceMobile, is more contested than Friday's tape suggested.

Start with a quick re-ground. SpaceX agreed to acquire up to fourteen megahertz of paired spectrum in the eight-hundred-megahertz band from Grain Management. Neither company has disclosed the price, and the deal still needs FCC approval. SpaceX says the spectrum would connect its satellite-to-mobile Starlink constellation with an advanced terrestrial network. You heard those terms here on Sunday. What's new is the market's full verdict, and a wrinkle in how to read it.

On Friday, AT&T and Verizon each dropped about seven percent, per 24/7 Wall Street. SpaceX's own stock, ticker SPCX, climbed about four percent. AST SpaceMobile fell twelve percent the same day, to just over fifty dollars a share. That cuts against this desk's own read. On Thursday we floated the idea that carriers facing a SpaceX that can go around them might lean harder on AST as a counterweight. On Sunday we said the Grain deal tilted the evidence toward that reading. Friday's move looks like the market voting the other way.

There are two ways to read that vote. The first is zero-sum. Low-band spectrum is scarce, and every block SpaceX owns is a block AST can't. On that reading, AST simply lost an option. The second is about timing. The counterweight thesis works through carrier contracts that take quarters to show up, and a one-day selloff can't price those. Both can be true on different horizons.

Now dig one layer deeper, because the evidence for the first reading is thinner than the headline. The only outlet framing this as SpaceX "closing off" AST's low-band option is 24/7 Wall Street. That framing is the outlet's own analysis. It cites no AST statement, no named analyst, and no filing. Meanwhile, two sell-side notes this week read the deal the opposite way. Berenberg reportedly kept its buy rating on AST, arguing the deal could make the company more strategically relevant to carriers. Roth Capital reportedly said the deal could strengthen AST's relationships with major U.S. telecom operators. So Friday's selloff looks less like a settled verdict and more like a sentiment call the sell side is actively disputing. The signal that settles it is a new carrier deal for AST, or carriers visibly distancing themselves from it. Watch that, not the tape.

AST's own fundamentals offer a second, simpler explanation for the selloff. Its second-quarter adjusted loss was seventy-seven cents a share, against a forecast loss of twenty-six cents. That was an August print, so it doesn't explain Friday by itself. It does explain why the market had little patience left for a thesis that needs quarters to prove out. The operating gap is just as stark. Per its second-quarter update, AST operates thirteen satellites in orbit and is targeting forty-five BlueBirds by early next year. Thirteen satellites is not yet a direct-to-phone business. And a target of forty-five is a plan, not satellites in orbit.

There's a second-order consequence worth naming. If the FCC lets satellite operators own terrestrial low-band spectrum, AST's addressable market tilts away from a standalone U.S. network position. It tilts toward partnerships with mobile operators abroad. The completed integration test with Canada's TELUS is the existing example of that path. That test is a step toward direct-to-phone service, not delivery of it. So the honest read on AST is that it's being priced on what it has today, not on who might eventually need it.

Spectrum is one scarce input SpaceX is buying. Compute is the other, and the company is reportedly borrowing to get it. The Financial Times reported, citing people familiar with the matter, that SpaceX is seeking about ten billion dollars in bank loans and thirty billion in investment-grade debt for a large Nvidia chip order. Apollo Global Management is expected to lead and place the debt. Bond fund Pimco is among a small group of lenders in talks. Now the caveats. Reuters could not independently verify the report, and SpaceX, Apollo, and Nvidia have not confirmed it. The deal is reportedly still at the discussion stage, with a close not expected until 2027. And the reporting does not say what the chips would run.

Put that next to what SpaceX has already bought. It secured a seventeen-billion-dollar spectrum license transfer from EchoStar, already approved. Grain is the second large spectrum purchase, and chip debt would be a third claim. On Wednesday we noted that in the second quarter, by our arithmetic, Starlink's operating profit covered under a tenth of the company's capital spending. An investment-grade label on thirty billion dollars of bonds means lenders would be underwriting existing cash flows, presumably Starlink's. They would be funding a compute bet whose returns are unproven. So lenders, not just shareholders, are increasingly underwriting SpaceX's ambitions.

That has a consequence for SPCX holders, and this is our inference, not reporting. The Starlink-as-carrier story and the chip-debt story draw on the same cash flows. The more Starlink's cash backs other bets, the harder a clean Starlink spin-out becomes. On Sunday we said every new Starlink ambition joins a longer capital queue. This would be that queue taking on a debt tranche.

And Apollo is showing up twice. On Thursday, our Throughline reported Apollo and Blackstone in early talks on financing tied to Broadcom's custom chip for OpenAI. Now Apollo reportedly sits behind SpaceX's chip order too. So private and investment-grade credit is becoming a common funding layer for the AI compute buildout. Space is now a node inside that cycle, not a sector sitting next to it.

All of that is still paper until something flies. Starship Flight 15 now has a tracked no-earlier-than date of November 8th. That comes from a tracking slide reported secondhand, not an official SpaceX announcement. This desk flagged the missing date on Thursday, Saturday, and again on Sunday, so this is the first date we've had to point to. A no-earlier-than date is not a launch date. But the lesson holds either way. Every spectrum license and every dollar of new debt waits on a rocket that flies on a predictable schedule. Right now that schedule is a tracking-slide guess, not a routine cadence.

Beijing is working through the same gap between announced and working capacity, just from the state side. China's Qianfan constellation, also called Thousand Sails, shows two hundred fifty-seven satellites in CelesTrak's active catalogue as of Sunday. Independent tracker Jonathan McDowell counted twenty-two Qianfan satellites as failed as of September 10th. These are tracker estimates, not operator disclosures. It also isn't clear whether the active count excludes the failed ones, so we won't compute a failure rate. On Saturday we reported that Qianfan's 2025 pause traced to thruster and gyroscope failures on the satellites themselves, not a rocket shortage. McDowell's count now puts a number on that satellite-side risk. So Qianfan is real and growing, but its binding problem is satellite engineering, and a state program, not a startup, absorbs the losses.

So where does this stand? What's genuinely inflecting is who owns the inputs. Spectrum, and possibly compute, are being concentrated on one company's balance sheet, increasingly with borrowed money. What's still a demo is nearly everything built on top of those inputs: AST's direct-to-phone network, Starship's reusability, and Qianfan's reliability. The biggest open question is whether the FCC will let a satellite operator hold terrestrial low-band spectrum at this scale. That ruling decides whether SpaceX's land grab sticks, and whether Friday's repricing sticks with it.

Now the catalysts. First, NASA's next-generation lunar lander award, the CLPS 2.0 contract, now postponed to November 7th. Intuitive Machines shares fell on that delay, and NASA gave no reason. For the lander companies, award timing, not landings, sets near-term revenue. Second, Starship's tracked flight date the following day. Third, the FCC's review of the Grain deal. Fourth, any confirmation or denial of the Apollo-led financing. And fifth, any carrier move toward or away from AST.

That's the briefing for October 12th. Thanks for listening, and I'll see you next time.
