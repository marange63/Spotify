Good morning, it's Wednesday, November 18th, 2026, and this is your private credit briefing.

This week the forward signal in leveraged loans turned, even though realized defaults barely moved. That split is today's story, and it runs straight into private credit.

Start with JPMorgan. As relayed in press coverage of the note, its strategists expect the U.S. leveraged-loan default rate to rise to four and a half percent in 2027, and four and three-quarters percent in 2028. That is a forecast, not a measured outcome. The mechanism is a maturity wall. Eight hundred sixty-two billion dollars of leveraged loans come due between 2027 and 2029. Forty-six percent of that is rated B3 or lower. Those are the weakest credits in the market, and they have the least room to refinance when the wall arrives.

The more telling number is the comparison. JPMorgan sees bond defaults rising only to two and three-quarters percent in 2027. So the forecast concentrates the stress in loans, not bonds. One competing explanation deserves a hearing. Loan borrowers pay floating rates, so they carry the full rate cost directly. Many bond issuers locked in fixed coupons. On that reading, the gap is partly a rates call, not purely a credit-quality call. Either way, here's the desk's inference. The borrower profile JPMorgan is worried about, floating-rate, lower-rated and sponsor-backed, is the same profile direct lenders underwrite.

Press coverage calls this a doubling of defaults. But JPMorgan's starting baseline isn't clear from what's been published, so treat four and a half percent as the destination, not the multiple.

Now the part that is price, not forecast. PitchBook LCD's October data show the leveraged-loan distress ratio jumped to eight point zero one percent, from seven point zero five percent in September. PitchBook says it's the first time the ratio has moved above eight percent since July 2020. That's the loan market repricing risk before anything defaults. It also breaks a line this desk held on Sunday and Monday. We said stress this cycle shows up in quantity, not in price. In the syndicated market, it's now showing up in price. Private loans have no equivalent gauge. That's the mark-to-model gap made concrete. The public book is flashing, and the private book has nothing to flash with.

The realized numbers, by contrast, haven't moved. LCD's payment-default rate by dollar amount held at zero point five five percent in October, unchanged from September. PitchBook recorded no legacy payment defaults in the month. LCD's own forward model, separate from JPMorgan's, estimates a six-month forward default rate of one point seven five percent by issuer count. So you have a realized rate around half a percent, a six-month model under two percent, and a bank's multi-year forecast at four and a half. They measure different things over different horizons, so they aren't directly comparable. What they share is direction. The further out you look, the worse the read gets.

One qualifier on that half-percent figure. A payment-default rate counts missed payments. It doesn't capture distressed exchanges. And last Friday this desk reported Fitch's finding that exchanges are now the dominant form default takes in the CLO portfolios it rates. So the payment-default gauge flatters the syndicated market. The competing interpretation of the wall follows from that. It may resolve through amend-and-extend and distressed exchanges, not Chapter 11 filings. We don't know whether JPMorgan's forecast counts those exchanges as defaults. If that pattern holds, rising defaults may mostly mean more liability-management deals. Those depress recoveries without ever producing a bankruptcy headline. The takeaway is that the forward signal has turned in the public loan market, and the real question is where that refinancing goes.

Here's where paper the loan market won't take ends up. Fullscript is being bought by the private equity firm L Catterton, so this is a sponsor-backed buyout credit. Per Bloomberg, citing people familiar with the matter, Morgan Stanley has syndicated the one point three billion dollar loan backing that buyout to direct lenders. The named group is Antares Capital, Blue Owl, Carlyle Group, and Golub Capital. That frees the Morgan Stanley-led banks from an underwriting commitment the syndicated market wouldn't take. Earlier reporting named Apollo and Blue Owl among Fullscript's existing private-credit lenders. Blue Owl is in the final group, but Apollo isn't named. Antares, Carlyle, and Golub weren't among the reported incumbents. That tilts the read toward genuine substitution rather than a refinancing in place, though it doesn't settle it. One deal is still an anecdote, and the final terms haven't been disclosed. But the transaction is now done. Risk moved from a market that prices it into books that mark it to model.

So how big is the balance sheet absorbing this, and how is it wired to the banks? The Fed just put numbers on both. A new Fed research note, dated November 9th, maps the aggregate balance sheets of U.S. private credit vehicles. It puts U.S.-domiciled vehicles at about one point nine trillion dollars in assets at the end of 2025. That's year-end data, so it predates this year's redemption queues. The growth mix matters more than the total. Total vehicle assets rose twenty-one percent from 2024 to 2025. BDCs grew thirty percent, and interval and tender-offer funds grew thirty-nine percent. That's nearly double the overall pace, in the semi-liquid wrappers sold to wealth clients against loans that don't trade. The Fed's own note says recent redemption pressures in these vehicles highlight the liquidity challenges.

Now the connection to the banks. The Fed's November Financial Stability Report, as relayed in press coverage, says bank credit commitments to other financial institutions passed two point eight trillion dollars in the second quarter of 2026. Be precise about that number. It's a broad nonbank category, not private credit alone. And it's commitments, not drawn balances. Still, here's the desk's inference. The bank that sheds a hung loan like Fullscript may keep lending to the same direct lenders that took it. The risk changes form, from borrower exposure to counterparty exposure, without fully leaving the banking system.

The counter-case is strong, and it comes from the same Fed note. BDC leverage averages around one times debt-to-equity, with the ninetieth percentile near one and a half. Losses hit vehicle equity first, long before they could trigger a bank-style run. So the absorber isn't over-levered. But its fastest-growing part is its least liquid part. Here's the inference worth naming. If refinancing stress lands in direct-lending books, credit losses and redemption requests could hit those wrappers at the same time. So bank this. The binding risk isn't leverage. It's liquidity mismatch, in a structure that isn't walled off from the banks.

One more realized number, this time from the private side. Fitch's trailing twelve-month private credit default rate edged down to six point two percent in October, from six point three percent in September. Fitch counted six defaults, against eight the month before. That's too small a count to call a turn. It's also a different universe and definition from the loan-market figures, so it can't be lined up against them. Read each gauge on its own trend instead. Private credit's own default gauge sits above six percent and is moving sideways. The syndicated payment-default rate is low, but it leaves out the distressed exchanges that now dominate. Neither market is offering a clean all-clear.

So where does the cycle stand? Payment defaults are calm in syndicated loans, though that gauge misses the exchanges. Private credit's own default gauge is above six percent and flat. The forward signals, JPMorgan's forecast and the distress ratio, have turned in the public market first. The biggest unresolved risk is that the 2027 to 2029 refinancing wave migrates out of a market that prices it. It would move, Fullscript-style, into private books and retail wrappers whose marks don't trade. Watch three things. First, November's distress-ratio and default data from PitchBook LCD and Fitch. Second, fourth-quarter BDC marks on comparable sponsor-backed credits, for whether private valuations follow the loan market or lag it. Third, whether more hung loans get handed to direct lenders in the weeks ahead.

That's the lending machine for November 18th. Stay cautiously optimistic.
