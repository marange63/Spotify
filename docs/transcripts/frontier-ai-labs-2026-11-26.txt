Good morning. It's Thursday, November 26th, and this is your frontier AI labs briefing. One standing disclosure before we start: Anthropic makes the model behind this show, and later in today's briefing we cover Anthropic's own stock.

Three times in the past week, this desk closed by saying nothing new from the Chinese labs cleared our confirmation bar. Today that pattern breaks. Three items converge on one story. China's independent frontier labs appear to have hit a compute ceiling that money alone can't lift. Moonshot walked away from a leased capacity deal it had offered to half-prepay. DeepSeek is reportedly rationing its own app. And Alibaba, which owns its own cloud, is doing the opposite. It's cutting prices on a closed flagship. So the Chinese bloc is splitting into haves and have-nots. On the US side, this week's news isn't about compute at all. Anthropic's stock closed below its IPO price. That's one data point on investor mood, and we'll be careful not to make it more than that.

Start with Moonshot AI, the maker of the Kimi models. The Information reported last week, and other outlets have relayed, that Moonshot terminated a 150 million dollar deal for access to Nvidia Blackwell Ultra B300 chips hosted in Indonesia. The load-bearing detail isn't the dollar figure. It's the deposit. According to Korea's Sedaily, Moonshot had agreed to put down roughly fifty percent upfront. The typical deposit in deals like this is about fifteen percent. That's more than triple the norm. A lab willing to prepay on those terms is not short of money. On the reported account, what it lacked was permission. Two follow-up details, relayed from the same reporting chain, sharpen the picture. Moonshot declined to comment. And it has paused negotiations on other new compute agreements. That makes this look less like one cancelled contract and more like a pause in a whole sourcing strategy.

Reports tie the cancellation to a new US Commerce Department export rule. Our follow-up research could not obtain the Federal Register text itself. But a summary by the law firm Squire Patton Boggs gives the shape. The rule adds license requirements for five countries: Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, and Brazil. It takes effect on publication in the Federal Register, which the firm expected on November 19th. And it targets Chinese and other companies' access to advanced computing power through third-country data centers. That is precisely the workaround Moonshot was reportedly using. It was renting chips abroad rather than buying them. On Tuesday, this desk said export friction would fall hardest on the labs already outside the gate. Moonshot is the first named lab reported to have hit it.

Now the honest limits. The cancellation traces to a single original report, and neither Moonshot nor Nvidia has confirmed it. The link to the rule is reported, not established. Moonshot could have walked away for its own reasons, such as cost or another supplier. And one lease is not a census of China's compute. It measures friction at one point in the system, not a system-wide seizure. Still, the takeaway is clear enough. The offshore-leasing route around US chip bans looks to be closing, in practice as well as on paper.

If capacity can't be rented abroad, it has to be rationed at home. That's the hand-off to DeepSeek. Since mid-November, users have reported new limits on DeepSeek's app and web chat. The reports describe idle conversations that can't be resumed, and caps on input length. One widely shared account puts it plainly: users are reporting restrictions attributed to insufficient computing capacity. DeepSeek itself hasn't confirmed the limits or their cause. We also couldn't confirm whether they're still in force. And there are other explanations. It could be abuse control, or cost discipline on free users who don't pay, or compute held back for a training run. US labs throttle their free tiers during demand spikes, too.

Even so, consider what DeepSeek's competitive weapon has been. It built its reputation, and its pressure on US lab pricing, on cheap and abundant access. Rationing, even temporary rationing, hits that weapon directly. Put it next to Moonshot, and you have converging evidence that China's compute bottleneck is reaching the product, not just the training cluster. The takeaway: on distribution and inference cost, DeepSeek is giving ground, whatever its models can do.

Not every Chinese lab is rationing, though. Alibaba went the other way. Its flagship, Qwen4-Max, went live in mid-November at a dollar fifty per million input tokens and four dollars fifty per million output. That's a twenty-five percent cut from its predecessor, Qwen3.8-Max. Those prices come from secondary reports, not from Alibaba's own page. We covered Qwen4-Max's closed weights on Tuesday, so the price is the new piece. On Tuesday, this desk also offered three explanations for keeping that flagship closed. They were monetization, compute scarcity, or a staged rollout. This week's evidence helps sort them. Scarcity now has support, but for the independents, DeepSeek and Moonshot. Alibaba owns its cloud, and it is cutting price while keeping the serving revenue. That looks like a lab with capacity to sell. So for Alibaba, monetization now looks like the better explanation.

Call that stratification, and treat it as our inference. A price cut isn't proof of spare capacity. It could be a market-share push subsidized by Alibaba Cloud. But if the reading holds, it changes where Chinese pressure on US pricing comes from. For OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google, that pressure increasingly arrives through a closed, cloud-owned model sold cheaply. It arrives less through free open weights. That's because open-weight diffusion depends on the very labs least able to serve it right now. The takeaway: on compute, Chinese AI is no longer one bloc.

Across the Pacific, this week's lab story isn't about chips. It's about a share price. The disclosure applies here. Per CNBC, Anthropic shares closed down six and a half percent on Tuesday. That sent the stock below its eighty-three dollar IPO price. It's the first close below issue this desk can confirm. Yesterday, we could only say the stock was trading near the offer, and at times below it. So yesterday's question, where ANT settles against issue, now has a first answer, if not a final one.

There's also a new insider figure. TipRanks, a secondary source, reports that all seven Anthropic co-founders sold 3,378,378 shares each at eighty-three dollars. By our own arithmetic, that's about twenty-three point six million shares, or close to two billion dollars in total. That total is our math, not a reported figure. Some coverage frames the founder selling as what's sinking the stock. That gets it backwards. The founders sold at the offer price, which points to a sale inside the offering itself. That supply was disclosed and priced before the stock ever traded. It isn't fresh selling into the decline. The takeaway: one close below issue, a week in, during a rough stretch for tech, is a weak signal of doubt about Anthropic. It is not evidence that investors are constraining the lab. And the founder sales don't make the signal any stronger.

Now a quick scorecard by dimension. Compute access: the Chinese independents lost ground, while Alibaba held its own. Pricing: Alibaba cut. Capital: Anthropic closed below issue on Tuesday, a mood reading rather than a funding problem. Enterprise trust: OpenAI is testing a confidential-compute feature called Private Inference. Sam Altman announced the alpha on X on Wednesday, with a small group of customers. There's no pricing, no list of supported models, and no timeline. So call it a positioning signal aimed at regulated buyers, not a shipped differentiator. Capability: nothing new and independently verified crossed our bar.

And one correction on safety and legal exposure. Yesterday, we said our research found no response from SpaceX to the Virginia and Oregon suits over Grok-generated deepfakes. There was one. NPR reports the company said on X that child sexual abuse material or nonconsensual explicit images of real people have, quote, "no place on X or in Grok." So that watch item closes. But a statement is not a product change, and the suits remain allegations that no court has ruled on.

Three things to watch. First, whether the Federal Register text, or any comment from Moonshot or Nvidia, confirms today's lead. Second, whether DeepSeek's limits get lifted or become formal policy. Third, where Anthropic's stock sits against that eighty-three dollar line in the weeks ahead, and whether a weak signal becomes a real one.

That's the briefing. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
