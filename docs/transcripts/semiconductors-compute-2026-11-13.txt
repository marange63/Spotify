Good morning. It's Friday, November 13th, 2026, and here's what's moving across semiconductors and compute.

Jensen Huang is out as Nvidia's chief executive, announced Thursday. Here's the allegation behind it. Per CNBC, scrutiny this week turned to Jensen Huang's affair with an alleged Chinese spy. The woman is Alice Huang, no relation despite the surname. She has been affiliated with Megaspeed, a Singapore-based reseller accused of helping restricted Chinese firms get Nvidia chips. On Tuesday, Nvidia's board said it had hired the law firm Wachtell Lipton to investigate the intelligence allegations.

On Thursday morning, this desk relayed Bloomberg's report that Nvidia's general counsel, Tim Teter, had called a senior Commerce official in late summer 2025. The subject was the Megaspeed smuggling probe. Teter reportedly asked whether Commerce's export enforcers were involved in Singapore's detention of Alice Huang. So Nvidia's top lawyer was asking regulators about the woman at the center of the CEO's scandal. That made it Nvidia's problem, not just Jensen Huang's. Bloomberg's account rests on unnamed sources, and Nvidia reportedly says Teter didn't know of the relationship when he called. The same day, Huang was gone.

In its release, Nvidia's board announced that Huang "has resigned as President, Chief Executive Officer, and a member of the Board, effective immediately." Colette Kress, the current CFO, "has agreed to serve as Interim Chief Executive Officer." She keeps the finance job too, and "will continue to serve as Executive Vice President and Chief Financial Officer." The board named Harvey Jones as its first-ever chair, and says it has "full confidence in Colette and the leadership team." The permanent search "will consider internal and external candidates." The release names no ongoing role for Huang.

CNBC tells it differently. A person familiar with the matter told the network the "board decided last night to oust longtime CEO Jensen Huang." Nvidia says resignation. CNBC's single source says ouster. We can't resolve that, so hold both. The spy allegation itself is still unresolved reporting. Nothing reported is a government finding against Huang or against Nvidia.

The market voted at the close. Per StockTitan, Nvidia finished Thursday down about 4.3 percent. That leaves the stock roughly 31 percent below its record close in early October. That isn't what a clean relief rally looks like.

Score this by competitive dimension. Our frame since Tuesday has been that the discount sits on two things, not on the product. One is the people, meaning leadership. The other is the permission, meaning the license from Washington and Beijing to sell into China. Architecture, the software ecosystem, and Nvidia's prepaid packaging position are untouched. What moved is leadership continuity, customer relationships, and political standing.

Putting the CFO in the CEO chair is telling, and this is our inference, not a company statement. It signals the board wants financial and operating continuity. It says nothing about who sets the product roadmap. Nor does it say who inherits Huang's role as the customer-facing evangelist.

That gap matters most to the buyers. Hyperscalers and labs negotiating allocation and pricing now sit across the table from an interim CEO. That sharpens their incentive to qualify second sources. AMD and Broadcom shares did rise while Nvidia fell on Monday and Tuesday, but that's sentiment, not share shift. No design win or allocation change tied to this week has been reported, so this is inference.

There are two honest readings of the exit, and we won't pick a winner. The first is relief. The board moved fast, so the governance discount can start to shrink. The second is validation. On CNBC's sourcing, a board forced out a founder-CEO before the facts were settled. That says the board judged the matter serious. And it hands Washington's hawks and any plaintiffs a stronger story, not a weaker one.

Several threads stay open either way. Nvidia reiterated that it will report fiscal third-quarter results on November 17th. No source yet says who leads that call. The Wachtell Lipton investigation has reported no findings. And House Foreign Affairs Chairman Brian Mast says Huang has agreed to testify. But in Mast's words, "we're in negotiations for which day." So the November 18th date we flagged earlier this week isn't locked. The people half of this week's discount has been dealt with. The permission half has not.

What a board can't remove is the channel that turned a personal story into a corporate one. Nvidia's China problem kept escalating this week. Al Jazeera reports, citing records, that a "Chinese university buys Nvidia AI H200 chips despite US ban." Axios reports that President Trump has now "backed Nvidia chip controls aimed at China 3 times" this year, with Commerce Secretary Lutnick stalling. Both are single-outlet reports, not government or company confirmations.

Now set that against the legal channel. In Al Jazeera's words, the administration has allowed those sales to China "through case-by-case licensing, with the US government taking a 25 percent cut." Yet that licensed flow is blocked at China's own border. DataCenterDynamics reported roughly 600,000 of these chips stranded in Hong Kong awaiting Beijing's import approval, as this desk flagged last week. So the legal channel is stuck, while records show a banned buyer got chips anyway.

Our inference is that the channel that actually delivers is the grey one. That's the same reseller layer as Megaspeed, the Singapore firm from the top of the story, which faces no filed charges. The alternative reading is that one documented purchase is an isolated leak, not a system. Records showing one buyer don't size the diversion, so don't extrapolate a volume.

Two second-order effects follow, and both are inference. First, enforcement aimed at resellers raises compliance costs for every US accelerator vendor selling through Asian intermediaries, not just Nvidia. Second, the policy collects little. Washington's cut applies to licensed sales that Beijing won't let in, while the leakage it targets carries on. So the direction is tighter, but the timing is unstable. The permission half survives the CEO change intact.

None of that has reached the wafers yet. On November 8th, this desk read Taiwan Semiconductor's roughly 9.6 percent slide in the midterm rout as a statement about crowding, not about its order book. October's numbers are the order book's answer.

TSMC's October revenue hit a monthly record of about 587 billion Taiwan dollars. That's up roughly 60 percent from a year earlier, on what reporting attributes to AI chip demand. So the scarcest supplier in the AI stack billed a record while the market sold its shares. Our read is that equity volatility is running well ahead of physical demand.

Treat it as a lagging read, though. Monthly revenue bills wafers ordered quarters ago, before this month's permission shocks. It also blends volume with pricing and node mix, so a revenue record isn't a wafer-start record. The risk to our read is that orders turn before revenue does. So the honest claim is narrower. Demand constrained by compute availability was intact through October.

The supply side of that demand just met a new kind of Taiwan risk, and it isn't the Strait. Bloomberg reports that Micron has paused construction on its 4.5 billion dollar Taoyuan memory fab amid a labor dispute. That's Bloomberg's reporting, not a Micron announcement. Per DataCenterDynamics, Taiwan's Ministry of Economic Affairs says it "supports the legal agreement reached between the company and the union." The ministry adds that it "would work with local authorities to resolve the differences." So an agreement exists, but in the reporting we have, construction hasn't restarted.

Now add the seller's own framing. Micron has warned that shortages will get worse in 2027, with its CEO pointing to "much higher prices." That's standing guidance from Micron's fiscal 2026 earnings call, from an interested seller, not this week's news. A supplier telling buyers that supply is short and prices are rising has little reason to hurry a restart. The fab would only add capacity later. Our inference is that the dispute works as supply discipline, whatever its cause.

It also feeds the memory split this desk has tracked since November 3rd. The stocks priced an end to the squeeze, while contract data and the sellers' guidance pointed the other way. This pause adds to the shortage side. Future DRAM and HBM capacity arrives later, which strengthens pricing power for all three memory makers. The bill lands hardest on the economics-constrained buyers we flagged last weekend: phone makers, PC makers, and leveraged neoclouds.

So where does this leave us? Nvidia's people problem is resolved, by removal. The permission problem is not, and it doesn't care who sits in the CEO chair. The physical demand underneath both, per TSMC's own invoice, was intact through October. Watch four things. First, Nvidia's November 17th earnings. Second, the CEO search. Third, whether Washington's crackdown gains teeth. And fourth, whether Micron restarts construction in Taiwan.

That's the briefing. Have a good Friday.
