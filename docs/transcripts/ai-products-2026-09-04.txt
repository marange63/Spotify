Good morning, it's September 4th, and this is your AI Products briefing.

Yesterday on this show, we called OpenAI's capability gating a moat that money couldn't cross. GPT-6 Astra had been announced with no general-availability date beyond "soon," no published price, and access rationed by a vetting program called Daybreak rather than sold. That framing lasted about a day.

On September 3rd, OpenAI actually shipped Astra, with a rate card attached. Standard pricing is ten dollars per million input tokens and fifty dollars per million output tokens. The context window is 1.05 million tokens. And the rollout path is now explicit. Daybreak enterprise customers get it first. Then it reaches ChatGPT Plus, Pro, Business and Enterprise, plus the API, over what OpenAI calls the coming days. So the moat didn't hold. It turned into a queue, and the queue is measured in days, not an indefinite waiting list. Say that plainly: capability gating on a frontier model is starting to look like a release-sequencing tool that buys enterprises a head start of days, not a durable allocation regime.

Set the safety tier aside in one sentence, because we covered it at length yesterday. Astra is the first OpenAI model rated "Critical" for cyber capability, scoring perfectly on the ExploitBench benchmark and finding two previously unknown vulnerabilities during evaluation.

What's actually new today is the product substance underneath that headline. On the computer-use benchmark OSWorld V2-Offline, Astra scored 72.6 percent, against 65.7 percent for the prior model. More interesting than the score is the clock. Average task completion time fell from roughly 75 minutes to 40. That is a supervision-economics change more than a capability change. The binding cost of running a computer-use agent in production is the human who waits on it and checks its work. Cut elapsed time roughly in half, and you change how many agent tasks one operator can oversee in a day. Note where that saving lands, though. The buyer only captures it if they are effectively paying for the agent in hours of attention. A tooling vendor reselling agent runs at a fixed price per task pockets it instead.

Then there's Codex, OpenAI's coding tool, which gained a "notes" mode. It keeps accumulated task context across long sessions instead of repeatedly compressing it into a single summary, and earlier context windows stay searchable. That targets the single most-cited reason enterprises abandon long-running coding agents: the agent forgets why an earlier fix failed. Two qualifications. The notes mode is opt-in today, enabled through a configuration file, with OpenAI saying it becomes the default in a few weeks. It is not yet how most users experience Codex. And Astra's knowledge cutoff is the end of April, which matters if you point it at a fast-moving codebase or dependency set. If persistent context does become native, though, it absorbs a whole layer of third-party tooling. Agent-memory services, session managers and summarisation middleware exist mostly to work around context compaction. A model that stops discarding context doesn't compete with that category. It deletes the problem the category was selling against.

Now, the number that arrived with Astra is stranger than the capability. Ten dollars per million input, fifty per million output. That is the exact same standard price Anthropic set for Claude Fable 5.1, which shipped two days earlier. Both labs also paired their flagship with a restricted, vetting-gated twin inside seventy-two hours. When the two leading US labs' flagships carry an identical sticker price, the headline rate has stopped functioning as a competitive variable at the frontier tier. Buyers who shop on that number are comparing the one thing that no longer differs.

Which moves the real competition to a line procurement has no spreadsheet column for: the cache. Anthropic charges a quarter of a dollar per million tokens to read from its cache. OpenAI charges a dollar per million for the equivalent read. On its face, a four-times gap.

But that is half a rate card. OpenAI's own API documentation shows a separate cache-write fee of twelve dollars fifty per million tokens, a premium over its standard input rate to populate the cache in the first place. So OpenAI's caching mechanics are structurally the same shape as Anthropic's: pay up to write, pay down to reread. What we still don't have is Anthropic's equivalent write-side rate for Fable 5.1. So a genuine session-by-session comparison of which vendor is cheaper for a real agentic workload isn't possible yet. Treat the four-times figure as a partial, read-only comparison, not a verdict on whose agent is the better buy.

The broader point survives that uncertainty. Identical sticker prices push differentiation into the cache-read rate, the cache-write rate, and how long a cache entry stays alive before it expires. None of those appear when you lay two rate cards side by side. All of them appear on the invoice. A vendor comparison built on headline pricing now predicts almost nothing about what you actually get billed.

Where cost stops binding, permission binds. That's the pivot from pricing to deployment, and it shows up clearest in healthcare. OpenAI connected ChatGPT for Healthcare to Epic's electronic health record system, letting clinicians query authorized patient records — labs, medications, specialist notes — inside ChatGPT or embedded directly in the chart. A companion plugin pulls in several official public health data sources, including ClinicalTrials.gov, PubMed and RxNorm. Per TechCrunch's reporting, Epic's system holds data for more than 325 million patients. Integration breadth is the whole story here. No single feature claim comes close.

The design choice that makes this deployable is that it's read-only. The AI doesn't write anything back into the record. OpenAI paired that with a published safety figure: 99.1 percent of ChatGPT's responses rated safe by physicians, across roughly 4,300 ratings spanning 27 clinical use cases. That figure also comes to us through the reporting, not a directly verifiable company post. Sit with the denominator. Spread across 27 use cases, that's on the order of 160 ratings each, and "rated safe" is a lower bar than "clinically correct." Roughly one response in a hundred failing a safety rating is not obviously reassuring in a clinical setting. Read-only caps the upside, too. It's a liability decision dressed as a safety feature, and an assistant that can't write to the chart cannot touch the documentation burden that is the real cost centre in clinical work. So this is augmentation by construction, not replacement. Still, the template is exportable: read-only access plus a published, physician-rated safety number. Expect it next in legal tech and financial services, where the winning move for entering a regulated vertical becomes trading away write access to get inside the system of record at all. That is bad news for vendors whose whole value proposition was the writing.

There's a counter-move worth closing on. Perplexity shipped a feature called Hybrid Compute for its Mac app that answers the same access question from the other end. Its Computer agent starts tasks in the cloud. When a step touches sensitive local files, the Mac downloads a local model and finishes that step on-device. The company names bloodwork, tax returns and litigation documents as the trigger cases, with an on-device classifier substituting personal information before anything reaches the cloud. Instead of negotiating read-only access to somebody else's system of record, this routes the sensitive step away from the cloud entirely. Both are liability architectures, not capability ones. The catch is the hardware floor: Apple silicon on macOS 15 or later with at least 24 gigabytes of unified memory, which quietly limits this to people who already own a high-end Mac.

So here's where the day leaves us. Two disclosures would settle most of what's unresolved. Anthropic's cache-write rate, which would tell us whether that four-times gap is real or an artifact of comparing half a rate card. And an independent reproduction of Astra's computer-use and task-time numbers, since every capability figure in this briefing is OpenAI grading its own model. Neither exists yet.
