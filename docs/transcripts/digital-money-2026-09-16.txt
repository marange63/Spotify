Good morning. It's Wednesday, September 16th, and this is your digital money briefing.

The binary this show has tracked all week resolved yesterday afternoon, and it resolved against the industry. The Senate's cloture motion on the Digital Asset Market Clarity Act failed, 49 to 50. Cloture needs 60. So this was not close. Every Democrat voted no, and four Republicans joined them: Susan Collins, Josh Hawley, Jerry Moran, and Thom Tillis. Democrats pointed to unresolved ethics demands tied to Trump-family crypto ventures as the sticking point.

Two precision points before the market reaction, because both cut against the easy read. First, this was a procedural motion to proceed, not a final up-or-down vote on the bill's merits. Nothing here forecloses reintroduction as a matter of law. Second, and pulling the other way, the blocking coalition was not a clean party-line stall that one concession fixes. It took four Republicans crossing over too, and the lead Senate sponsor, Cynthia Lummis, said on vote day that on the ethics language there is nothing left to give. Majority Leader Thune, for his part, framed the loss afterward as Democrats choosing, in his words, politics over good policy. That is a blame statement, not a concession the bill is finished. No senator or sponsor is on the record saying whether a revised bill comes back this year or slips to 2027. What is reported, and it is a reporter's characterization rather than a sponsor's own words, is that the industry now waits until next year for clearer rules.

Now the correction, and it is this program's own. This show closed Tuesday by telling you the legislative path was already priced for failure. It wasn't. Going into the vote, prediction markets still had real money on passage. Secondary trackers put Polymarket's 2026 passage market somewhere around 35 percent and Kalshi in the mid-twenties to high-thirties. Treat those as directional rather than exact, because no single timestamped print could be pinned down. But the direction is the whole point. That is a market assigning a real, non-trivial chance to statutory clarity, not one braced for a loss.

The tape confirms it. Coinbase fell 10.1 percent to $172.11. Circle fell 11.5 percent to $86.25. Together the two companies shed roughly $7.9 billion of market value in a single session. Those are same-session market prices as reported, not company disclosure. And here is the comparison that carries the rest of this briefing: Bitcoin, the asset these companies are supposedly a leveraged play on, fell only about 3 percent, to roughly $75,785. If the outcome had genuinely been discounted, the equities would not have moved three to four times harder than the underlying asset on a pure Washington headline.

So the honest read is not that clarity died. It is that the market had been paying for a real chance of getting it, and when that chance went to zero the repricing landed almost entirely on the equities. Fading odds and a discounted price are not the same thing. We conflated them, and it cost two companies eight billion dollars of market value in an afternoon.

That divergence raises the obvious question. If these stocks are not levered Bitcoin, what are they?

Circle's second-quarter 2026 filings answer it. Reserve income was $667.7 million out of $701.3 million in total revenue. That is about 95 percent of the business. The other five percent is everything else Circle does. This is a short-duration money-market business with a crypto ticker attached.

And there is a second correction to make here. On Monday this program told you neither major stablecoin issuer discloses a per-basis-point reserve sensitivity, and that nobody should invent one. Circle does disclose one. In its own filings, a 100-basis-point increase in rates would add roughly $737 million to reserve income over twelve months. That is the company's static estimate applied to its current float, not guidance, and it assumes the float doesn't shrink. But it is disclosed, we said it wasn't, and now we can use it.

Coinbase sits on the same transmission line, one step removed. Its stablecoin revenue, mostly its share of USDC reserve income, was $292.1 million in the second quarter. That is 25.3 percent of Coinbase's $1.15 billion of net revenue. A quarter of the top line moves with the front end of the curve rather than with trading volume.

Put that together and you get a positioning error worth naming. An allocator holding Coinbase as a leveraged Bitcoin expression owns something close to the opposite of what they think. A hike helps that position through reserve income. A dovish surprise hurts it. Sitting next to a long Bitcoin book, that is not a doubled bet. It is a partial hedge the holder didn't know they bought.

Which is exactly why this afternoon's Fed decision matters more to these two companies than any headline out of Washington. The FOMC announces at 2 p.m. Eastern, with the target range having sat at 3.50 to 3.75 percent since December, after three quarter-point cuts through the back half of last year. I am not going to tell you which way it goes, because it has not happened as I record this. And the forecaster disagreement here is real rather than decorative. Published hike odds across trackers have ranged from roughly 30 percent to near-certainty depending on which one you read and when. When the trackers are that far apart, there is no consensus to quote. What is new today is not the odds. It is that you now have the dollar figure sitting behind the decision. For Circle and Coinbase this is a quantified earnings event, not a mood swing.

One caveat on the bullish version of that. The rate sensitivity cuts both ways, because the pool these issuers earn on has stopped compounding. Total stablecoin market cap was $302.8 billion on September 10th, up just 0.37 percent on the week by one tracker's count. A higher front end lifts the income per dollar of float, and it simultaneously raises the opportunity cost of holding a coin that by law cannot pay its holder. More margin on fewer dollars is a real outcome, not a growth story.

There is a third leg to this same spine, and it is about who is still buying Bitcoin itself. Strategy, formerly MicroStrategy, broke a roughly ten-week pause in early September, adding 4,603 Bitcoin for about $370 million, reportedly funded through new equity. That took total holdings to 845,050 coins. Then, on September 14th, it did something different. It disclosed a $139 million buyback of its own STRC preferred stock, with Bitcoin holdings unchanged and $6.4 billion of dollar assets still on the balance sheet.

Be careful with the read there. The company frames the sequence as part of its long-term accumulation plan, and a single buyback is not a regime change. Ten-week gaps have happened before. The cost-of-capital interpretation is inference, not company-confirmed, and no NAV multiple is in evidence. But the disclosed fact pattern stands on its own: the largest corporate Bitcoin holder, with $6.4 billion of dollars available, chose to repurchase its own securities rather than the asset it has built its identity around. That is the same story the lead told, expressed through a balance sheet instead of a tape.

Which brings the whole thing back to what was actually lost yesterday. It was not rules. The SEC and CFTC still govern this sector through guidance and enforcement discretion, and industry executives spent yesterday predicting both agencies will keep writing rules to fill the gap. Note that this is industry expectation. No agency chair put out a statement after the vote. What the sector lost is irreversibility. A perimeter drawn by rulemaking and interpretive guidance can be redrawn by the next administration without a floor vote, and it was that durability the equity multiples were paying for. The collateral damage is the clean federal charter pathway the bill would have created for stablecoin issuers, which raises the execution risk on the bank and community-bank rollouts this show covered last week.

Close on the part of this sector that never needed a Senate vote. J.P. Morgan Asset Management's tokenized money-market fund passed $800 million by early September, inside a tokenized-Treasury market of roughly $15.9 billion. That total aggregates third-party trackers, so treat it as directional. But a JPMorgan-branded money fund compounding on-chain while the statute dies in the Senate tells you the institutional bid here was never contingent on Congress.

So the theme for today is this. The regulatory perimeter for digital assets is still drawn in pencil, and yesterday the market spent $7.9 billion finding out what pencil is worth.

That's the briefing. Talk tomorrow.
