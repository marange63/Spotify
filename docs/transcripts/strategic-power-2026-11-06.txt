Good morning. It's Friday, November 6, 2026, and this is Strategic Power.

Here's the thesis for today. Tuesday's vote turned data-center consent from a town-by-town zoning fight into statewide political power and a priced market risk. The two fastest responses both try to avoid winning that consent from scratch. OpenAI is offering to pay its own way with ratepayers, in public. Washington is financing upgrades at reactors where consent is already banked.

Start with the statewide layer. You already know the ballot sweep: forty-one local measures, Ohio alone accounting for more than half of them, and only one town siding with the industry. What's new is that the backlash reached governors' mansions. Democrats flipped five seats: Texas, Georgia, Ohio, Iowa, and Nevada. That gives the party a majority of U.S. governorships for the first time in sixteen years, per NBC News. Two of those flips land exactly where this desk has been watching. Texas is the state whose governor, Greg Abbott, ordered the audit of ERCOT's data-center queue we covered on Wednesday, and he lost. Georgia is home to OpenAI's Project Camellia, the pilot site for the Grid Protectors deal with Georgia Power we covered on Tuesday.

Be careful about causation here. Nothing in the record shows data centers alone decided these races. Texas went for Trump by nearly fourteen points two years ago, and a broad anti-incumbent wave explains a lot. But the floor under that caution just got firmer. Texas governor-elect Gina Hinojosa didn't just benefit from data centers polling as salient. She ran on the issue by name. In an October campaign video, reported by the Texas Tribune, she said, quote, "When I am governor I will issue a moratorium — a ban — on all data center approvals." That's a platform, not a poll result. We don't have a comparable statement from Georgia's governor-elect, so don't stretch it there. And one caveat holds everywhere. Governors-elect take office in January 2027. Nothing in permitting or interconnection rules has actually changed yet. What changed is the risk premium markets attach to the moment it does. So the voter gatekeeper now has an executive branch, starting in January.

The market didn't wait for January. The sharpest repricing hit the power names first. On Wednesday, the day after the vote, Vistra fell 13.5 percent, according to Investing.com. KeyBanc downgraded the stock. Its analyst said the result introduces significant policy uncertainty for companies supplying electricity to data centers and large industrial customers. KeyBanc called Vistra the most exposed name it covers. Other generators exposed to ERCOT and PJM reportedly fell sharply too.

Then on Thursday the selloff went broad. The Nasdaq-100 fell 6.3 percent, erasing 2.3 trillion dollars in market value, by Yahoo Finance's tally. The Composite closed in correction territory. Coverage splits on why. Yahoo's own read is that chip and data-center stocks were among the biggest laggards, as investors weighed what the Democratic sweep means for AI infrastructure spending and regulation. A Nomura strategist pushed back. In that strategist's words, the primary catalyst "isn't about a sudden deterioration in fundamentals." The argument is positioning and profit-taking instead. This week's tape gives that second read some support. On Wednesday, neocloud names were already falling even as the Dow rallied, in a group that was already well off its peak. Both readings can be true. The vote may be the trigger for an AI-capex repricing that was already building, rather than its sole cause. Either way, the takeaway is the same. Markets are starting to price political exposure into power generation state by state, the way they price commodity exposure.

Bitcoin caught the same downdraft. It slid to roughly 75,650 dollars, wiping out the rebound above 80,000 that followed the midterms. Crypto liquidations topped 1.25 billion dollars in twenty-four hours, and nearly 89 percent of that was long positions. That's the hard-money hedge thesis failing a real-time test. In an AI-capex de-risking, Bitcoin traded as a high-beta risk asset alongside the Nasdaq, not as a haven from it. The long-heavy skew says positioning drove the move, so one day doesn't settle the broader argument. But watch the level. It's right back at the election-night floor that our Digital Money desk said yesterday had held through two flushes in a week. Now it's being tested again.

So how is the industry answering? OpenAI moved first. On Thursday, two days after the vote, it issued what it calls a National Community Compact. It pledges to cover the full cost of electricity and related infrastructure at future data centers it owns and operates. It offers financial guarantees to protect utility customers if projects are delayed, scaled back, or abandoned. And it largely stops signing nondisclosure agreements with public officials during site negotiations. OpenAI says the plans were prepared before the vote. That's its position, and the timing makes it a hard sell. Set this against the arc. The Grid Protectors deal we covered Tuesday made concessions to a utility. Yesterday we argued that concessions to a utility don't buy consent from a town. This compact goes a rung lower, straight to the ratepayer and the local official. So the price of consent is now being paid in public.

But read the fine print. The pledge covers only facilities OpenAI itself owns and operates. It doesn't bind partner-owned sites like the Stargate joint ventures, or existing contracts. No dollar figure is attached, so markets can't price it. And it's voluntary. Nothing binds until a state utility commission adopts large-load cost-allocation rules. It only resets the industry if rivals like Microsoft, Google, Meta, and Amazon match it. Here's my inference on the second-order effect. If large loads end up paying full cost of service, as OpenAI is now urging state commissions to require, the all-in cost of a data center rises. That favors hyperscalers with balance sheets over debt-funded developers and neoclouds. So for now, the compact is a reputational bid, not an industry reset.

Washington's answer runs through buildings that already exist. The Department of Energy has now authorized more than 9 billion dollars in conditional loan commitments to upgrade operating nuclear plants, as reported by the Butler Eagle. Constellation gets up to 5 billion dollars to add an estimated 1.1 gigawatts across plants in Pennsylvania, Maryland, and Illinois. Vistra gets up to 4.2 billion dollars to add 433 megawatts of new capacity. Vistra's loan also preserves nearly 4 gigawatts of existing baseload across Pennsylvania and Ohio. That 4 gigawatts is retention, not addition.

Notice where that preserved baseload sits. Ohio is the state that just voted down data centers almost everywhere. It also elected Amy Acton, who backed a conditional moratorium. Yet a reactor already connected to the grid, and already accepted by its community, doesn't need a new vote to keep running. Yesterday we made the same argument about Drax's existing power station in Britain, a place where power and consent may already be banked. The DOE loans are the federal version of that brownfield logic. Where OpenAI is paying for consent, Washington is financing sites that already have it.

Keep the scale honest, though. These are conditional commitments, not disbursed capital or completed construction, and the reporting gives no in-service dates. Roughly a gigawatt and a half of combined new capacity is small next to the multi-gigawatt loads hyperscalers are chasing. And a federal loan helps the financing, but it doesn't remove state political risk if a future governor turns hostile to large-load hookups. So the fastest gigawatts are the ones that don't need a new vote, but the existing fleet can only be stretched so far.

Three things to watch into next week. First, China's rare-earth export-control pause is set to expire Tuesday, November 10th. As of November 5th, Beijing still had not published the formal notice needed to extend it, and without it the legal suspension ends. Second, Russia, Ukraine, and the US meet in Abu Dhabi on November 10th and 11th. Ukraine accuses Moscow of using the limited ceasefire in Oleshky to reinforce its positions. Separately, US envoy Steve Witkoff says American negotiators meet this week with France, Germany, the UK, and NATO on security guarantees for Ukraine. Our read from Wednesday stands: treat Abu Dhabi as choreography until someone moves on territory. Third, whether any hyperscaler matches OpenAI's compact.

The common thread: consent, not capital, is now the binding constraint on how fast the AI build-out moves. Industry is starting to pay for it in public, and Washington is building where it's already banked.
