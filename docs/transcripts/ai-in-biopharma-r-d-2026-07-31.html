Cautious Optimism Briefings

AI in Biopharma R&D

Transcript · July 31, 2026

Good morning. It's Friday, July 31st, and this is your briefing on AI in biopharma R&D.

This week's arc has been that AI's proven value keeps landing in the periphery of drug development, not in the discovery core. Today that arc sharpens into a specific claim. Two deals dated yesterday show where the capital is actually going: not to the cleverest model, but to whoever controls the raw biological data feeding it. The moat is moving from architecture to ownership.

Start with GSK and Relation Therapeutics. GSK is expanding its collaboration with the UK biotech in a deal worth, in the company's own words, "up to $110 million in upfront and success-based milestones." Relation will run automated wet labs to generate what it calls petascale multi-omic perturbation data — how cells respond to genetic and drug interventions over time — to train MORGAN, its foundation model of cellular perturbation response across disease contexts. The point isn't the model. It's the data factory underneath it. Relation's CEO put the logic plainly: deepening the understanding of disease biology "starts with richer data, to be used in models that can give us greater confidence in the discovery of therapeutic targets." That's a direct answer to an argument this briefing made earlier in the week, that data quality, not compute, is the binding constraint on AI drug discovery. Forty-eight hours later, that argument became a deal.

Two caveats matter here, and a targeted follow-up search this morning confirmed neither has been closed by any source, primary or secondary. First, nobody has disclosed the split between upfront cash and milestones, so treat the $110 million as a ceiling, not committed capital. Second, there is no public benchmark of MORGAN against existing perturbation models like scGPT or Geneformer, and no quantified figure behind the "petascale" label — just the qualitative claim that human biology data is thin. So this is an infrastructure bet, not a validated capability. The so-what: big pharma is now willing to co-fund physical lab automation, not just license outputs, which raises automated wet-lab capacity itself into a competitive asset, alongside compute.

That same data-ownership logic is playing out on the public tape. Tempus AI reported second-quarter results also dated yesterday, and they're the clearest existing proxy for monetizing AI on real-world multimodal data in oncology. Revenue rose 22 percent year over year to $382.5 million. More telling is the Data and Applications segment — the foundation-model licensing business — which grew 28 percent to $93.2 million on $200 million of new data and modeling bookings. Net income turned positive at $5.6 million, versus a $42.8 million loss a year earlier. And Tempus disclosed a pending $1.5 to $1.7 billion acquisition of Personalis to expand its minimal-residual-disease and oncology diagnostics footprint.

Read that sequence together and it's the same GSK-Relation logic playing out on a public balance sheet: buy the longitudinal data asset to deepen the moat that feeds the model. But pace the caveats. The $200 million is bookings, not revenue recognized this quarter, and the timing of recognition isn't disclosed. The profitability headline leans on non-GAAP adjusted EBITDA, up 244 percent, while GAAP income is still only near breakeven. And the Personalis deal hasn't closed — it's expected late this year or early next. So what this tells you: the market is starting to reward data-licensing revenue as a distinct, higher-margin line from lab-services economics, and that's a read-through worth watching on other diagnostics-plus-AI names like Guardant, Natera, and Exact Sciences, on whether they get credited the same way.

That premium is really about one thing, and it's worth answering the question every investor asks here: can AI actually exploit the real-world data locked inside health systems? The honest answer is that the binding constraint is plumbing, not models. The post-HITECH electronic-record world is balkanized across incompatible formats, and HIPAA governs every de-identified extract. The progress is coming from the linkage and analytics layer that standardizes that fragmented feedstock — Truveta, for one, now runs an AI analytics platform sitting on de-identified records from more than 130 million patients. That's the unglamorous infrastructure that makes external control arms and label expansion feasible, and it's the same logic as the GSK deal: whoever owns and harmonizes the data captures the moat, not whoever writes the model.

Now the counterpoint, because the data-moat thesis shouldn't be taken as the whole story. Chai Discovery, the antibody-design startup, just closed a $400 million Series C at a $3.8 billion valuation — roughly triple the $1.3 billion mark it carried seven months earlier. That round followed a June license agreement giving Pfizer early access to Chai-3, Chai's most advanced antibody and multi-specific molecule design model, plus a custom Pfizer-tuned variant. Eli Lilly and Novartis are also customers. So capital is still pouring into the model layer, not just the data layer.

But look at what's actually happening underneath that valuation: several large pharmas are licensing the same design tool in parallel. Pfizer's arrangement is the one we can source directly — early access to Chai-3 plus a bespoke tuned model — and that hybrid build-versus-buy pattern looks less like a differentiated technological edge and more like a shared CRO relationship. That raises a real differentiation and IP-overlap question as more pharma customers stack onto the same underlying platform. And it's worth saying plainly: no Chai-designed molecule has publicly reached human trials. This is a design-tool licensing story, not a clinical validation story. So the honest read is that the model layer is commoditizing even as its valuation expands, while the data-generation layer, because it's inherently proprietary to each partner, is where the moat is actually holding.

Which brings the thread to where it should always land: does any of this move the needle on clinical success rates. The closest real test case is still Insilico Medicine's rentosertib, the TNIK inhibitor the company says came from an AI-first target-discovery workflow rather than a conventional target. It just entered Phase III — 320 patients, 47 centers in China, a 52-week endpoint on lung-function decline in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. Encouraging Phase II data showed a 98.4 milliliter improvement in forced vital capacity at 12 weeks versus a 20.3 milliliter decline on placebo. But that's a surrogate endpoint, this is a program-progression event, not a readout, and Insilico's own timeline puts the actual Phase III result around December of 2029.

So here's the scoreboard, three years and change of deals deep into this cycle: capital keeps flowing to data factories and design tools, valuations keep climbing, and the count of drugs that were both AI-discovered and AI-designed to reach approval still sits at zero. That's the tension worth carrying forward. The moat argument tells you where pharma believes the next edge is. It doesn't yet tell you whether that edge translates into higher probability of clinical success, or just a faster, better-capitalized version of the same base rates. And for R&D budgets that distinction is everything. If data moats only speed up the same-probability shots on goal, AI lowers cost per program without lifting the success rate — which means total R&D spend may not fall at all, it just buys more attempts. Until rentosertib or something like it reads out, that remains the open question sitting underneath every one of these deals.

That's the briefing for today. Have a good weekend.
