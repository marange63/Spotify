Good morning, it's Saturday, November 7th, and this is your Cautious Optimism briefing on the space and orbital economy.

All week, this desk has watched the non-SpaceX launch bench sit on the ground. Rocket Lab's Neutron slipped to 2027. Blue Origin's New Glenn pushed its return to January. ULA's Vulcan has been grounded since February. Yesterday, a satellite operator answered with a plan to build its own rocket. Whether that is a reaction to the bench or a long-planned move is the interesting question, and the company's own filing has a view. That's our lead.

AST SpaceMobile, the direct-to-cell operator, announced a reusable rocket called Blacklift. It is buying the engines from Firefly Aerospace. Firefly puts the deal at more than one point three billion dollars in cash and stock over three years. That buys thirty-six engines, engineering support, and a license for AST to manufacture the engines itself.

AST's own SEC filing breaks the bill into three pieces. About eight hundred million dollars is upfront. That is four hundred million in cash, plus ten to fourteen million shares of AST's Class A stock. The exact share count is set later by a forward VWAP formula, so the final dilution isn't fixed yet. The second piece is two hundred fifty million dollars for delivering the engines for the first three rockets. The third is another two hundred fifty million over three years for engineering support.

Those first three rockets will fly on twenty-seven Miranda engines and nine vacuum-variant Vira engines. AST classifies Blacklift as a twenty-ton rocket, in the same class as Falcon 9 and Vulcan. It is designed for reuse, and it can carry five BlueBird satellites per launch. Per Spaceflight Now, the target start of service is 2028, "if all goes to plan."

That hedge is doing real work. Neither engine has flown an orbital mission. Miranda has completed more than one hundred seventy-five hot-fire tests, totaling over forty-two hundred seconds. That's a serious campaign, but it is still ground testing. Vira, the upper-stage engine, is still in development. Then there's the base rate. Spaceflight Now cites a Payload analysis that puts development to first successful launch at about six years. AST's 2028 target implies roughly two. AST also disclosed no per-launch cost, so there is no basis yet for claiming Blacklift beats buying rides. Blacklift today is a commitment of capital, not a unit of launch capacity. Until an engine flies, AST's launch-access problem is unchanged.

Now follow the money, because it doesn't flow evenly. Firefly collects four hundred million dollars in cash upfront. It also gains an anchor external customer for its Miranda and Vira engine line, which it already shares with its Eclipse rocket. The other two payments are for engine deliveries and support work. None of that money depends on Blacklift reaching orbit. The market noticed. Firefly's shares jumped sharply on the news, though reports of the size of the move conflict, so I won't give you a number.

AST carries the other side of the trade. It pays a meaningful slice of the upfront bill in its own stock. It also owns the schedule and integration risk on a vehicle with unflown engines. Two alternative readings are worth holding. One is that the deal works as a customer-funded capital injection into Firefly. The other is leverage. An operator with its own rocket program may negotiate harder with third-party launch providers, even if Blacklift runs late. Either way, on the terms in this filing, the certain money runs to the supplier first.

Now a caution on the obvious reading of this deal. The tempting story is that AST got tired of waiting on a failing bench. On Friday, this desk noted that AST had tied its 2027 satellite target to New Glenn's availability. But AST's own filing doesn't tell that story. It calls Blacklift the completion of a "long-held strategy to achieve end-to-end vertical integration." It names no launch provider's failure as the trigger. And it says AST will keep using external providers for over one hundred satellites in 2027 and 2028. So Blacklift adds future capacity. It is not an exit from the launch market. For the next two years, AST's schedule still runs through other companies' rockets.

And that bench is why owning a rocket looks attractive, whatever AST's stated motive. ULA's Vulcan is scheduled to return to flight on November 13th, carrying forty Amazon Leo satellites. That ends a nine-month grounding, which began in February when a solid-booster nozzle failed less than thirty seconds into flight. ULA has disclosed only "several potential contributors," which it says were mitigated through nozzle design and process changes. It has not named a single root cause. The return flight is also Vulcan's first with six strap-on boosters, so a new configuration debuts on a vehicle fresh off a grounding. Meanwhile, a New Glenn meant for Leo satellites was lost in a pad explosion in May. Outside SpaceX, each heavy vehicle has proven to be one anomaly away from a long grounding.

There's a second way to respond to SpaceX's grip on orbit, and it doesn't involve building a rocket. Viasat, the legacy satellite-communications operator, has won a Golden Dome contract on the ground. Breaking Defense reports that the Space Force gave Viasat an indefinite-delivery contract with a four hundred fifty million dollar ceiling. The job is a Mission Operations Center for the Space Data Network Backbone. That's the relay network meant to connect Golden Dome's tracking sensors to decision-makers and weapons. On Thursday, this desk covered Golden Dome's fiscal 2027 budget request. Now the contract flow is reaching the ground segment.

Put the Viasat number in context. In May, SpaceX won two Space Force contracts totaling six point four five billion dollars. One of them, at two point two nine billion, was for that same backbone. Separately, in July, the Space Development Agency awarded one point seven five billion dollars for thirty-six missile-tracking satellites. Those were split evenly between L3Harris and Sierra Space. These are separate awards with possibly overlapping scope, so they shouldn't be added into a program total. But the shape is clear. This is a multi-vendor architecture with one dominant vendor, and it's SpaceX.

Viasat's figure also needs a caveat. Four hundred fifty million is a ceiling, the most it may bill, not booked revenue. The reporting gives no initial task-order value. Still, Viasat has found a defensible niche. It isn't competing with SpaceX in orbit. It's the ground-side integrator on a backbone SpaceX is building. For satcom incumbents facing low-Earth-orbit disruption, that is a complement to SpaceX, not an alternative.

Pulling the threads together. Two responses to SpaceX's dominance are now visible side by side. AST is trying to own the rocket, which means heavy capital spending and binary development risk. Viasat is taking a slot on the ground, which means light capital spending and a role that complements SpaceX. What's genuinely inflecting is the money. Real cash is moving to Firefly, and real contract flow is reaching Golden Dome's ground layer. What's still a demo is the hardware. Blacklift's engines haven't flown, and Vulcan's fix is unproven until it flies again. The biggest open question is whether a satellite operator can field a rocket on a roughly two-year clock when the base rate is six.

For catalysts, start with AST's third-quarter call early next week, and listen for how it plans to fund Blacklift. Then comes the Blacklift unveiling in New York on Thursday, November 12th. Vulcan's return flight follows the next day.

That's the briefing. Have a good weekend, and stay cautiously optimistic.
