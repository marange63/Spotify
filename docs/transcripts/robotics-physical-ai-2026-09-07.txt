Good morning. It's September 7th, and this is your briefing on robotics and physical AI.

For the past several sessions this show has made one argument about this sector. Almost every number in physical AI is self-reported, and there is no outside authority checking it. Today that argument gets sharpened rather than broken, and the sharpening turns out to be more interesting than a clean contradiction would have been.

Start with the milestone. Tesla says its robotaxi fleet has now crossed one million unsupervised miles, meaning miles driven with no safety monitor onboard. That is up from the 380,000 unsupervised miles Tesla disclosed on its July 22nd earnings call. So the fleet added roughly 620,000 miles in about six weeks. On its face, that is real acceleration.

Then ask the obvious follow-up question. Across how many cars? Nobody outside Tesla knows, because Tesla has never published a fleet size at all. What exists instead is community tracking, and the trackers do not agree with each other. One crowdsourced count put the unsupervised fleet near 200 vehicles across Austin, Dallas and Houston as of August 31st. This show cited a different community count on September 2nd covering essentially the same window, and that one put the active fleet at roughly 85 cars. Same fleet, same few days, and a spread of more than two to one between the estimates.

Take the whole range seriously and the picture is the same at either end. One million miles divided across somewhere between 85 and 200 vehicles is somewhere between five thousand and twelve thousand miles per car. Both ends of that band describe a few months of ordinary driving. So the headline is fleet build plus odometer, not demonstrated maturity. And the fact that the denominator itself is a guess, with credible trackers off by a factor of two, is the more telling fact of the two.

Because cumulative miles is an odometer reading, not a reliability metric. Tesla publishes no intervention rate, no disengagement rate and no crash rate measured against those unsupervised miles. For scale, Waymo has logged roughly 200 million autonomous miles, two orders of magnitude more. But treat that strictly as a scale comparison and not a safety ranking, because the two companies do not report on the same basis.

Now to the part of this story that needed correcting overnight, and it is worth being explicit about what the disputed claim was. The mileage milestone was reported by Electrek. The same piece made a second and much sharper claim alongside it: that Tesla had publicly asserted zero notable incidents at the 380,000-mile mark, while at least seventeen incidents appeared in government records. That was widely read, including in the plan behind this briefing, as an outside filing finally catching a company's safety claim in a lie.

It does not hold up, and the evidence that undoes it comes from Electrek itself. Back in May, the same publication went through the unredacted crash narratives filed with NHTSA. Those seventeen incidents span July of 2025 through March of 2026. Every one of them involved a Model Y with the driver-assistance system engaged and a safety monitor present. That is supervised testing. Tesla's zero-notable-incidents claim was specifically about the unsupervised population, meaning cars with nobody behind the wheel able to intervene. Those are two different populations of miles, collected under two different operating conditions, and they do not measure the same thing.

So this is not a government record catching Tesla in a false claim. It is something narrower and, honestly, more interesting. There is no public incident count, from Tesla or from NHTSA, for the unsupervised miles the milestone is actually about. The gap is not a lie exposed. It is a number nobody has published.

It is still worth knowing what those seventeen supervised-mode incidents looked like, since they get invoked so often. Thirteen were property damage only. Two had no injuries reported. One involved a minor injury without hospitalization, and one, the most serious, involved a minor injury requiring hospitalization. Not catastrophic, but also not nothing, and not a grade on the unsupervised fleet either way. Two caveats on that accounting. It reaches us through a publication's reading of the filed narratives, not through a document I can quote from NHTSA directly. And Tesla's own language has the mirror-image problem. Zero notable incidents is a company description, not a federal safety classification, and it covers only the subset of miles Tesla itself chooses to define.

So the so-what is not that Tesla got caught. It is that the sector still has no audited, apples-to-apples safety number for the exact population everyone cares about, and that absence is the finding. It matters commercially right now. Tesla is simultaneously soliciting third-party buyers for Cybercab fleets, and whoever underwrites one of those fleets is underwriting an exposure nobody can currently price with real data.

Where nobody gets to grade their own homework, what does the timeline actually look like? That question has an answer in defense.

On August 31st, Naval Air Systems Command opened a Request for Information for a carrier-based Collaborative Combat Aircraft, with industry responses due September 18th. The Navy's target is roughly thirty million dollars per aircraft, carrying up to five tons of weapons payload, with carrier certification achieved within three years. That is a heavier, pricier and more demanding machine than the Air Force's cheaper loyal-wingman concept.

Set that against where the Air Force program already is. Anduril's FQ-44 fired a live AIM-120 AMRAAM in a test in California in July. That is a genuine first, and it is also one shot on a range. A single successful firing tells you the weapons integration works once under prepared conditions. It does not tell you the aircraft can do it repeatably, under jamming, off a moving deck, or at a cost anyone has demonstrated. Treat it as a demonstration, not a fielded capability.

Even granting the demonstration, the shape of the Navy program is the point. What is not done is getting a Navy authority to certify an airframe for carrier operations, and that is budgeted at three years regardless of how good the software already is. This is the same finding as the one on the road, inverted. The binding constraint is not the model or the autonomy stack. It is the certification process sitting on top of it, set by an authority the vendor does not control. And flag the obvious limit here: this is only a Request for Information. No vendor has been selected, no funding is committed, and thirty million dollars is a planning number the Navy wrote before industry tells it what these things actually cost.

There is a strategic tension worth naming in that figure. The Navy is going heavy and expensive while the Air Force bets on cheap, attritable drones. That is a real disagreement inside the Pentagon about the future cost curve of autonomous aircraft, and it complicates the thesis underwriting a lot of private defense-autonomy valuations, which is that this technology gets cheap and disposable at scale. A thirty-million-dollar carrier-capable aircraft is not attritable. Two incompatible cost curves inside one Pentagon do not add up to a single addressable market that private marks can extrapolate from.

One more data point belongs in this frame, because it comes from the operator with the strongest safety claim of all. Waymo says its driver is involved in ninety-four percent fewer serious injury or worse crashes compared to human drivers. That is the boldest safety assertion anyone in this sector has made. It is also graded entirely by the company making it. The human-driver baseline, the crash-severity threshold and the exposure adjustment are all Waymo's own methodology, not independently audited here. Waymo is very likely the safer operator today. The point is not that the number is wrong. It is that nothing external forces it to be right, any more than anything external currently exists to check Tesla's mileage claims.

Which leads to the conclusion an investor should act on. Without a shared definition of an incident, per-mile safety figures across these operators are not merely imprecise, they are non-comparable. Nobody outside these companies can rank this sector on safety today, so every safety-based valuation premium in it rests on trust rather than measurement. And if certification rather than capability is what sets the schedule in both domains, then the scarce asset is a permit, an exemption or a qualification already in hand. That is an asset a competitor cannot train its way past, which argues that the capital pouring into compute for this sector is running ahead of the capital going into compliance and operations.

Put the three cases side by side. Tesla's headline number turns out not to be contradicted so much as simply unmeasured where it counts, and its denominator is a crowdsourced guess. Waymo's number is unaudited rather than unmeasured. And the Navy is the one case where an outside authority holds the pen, which is exactly why its schedule runs in years rather than months. What would settle any of this is easy to name and still does not exist. A primary incident count against Tesla's unsupervised miles specifically. A published intervention rate from any robotaxi operator. Or a real Navy contract with a real bid price attached. Until one of those shows up, treat every safety and reliability claim in this sector as a claim, not a measurement.
