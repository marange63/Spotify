Good morning, and welcome to Cautious Optimism Briefings for October 28th, 2026.

A standing disclosure first, because it matters more than usual today: Anthropic makes the model behind this show. Keep that in mind for everything that follows. Yesterday, October 27th, Anthropic published its first progress report on Claude in drug discovery. It covers six outside partners plus Anthropic's own in-house therapeutic program. It is the first quantified field report of its kind from a frontier lab. The headline pattern is simple. The measurable value is showing up in labor first. The biology claims are still preclinical, self-reported, and mostly best-of-N. Anthropic itself says the work is still at an early stage.

Start with the one number in the report that's actually decision-useful. Enveda Biosciences had Claude review 1,061 pages of manufacturing documentation. Claude caught all eleven issues the human reviewers found, and it flagged seven they missed. Human review time fell 89 percent, from more than 25 hours to under three. Here's why that matters. On October 24th, this show noted that OpenAI's Orchestra Bio acquisition into its Rosalind platform came with no measured customer outcome at all. Enveda's figure is the first measured operational outcome in this arc. It is still self-reported, and it comes from a single document set whose exact type the post doesn't break out. The takeaway is that this is a budget line, not a change in clinical success rates. If it generalizes, the first cost line exposed is CMC and regulatory-affairs review labor, along with the CRO and consulting fees attached to it.

Now to the bench work, where the evidence gets thinner. Generate Biomedicines had six scientists use Claude Code to redesign a genetic component of a CAR-T therapy. One design came out about 70 percent shorter than the standard version. In lab tests, it let immune cells make roughly ten times as many receptors. Per the post, it also killed tumor cells more efficiently and stayed active longer. That's a genuine wet-lab result, but it is in vitro, and it is one design.

BigHat Biosciences is the more instructive case, because of how the work was wired. Claude didn't invent molecules from a blank prompt. It was connected to BigHat's own lab-data system, called Reccy, and proposed changes to two antibody-fragment leads. BigHat's lab then made and tested about 1,010 variants in about ten weeks. The best versions bound more than ten times more tightly than the originals. Flag that for what it is. It's a best-of-N result, the top performers out of roughly a thousand. The post gives no share of variants that improved, and no developability, PK, or immunogenicity data. So the language model is acting as the orchestration layer over a proprietary data loop. The data loop, not the model, is the part BigHat owns.

That raises the model question this show keeps asking. The report never names a Claude model version for any of the six partnerships. It says only "Claude" or "Claude Code." So it cannot tell you which frontier model is best for which task.

Divergence Labs pushes the autonomy further. Claude turned the team's goals into a design plan. It then generated roughly 35,000 antibody designs in about a month, largely autonomously, for a food-allergy program. Early tests produced 3 designs that bind IgE and 20 that dislodge it from immune cells. On October 23rd, this show complained about numerators showing up without denominators. Today's report only partly answers that. BigHat gives a tested count. Divergence does not. The post says the 35,000 designs were narrowed down and tested, but it never says how many reached the bench. That isn't a gap in our research. It is something Anthropic chose not to disclose.

So you cannot compare Divergence with the roughly 8 percent binding rate this show reported for Anthropic's own toxin-binder campaign on October 22nd. Nor can you compare it with Xaira's 10 percent from October 25th. Generated is not tested, and the denominator is what makes a hit rate.

Bristol Myers Squibb's contribution is the weakest of the six. In the company's words, a single prompt produced "a robust initial analysis" that helped inform a trial-success-probability assessment. That's a testimonial. It has not been validated against actual trial outcomes, and it deserves no more weight than that.

The most consequential piece is Anthropic's own asset. Anthropic acquired a one-person biotech, Mithril Medicine, in July, and only disclosed the deal yesterday. No terms were given. Mithril's first results concern LAMA2-related congenital muscular dystrophy. The modality is bispecific antibodies that bind two targets to form a molecular bridge. Anthropic says the program went from idea to promising mouse results in under 18 months. It adds that a drug could reach human trials as soon as late 2028, if the candidates keep holding up. That compresses the discovery timeline meaningfully.

But apply the same scrutiny this show applied to OpenAI's Orchestra deal on October 24th. Then, we noted the deal helped OpenAI's equity story. Now, this report landed the day after Anthropic's own IPO filing went public. The motive is the same, so the scepticism should be too. There's also a structural shift here, and this part is our inference. An AI lab that owns a drug program is no longer just selling model access. It becomes both a supplier and a competitor to the platform biotechs that license its models. And late 2028 is the earliest case, conditional on the data. It says nothing about Phase 2 attrition, which is the clock that decides whether any of this changes the odds of an approved drug.

That's the language-model half of the stack. The vision half is being priced very differently. Agilent Technologies has agreed to acquire digital-pathology company Proscia, in an all-cash deal worth up to 270 million dollars. That's 240 million at closing, plus up to 30 million in milestones. The deal hasn't closed yet. Closing is expected early in Agilent's fiscal 2027.

Proscia's Concentriq platform is a software layer linking slide scanners, lab systems, and AI tools. Its customers include 16 of the top 20 pharma companies, and trailing revenue is above 18 million dollars. On our own arithmetic, that's roughly 15 times trailing revenue at the maximum price, or about 13 times on the guaranteed cash alone. What Agilent is buying is distribution and installed base, not validated model accuracy. There's no independent accuracy data for Proscia in view. And there's a risk to the very thing being bought, which is our inference. A vendor-neutral platform owned by an instrument maker may stop looking neutral to the rival scanner makers it has to keep connecting to. This fits a pattern we tracked on October 22nd and 25th: the money flows to whoever owns the physical and data plumbing. Proscia is the same story on the slide-data side.

The second-order threat sits one layer down. At ESMO this week, Agendia presented an investigational computer-vision model that estimates MammaPrint breast-cancer recurrence risk directly from routine H&E slides. We haven't seen the validation statistics, so treat it as a direction of travel, not a result. Here's the implication, and it's our inference. If models like this mature, the slide image could start substituting for some molecular send-out tests. That would shift diagnostics revenue toward whoever owns the imaging layer, which is exactly what Agilent just bought.

So pull the threads together. Three open questions matter most. Will any of these partner results be independently replicated? Will Bristol Myers Squibb's probability tool ever be scored against real trial outcomes? And will Anthropic's muscular-dystrophy program actually reach an IND filing?

On clinical success rates, the realistic read is narrow. The field's first real test case is Insilico Medicine's rentosertib, the generative-AI-designed TNIK inhibitor that began Phase III dosing on September 9th. ClinicalTrials.gov puts its estimated primary completion at October 30th, 2029. So set an 18-month path to mouse data against a three-year wait for the first Phase III proof. Today's evidence moves cost and cycle time. It does not yet move the odds.

For R&D spend, that means reallocation, not shrinkage. Specifically, money moves toward tools that compress review and design labor, like Enveda's document work. And it moves toward whoever owns the data infrastructure underneath the models, like BigHat's lab loop and Proscia's slide layer.
