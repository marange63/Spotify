Good morning, it's Friday, November 6th, and this is your private-equity briefing.

The test for this week's manager prints is simple. Read the realization line before the fee line. Carlyle reported Thursday, and the answer is more mixed than the headline.

Start with carry. Carlyle's realized net performance revenue is its share of gains on deals that have actually exited. It came in at 100 million dollars in the third quarter, and per the earnings call, that's up fivefold from a year ago. Carlyle's investor presentation says the jump was driven by its second U.S. equity opportunities fund. The notable transactions it names span its Japan buyout, energy, and European technology funds, including Rigaku in Japan and Tescan in Europe. So the private-equity book did the work this quarter, not credit.

Now scale it. Year to date, realized performance revenue is 235 million dollars. That means the first two quarters together produced only about 135 million. One strong quarter sits on top of two soft ones, which is lumpiness, not a run-rate. And the quarter's carry is roughly a third of its fee-related earnings. Carlyle is still overwhelmingly a fee business.

Here's the detail that cuts against the celebratory read. Carry is the general partner's cut, not cash in limited partners' accounts. The closer proxy for distributions is realized proceeds, the cash that carry funds generate when they sell. Carlyle put that at 8.8 billion dollars in the quarter, against 39.6 billion over the trailing twelve months. Do the division, and the third quarter ran slightly below the trailing quarterly average of roughly ten billion. The private-equity segment shows the same shape. It returned 5.7 billion dollars in the quarter, against a trailing quarterly average of about 5.9 billion. So the carry line jumped fivefold while the cash line didn't accelerate at all. The likeliest reading is that the spike reflects which funds were in carry when they sold. It is not a step-change in how much cash is coming back.

One more number from the same presentation points the same way. Net accrued performance revenue, the stock of carry that's earned on paper but not yet realized, was 2.1 billion dollars at quarter-end. That's down 12 percent from the prior quarter. Read simply, Carlyle drew carry out of the existing pool faster than new gains refilled it. So the realization line moved, but it's small, it's early, and it drew down the reservoir rather than signaling a broad thaw.

Now the fee side, which is still the bulk of the business. Third-quarter fee-related earnings were 293 million dollars. Carlyle's own presentation says that's down 6 percent from a year earlier. That deceleration sits underneath a headline that otherwise looks great. Year-to-date fee-related earnings hit a firm record of 950 million dollars, and management expects the highest full-year number in Carlyle's history. So it's a record year and a soft quarter at the same time. Both are true, and the quarter is the better read on near-term momentum.

So what is management actually selling? Forward fees, not this quarter's exits. Carlyle is guiding to about 60 billion dollars of inflows for the full year, more than 10 percent above last year. But read the mix before you hear that as a private-equity signal. Per the segment tables in Carlyle's presentation, Global Credit and the AlpInvest platform each raised more this year than the private-equity segment did. Credit's third-quarter inflows came from opportunistic credit, aviation, insurance solutions, and liquid credit. AlpInvest's came mainly from secondaries and portfolio finance. Even inside private equity, the quarter was led by the U.S. growth strategy. So those inflows are a diversified-manager story, not evidence that the buyout fundraising cycle has turned.

The forward number that really carries weight is pending fee-earning assets under management. That's capital already committed but not yet paying fees. It rose to 28 billion dollars, up almost 50 percent from a year ago. Management says that pool is worth more than 230 million dollars of annualized management fees once fully activated. That's a quantified claim that a big slice of next year's fee growth has already been raised. The catch is the word activated. Where fees switch on at deployment rather than at commitment, activation runs through the same deal market that's been starving exits. So that fee growth isn't locked in regardless of markets. It's locked in if deployment cooperates.

The same conversion engine shows up at Apollo, which reported on Tuesday. Apollo's fee-generating assets grew 28 percent to 880 billion dollars. That's faster than its total assets, which grew 18 percent. Apollo also reaffirmed its full-year guidance for fee-related earnings growth. One caution: Apollo's engine is largely credit and insurance, not buyout. The broader point is that across managers, the forward-growth pitch rests on turning committed capital into fee-paying capital, not on exits. That shapes how these firms get valued. If carry stays this lumpy, public investors will keep pricing alternative managers on fees and discounting carry. That rewards managers for growing fee-paying capital, whether or not cash comes back to limited partners.

Put the three big prints side by side. Blackstone's third-quarter realizations, reported in October, fell from the prior quarter, though management guided to a step-up in the fourth quarter. Apollo's realizations rose, and now Carlyle's carry has too. That's a selective thaw, two of the three big prints so far, one quarter each. And at Carlyle, the cash proceeds didn't speed up. That is not evidence that the industry's exit logjam has cleared.

If cash is only trickling out at the top, look at who's putting capital in, and who gets to set the price. The recent deal pattern has been take-privates where the biggest existing holder stays in. Clayton Dubilier and Rice is reportedly exploring a take-private of Global Payments, valued at around 40 billion dollars including debt. GTCR, the company's largest shareholder, would roll its stake, and nothing is signed. At Surgery Partners, the buyer is HealthSpan Partners, an affiliate of the Bon Secours Mercy Health system. Bain Capital, which owns about 39 percent, is rolling its stake in. In both cases capital goes in, but the biggest holder's limited partners get no cash on that stake.

ADT is the useful contrast. Thrive Holdings, a private capital vehicle rather than a classic buyout sponsor, agreed to take ADT private at about 15.3 billion dollars including debt. That's a premium of nearly 46 percent to the unaffected share price. And the deal carries a 30-day go-shop, which gives outside bidders a formal window to top the price.

Then on Thursday, a board pushed back for the first time in this wave of insider-led bids. An independent committee of Wendy's board unanimously rejected a roughly 1.4 billion dollar buyout offer led by Trian, already a major Wendy's shareholder.

Be precise about what that is. Trian is an activist investor, not a classic buyout sponsor, so Wendy's is a weak read on sponsor deal flow. A rejection is also often an opening move in a negotiation, not a verdict. But it does speak to the question hanging over insider-led bids. Will boards test them at arm's length, or wave them through? This board didn't wave it through. If others follow, and that's an inference from a single case, insider bidders will have to pay more, run more open processes, or walk away. That would make arm's-length tests, like the ADT go-shop, more informative than they'd otherwise be.

So where does the cycle actually stand? There's a real but selective realization thaw at the largest managers. Forward fee growth is being sold on converting committed capital, which is a cleaner story for managers than for limited partners waiting on distributions. The exit-and-distribution logjam remains the binding risk. Where's the market complacent? In reading record fee earnings and record inflows as proof the cycle is healthy. Where might it be over-discounting? In dismissing the cash that is moving. Carlyle's private-equity segment still returned 5.7 billion dollars of proceeds this quarter. That isn't a frozen book. It's a steady one that simply hasn't sped up.

Watch four things from here. KKR's parent-company results are scheduled for Monday, November 9th. Treat that as a date, not a number in hand, and read it for proceeds and carry before fees. The go-shop on Thrive's ADT take-private closes November 28th. Silence would prove little, but a topping bid would say the price wasn't fully tested. Watch whether Clayton Dubilier and Rice's approach for Global Payments becomes a signed deal. And Blackstone's January print will show whether its guided fourth-quarter step-up arrived. That will tell us whether the thaw reaches Blackstone too, and whether cash, not just carry, starts to accelerate.

That's the briefing. Have a good weekend.
