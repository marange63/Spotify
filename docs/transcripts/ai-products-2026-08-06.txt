For the past few days, this show has been tracking the machinery of autonomous agents — what they run on, what they cost, and whether users and businesses can trust what they do on their behalf. This week the question shifted. It's no longer just "can we trust the agent" — it's "who gets to decide the agent is allowed to act at all." A federal court, a security researcher, and an open-source maintainer group each drew a different boundary around agent authority, and together they tell you more about where autonomous software is headed than any single product launch would.

Start with the court. On Tuesday, the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals vacated a preliminary injunction that had barred Perplexity's Comet browser agent from operating on Amazon.com. Amazon had argued that Comet's autonomous shopping actions — clicking, filling forms, completing purchases without Amazon's permission — violated the federal Computer Fraud and Abuse Act. The panel disagreed, and the reasoning is the important part. As the court put it, "Perplexity itself does not access Amazon's servers—users of the Comet browser do." The agent vendor isn't the accessor; the person who told it to shop is. And the court added that an injunction against conduct that likely doesn't violate the law in the first place "would not serve the public interest."

Two things make this ruling matter more than a single case. First, the opinion is marked for publication, meaning it's precedential — other litigants can cite it directly in the Ninth Circuit, not just point to it as persuasive commentary. Second, the panel noted there's essentially no existing caselaw on how to assign this kind of responsibility for AI agents, which is part of why it leaned toward Perplexity. So the legal ground here is still being poured, not set. The court itself said its ruling was confined to this record and didn't announce a broad rule for agentic AI generally — and the underlying CFAA claims haven't been decided on the merits, only this injunction. It's also worth remembering how high the CFAA bar sits: a claim requires showing at least five thousand dollars in losses over a one-year period, a threshold Amazon would still have to clear even if it kept fighting. Amazon is reportedly weighing its options, whether that's a rehearing request or an appeal further up.

So don't read this as agentic commerce being blessed forever. Read it as a first data point that, for now, favors the agent builders over the platforms trying to keep them out through federal computer-crime law. And it doesn't just help Perplexity — it lowers the legal temperature for OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and everyone else shipping browser agents that shop, book, and check out on third-party sites. Expect Amazon and its peers to respond not with more CFAA lawsuits but with terms-of-service changes, robots.txt blocks, and technical bot detection. The fight moves from courtrooms to code.

If that's the legal boundary moving outward, the next story is about the plumbing those newly-emboldened agents actually run on — and it's shakier than the industry has been claiming. Anaconda announced this week that it's acquiring the AI-security startup Enkrypt AI, and the acquisition was framed around Enkrypt's own research: scanning more than 268,000 tools across 25,000 servers running the Model Context Protocol, the connective tissue most agent products use to call outside tools. The finding: more than 143,000 vulnerabilities, affecting 73 percent of those servers. Anaconda's own framing is that every model an agent runs on, every tool it calls, and every MCP server it touches is a potential exposed attack vector. That's a real counterweight to the industry's recent claim — one this show relayed on Tuesday — that MCP had matured into production-grade infrastructure.

Take the number with a grain of salt, though. Enkrypt is now owned by Anaconda, the company selling the fix, so this is a vendor making the case for its own acquisition, not independently audited research, and we don't know how many of those servers were live production systems versus test instances. Still, the direction is clear enough. MCP server security is turning into a standard line item in enterprise AI procurement — something a buyer now checks before approving an agent deployment, not after. It also puts pressure on Anthropic, which created the protocol, to harden MCP's default security guidance and add native authentication and sandboxing rather than leaving each server operator to bolt it on. And expect more of this pattern: data platforms and dev-tool vendors absorbing AI-security startups rather than enterprises building that layer themselves. Watch Databricks, Snowflake, and the major clouds for the next version of the same move.

Now the human boundary, and it cuts the other way. Five teams on the Rust programming language project published a formal policy this week restricting how large language models can be used in contributions to the core compiler repository. The policy's own words: "It's fine to use LLMs to answer questions, analyze, distill, refine, check, suggest, review. But not to create." LLM-authored code, comments, or documentation can't be merged without disclosure — and, as the policy bluntly puts it, "You may not hide LLM involvement." Maintainers can close non-compliant pull requests without further explanation. This is a genuine counter-signal to the story that AI now writes most software. One of the most safety-critical, widely-depended-on open-source projects in the world just decided that agentic coding tools earn trust for analysis and review, but not for authorship, in code millions of systems depend on.

That's not a broad retreat from AI coding — adoption keeps climbing everywhere else — but it does mark where the line currently sits between genuine leverage and unreviewable noise. And it has a product implication. If more critical-infrastructure projects follow Rust's lead, the coding-tool vendors — GitHub Copilot, Claude Code, Cursor — get pushed to build provenance and disclosure directly into the commit workflow, so a maintainer can see at a glance what a model touched. Trust becomes a feature you ship, not a claim you make. And other critical-infrastructure projects, from language runtimes to the Linux kernel, are likely watching.

We'll close on the one item this week that's a pure capability unlock rather than a boundary dispute. xAI updated Grok Imagine Video 1.5 to accept up to seven image and voice references per generation, letting a creator lock a face, a product, and a location consistently across multiple shots, and pair a character image with a voice reference to hold the same face and voice across scenes. The product also added native 1080p and moved from image-led animation to full text-to-video, with the whole capability exposed through xAI's API for developers to build on. Multi-shot consistency has been the single biggest practical gap keeping AI video out of real narrative production — ads, explainers, short-form series — rather than one-off novelty clips. So this is a real unlock for solo creators and small studios, not just a resolution bump, and it puts fresh pressure on Runway and Google's Veo to match multi-reference consistency quickly.

But notice where it lands us, right back where we started. A tool that clones a specific face and voice from reference images reopens the provenance and deepfake questions that ran under every story in this briefing. The theme holds. The harder question in AI products right now isn't what the technology can do, it's who gets to authorize it — and this week three different gatekeepers each answered that differently.
