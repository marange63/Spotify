Good morning, it's Tuesday, August 25th, and this is Cautious Optimism Briefings.

Start with the number this show has been circling all week. Unitree closed Monday at 603.08 yuan. That is more than 40 percent below its opening-day price of 1,100 yuan. Across four trading sessions, roughly 200 billion yuan of market value came off. This is the first time public markets have ever put a single, daily-marked price on a pure-play humanoid maker. Within a week, that market took a large piece of its own opening verdict back.

We spent last week asking humanoid makers for one thing: a paying-deployment count, or anything harder than a demo. Monday the closest thing to an answer arrived, and it came from analysis of Unitree's own offering materials rather than an audited segment disclosure. Research and education customers accounted for 73.6 percent of humanoid revenue in 2025. Industrial applications accounted for about 9 percent. Now be careful with the causation, because it is tempting and only half right. The slide ran across four sessions. The mix analysis surfaced near the end of it. The tape and the disclosure point the same direction, but the tape did not wait for the disclosure.

And don't overclaim the crash either. Even at 603 yuan, Unitree trades far above where it priced. Its debut close was 460 percent above the offer price. The offer itself implied 219 times 2025 adjusted diluted earnings. And no institutional model ever endorsed the opening print in the first place. Nomura's pre-listing target was 370 yuan, which is still well below Monday's close. What set the opening price was a retail-driven book discovering its own ceiling.

So what happened this week is the repricing of a timeline, not the repudiation of a company. Unitree is profitable, and it shipped more than 5,000 humanoid units last year. Layer in the detail we flagged last week, that first-quarter adjusted net profit fell 52.55 percent year on year while operating cash flow fell 85.65 percent, and you get a real, cash-generative hardware business whose growth story ran ahead of its industrial customer base. The market just made it catch up.

Now the other side of that same trade, priced in the same session. On Monday, XPeng's robotics unit said it had signed share purchase agreements to raise over 900 million dollars at a post-money valuation above 6.3 billion. That figure comes from XPeng's own press release. The round is led by IDG Capital, with Gaorong Ventures alongside, and with Tencent and Alibaba as strategic investors. Reporting describes it as the largest single private round to date in China's embodied-AI industry. Two precisions before we build anything on it. XPeng's own earnings call the same day put the valuation at over 6.2 billion rather than 6.3 — a small gap between a company's press release and its own transcript. And the release still frames closing as a future step, saying XPeng will retain controlling ownership upon closing. So treat this as signed and priced, not yet fully wired.

That contrast is the engine of the week. The one venue with a daily mark discounted the category. The venue with no daily mark, private and strategic capital, did the opposite inside the same 24 hours.

XPeng's model is also structurally different from the American humanoid field. Its Iron robot runs on three in-house Turing chips, rated by the company at up to 2,250 TOPS combined. Those are company specifications, not benchmarked results. Figure, Apptronik and 1X raise pure financial capital and lean on Nvidia's compute stack. XPeng is an auto maker bolting robotics onto silicon it designed and lines it already owns. Watch whether in-house edge silicon becomes a genuine alternative to Nvidia's Jetson-and-GR00T stack, because that has export-control implications running in both directions. It also sharpens the argument this show made on Friday. If the robot brain commoditizes, advantage relocates downward, to whoever controls hardware, compute and actuator cost. Vertical integration is exactly the bet you make if you believe that.

Now turn skeptical on XPeng, because the same demand-quality problem just showed up in a different costume. XPeng is targeting mass production by the end of 2026. Its own disclosures put the first deployments inside XPeng's retail stores and campuses. Third-party deliveries do not start until 2027. And management's own description of that 2027 phase is deliveries to external customers in the retail and service sectors. That is a market segment. It is not a named buyer, a purchase order, or a letter of intent. On the same call, management said Iron will be priced at two and a half to three times its bill-of-materials cost, while disclosing no bill-of-materials figure, no revenue, and no unit-shipment number. So XPeng's first customer is XPeng, the same way Unitree's first customers were universities. Neither buyer is clearing a payback period against a loaded labor rate.

There is a third venue pricing this industry, and it disagrees with both. Morgan Stanley raised its China humanoid shipment forecast in June for the second time this year, to 50,000 units for 2026 — nearly double the number it carried in the spring, and more than triple its January call. Its 2030 figure is 446,000 units. That is a sell-side projection, not a realized number, and two upward revisions inside eight months tell you how unstable the modeling underneath it is. But here is the part worth sitting with: a rising shipment forecast and a falling share price are not in conflict. In a category where industrial applications are 9 percent of the leader's humanoid revenue, tripling a unit forecast mostly forecasts more laboratory equipment. Shipments and value capture are decoupling, and only one of them is being forecast.

Beijing supplied the testimony for why. The World Humanoid Robot Games gave the world its viral clip over the weekend, a humanoid beating Usain Bolt's hundred-meter mark on a straight track, and this show credited that engineering delta on Sunday. Here is what didn't make the highlight reel. At the same event, robots tripped, broke apart, caught fire, and had to be carted off on stretchers. And at the conference co-located with it, the people running actual commercial deployments were unusually candid. Unitree's founder, Wang Xingxing, said robots are not yet as efficient as humans and take time to learn new skills. Keenon's chief operating officer was sharper: getting a robot to complete 50 percent of a task, even 80 percent, is relatively easy, but reaching a 99.9 percent completion rate really tests engineering. And the president of the Association for Advancing Automation, speaking for the buyers, said the quiet part outright. We don't care if it's a humanoid, he said. We need a solution. So the buyers are indifferent to the form factor. The capital, in the same week, in the same city, is anything but.

Pull the venues together and you have this week's thesis. A public tape marked the humanoid volume leader down more than 40 percent. A private round marked a pre-revenue robotics unit above six billion dollars. A sell-side desk has marked the category's shipment volume up twice this year. All three are pricing the same underlying industry, and they disagree, because only one of them requires a daily mark and forces contact with an actual revenue mix.

Two consequences follow, and they cut against each other. First, Unitree is now the public comparable for the entire category, and that comparable just broke. Figure, Apptronik and Physical Intelligence inherit a public read-through nobody asked for, unless one of them can show a sales mix the volume leader's own disclosure says it does not have. AgiBot's reported Hong Kong listing, which we named as a catalyst on Monday, now prices against a broken tape rather than a pre-crash one. Second, and this is the uncomfortable part. If public markets have started disciplining humanoid valuations while private and strategic money has not, the arbitrage runs one way. Issuers will prefer private rounds and let listing timelines slip. That withholds exactly what this show has spent a week asking for: audited mix, unit economics, a deployment count. Discipline in one venue can buy less disclosure everywhere else.

So watch three things from here. Whether AgiBot's listing prices against Unitree's broken tape. Whether XPeng's production line has a real third-party customer behind it by the time it ships next year. And whether any humanoid maker, anywhere, publishes a commercial-deployment count instead of a shipment count. Until one does, the honest read is that this industry is being priced by venue, not by evidence.

That's the briefing. Talk tomorrow.
