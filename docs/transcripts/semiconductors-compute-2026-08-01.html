Good morning, this is Cautious Optimism Briefings for August 1st, 2026.

We're starting with a genuine divergence in Arm Holdings, and what it tells us about how the market is now pricing the entire custom-silicon build-out. Arm reported fiscal first-quarter results on July 29th: total revenue of $1.29 billion, up 22% year over year and above the roughly $1.27 billion consensus, according to the earnings coverage. Royalty revenue hit a first-quarter record of $715 million, also up 22%, and licensing and other revenue rose 23% to $574 million, so the beat wasn't carried by one line alone. Non-GAAP earnings beat estimates by 13%. On the call, management said customer demand for Arm's AGI custom-CPU business now exceeds $2 billion across fiscal 2027 and 2028 combined, with initial product already delivered to multiple customers. And Arm stock fell 8% that same day.

Here's the nuance worth getting right, because the easy version of this story is wrong. That doubling from $1 billion to more than $2 billion in AGI CPU demand didn't happen this quarter. It happened back in May, at Arm's fiscal fourth-quarter call, when the CFO said demand was "more than double what we stated at launch," referring to the $1 billion figure from March's Arm Everywhere event.

What Arm reported on July 29th was a reiteration of that same $2 billion-plus threshold, plus a related but distinct point: the company has now secured the manufacturing capacity to actually support the $1 billion tranche of that demand. So the pipeline hasn't doubled again. It's held above $2 billion for two straight quarters, and Arm still hasn't named a single customer or disclosed what segment they're in. Management was also explicit that it won't give visibility into when that demand converts to recognized revenue until the fiscal third-quarter results. That's a durable signal, not a fresh inflection, and durability is actually the more interesting story, because it means the demand isn't a one-quarter fluke.

So what actually explains the 8% drop? Operating expenses rose 28% to $1.16 billion, driven by R&D and headcount growth, which compressed Arm's GAAP operating margin to 7% from 11% a year ago. The market is pricing that near-term margin compression more heavily than a multi-year backlog that's still just a management-stated pipeline number, not booked revenue. Some of that selloff likely overlaps with the broader AI-chip risk-off that hit AMD, Marvell, and Intel the same week, so don't read it as a pure Arm-specific repricing. But the through-line holds: investors are now discriminating on whether AI-infrastructure spending converts to cash, not on whether the growth story is real.

There's a competitive read here beyond the stock, too. A custom-CPU pipeline that holds above $2 billion strengthens Arm's case as a genuine silicon competitor, not just an IP licensor, against x86 in AI-server CPUs. That's a slow-building pressure on Intel's and AMD's server roadmaps over the next two fiscal years, and it's the part of this story the tape isn't paying for today.

That same growth-versus-drag trade shows up at Qualcomm, reporting fiscal third-quarter results the same day. Qualcomm posted total revenue of $9.9 billion, with $8.5 billion of that from the chip segment, and non-GAAP earnings of $2.21 a share. Automotive revenue hit a record $1.6 billion, up 61% year over year, and management raised its fiscal 2026 exit run-rate target to about $7 billion from $6 billion previously. On data center, two hyperscaler custom-silicon engagements will start generating revenue in the December quarter. Wafers are already in production, putting Qualcomm on the path toward $5 billion in data-center revenue by fiscal 2027 and $15 billion by fiscal 2029, per prior guidance the company reiterated. Both hyperscaler customers remain unnamed, and that $15 billion figure is old guidance, not a new commitment. The core AI accelerator line still hasn't shipped in volume.

Where Arm's drag is margin, Qualcomm's drag is its single biggest customer walking away. Management guided that Apple-related revenue will fall roughly 50% sequentially from the September to the December quarter, and that Qualcomm's modem share of the next iPhone will land materially below the roughly 20% previously assumed. That's the long-feared Apple in-house modem transition finally converting from an overhang into a dated, guided revenue cut. So Qualcomm is proving the same thing Arm is: diversification into custom AI silicon produces real, dated revenue, but the market still has to net that against a legacy franchise eroding faster than expected.

The same AI demand pull funding those two silicon pipelines is now visibly starving a different part of the chip world: consumer graphics cards. AMD notified board partners including Sapphire, ASUS, XFX, and Vastarmor of roughly 10% price increases on GPU-and-memory kits, effective this July. That's AMD's second such hike in about six months. TrendForce, which reported the AMD increase, attributes it to a structural shortage in GDDR graphics memory, with consumer-grade GDDR6 spot prices surging from around $2.50 to $7.50 per gigabyte, a threefold jump, as AI data-center demand pulls fab capacity away from graphics memory production. Nvidia is reportedly following with a broader GeForce lineup price increase of its own, though that side of the story is less well sourced and worth treating cautiously.

The signal, though, is clear even from AMD's numbers alone: the memory shortage that's been playing out in server DRAM and HBM contracts all week has now broken out into an entirely separate product category. Fab capacity is being reallocated toward AI-grade memory at the direct expense of gaming hardware. If that persists, expect PC gaming refresh cycles to slow as a genuine demand-side consequence, distinct from anything happening in the data center, and expect it to nudge the marginal buyer toward console and cloud gaming instead. That's the memory shortage arriving at the consumer's wallet, not just the hyperscaler's capex line.

Closing the loop, there's a supply-side response worth flagging briefly. GlobalFoundries signed a letter of intent with the US Commerce Department's CHIPS Research and Development Office for a $300 million award to advance next-generation silicon photonics, optical materials, and advanced packaging. It's not a finalized or disbursed award, and terms remain to be set, but it's a concrete, dollar-figure bet that as AI clusters scale toward millions of GPUs, optical interconnect becomes the next bottleneck after memory.

Put it together and the pattern across this whole briefing is the same: the market isn't rewarding headline AI-demand growth anymore. It's sorting semiconductor names by what that growth costs in margin, and by how exposed each one still is to a legacy business in decline. Arm and Qualcomm both have real, converting demand, and both are being priced on their scars, not their pipelines.
