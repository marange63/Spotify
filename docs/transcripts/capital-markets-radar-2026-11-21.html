Capital Markets Cross-Asset Radar

Transcript · November 21, 2026

Good morning, it's Saturday, November 21st, and this is the Capital Markets Radar.

Friday changed Thursday's read on this selloff in two ways. The damage re-concentrated in AI, on an integrity shock rather than a growth one. And for the first time all week, Treasuries acted like a hedge, while gold and bitcoin kept behaving like sources of cash. Let's take those in order.

Start with Nvidia. The shares closed down 14.01% on Friday at $125.53, after touching a fresh 52-week low during the session. The trigger was reports, led by Reuters, that the SEC is investigating the company's accounting. The selling ran through the chip complex, with AMD down more than 13% in sympathy, per CNBC.

Now set that against Thursday's tape. In yesterday's briefing, this desk leaned toward a broad de-grossing story, because the Dow fell about as hard as the Nasdaq. Friday looked different. Per Motley Fool's market data, the Nasdaq fell 5.13%. Per CNBC, the Dow fell 1.93%, well under half the Nasdaq's drop. The Dow and the S&P both closed in correction, so the de-risking is still broad. But the marginal selling went back to AI. Yesterday we also said Nvidia was carrying a leadership discount from its CEO's exit, on top of the broad move. On Friday, that Nvidia-specific leg became the story. The two readings were never mutually exclusive. Friday shows an AI-specific catalyst landing on a market that was already de-grossing.

Here's why the kind of shock matters. This is not a demand story. Nvidia's fiscal third quarter, reported Tuesday, beat Wall Street on both earnings and revenue. An accounting probe doesn't argue with the demand behind those numbers. It argues with whether the numbers can be relied on. So "falling despite the beat" is the wrong frame. The beat is exactly what's being questioned. That makes this stickier than a growth scare, until someone with authority clarifies.

And nobody has clarified. The probe exists only as reports. Neither Nvidia nor the SEC has confirmed, denied, or commented, and the scope is unknown. A fourteen percent drop prices something close to a confirmed enforcement action. If the scope proves narrow, that gap is where the overshoot lives. The downside case needs one piece of backstory. Jensen Huang resigned as Nvidia's chief executive just over a week ago, after a board investigation. This week the chairman said he isn't coming back, and Colette Kress is running the company on an interim basis. So if the probe is confirmed and broad, an accounting question lands on top of an unfinished CEO search. That turns a governance worry into a full crisis. The so-what: until the SEC or Nvidia speaks, this is headline risk, and headline risk doesn't clear on fundamentals.

Which raises the obvious question of who was left to sell. Goldman and Morgan Stanley prime-brokerage data, cited by LPL Research, already had hedge funds underweight the broad AI trade for the first time since early 2023. That data was published before this week's selloff. Goldman also described the unwind as closer to its start than its end. The data can't tell us who sold on Friday. But it's a useful prior, because the fast money had largely left this trade already. So our inference is that Friday's air pocket hit the remaining long-only, passive, and systematic holders. That matters for what comes next. Those holders sell on mandate and volatility, not on a view, so they don't need a fresh headline to keep selling.

Now, if AI-specific risk is back on top, the next question is what hedged it. For the first time this week, Treasuries did. Per the Treasury Department's daily par yield curve, the 10-year closed Friday at 4.92%. That's back below five percent. Per Trading Economics, it was the fifth straight down session, on explicit safe-haven demand. In yesterday's briefing, this desk said there was no duration bid at all. Friday broke that pattern.

What makes the rally unusual is the Fed backdrop. CME FedWatch, in the latest read we have, prices a 96.9% chance of a quarter-point hike at the December 8th-to-9th meeting. A month ago, that was 76.3%. Bonds rallying with a hike pinned says the long end is pricing risk to growth and financial conditions beyond December. One LPL strategist made the bridge. If the AI selloff tightens financial conditions more broadly, the Fed has the flexibility to slow down.

This desk also owes you a correction. Yesterday we argued, from the Philadelphia Fed survey, that weak activity with firm prices meant bad growth news couldn't rally bonds. Friday's rally says otherwise. Either growth fear now outweighs inflation fear, or one regional survey was too thin to carry that call. The plain admission is the second one. We hung a regime call on a single survey. But one good hedging day isn't a regime either. The so-what for discount rates is this. If Treasuries keep hedging with a December hike fully priced, the long end is voting against the hiking path after December. That includes the March hike Goldman added to its forecast, which this desk flagged on Tuesday. That helps long-duration equity multiples, but only if the AI-specific risk premium stops rising.

What didn't hedge is just as telling. Per CoinDesk, gold fell nearly 3% on Friday to $3,514, its lowest since early September. It did that on a day when nominal Treasury yields were falling. That tilts the open question on gold, margin selling versus a real-rate story, toward margin selling. Treat that as directional, since we don't have Friday's real-yield print. The so-what: gold is trading as a source of liquidity, not a haven.

Bitcoin didn't hedge either, and its institutional wrapper is now the marginal seller. Spot bitcoin ETFs lost $582.3 million on Thursday, per CoinDesk. That's nearly double the $306.4 million this desk reported for November 18th. BlackRock's IBIT accounted for more than nine-tenths of it. One caution there. A single fund carrying that much of the outflow could be one large holder rebalancing, not a broad exit. Either way, a wrapper that sells on risk-off days undercuts the uncorrelated-store-of-value pitch. And it does so just as allocators are cutting risk budgets.

The mechanism tying these together is volatility rising everywhere at once. CoinDesk's Friday daybook had the VIX at 29.5, in an intraday read. Bitcoin's 30-day implied-volatility index hit 52%, a level last seen in the early-June crash. When implied vol rises across equities, gold, and bitcoin together, correlations rise with it. Diversification fails exactly when portfolios need it. For balanced books, that leaves duration, and possibly cash, as the only working diversifier. Our inference is that this raises the odds of further de-leveraging in the hedges that didn't work. On Friday, duration was the one that did.

Last stop is credit, which decides whether this stays an equity-and-crypto story or becomes a funding-cost story. Per The Private Banker, CoreWeave's five-year credit default swaps reached 1,232.6 basis points on Friday. On Wednesday, this desk had them around 1,100, and called CoreWeave off its early-November spike. Friday undid most of that retreat. Here's a risk channel worth naming, as our inference rather than a sourced cause of that move. An accounting question at the largest AI supplier gives lenders a governance reason, not just a demand reason, to reprice the neocloud and data-center debt layer.

The flow signal points the same way. High-yield funds worldwide saw $1.36 billion of outflows in the week to November 18th, per LSEG Lipper data reported by Reuters. But with no broad spread print since the 18th, CoreWeave is the canary, not yet the confirmation. The so-what: watch CoreWeave first, and treat the next broad spread print as the verdict, in either direction.

Heading into next week, the test list is short. Any confirmation or denial on the Nvidia probe. The next high-yield spread prints. And whether Treasuries keep hedging as the calendar turns. That means PCE on November 25th, payrolls on December 4th, the Fed decision on December 9th, and CPI the day after. None of those has happened yet. The question to carry: does the bond market's new willingness to hedge survive the next inflation print?

That's the Capital Markets Radar for November 21st. Stay cautious, stay optimistic.
