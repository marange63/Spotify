Good morning. It's Monday, November 23rd, and this is your private equity briefing.

Let's start with a reversal, because this desk owes you one. In each of our last three briefings, we cited the collapse of the CD&R and GTCR consortium chasing Global Payments as evidence that the buyer pool for megadeals had gotten short. On Friday, that read got complicated. GTCR disclosed in an SEC filing that it is now weighing a bid for Global Payments itself.

The filing is deliberately thin. GTCR says it is evaluating the move and has made no definitive decision. Bloomberg, citing unnamed sources, puts a potential deal at about fifty billion dollars, including debt and a premium. That is an estimate, not a disclosed price. There is no offer, no equity value, and no premium on the table. Nor do we know whether GTCR would go it alone or bring in new partners.

Here's what makes this more than a rumor worth tracking. GTCR is not a fresh third party. It is already Global Payments' largest shareholder. The shareholders agreement filed with the SEC puts its stake at just under fifteen and a half percent. GTCR got that stake by selling a majority of Worldpay to Global Payments. And it took payment partly in paper. Per GTCR's own announcement of that sale, its consideration was fifty-nine percent cash and forty-one percent stock.

That matters for realized versus paper returns. The Worldpay sale was an exit. But roughly two-fifths of GTCR's proceeds came in the buyer's own stock rather than cash. And even that stock came with a lock-up. The shareholders agreement releases it in tranches, the last one eighteen months after the deal closed. A take-private now would turn that listed position back into a private one. It would be carried at a model's mark instead of a public price. For GTCR's limited partners, part of a realization would roll back into paper. That last step is our inference. We don't know whether GTCR has sold or distributed any of those shares. But the mechanism stands on its own logic. And if it works, it becomes a template. A sponsor that takes acquirer stock in a sale keeps an option to come back for control. The trade-off is that its exit was never fully cash.

There's also an insider-bidder problem. When your largest shareholder is also your would-be buyer, the board isn't running a clean third-party sale. Any rival bidder faces a holder with a big head start in the vote and an information edge. That's the logic of the position, not a claim about anything Global Payments' board has done. We also checked whether GTCR's standstill would stop it from bidding. The text we found bars GTCR from acquiring additional voting securities. It doesn't, on its face, bar an acquisition proposal. The full agreement could hold a provision we haven't seen. But on the available text, the real obstacles are financing and the board's reception, not a contractual wall.

Financing is where the earlier attempt broke. Reuters' reporting on the consortium's collapse said the talks never moved past an early stage. Its sources cited the difficulty of a deal that size. That reads as a scale and financing problem, not a price fight or a board rejection. And that problem hasn't gone away because one partner left. At fifty billion dollars including debt, a sponsor without a club has to write a far bigger equity check. So read Friday's filing as GTCR testing whether it can clear a bar that got higher, not lower, when CD&R walked.

So what actually clears in this market? Line up three take-privates by how far each has gotten. Global Payments is being evaluated. The BILL deal we covered Friday, with its strategic buyer wired in, is signed but not due to close until the first half of 2027. And KKR's five-point-seven-billion-dollar take-private of Integer Holdings, the medical device maker, closed on November 17th after shareholder approval. Evaluated, signed and closed are three different states. Only the last one proves the capital actually got funded. The lesson, from a small sample, is that mid-size take-privates of defensible cash-flow businesses are getting financed and finished. The megadeal tier is where things still wobble.

The pressure behind those big tickets is uncalled capital. On its third-quarter call, KKR reported a record one hundred forty-six billion dollars of dry powder. That's firm-wide, not a buyout-only number. But it shows the scale of committed money looking for a home. And record dry powder is a liability as much as a war chest. It either goes out at today's entry multiples, or it sits idle and drags on net returns. Either way, it keeps the largest sponsors bidding for a handful of very large tickets. That's the top-heavy deployment pattern we flagged on Friday, not a broadening of deal flow.

That capital comes from limited partners, and one of the biggest is already past its own target. These figures come from a single secondary summary of CalPERS disclosures, by BigGo Finance, so hold them with some care. As of September 30th, private equity was twenty-two point four percent of CalPERS's total assets. That's one hundred forty-five point six billion dollars. The board's target is seventeen percent. And the board only raised that target from thirteen percent in March 2024. So CalPERS sits more than five points over a target that had already been lifted. Separately, in the first half of the year, CalPERS cut its directly held Magnificent Seven shares by twenty-six percent. CalPERS hasn't linked that trim to its private equity position. Treat the pairing as coincident, not causal.

There are two honest readings of that overweight, and they point in opposite directions. The passive reading is drift. Capital keeps getting called, distributions stay thin, and the managers' marks haven't been forced down. So the private sleeve grows whether or not anyone chose it. The active reading is that CalPERS is deliberately leaning into private equity on a relative-value call. Nothing disclosed settles which it is, so we'll leave it open. But the implications diverge sharply. Under the passive reading, CalPERS is latent supply for LP-led secondaries the moment it rebalances toward seventeen percent. That supply would land in a market where, as we noted last Monday, secondary buyer capital was being used up faster than it was replenished. Under the active reading, big public pensions keep committing to new funds through a distribution drought. That lets the fundraising machine outlast the exit problem. It pushes the reckoning out rather than resolving it.

There's a tie back to the lead here. CalPERS's twenty-two point four percent is measured on the general partners' own net asset values. So the overweight is only as good as those marks. If the marks are too high, the true overweight is smaller than it looks. The catch is that the gap only gets realized if CalPERS sells, at whatever discount a secondary buyer demands. That's why a negotiated take-private price matters beyond one deal. If Global Payments gets one, it's among the few events in this asset class that force a hard, market-tested number onto a holding.

So where does the cycle actually stand? It's being sustained less by cash coming back to limited partners, and more by owners already inside leaning further in. GTCR is weighing a bid for the company it took stock in as payment. CalPERS is running more than five points over its own target. Neither move resolves the exit-and-distribution logjam for the median asset. Both work around it, and that logjam remains the biggest unresolved risk. The complacent read is that GTCR's return means the megadeal financing window is wide open again. The over-discounting read is to wave off an evaluation filing from a fifteen-percent holder as noise. The better read sits between them. This is a real option on a deal, with no price and an unsolved financing problem.

Watch four things. First, whether GTCR makes a formal proposal, and how Global Payments' board responds. Second, what financing package, if any, gets assembled behind it. Third, BILL's shareholder vote on the way to its 2027 close. And fourth, Blackstone's guided step-up in fourth-quarter realizations. We've flagged it in every briefing this past week, and it should show up in the manager prints early next year. One loose end from Friday is the Partners Group re-vote on splitting its Global Value evergreen fund. Our sources don't show a result yet.

That's the state of the machine. Thanks for listening, and I'll see you next time.
