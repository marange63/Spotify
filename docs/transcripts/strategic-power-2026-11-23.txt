Three shifts are under way in Washington right now, and none of them adds capacity. There's a political clock on Ukraine, a compliance shift on chips, and a jurisdictional split on the power grid. In each case, a deadline or a rule is standing in for a physical result. We'll take them in that order.

Start with Ukraine. The Financial Times reported on November 20th that the White House would like Russia and Ukraine to reach a peace deal by early January 2027. The FT cited four people familiar with the matter. It placed that target before Democrats take control of both chambers of Congress. The FT gives the congressional handover as the timing, not as the stated reason. Either way, the date comes from Washington's calendar, not from the battlefield or from agreed terms.

Last Thursday, this desk flagged Ukraine as reportedly on the table at the Trump-Xi-Putin meeting in Shenzhen. We still have no readout from that room. What we do have is a public record of two sides describing different negotiations. According to NBC News, Zelenskyy says the US floated the idea of Ukraine ceding Donbas in exchange for NATO membership. Al Jazeera reports that Jared Kushner says Russia is okay with a NATO-like security pact for Ukraine. Moscow's on-the-record answer came from Deputy Foreign Minister Sergei Ryabkov. He said Kyiv should recognize, quote, "the irrevocable nature of our demand that this state must not join NATO." He called any quasi-membership formula, quote, "an option that is unacceptable to us." Ryabkov's rejection and Kushner's claim were both reported on November 20th.

Now set that against the Western position. US negotiator Steve Witkoff has said participants emphasized that any arrangement must, quote, "respect sovereignty, territorial integrity, independent decision-making, and provide credible security guarantees for Ukraine." Ryabkov's red line rules out membership, and he extends it to any quasi-membership formula. A swap of territory for membership strains the territorial-integrity language from the other direction. Both positions can't be honored in the same document. As of November 20th, the freshest reporting we have, there's no confirmed breakthrough on territory or alliance status.

There's one more piece of context on leverage. In mid-October, President Trump waived two parts of the Graham Act, two days before the law's implementation deadline. That suspended new tariffs on Russian goods and penalties on major buyers of Russian oil and gas. Existing sanctions under earlier executive orders stayed in place. For oil, that took away the most direct new threat to Russian export volumes. With no new penalties on the big buyers, a Washington-forced drop in Russian barrels is off the table for now, and so is that slice of risk premium in Brent. Zelenskyy called the decision wrong. He said, quote, "it definitely helps only the Russians." No reporting ties that waiver to the terms of a settlement, so don't read it as a down payment on a deal. On our read, though, it means the January push runs on persuasion, with the biggest new stick already set aside.

So here's the takeaway. A deal dated to a calendar rather than to converged terms is more likely to be signed than enforced. And right now, the two sides can't even agree in public on what's being offered.

Whatever gets signed, Europe is already paying for the alternative. Defence Matters reports that European defense spending is set to reach about 454 billion euros in 2026. That's about 2.4 percent of GDP. It's a projection that framed a November 9th meeting of NATO and EU finance ministers, not a new release. The money is real. But the ministers' focus was fragmented national procurement, which is where budgets stall before they become hardware. On our read, a deal that Europeans see as weakening the American guarantee only adds to that spending pressure. Still, bigger budgets are not more munitions on the shelf. Investors pricing European defense primes on topline growth are underwriting an execution bet on order-to-delivery conversion. That's not a production certainty.

Now the second shift, on chips. Last week, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said the current chip export rules are sufficient. In plain terms, Washington is signaling no new federal tightening. Nvidia's own posture points the other way. CNBC, citing unnamed sources, reported on November 17th that Nvidia had warned its customers. The warning was that a new law could expose them and the chipmaker to, quote, "legal action" over Chinese access to chip capacity. The report doesn't name the statute, so we won't characterize it.

Treat the report with some care. The sourcing is anonymous. The warning may be legal self-protection rather than a real cut in flows. And a vendor that has already written China out of its outlook gives up little by warning customers off.

Still, the direction matters. Nvidia is passing legal exposure down to its own customers. That makes Nvidia an enforcer of limits on Chinese access, alongside the Commerce Department. For Nvidia shareholders, export-control risk changes character. It moves from headline policy shocks toward customer-level revenue uncertainty, account by account. And on this desk's inference, that ambiguity alone pushes Chinese buyers toward domestic silicon, whatever Washington licenses. The takeaway: Treasury's assurance of no new rules matters less than Nvidia's compliance posture. The binding constraint on China's chip access is drifting from a government rule to a corporate legal department.

Liability and jurisdiction are also reshaping the power grid. PJM's own Inside Lines post describes what happened on July 22nd. A routine transmission disturbance caused nearly 3.9 gigawatts of data centers to switch unexpectedly from utility power to their own onsite generators. PJM calls it the largest such load transfer in its history.

The new part is the sizing. Startup Fortune's reporting on PJM's filing puts today's large computational load at about 10 gigawatts. It puts forecast additions at up to 30 gigawatts by 2030. On rough arithmetic, one routine disturbance moved something like two-fifths of today's fleet. The 30 gigawatts is a forecast, not built load. But if even part of it arrives, the same kind of disturbance hits a much larger base. The reliability problem scales with the fleet.

PJM's answer is a ride-through proposal, now pending at FERC. Large data centers would have to stay connected through grid disturbances instead of dropping to backup power. New loads would have to demonstrate compliance as a condition of interconnection. Existing loads would get one year from the effective date. Keep the status straight. This is a filing, not a rule, and FERC has already turned down PJM's more aggressive plan once.

That earlier rejection is the structural piece. In October, the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission largely rejected PJM's so-called bring-your-own-capacity curtailment plan. Its reasoning was that the curtailment terms intruded on state retail-rate authority. FERC did keep PJM's 50-megawatt definition of a large load, and a registry of those loads. So the federal toolkit has a clear edge. Regulators can dictate how a data center behaves electrically. They can't ration its power through curtailment. And the question of who pays for the buildout sits with the states.

For utilities and independent power producers, FERC's split means state-by-state regulatory risk, not one federal answer. The takeaway: the reliability fix is real and specific, but it adds no generation and settles no cost allocation. The harder fight is still ahead, and it's being fought one state commission at a time.

Pull the three threads together. Politically, Washington wants a Ukraine deal before Congress changes hands, while the two sides dispute in public what's on the table. On chips, the shift is to compliance, with the constraint on China's access running increasingly through Nvidia's own legal decisions. Jurisdictionally, federal regulators can mandate grid behavior, but they've left the cost fight to the states. None of those measures capacity. Two markers to watch: the early-January Ukraine target, and FERC's handling of PJM's ride-through proposal.
