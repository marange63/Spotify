Good morning, it's Saturday, September 26th, and this is your space and orbital economy briefing.

This week the real money moved toward owning a constellation that already works. Meanwhile, the constellations still under construction kept showing how far announced cadence runs ahead of working hardware. That gap is today's thread. It runs from a merger vote, to a Chinese sea launch, to a direct-to-cell ride schedule, to a ground-station tender.

Start with Rocket Lab and Iridium. On the 20th, we told you Rocket Lab had swapped a bridge loan for fresh stock to fund its purchase of Iridium. On Thursday, Iridium's owners said yes. According to GovCon Wire, about 99.6 percent of the votes cast at the special meeting supported the merger.

The terms are worth hearing precisely, because they carry the risk. Per GovCon Wire, Iridium holders get a notional 54 dollars a share in total. Half of that, 27 dollars, is cash. The rest comes in Rocket Lab stock, at an exchange ratio set inside a collar. Rocket Lab's own merger announcement, filed with the SEC, puts Iridium's enterprise value at approximately 8 billion dollars. So the headline number is now company-stated, not just secondary reporting.

Here's the skeptical turn. A near-unanimous approval on an agreed cash-and-stock deal is close to a formality. It doesn't de-risk much. The real gates are regulatory, and one is already cleared. The Hart-Scott-Rodino antitrust waiting period expired in mid-August. What's still open is the FCC's consent to transfer Iridium's licenses. The companies filed their FCC applications in August, and no grant has been reported. So the remaining risk is a satellite licensing review, not an antitrust fight. That's narrower than a generic pending-approvals caveat. But the targeted close is still mid-2027, more than nine months away.

The collar is the other moving part. It's banded between 67 and a half dollars and 112 and a half dollars on Rocket Lab's share price. Inside that band, the stock portion's value isn't fixed. It moves with Rocket Lab's stock. So what Iridium holders actually realize, and how much Rocket Lab dilutes its own owners, both stay open until closing.

The consensus read on this deal is vertical integration. A launch provider buys a satcom operator and captures the whole stack. There's a competing read worth holding alongside it. Rocket Lab is using a richly valued stock as currency to buy durable cash flow. Its own launch growth still depends on Neutron, a medium-lift rocket that hasn't flown.

And what it's buying is genuinely durable. Iridium's annual report says its Pentagon satellite-services contract, known as EMSS, carries a service fee fixed at 110.5 million dollars a year. That fee isn't based on subscribers or usage. Iridium's government engineering and support revenue also grew last year, to 149 million dollars from 117 million. That's a demand-insensitive income stream. For Rocket Lab holders, the risk profile shifts from launch and components toward owning a legacy constellation. The stock now trades partly on an FCC timeline and on where Rocket Lab's price sits inside that collar. The takeaway: Rocket Lab chose to buy revenue that's already in orbit rather than wait on a vehicle that isn't.

If Rocket Lab is paying up for a network that already works, China's Qianfan constellation shows what it takes to build one from scratch. On September 16th, Orienspace's solid-propellant Gravity-1 rocket lifted off from the East China Sea near Shanghai. It carried eight Qianfan satellites. Per the newsletter China in Space, it was the constellation's sixteenth launch. It was also the first time Qianfan satellites rode a solid-fuel vehicle.

That matters for resilience. A sea-launched solid rocket doesn't need a fixed pad. So it reduces the buildout's dependence on the liquid-fuelled Long March vehicles that have carried most of it. Yesterday we cited an outside projection that China's two megaconstellations would consume seventy or more launches this year. Every additional launch path makes a tally like that more reachable. Still, one sea launch is a resilience hedge, not a step change in cadence.

Now the count, and here the skepticism sharpens. The same source says Qianfan's first 238 satellites went up on fourteen launches through early July. Add September's eight and you get at least 246 launched. That's desk arithmetic, and it's a floor. September's flight was the sixteenth, so one launch in between isn't in that tally, and our sources don't give its payload.

But launched is the operative word. The same reporting says some Qianfan satellites suffered thruster or gyroscope failures in orbit last year. Those failures paused deployment for several months. It resumed only in April, after corrective measures. So the launched tally overstates the working fleet by an amount nobody has published. The so-what: China's launch supply is diversifying, which sharpens its contest with Starlink and Amazon's Kuiper for spectrum and orbital shells. But until a working count appears, Qianfan is a launch statistic, not a proven network.

The same launched-versus-working question hangs over the leading American direct-to-cell bet. On Wednesday, we described AST SpaceMobile as hardware waiting on rides. Now there's a number on the ride schedule. According to a secondhand summary of AST's latest company update, its two newest BlueBirds are fully deployed. It has also opened a new ground gateway in New Zealand. The next three satellites are complete or close behind.

The headline is the target. AST is aiming for roughly 45 satellites in orbit by early 2027, at a deployment cadence of six a month. Treat both as company targets reported secondhand, not achievements.

Now run the arithmetic. On Wednesday we reported thirteen BlueBirds in orbit. Getting to about forty-five means roughly thirty-two more satellites. At six a month, that's more than five straight months at a rate AST has never shown. Its last mission, on a SpaceX Falcon 9 in August, carried three.

The consensus take is that this target de-risks AST's growth story. The better read is the opposite: it concentrates the risk. For AST and its carrier partners, revenue timing now depends less on demand and more on launch scheduling AST doesn't control. And look at who controlled the last ride. It was SpaceX, which runs its own direct-to-cell service with T-Mobile. So the leading independent challenger has been pacing its buildout at least partly on a competitor's manifest. The so-what: AST's target makes launch cadence, not demand, the swing factor for its revenue into next year.

And satellites in orbit are only half the system, even in defense. Last week, the Space Development Agency put out a 369 million dollar solicitation for missile-tracking ground entry points. It covers five to eight newly built sites worldwide, plus retrofits at a handful of existing stations. Those sites feed the proliferated architecture behind the Golden Dome missile-defense buildout. This is a solicitation, not an award. Proposals run through late this year, and the prime integration contract isn't due to be picked until early next year.

Here's a desk inference, and label it as one. Every constellation in today's briefing, Iridium's, Qianfan's, and AST's, needs ground plumbing and downlink capacity as well as satellites. SDA is now bidding that layer separately from the satellite buses. That opens a lane for ground-systems integrators distinct from the bus primes. It also means Golden Dome's tracking timeline could hinge as much on ground sites as on satellite production. The binding constraint keeps migrating down the stack: from launch rides, to spectrum, and now to ground entry points.

So where does this actually stand? What's genuinely inflecting is an operating, revenue-earning constellation changing hands. China is also diversifying the launch supply behind its megaconstellation. What's still demo is AST's six-a-month target, and the working fleet behind Qianfan's launch tally. The biggest open question is whether capital keeps paying up for assets already flying because new cadence keeps disappointing.

Catalysts worth watching. SpaceX's Starship Flight 14 is targeted for as soon as Monday, September 28th, pending FAA approval of the orbital flight plan. That license was still outstanding at last report, so this is a forward catalyst, not a result. Watch for the FCC's consent on the Iridium license transfer. Watch AST's next BlueBird mission against that six-a-month claim. And watch the Space Development Agency's ground-segment award early next year.
