Cautious Optimism Briefings

AI in Biopharma R&D

Transcript · August 19, 2026

Good morning. It's Wednesday, August 19th, and this is your briefing on AI in biopharma R&D.

Today's hard number comes from Recursion Pharmaceuticals, the largest publicly traded pure-play AI-native drug discovery company, and it puts a figure under an argument this beat has made qualitatively for a week: the capital-light licensing model underneath AI drug discovery is under real strain. Recursion's second-quarter revenue came in at $7.7 million, down from $19.2 million a year earlier — a roughly 60% year-over-year decline. On the earnings call, management attributed that drop to what it called the completion of specific project phases in the prior-year period, in other words milestone timing rather than a structural break in the licensing book. Take that explanation for what it is: the company's own framing, not an independently confirmed breakdown of which partner milestones fell off. No analyst pushback or itemized schedule surfaced to test it either way.

The other half of Recursion's story is the owned clinical pipeline, and here the news is genuinely better than the vague coverage suggested going in. On the call, Recursion's chief medical officer gave a verbatim figure for REC-4881, its MEK inhibitor in familial adenomatous polyposis: a 43% median reduction in polyp burden after three months of treatment in post-colectomy patients, in the Phase II TUPELO study, described as durable off treatment. That is a real, quantified signal — better sourced than the "significantly reducing polyp burden" language that first circulated. But hold that number in the right box. It is evidence that an AI-originated molecule can generate a candidate that moves a biomarker in an enriched population. It is not evidence about the step that actually decides the platform thesis — whether an AI-originated drug succeeds in a pivotal, mortality-or-function endpoint in humans. That developability-versus-efficacy split is the through-line of this whole field: AI keeps proving it can produce a tractable, developable candidate, and keeps not yet proving it can raise the odds of clinical success. One early-phase readout in one biomarker-selected population does not close that gap.

Meanwhile, net loss narrowed to $131 million from a wider prior-year loss, and Recursion cut its full-year 2026 cash operating expense guidance to $375 million, with roughly $557 million in cash giving it runway into early 2028. That is cost discipline, not revenue health — don't conflate the two. And notably, Recursion gave no forward revenue or partnership-inflow guidance at all, only a cumulative figure of over $500 million in realized partnership inflows since inception. The absence of a forward number is itself informative: even management isn't willing to underwrite where the licensing line goes from here.

So the platform-versus-pipeline tension that's been building all week just became concrete in one earnings print. The near-term, capital-light revenue model is weakening in the same quarter that the long-duration, owned-pipeline bet produced its best efficacy data yet. Those are two different investment theses wearing one ticker, and this quarter they moved in opposite directions.

That split matters for how you're supposed to value the entire AI-native cohort, not just this one name. The original pitch for these companies was a software-like story: license the platform, book partnership and milestone revenue, compound at a recurring-revenue multiple with someone else carrying the clinical risk. A quarter like this pushes them off that pitch and toward a plain biotech shape — own the pipeline, burn cash toward a handful of binary readouts, and get valued as a basket of options on clinical success rather than as a revenue compounder. Those are radically different multiples and radically different holders. And the read-through runs past Recursion. If the largest public pure-play is watching its licensing line shrink and declining to guide it, big pharma sitting across the table from every other AI-native platform this year has just been handed more leverage on deal terms. The capital-light model doesn't only get repriced in the public comps — it gets renegotiated in the private ones.

That raises the natural next question: if the molecule layer is this unresolved, where is AI value in biopharma actually provable right now? The answer, increasingly, is further down the stack, in operations and diagnostics rather than in discovery.

The clearest example this month is Leica Biosystems, which won FDA 510(k) clearance for Aperio iQC DX, described as the industry's first standalone AI-assisted quality-control software for clinical digital pathology. It flags slide-quality defects — air bubbles, pen marks, clipped or missing tissue, out-of-focus regions — automatically while slides are still on the scanner. In a real-world study with Heidelberg University's Institute of Pathology, the AI models detected up to 24% more artifacts than histotechnicians caught manually, and cut hands-on review time by 69%. Those figures carry footnotes in the source material, so treat them as a single-site institutional study result, not a universal benchmark — and clearance is not the same as adoption; we don't yet know how many labs are actually running this software. But directionally, this is exactly the kind of auditable, regulator-cleared, narrow win the discovery-stage hype rarely delivers. And it compounds rather than sitting still. Better slide capture raises the ceiling for every downstream AI model that scores biomarkers or grades tumors off those images. That's the quiet asymmetry worth naming: the auditable operations-and-diagnostics layer is self-reinforcing — each cleared tool improves the inputs for the next — while the molecule-design payoff remains a promise. The gap between where AI capital is being spent and where AI returns are actually provable is widening, not closing.

One more auditable node down the stack: a peer-reviewed paper in Nature Communications this month described TrialMatchAI, a patient-to-trial matching system built on fine-tuned, open-source large language models in a retrieval-augmented generation framework, doing criterion-level eligibility assessment across both structured records and unstructured physician notes. No prospective, real-world accuracy or recruitment-speed numbers were published alongside it, so don't overstate the operational impact yet. But it's a clean illustration of where open-weight models are already competitive: a well-defined, bounded task — trial eligibility screening — is commodity work for a fine-tuned open model, not a job that requires frontier proprietary compute. The economic point is that a growing slice of the clinical-operations stack does not carry a frontier-model bill at all.

Now the cautionary bookend, back up at the discovery end. Evaxion announced today that it's discontinuing its EVX-03 solid-tumor vaccine program and replacing it with EVX-05, an AI-designed glioblastoma vaccine developed with Duke University. The AI-Immunology platform identified endogenous-retrovirus-derived antigens from patient tumor samples that are shared across glioblastoma patients, which is what makes an off-the-shelf, non-personalized design possible instead of a fully personalized neoantigen vaccine. That's a legitimate use of AI to narrow a target-search problem. But there's no clinical or even IND-enabling data yet for EVX-05 — this is a preclinical target-selection announcement — and the sources don't tell us whether EVX-03 was dropped for a scientific failure or simply reprioritized. Read it as what it is: one more data point that a lot of the "AI-designed" pipeline narrative is still preclinical churn, swapping one unproven candidate for another.

Pulling the threads together: value in AI-driven biopharma keeps sliding down the stack, away from the molecule and toward operations and diagnostics, where results are narrower but provable. Recursion's quarter is the sharpest evidence yet — a shrinking licensing top line against an early but real clinical signal, two separate stories that haven't resolved into one, and a market that increasingly has to price these names as options on clinical success rather than as revenue compounders. Leica's pathology clearance and TrialMatchAI's open-weight trial matching are the auditable wins the field can actually bank today. And still, no AI-discovered drug holds full FDA approval anywhere. The platform-revenue story and the clinical-success story remain distinct bets, and this quarter, the platform-revenue side got measurably worse while the clinical side got measurably better documented. Watch whether that gap closes or widens as more Q3 prints come in.

That's your briefing. Have a good day.
