Cautious Optimism Briefings

Semiconductors & Compute Infrastructure

Transcript · July 31, 2026

Good morning. It's Friday, July 31st, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

We've spent the week tracking a standoff between what memory executives are telling investors and what memory investors are actually doing with their money. Today that standoff got its clearest data point yet, and the resolution is messier than the tidy story most people are telling.

Start with the print. Samsung reported second-quarter revenue of 171.5 trillion won, about 119 billion dollars, up 130 percent year on year. Operating profit rose more than nineteenfold to 89.5 trillion won, roughly 62.4 billion dollars. SK hynix, which reported a day earlier, posted 79.3 trillion won in revenue, up 257 percent, at a 76 percent operating margin — numbers that would normally be described as spectacular. Both companies then told investors the shortage isn't ending. Samsung's memory chief said supply constraints will be "even more severe in 2027 than 2026," and that the company doesn't expect "any significant increase in incremental supply through 2028." That's about as unambiguous as management guidance gets.

The market's reaction was the opposite of celebratory. SK hynix shares fell more than 11 percent intraday on its own earnings day. Micron and Samsung have been sliding for a week too. The trigger, as we've covered, is China's ChangXin Memory Technologies — CXMT — which went public in Shanghai on July 27th, raised about 8.6 billion dollars in the largest mainland listing since 2010, and saw its shares jump roughly 470 percent on debut. The market's read: if CXMT and a similar raise from YMTC fund a new wave of Chinese memory capacity, the shortage the incumbents are describing gets undercut before it ever peaks.

So who's right? The honest answer, once you look closer, is that both sides are describing real things — just not the same market. New reporting on CXMT's own IPO prospectus, cross-checked against SemiAnalysis capacity modeling, shows CXMT is overwhelmingly a commodity-DRAM producer. About 99 percent of its 2025 bit shipments were traditional LPDDR and DDR product. Of its roughly 265,000 monthly wafer starts, only about 5,000 were allocated to HBM. Its prospectus contains no dedicated HBM project at all. By the numbers, CXMT is not an HBM competitor — it's a conventional-memory volume producer, projected to reach around 350,000 wafer starts a month by the end of this year, closing in on Micron's scale though still well behind Samsung's roughly 720,000 and SK hynix's roughly 595,000.

That should mean CXMT and the incumbents are simply talking past each other — CXMT adding commodity supply, the incumbents guiding on a scarce, high-margin HBM market they still control. And that was the clean story going into this week. But Samsung's and SK hynix's own commentary complicates it. Both companies say the shortage isn't confined to HBM. Because HBM is itself a form of DRAM, they're pulling manufacturing capacity, engineering resources and investment away from conventional DRAM to build more of it — and that reallocation is what's tightening supply of the ordinary DRAM that goes into servers, PCs and phones. SK hynix's head of DRAM said directly that PC and mobile customers are struggling to secure supply because of that spillover. So the segment where CXMT's new capacity actually lands over the next two years — commodity DRAM — is the same segment the incumbents are separately describing as constrained. It isn't a market they've conceded as adequately supplied; it's the one they're short-changing on purpose to chase HBM margin.

What that means in practice: CXMT's supply growth probably does bring relief, but to conventional-DRAM pricing specifically, not to HBM pricing, which stays governed by a market CXMT barely participates in — analyst estimates put its HBM capacity at only around 30,000 wafer starts a month by year-end, versus roughly 350,000 total. Expect a split outcome inside memory itself: commodity-DRAM prices cooling faster than HBM prices, even though both are currently described as tight. And "cooling" is relative to a very high base. Micron's own fiscal first-quarter conventional-DRAM contract prices rose between 93 and 98 percent quarter on quarter, with another 58 to 63 percent increase guided for the following quarter. Even if CXMT eventually caps the top of that curve, the base case the incumbents are underwriting is pricing that keeps compounding into 2027 — and that lands straight on hyperscaler capex, where memory is a rising share of the bill of materials on every AI server. That's a more useful frame than "shortage versus oversupply" — it's shortage in HBM, and a race between Chinese supply growth and incumbent capacity diversion in everything else. The tell to watch is whether CXMT's roadmap ever moves meaningfully into HBM; right now it hasn't, and its funding doesn't show it planning to.

Now the upstream read on whether any of this demand is durable. ASML, which sits above the entire leading-edge chain, posted second-quarter net sales of 9.3 billion euros at a 54 percent gross margin, both ahead of guidance, and raised its full-year outlook to 43 to 45 billion euros. Management said it's close to having all the orders it needs for 2027, even as it plans to add about 30 percent more EUV capacity than this year. That's a supplier telling its largest customers it's building for sustained, not peaking, demand — corroboration for the incumbents' side of the memory argument, since ASML's tools feed both logic and memory fabs alike.

But here's the fact worth flagging on its own: starting this year, ASML stopped publishing quarterly bookings, saying lumpy large orders were distorting the signal. That's a real transparency change, and it lands at exactly the moment investors most want an independent check on whether AI-infrastructure demand is durable. It can be read as routine housekeeping or as convenient timing — there's no way to prove intent — but either way, the market just lost a tool for verifying the order book it's relying on to judge this whole debate.

The same durability question shows up on the manufacturing side. Intel became the site of the first high-volume production use anywhere of ASML's High-NA EUV lithography, deploying it for a subset of Core Ultra Series 3 chips, while its 18A-P node entered risk production on schedule. That's a genuine validation of ASML's next lithography generation and of Intel's process roadmap. It is not, however, a validation of Intel's foundry economics: the Foundry division still posted an operating loss of about 2.1 billion dollars for the quarter, even as revenue grew 31 percent. Execution is real at the process-technology level. It is not yet real at the level that determines whether Intel becomes a credible third leading-edge foundry.

Pull the threads together. The incumbents and the equipment supply chain are both positioned for a shortage that persists into 2028. The equity market is pricing a nearer-term glut. The reconciliation isn't that one side is wrong — it's that the shortage is real and durable in HBM, where CXMT barely competes, while conventional DRAM is genuinely getting squeezed from two directions at once, incumbent capacity diversion and, soon, Chinese supply growth working in opposite directions on price. And there's a strategic wrinkle under the pricing: every wafer of domestic commodity DRAM that CXMT and YMTC bring online is a wafer of leverage the US and Korea lose in any future memory-related export fight, because China gains a homegrown cushion in exactly the segment sanctions would target. Watch CXMT's actual HBM roadmap, not its prospectus language, for the signal that would change the pricing story.

That's the briefing for July 31st. Have a good weekend.
