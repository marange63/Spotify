Good morning, it's Sunday, July 26th, and this is your Capital Markets Radar.

Let's start with a question the market answered with cash: how much more will investors pay for the AI trade, if you let them buy it fresh instead of secondhand. SK Hynix priced its Nasdaq listing on July 9th, selling 177.9 million American depositary shares at $149 each. That raised $26.5 billion gross. Aljazeera, citing SK Hynix's own statement, called it the largest-ever US listing by a foreign company, ahead of Alibaba's $25 billion debut in 2014, and the second-largest listing anywhere after SpaceX's $85.7 billion Nasdaq deal in June. Bloomberg reported the book was more than seven times oversubscribed. That is not a company scraping together demand. That is a queue.

Now put that next to the other side of the same trade. Nvidia, the incumbent everyone actually owns, logged $81.6 billion in quarterly revenue, up 85 percent year over year, and its stock has stayed roughly flat for most of 2026. So here is the tension. A brand-new AI-memory listing gets bid up seven times over, while the AI-trade bellwether posts a record quarter and goes nowhere. One reading is healthy: capital is simply rotating from richly-valued secondary shares into fresh primary paper, chasing the same AI-capex story through a cheaper entry point. The less comfortable reading is that this is late-cycle distribution — insiders and early holders moving fresh supply into a market that's already stuffed with the trade, right as the old guard stalls. Either way, the so-what is the same: the market's tolerance for backward-looking record earnings, versus its appetite for new paper, is now the swing factor for the whole AI-capex debate, and Nvidia's next earnings report in late August is the next test of which reading wins.

And there's a scale point sitting underneath this. A raise this size doesn't land in a vacuum. It competes for the same marginal dollar as a record year of Treasury and corporate issuance. So when primary AI paper this large clears seven times over, it tells you the bid for the capex trade is still deep enough to absorb supply that would have choked a thinner market. That's the constructive read on liquidity. It's also the one worth stress-testing the moment rates come back into the frame, because absorption that looks effortless at today's discount rate can turn brittle if that rate stops falling.

That question — how much leverage the AI build-out can really support — is exactly where the next crack is showing, and it's not where you'd expect. Public credit still looks calm. High-yield spreads compressed below 300 basis points, a level iShares data says has occurred only 5 percent of the time since January 2000. That's about as tight as public credit gets. But private credit is telling a different story. Industry-wide BDC data through the end of the first quarter show that of roughly 5,000 companies held by business development companies, 538 — 10.6 percent — now show signs of credit pressure, up 15 percent from the prior quarter. The volume of stressed first-lien and unitranche loans jumped 44 percent quarter over quarter, to $35.4 billion. And here's the detail that connects back to the first story: software borrowers are now the single largest stressed cohort, representing 26 percent of investments at fair value, up from 19 percent at the end of 2025.

That software concentration is worth sitting with. It's a specific, checkable hypothesis, not a proven fact, but it's a plausible transmission channel from AI capex straight into private credit losses — if AI spending is compressing software budgets and pricing power even as leveraged software borrowers need that revenue to service debt, that's exactly the kind of stress you'd expect to show up first in the middle market, not the S&P. A BlackRock-managed fund, TCP Capital, has already seen its CEO exit amid a federal valuation probe — that's the visible trigger, not the story itself. The real story is that the strain has spread beyond one manager into the sector-wide data. So while public spreads sit near two-decade tights, private marks are quietly deteriorating in exactly the sector most exposed to the AI trade. That's a valuation-and-mark-to-model risk worth watching, not yet a systemic one — but it's the gap between complacent public pricing and honest private pricing that tends to widen before anyone official says so.

Both of those stories — the primary-market AI bid and the private-credit software stress — sit on top of a discount rate that just got harder to predict, which brings us to the Fed. Kevin Warsh, confirmed as chair by the closest modern-era vote, 54 to 45, has scrapped the old forward-guidance playbook entirely. The June dot plot moved the median year-end 2026 rate projection up to 3.8 percent, from 3.4 percent in March — a flip from expecting a cut to signaling a hike. Nine of eighteen committee participants now pencil in at least one hike this year, versus eight for no change and just one for a cut. Then June CPI came in at 3.5 percent year over year, below the 3.8 percent consensus and down from 4.2 percent in May — a clean disinflation surprise. Warsh's response, in July testimony, was explicit: "There might be some who look at this morning's data and say, well, mission accomplished, everything is swell. That is not my view."

So heading into Tuesday's decision, the setup is genuinely two-sided, not a foregone hawkish hold. Fed funds futures put the odds of a July hike at roughly 35 to 36.5 percent, with a hold the more likely outcome at 63.5 to 65 percent, and a move of 50 basis points or more essentially priced at zero. Longer-dated contracts for September carry a higher cumulative probability of tightening than July does — that's delay, not dismissal, of the hike case. Meanwhile the 10-year Treasury yield sits at 4.69 percent as of July 24th, essentially flat versus the prior session. That flatness is itself a signal: the bond market has not meaningfully repriced for a hike scenario, so if Warsh's Fed does move, or September's updated projections confirm the hawkish bias, the term-premium adjustment could be sharper than current levels suggest.

There's a structural wrinkle here too. When a Fed abandons forward guidance, it stops pre-committing markets to a path, and that alone raises the realized volatility around every meeting, not just this one. Front-end option premium gets richer by design. So even a hold on Tuesday doesn't buy the calm it used to — the Warsh regime makes each decision genuinely live in a way the old guidance language used to smooth over. That's a slow tax on anyone who was short front-end vol as a carry trade.

Here's how it all connects. A Fed that's abandoned forward guidance and is explicitly refusing to declare victory on inflation means higher-for-longer stays live as a base case, even with cooling CPI. That is precisely the discount rate sitting underneath both stories we opened with — it's what makes SK Hynix's timing bold, pricing a record raise into a rate environment that isn't getting easier soon, and it's what keeps pressure on exactly the leveraged, long-duration corners showing stress first: private credit's software borrowers. So the net read for positioning is this: watch whether Tuesday's decision, and September's dot plot behind it, resolve toward hold or hike — because that outcome determines whether the AI trade's primary-market enthusiasm and private credit's early cracks stay a contained divergence, or start to reinforce each other.

That's your Capital Markets Radar for July 26th. Stay sharp out there.
