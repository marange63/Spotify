Good morning, it's Friday, October 9th. Here's the private credit and corporate credit briefing.

The headline this week isn't that money is fleeing private credit. It's that money is re-sorting. And the newest place it's going is leverage stacked on top of private marks, right as supervisors start asking hard questions about exactly that layer.

Start with the lead. Yesterday, Ardian and SMBC announced a platform called Direct MidCap NAV Financing. Ardian calls it a strategic partnership. The idea is to lend against the net asset value of mid-market buyout and infrastructure fund portfolios, not against the operating companies underneath them. SMBC brings large-cap NAV-lending experience and bank distribution. Ardian brings the underwriting edge, which is data from its secondaries business. That dataset covers more than seventeen hundred funds, over seven hundred GPs, and more than twelve thousand underlying companies. Ardian's head of NAV financing says, in his words, "we are already seeing strong deal flow, and we believe the pipeline will continue to grow as fund managers increasingly recognize NAV financing as a strategic tool."

Here's why the timing matters. On Monday, Semafor reported that Fed examiners have been inside four large banks: JPMorgan, Wells Fargo, Barclays, and Morgan Stanley. According to Semafor's unnamed sources, they asked about overall exposure, risk management, and collateral quality on lending to private credit firms. The Fed itself has said nothing publicly. Three days later, a global bank launches a new platform lending against exactly that kind of collateral, which is private fund NAVs. Nothing reported ties Ardian or SMBC to those exams. The connection is our read. But the read is this: supply of the exposure under supervisory review is expanding, not contracting, while that review is live.

Give both sides of this fairly. The bull case is real. A loan against a diversified pool of mid-market fund NAVs sits senior to fund equity. And Ardian's secondaries data arguably lets it price that collateral better than a bank lending blind against marks it can't independently verify. The bear case is that mid-market fund NAVs are among the least transaction-tested valuations in private markets. They are model outputs, not clearing prices. So the loan's collateral value is itself an estimate. Treat that "strong deal flow" line with the same care. It's the lender's own characterization. It could mean general partners are reaching for NAV leverage to fund distributions while exits stay slow. That's a question worth asking, not a confirmed fact.

No fund size, committed capital, or loan-to-value range has been disclosed for this platform. The only size anchor is market-wide. An AllianceBernstein estimate, relayed in a July summary by Cayman Finance, puts total NAV financing at around one hundred billion dollars. That market grew roughly thirty percent a year from 2019 to 2023. The same estimate projects about three hundred fifty billion by 2030. That tells you the category's trajectory, not this deal's scale. The upshot: fund-level leverage against untested marks is growing in the exact week it's under the microscope.

So who's still putting money into private credit, and who wants out? Robert A. Stanger has the first industry-wide tally for the third quarter. Across the nineteen NAV BDCs that had reported tender results, redemption requests totaled 13.8 billion dollars. That's 11.5 percent of estimated tender NAV, down from 12.7 percent on a same-store basis in the second quarter. So the aggregate is easing. But it isn't easing evenly. Blue Owl's technology-focused fund is still taking requests well above industry peers, on fears around artificial intelligence. Redemption demand is sorting by sector, not draining across the board.

Inside KKR, the same split now shows up between two sister funds. The smaller one, K-FITS, has about a billion dollars in net assets. Its redemption requests rose from 3.43 percent of shares last quarter to 5.06 percent this quarter. That's just over its 5 percent cap, and KKR chose to honor the requests in full. At that size, a handful of holders can move the number.

KKR's larger sister fund, with about 1.7 billion dollars in assets, moved the opposite way. Requests there eased from 1.65 percent to 1.53 percent. So one manager, two funds, two directions. The kind of split that separates Blue Owl's tech fund from its peers now shows up inside a second manager.

Take the industry easing with some caution. It's a same-store comparison of the funds that have reported. And the aggregate request rate still runs more than double a typical 5 percent cap. The easing could mean genuine stabilization. Or it could mean investors are fatigued after partial fills and simply not resubmitting. The data can't tell us which. Either way, the redemption queue is shorter, not gone, and what's left is concentrating in the AI-exposed corners of the asset class.

Now contrast that with how public markets are voting. The wealth channel is calming down. The short-sellers are not. Hedgeweek reports net short selling across a basket of fourteen BDC, CLO, and alt-manager proxy ETFs. It reached 88.6 million dollars in the first half of September. That's about double August's level, and near March's 102 million. Short interest as a share of assets under management rose to 2.7 percent, up from 2.1 percent in August. That's still below March's record of 3.4 percent.

Two caveats. This is mid-September data, three to four weeks stale, and there's no October read. And it's a sentiment proxy on listed vehicles, not a direct credit signal, because you can't short an illiquid direct loan. Still, which signal deserves more weight? I'd lean toward the shorts as the earlier tell. Wealth-channel requests are partly mechanical, shaped by caps and resubmissions. Shorts are a deliberate, priced bet. That means if stress transmits anywhere first, it's more likely to show up as wider discounts to NAV in listed BDC shares than as new gates in the semi-liquid funds.

Lenders themselves are doing their own version of this sorting, inside software specifically. LCD's data, relayed via Bloomberg, shows software at 12 percent of PE-backed direct-lending deals so far this year, down from 16 percent last year. Underwriters are reassessing recurring-revenue software credits for AI disruption risk. That's a share of deal count, not dollars. And it doesn't shrink the legacy software books already on balance sheets.

Here's the second-order effect. A falling new-issue share for software means less refinancing capacity for the 2020-to-2022 vintage of software borrowers. That pushes stress toward amend-and-extend and payment-in-kind, not a clean refinancing or a visible default. The freshest count of that shift is first-quarter data from Octus. It found thirty-two borrowers, with 1.3 billion dollars of principal, that moved some or all of their cash interest to payment-in-kind after paying fully in cash the quarter before. That's why headline default counts will flatter the sector.

One live example shows the new discrimination at work. Brookfield is reportedly lining up roughly 800 million dollars of private credit to finance a proposed carve-out of compliance-software company Actimize from NICE. The deal isn't closed, and no terms beyond that size are public. Lenders reportedly view Actimize's niche compliance focus as more resilient to AI disruption than general-purpose software.

That label deserves the skepticism Carlyle aimed at the last cycle. In a white paper earlier this month, Carlyle argued that the 2020-to-2022 software lending boom looked diversified across many borrowers. In practice, Carlyle said, those deals depended on the same small group of hyperscaler counterparties. "Resilient to AI disruption" is this cycle's underwriting label, and it's just as untested. Lenders aren't abandoning software. They're sorting it by label, and the label hasn't been through a downturn.

So where does the cycle actually stand? There's no fresh default or non-accrual print this week, and the latest BDC credit-quality data is from the second quarter. So this read rests on flows, positioning, and new-deal data, not on defaults. On that evidence, it looks like a slow grind rather than a turn. Exit demand is easing, not spiking. Lenders are sorting software, not fleeing it. But the risk is migrating up the stack, into leverage secured on private marks and into listed public proxies, rather than disappearing. The single biggest unresolved risk is that more of the system's return now depends on valuations that nobody has forced to clear in a real transaction. If NAV lending keeps growing while exits stay slow, supervisors could push to tighten those loan terms. That would raise funding costs inside funds without a single underlying borrower ever defaulting.

Watch three catalysts from here. First, alternative-manager third-quarter earnings starting late October. Second, the round of BDC filings in November. That's the first update on non-accruals and payment-in-kind income since the second quarter, along with where listed BDCs trade against NAV. It's where the slow-grind call gets tested against hard credit numbers. And third, any public output from the Fed's bank reviews.

That's the briefing. Stay cautious, stay optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
