Private Equity & the Deal Economy

Good morning, it's September 4th, 2026, and this is your private equity briefing.

On Monday this show flagged one thing to watch: whether EQT would confirm the Coller Capital close inside its own guided window, or let it slip. It closed on August 31st, on time. So here is today's thesis. EQT just paid to own the liquidity drought rather than to escape it, and the backlog data says that bet is winning.

Start with the deal itself, which is now done rather than announced. That distinction matters, because so much of this beat is transactions that settle sometime in 2027. This one has actually happened. The base consideration is $3.2 billion, funded largely with just over 80 million newly issued EQT shares, equal to roughly 7% of EQT's share count. There is up to $500 million more on top, contingent on Coller's performance in the twelve months through March 2029. That contingent piece is not money that has changed hands.

The structure is the interesting part, because the structure is the forecast. EQT paid in dilution rather than cash. That caps EQT's downside if secondaries volumes ever revert, and it pushes part of that risk onto Coller's own principals, who now hold EQT paper rather than a completed cash exit. Then look at how far out the earnout runs. A firm that thought the exit drought was a cyclical window it merely had to survive would not write a performance test that resolves in 2029. EQT is underwriting the drought as a multi-year condition, and that view now sits on its balance sheet.

Now we can put a price on what EQT actually bought, because EQT disclosed Coller's fee economics back at signing in January. The guidance for 2026 was roughly $40 billion of fee-generating assets throwing off $175 to $200 million of fee-related EBITDA. Set the $3.2 billion base consideration against that range and you get roughly 16 to 18 times fee-related EBITDA. EQT itself never states a multiple, so treat that as arithmetic we are doing on its disclosed figures. But the conclusion is hard to avoid. For a fee stream, in a business with no proprietary assets and a well-capitalized competitive set, that is a full price. EQT did not find a bargain here. It paid a scarcity premium for scaled secondaries capacity.

So what makes a check that size pencil out? The exit backlog, and this is the number underneath all of it. Multiple estimates now put the private-equity industry's unsold portfolio-company inventory at roughly 32,000 companies worth roughly $3.8 trillion. Say roughly on both, because those come from different citations of Bain- and PitchBook-style analysis rather than one primary report. A year ago the same body of work put it at roughly 29,000 companies and $3.6 trillion. The inventory is not draining. It is compounding.

Then there is the clearance math, and it is worse than the stock figure alone suggests. At the current pace of exits, one estimate says it takes nine years to work through that backlog. A separate estimator puts it at eleven years, two years longer than that same estimate implied last fall. Those are different analysts with unverified assumptions about exit pace, so treat them as a range rather than a consensus. But the direction is what matters, and the direction is wrong in a year when headline deal and IPO sentiment actually improved. The flow clearing the backlog went up. The stock sitting in it went up more. That is the case that this is a structural condition rather than a hangover from 2023 and 2024.

There is an adverse-selection angle worth naming, because it changes what the backlog means. General partners sell their best assets first, through strategic sales and contested auctions. So a backlog that keeps growing even as activity improves is not simply a pipeline of deferred exits. The residual is skewing worse in composition, not just older. That is the mechanism by which a distribution problem quietly turns into a markdown problem, and it is why a growing backlog is a warning rather than a forward order book.

The secondaries numbers themselves deserve a conflict check. EQT's closing release cites secondaries transaction volume above $120 billion in the first half of 2026, the strongest first half on record and up nearly 20% year over year. The easy skeptical move is to call that the acquirer marketing the market it just bought into. That is not what it is. The figure is footnoted to Evercore's H1 2026 Secondary Market Review, a named third-party provider, and Evercore's own published report corroborates it independently. So the number stands on its own.

Evercore's breakdown is where the real signal sits, because it tells you which half of that market is growing. GP-led transactions, the sponsor-controlled side, hit $65 billion in the first half, up 35% year over year. Limited-partner-led deals, where an institution simply sells its fund stakes to another investor, totaled $56 billion and grew just 4%. So essentially all the growth in the record secondaries market is coming from the side the general partner organizes and prices.

Push one level further and it gets sharper still. Single-asset continuation vehicles alone accounted for $34 billion of volume, more than half of all GP-led activity. Secondaries dry powder, meanwhile, stands at $194 billion, down 10% year to date, which tells you capacity is being consumed rather than accumulated. That is a market absorbing supply at pace.

But here is the distinction that matters more than the size of any of those numbers. Secondaries volume moves ownership of existing fund stakes and portfolio companies between limited partners and sponsor-controlled vehicles. It does not sell a single company to an outside buyer. It does not reduce that 32,000-company inventory by one name. A single-asset continuation vehicle in particular keeps a business inside the same sponsor's orbit, with the same manager, often at a mark the same manager influences. Record secondaries activity is liquidity engineered inside the asset class. It is not cash leaving it.

So that is the supply side, and it is mostly stuck. The bid, though, is functioning. It just is not functioning where it would clear the inventory. Consider Bodycote, the UK industrial heat-treatment company. On September 1st, Veritas Capital won a contested auction against CVC Capital Partners, agreeing to pay 940 pence a share and valuing Bodycote at £1.85 billion including debt. That is a 34.5% premium to where the shares traded before takeover speculation started in May.

Two things about that are worth banking. There is no AI-infrastructure story attached to this asset, which cuts against the rule this show drew on Tuesday that exits clear only for assets with a narrative. Heat treatment for industrial components is about as story-free as a business gets, and two sponsors still competed hard for it. The distinction may be buyer type rather than narrative. Strategics buy stories; sponsors buy cash flows. And a premium is not a valuation. Nobody has given us an entry multiple or an earnings figure here, so the informative fact is not the 34.5% itself. It is that a rival fund was willing to be in the room at all, a financing-conditions signal for the sub-two-billion tier while megadeal financing stays tighter.

Now keep the hinge in view, because it is the whole point. When a sponsor wins that kind of auction, the company moves from a public shareholder register onto another fund's books. That adds one more name to the 32,000-company backlog. It does not take one away. Contested bidding tells you appetite exists. It tells you nothing about clearance.

So where does the cycle actually stand? There are three routes out of a private-equity portfolio: sale to a strategic buyer, an IPO onto public markets, or transfer into a sponsor-controlled vehicle. Only the third is scaling, and that happens to be precisely the business EQT just spent $3.2 billion and a full multiple to own. Which tells you the smart money is not forecasting a thaw. It is forecasting a toll booth.

And that third route keeps consolidating. With Coller now inside EQT, the pricing venue for secondaries sits with a handful of multi-strategy platforms: Blackstone, Ares, Franklin and now EQT. Private equity's closest thing to a live market quote is being set by firms that are also sellers on the other side of the table. The new fact today is that one more independent has been removed from that list. That raises the pressure on the ones left, meaning Ardian, HarbourVest and Pantheon, to either scale up or sell out themselves.

Watch three things from here. Whether the first continuation vehicle Coller prices under EQT ownership comes in at or below net asset value. Whether CVC comes back for another UK industrial target after losing Bodycote. And whether any sponsor exit announced this year actually converts to cash before the end of it, rather than joining next year's backlog count.

That's the state of the machine. Thanks for listening, and I'll see you tomorrow.
