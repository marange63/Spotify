Good morning, it's Sunday, September 6th, and this is your digital money briefing.

Let's start with a correction to our own read. Earlier this week, on this show, we told you the bitcoin derivatives book heading into the next two weeks looked cleaner and less levered than it had going into Jackson Hole. That was wrong, or at least it did not hold. When we said it, aggregate bitcoin futures open interest was sitting around 53 and a half billion dollars and falling. It has since gone the other way, rising 6.19 percent over the three days through September 4th, to 57.68 billion dollars. Call it an add of 3.36 billion dollars of notional in a long weekend.

And it rebuilt while spot went nowhere. Bitcoin failed to hold 80,000 dollars and has been trading just under that level since the August jobs report came in far stronger than expected. You already know the jobs number and the price break from Friday's briefing, so I will not re-narrate them. The new fact is the positioning. Leverage came back faster than the price it is levered to.

Here is why that matters more than the headline number. Funding on those positions is running around 0.0047 percent per four-hour period. Annualize that and you get a rate near 10 percent. That is positive, and it is what the bulls are pointing at — longs are paying to be long, but not painfully. I would read it the other way. Cheap carry is exactly what lets net-long positioning grow large without ever tripping the funding alarm traders normally watch for. The warning light stays green precisely because the position is inexpensive to hold. So the failure mode here is not a funding blowout that gives you days of notice. It is a gap. If spot cannot retake 80,000 dollars, the risk is a fast, mechanical long liquidation rather than a slow leak. And I want to be specific about what would tell you this read is wrong: funding pushing materially above where it sits now, or open interest falling back while price holds. Neither has happened yet.

Now, do not turn that into a directional call, because the same book cuts both ways. A rebuilt open-interest position of that size sitting in front of a binary mid-September calendar is a volatility amplifier, not a short signal. The same crowded longs that would accelerate a break lower are also the reason a favorable outcome gets bought harder than the news deserves. Every stop that gets run in either direction is fuel, and there is more fuel on the pile than there was a week ago. The honest takeaway is about the size of the move, not its sign.

The same distortion shows up on the cash side, if you stop counting daily flows and look at the stock instead. Aggregate spot bitcoin ETF net assets sit near 103.3 billion dollars. Cumulative net inflows since the products launched are roughly 55.4 billion dollars. Sit with that gap for a second. Roughly half of the headline asset number is price appreciation, not money that walked in the door.

August makes the same point in miniature. Net assets jumped from 76.29 billion dollars at the end of July to 99.61 billion at the end of August. Only 3.52 billion dollars of that increase was actual net inflow. The rest was bitcoin going up. That reframes the figure most often cited as proof of institutional adoption, because it makes the asset number reflexive. It validates the story on the way up, and it unwinds it on the way down, without a single share being created or redeemed. So I would retire the daily flow scorekeeping this show has run for four days straight — including my own. The number that matters is the stock, and the stock is mostly mark-to-market. Whatever downside support you thought the permanent institutional bid provides, halve it.

Everything so far is a derivative of one date, the Fed's September decision. Here is the part of the digital-money stack that is not. Total stablecoin supply stood near 301.7 billion dollars as of September 3rd. That is below the record set in the first quarter of this year, which one exchange data set puts at about 315 billion. So the pool has been shrinking, modestly, for two quarters. Inside it, Tether's USDT accounts for roughly 183 billion dollars and Circle's USDC for roughly 74 billion.

The interesting thing is what happened between those two, and here I have to correct a framing I would have given you a few days ago. Back in the first quarter, USDC supply rose about 2 billion dollars while USDT fell about 3 billion. That was the first meaningful split between the two issuers since the 2022 bear market, and it is a real fact. But it is not a trend still running. Circle's own second-quarter filing put USDC circulation at 73.3 billion dollars at quarter-end, and an aggregator report in late August had it near 74 billion after a heavy week of minting, with Tether roughly flat in the same window. Those last figures are press estimates rather than issuer disclosures, so hold the decimals loosely. Directionally, though, they are all the same number. USDC has held its first-quarter gain. It has not kept extending it. Treat the share shift as a one-time repricing of who won the first quarter, not a live momentum trade you can extrapolate.

Now the skeptical read on even that one-time shift. USDC is disproportionately the dollar of trading venues rather than of payments. So a regulated issuer taking share inside a shrinking pool can simply mean the regulated venues held up better than the offshore ones. That is a competitive fact about where volume sits. It is not an adoption fact about broadening demand. What would actually settle it is composition rather than size: USDC supply growing while exchange-held balances stay flat, or the total pool turning up again rather than continuing to drift lower. Neither is visible in the data we have.

The bullish counter is dated and specific, which at least makes it testable. Circle's own press release confirms its Arc mainnet is on track to launch on September 16th with eleven founding validators. The roster is the argument. BlackRock, the DTCC, ICE, Mastercard, Visa, Standard Chartered and Galaxy are among the names, which is a materially heavier list than the two-name shorthand this show used a week ago. If that group actually validates a USDC-native settlement chain for tokenized securities, Circle's business shifts from float yield toward settlement economics. That is a lower-multiple business than the rate annuity the equity has been priced as, but a more durable one, and it does not reset on the front end of the curve. It would also put pressure on Tether to find a comparable institutional rail of its own, which it does not have today.

One more piece of plumbing worth naming, because it is the transmission line to Treasury demand. The GENIUS Act bars stablecoin issuers from paying yield directly to holders. That pushes the marginal yield-seeking on-chain dollar out of stablecoins and into tokenized Treasury and money-market funds instead, which is where BlackRock's BUIDL sits. That segment was around 15 billion dollars as of the middle of May — background scale rather than a fresh print, and a rounding error against a 300 billion dollar stablecoin pool, let alone the bill market. But the mechanism matters more than the size. It means total stablecoin supply is becoming a progressively worse proxy for on-chain dollar demand, and that the supply charts understate the bid for short-dated Treasuries rather than overstating it. It is also why deploying BUIDL on a USDC settlement chain is commercially interesting rather than merely technical.

Close on the calendar, because three separate catalysts now sit inside a single 48-hour window, and that window is only ten days out. The Senate holds a cloture vote on the CLARITY Act at 2:15 in the afternoon, Eastern time, on September 15th. Republicans hold 53 seats, so at least seven Democrats or independents have to cross over just to open debate. And cloture only opens debate. It does not deliver a statute. The next day, September 16th, brings both the Fed's decision and Arc's scheduled mainnet launch. So you have a derivatives book that just added more than three billion dollars of open interest, sitting in front of a procedural vote, a rate decision and a settlement-chain launch, all inside two sessions. None of those three outcomes is predictable from here. What is checkable is narrower: whether funding stays this calm, whether cloture finds its seven votes, and whether Arc actually goes live on schedule.

That's the briefing. Have a good day.
