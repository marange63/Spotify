Capital Markets Cross-Asset Radar

Transcript · October 18, 2026

Good morning. It's Sunday, October 18th, and this is your Capital Markets Radar.

Yesterday's briefing left one question open. Does oil keep pushing against a Fed that only just resumed hiking? Friday answered it. Oil kept pushing, and the rates market leaned the other way.

Start with oil, because that's the new information. Brent crude settled at $109.86 a barrel on Friday, per FinanceFeeds. That's Brent's highest settlement since May 19th. West Texas crude did even more of the work over the week, up 10.4 percent against Brent's 4.9. The reporting ties the move to supply-risk headlines, not to demand. On Thursday, this desk had Brent near $103.75, sliding on talk that Washington might ease restrictions on some Russian-oil shipping. That relief didn't last. The so-what: the cleanest input for keeping the Fed cautious on inflation got worse heading into the weekend.

Now the Fed side of the same day. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh spoke on Friday and gave no forward signal. He said he doesn't believe in saying what he might do at meetings for the rest of the year. He pointed to the October and December meetings as the moments when policymakers will take stock. While he spoke, market-implied odds of an October hike fell to about 17 percent, from roughly 30, according to InvestingLive's account. Trackers differ on the exact readings, so take that as direction, not a settled close.

Set that against the whole week. On CPI day, October hike odds spiked to about 50 percent, then unwound to 31.6 percent that same evening. Friday took them to about 17. So the market has walked the hike out of October all week, even as Brent kept climbing. The so-what: the rates market moved against its own inflation input on the very day that input got worse.

There are two honest ways to read that, and neither has earned a verdict yet. The first is that a supply shock is a growth tax. It squeezes real incomes and margins before it ever reaches wages. That's the demand-destruction case this desk aired on Friday, and on that view fading the hike is rational. The second reading is that lower October odds aren't a dovish verdict at all. Warsh kept December explicitly live. In October pricing, a hike deferred to December looks identical to a hike abandoned. Our follow-up research looked for a clean, dated December probability and couldn't verify one. So we can't yet tell deferral from abandonment, and we won't pretend to.

One small data point leans toward the inflation reading. The five-year breakeven inflation rate, per the St. Louis Fed's FRED data, rose from 2.33 percent on Thursday to 2.37 percent on Friday. A four basis point move is weak evidence, and it could be noise. But it suggests markets read at least part of the oil spike as inflation, not purely as a demand hit. The so-what: on Friday, inflation expectations drifted up while hike pricing fell, and that tension is what to carry into the meeting.

So why can the market afford to fade the Fed at all? The answer is the credit story, and that story is bigger than the word "edge" implies. Forbes, citing Barclays data, reports $80 billion in data-center bonds raised so far this year. Of that, $37 billion, nearly half, is rated junk. Last year, junk was just $8.6 billion out of $36 billion raised. That's more than four times last year's junk volume, and the junk share has roughly doubled.

That forces a revision to this desk's own framing. On Saturday, we called the stress concentrated at the bottom of the credit stack. Yesterday's Throughline put it as markets pricing a contagion that the data still confines to the edges. On location, that framing still holds. As of Saturday's read, investment-grade spreads had barely moved. But concentrated no longer means small. Nearly half of this year's marginal data-center financing now sits in the tier that's repricing.

Here's the steelman for the benign side. Part of the rising junk share is simply who's issuing. Neoclouds and project vehicles were always going to come to market below investment grade. This is the year they showed up at scale. So some of the shift is a composition effect, not pure deterioration in credit quality. Severity is also contested, even inside Forbes's own coverage. A separate Forbes piece argues CoreWeave faces no imminent cash crisis. Still, composition doesn't shrink the dollar amount that's now exposed to repricing.

And that repricing has reached the project layer. Forbes says the average yield on bonds financing several data centers CoreWeave has leased climbed to about 11 percent over the past week. Investors typically demand roughly 7 percent on BB-plus paper. That's not CoreWeave's own corporate debt, whose distress-level yields we've covered all week. It's the project-finance layer underneath it. That layer sets the hurdle rate for every new facility that has to fund there. The so-what: the cost of capital for the AI buildout has risen where the next facilities actually get financed, not just on one issuer's balance sheet.

One inference follows, and it's ours, not a reported fact. Private credit funds and BDCs that lend into the same neocloud and data-center complex don't mark to a screen every day. If public project bonds keep widening, those private marks have a catch-up to do. We have no figure for that exposure. But it's the channel through which a public junk repricing becomes a problem for semi-liquid vehicles. That's where the market may be under-reading this story.

That credit repricing is what hit equities on Friday. Per BNN Bloomberg's market wrap, the Nasdaq fell 2.1 percent, against just 0.3 percent for the Dow. Breadth inside tech was brutal. All but seven of the S&P's 75 technology stocks closed lower. Chipmakers led the slide, with AMD down 6 percent and Micron and Intel each down more than 5.

This desk has flagged a rotation away from AI-exposed mega-caps before. Earlier in the week it showed up as the equal-weight index and the Dow outperforming. But Friday's version was different in character. It came with an outright market decline, not a broadening rally underneath one. That reads less like healthy diversification and more like de-risking out of leveraged AI exposure specifically.

Two guardrails on that read. First, the Dow is price-weighted. With oil up sharply, part of its resilience may simply be energy and value exposure, not a quality rotation. And one session is not a factor regime. Second, as of Saturday's reporting, no chip supplier had cut a forecast. So don't let "chips fell" stand in for "AI demand is cracking." That's where the market may be over-reading the tape. The so-what: equities are repricing who pays for the buildout, not what's demanded inside it.

Briefly, the same risk-off tone reached crypto. Bitcoin and Ethereum ETFs saw a combined roughly $152 million of net outflows on Friday, per Crypto Briefing's tally. Other trackers show a smaller bitcoin outflow, but still a negative one. One session of flows is small against the size of the ETF complex. The direction is what matters here. Yesterday, this desk called bitcoin's quick recovery from below $80,000 a first, tentative sign of decoupling from tech. We said it was noise until it repeated. In the flows, it didn't repeat. The so-what: crypto traded Friday like risk beta, not like insurance against the oil shock.

Pull it together. The front end is betting that credit fear keeps the Fed on hold, even as oil and a small uptick in breakevens argue the other way. If that bet is right, credit stress is doing some of the Fed's tightening for it. If it's wrong, and oil forces a hike into widening AI credit spreads, that's the policy-error scenario the front end isn't pricing. Two things to watch into the meeting. First, does December hike pricing climb as October's keeps falling? That would point to deferral, not abandonment. Second, does that 11 percent yield on CoreWeave-leased project bonds keep rising? That would say the buildout's hurdle rate is still resetting. The Fed's October 28th decision hasn't happened, so treat all of this as positioning, not a forecast.

That's the radar for today. Trade carefully, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
