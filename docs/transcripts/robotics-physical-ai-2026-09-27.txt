Good morning. It's September 27th, 2026, and this is your briefing on robotics and physical AI.

All month this beat has graded nameplates: pledges, targets and design capacities. This week, for the first time, we got a count of what is actually installed and running. That count reframes everything else we've covered, so let's start there.

The International Federation of Robotics released its World Robotics 2026 report on September 24th. The headline: the global operational stock of industrial robots rose 9 percent in 2025, to a record 5 million units. That is the base rate. Any humanoid pledge has to either compete with that installed capital stock or add to it.

Two caveats before we use it. First, the IFR is an industry body whose members sell robots, so it has an interest in a growth story. Second, this is a count of installations, not utilization. A unit bolted to a factory floor says nothing about uptime, cycles, or labor displaced. And the data covers calendar 2025, so it looks backward by design.

Now the concentration. China installed 354,000 industrial robots in 2025. That is 59 percent of every industrial robot installed on the planet. Earlier this month, this beat found Chinese vendors behind more than 97 percent of first-half humanoid shipments. We also found China controlling about 90 percent of global magnet processing. The IFR count says the same thing about the entire automation base. This is not a quirk of the humanoid form factor.

Here's the second-order effect, and it's our read. Installing that volume gives Chinese component makers the learning curve on actuators, reducers and motors across all of automation, not just humanoids. That compounds the cost gap we documented earlier this week, where a humanoid built without Chinese suppliers is estimated to cost far more than one built through them. So what: the dependency problem this beat keeps finding in exotic new categories is really a property of the whole system.

Now the non-consensus piece. US installations grew 12 percent in 2025, to almost 38,500 units. IFR says that is the third-highest total in the history of its statistics. It was also enough for the United States to overtake Japan as the world's second-largest market. Japan installed 36,219 units, so the margin is narrow.

Keep the scale honest. The US figure is roughly a ninth of China's. So this is a growth story, not a share shift. But it is the first installed-unit evidence this beat has had that automation-led reshoring is real. All week we tested reshoring through Hyundai's planned Atlas humanoid program at its Georgia plant. The signal turns out to be in plain industrial arms, years before any US humanoid factory ships a unit.

That reading is ours, not the IFR's, and there is a credible alternative. The US jump could be auto, EV and battery-plant capex timing. It could be tariff-driven pull-forward of equipment orders. This data cannot tell those stories apart from a durable trend. Either way, the arms are already on the floor. The humanoids are still slide decks.

Zoom out one more level. IFR says factories worldwide installed more than 600,000 new industrial robots in 2025. It forecasts 655,000 in 2026.

Here's what that means for incumbents, as we see it. A plant manager's budget for a humanoid usually comes from the same capex line that buys arms, mobile robots and conveyors, not from the headcount line. So a humanoid pilot doesn't just have to beat a human worker. It has to beat the next arm on that purchase order. On this forecast, the arm vendors are defending a market that is still growing steadily.

And here is the number that puts the humanoid conversation in proportion. This year the IFR ran its first-ever tally of humanoid robots. It found about 7,000 humanoids sold worldwide in 2025, for industrial and professional-service use. Set that against the more than 600,000 industrial robots installed the same year, by the same counting body. By our arithmetic, that is fewer than one humanoid for every eighty arms.

There's a further discount. Reuters' coverage of the tally notes that many of those 7,000 humanoids weren't doing productive work at all. They were bought by research institutes and companies to collect training data for AI models. That matches Tesla's own disclosure that its early Optimus units go to an internal program, Optimus Academy, for training-data collection. So even that small number overstates working, deployed humanoids.

Hold that ratio in mind the next time you hear about Hyundai's planned US factory, sized to build 30,000 Atlas robots a year. That is a pledge sitting on top of an installed base that is, today, overwhelmingly conventional arms. So what: the right denominator for any humanoid capex story is not the next pledge. It is 7,000 units sold, many of them into data collection.

That's the frame: nameplate against installed base. Two fresh items this week are worth running through the same test.

First, trucking. Aurora Innovation held its investor day on the 23rd. We walked through its build rate and revenue guide in our last briefing. The new number is its 2030 target: more than 30,000 driverless trucks in operation. That is the same round number as Hyundai's 30,000-a-year Atlas factory. In both cases, it is stated years before anyone can check it.

Now apply the installed-base test. Aurora's nearest countable base is its own year-end goal of 200 driverless trucks. Against that, the 2030 target implies roughly a 150-fold fleet expansion in four years, by our arithmetic. It comes from a company whose driverless timelines have slipped before. And it is a company target, not an order book.

What you can check this year is a pair of near-term numbers. Aurora is targeting a production rate of 20 trucks a week starting in October. And it aims to exit the year with those 200 trucks in operation. Its operating base is more than 500,000 fully driverless miles, by its own count. So what: equity stories are being underwritten on the 2030 number, but only these two checkpoints can re-mark them this year.

The second item goes to the blind spot we flagged at the top. The IFR counts installs, not duty cycles. Skydio has just unveiled a product designed around exactly that missing number: the F10 Lightrunner, shown at its Ascend conference on September 23rd. It is a fixed-wing drone that launches and lands itself from a robotic dock, with no runway and no crew on site. Per DroneXL's account, Skydio rates it at 100 miles an hour and up to two hours of flight.

The real product isn't the aircraft. It's the dock. Skydio's MegaDock holds up to five F10s and is designed to keep three in the air while two charge. That is a persistent-coverage design, built to take the operator crew out of the loop. But three of five airborne is a design duty cycle, not measured availability.

And the coverage gives no price, no ship date, and no named customer. Our read is that it competes for the same critical-infrastructure and defense budget lines as the autonomous surveillance approaches from Anduril and Shield AI. But with no contract, it can't be compared with either on results. So what: until Skydio posts a price or a first buyer, the F10 is a product launch, not a deployment.

Pulling it together. Where embodied AI is genuinely inflecting this year is the counted, installed, structured world of industrial automation. That world is still overwhelmingly Chinese by installation share. The United States is posting its first real reshoring signal, and it is in conventional arms rather than humanoids.

Everything with a bigger, more exciting number this month is a target. Hyundai's 30,000-a-year Atlas factory and Aurora's 30,000 trucks are both far-dated nameplates. On the humanoid side, the counted base is about 7,000 units sold last year.

On the model and data layer, nothing new cleared our bar this week. Figure's Helix 2.5 home-generalization results, graded here earlier this month, remain the latest read. But the IFR tally adds an indirect signal, and this is our interpretation. If many buyers are acquiring humanoid bodies to harvest training data, then real-world robot data is still the scarce input for robot foundation models. The biggest open question: does the IFR's humanoid tally become an annual series, giving us an independent yardstick for vendor claims?

For near-term catalysts, watch whether Aurora actually hits 20 trucks a week in October. Watch its December fleet count against the 200-truck goal. And watch for a price or a first customer on Skydio's F10. Those checkpoints will tell you whether this year's nameplates turn into next year's installed base.
