Today's theme is that, outside SpaceX, the Western orbital economy is still selling capacity it hasn't fielded. Three stories make that case in three different forms: a partnership, a delayed rocket, and a cadence request. Then we'll turn to the one company that has fielded capacity at scale, and to what it is actually growing instead.

Start with AST SpaceMobile. On October 23rd, the direct-to-device satellite company signed a commercial agreement with three Brazilian regional carriers. They are Brisanet, Unifique, and iez! Telecom. Per the Brazilian trade outlet DPL News, which reported the carriers' filing with Brazil's securities regulator, the operators will supply their 700 megahertz spectrum. They will also invest in the ground gateways that connect their networks to AST's constellation. That is a capital-light structure for AST. The carriers put up the spectrum and the ground capex, not AST.

But follow-up reporting from another Brazilian outlet, Telesintese, adds a sharper point. The value of each carrier's contribution was not disclosed. Neither was the split of resources between the three. And although the commercial agreement has been signed, the contract's final details remain under negotiation. So this isn't just a partnership without a launch date. It's a signed deal whose own commercial terms aren't settled yet.

There is also no launch date and no pricing attached to any of it. The expectation, per DPL News, is that satellite coverage will be sufficient for permanent service only by the end of 2027. That is more than a year out, and it is an expectation, not a commitment. So each new carrier adds optionality for AST, not near-term revenue. No revenue can start until the constellation can actually serve.

That matters because Brazil is now the third AST partnership or readiness claim this show has tracked in about a week. On October 20th it was the European carriers' letter of intent. On October 21st it was AST's claim of service readiness in five markets. On October 23rd we noted AST needs about ninety satellites on orbit to begin global service. The partnership column keeps growing. The satellite column hasn't visibly moved.

Two readings compete here. The bullish one says signing real carriers with real spectrum in a large emerging market, at no spectrum cost to AST, is genuine commercial validation. The skeptical one says a signature costs a carrier very little. It is an option on a second direct-to-device supplier and a bargaining chip against SpaceX, not a committed buyer. For those Brazilian carriers, an end-2027 coverage date means their AST plans stay an option through all of next year. That keeps their posture toward SpaceX hedged rather than committed.

Wall Street's read leans skeptical, and it aims at the right variable. Piper Sandler downgraded AST SpaceMobile to Neutral in mid-October. According to reporting on the note, it cited competitive pressure from SpaceX. It also flagged reduced conviction in the pace of AST's commercial satellite launches as a central reason. Around that downgrade, the stock broke below its prior fifty-two week low of $47.57.

It hasn't recovered that level. On October 23rd, the day of the Brazil news, AST closed at $39.79, up 2.63 percent on the session. That is still roughly sixteen percent below the old low it broke in mid-October. One day's bounce on a partnership doesn't undo a downgrade about launches. The so-what is that AST's gating variable is satellites on orbit, and AST doesn't control its own rides to get them there.

That's the hand-off to the Western launch bench, and it got thinner this month. Rocket Lab's Neutron is its bet on medium-lift reusability. It is the vehicle class Rocket Lab needs to compete for national-security and constellation launches beyond the small Electron niche. Neutron has now slipped again, to a target of early 2027 for its commercial debut. We've mentioned that slip in passing several times. What's new is the cause.

Engineers found air pockets, or voids, in the carbon-fiber layup of the first stage's forward propellant-tank dome. Rocket Lab says no other defects were found on the vehicle. That is the company's own claim, and no third party has verified it. Again there are two readings. The bearish one is that a void in a primary composite structure raises a real process-control question. Rocket Lab chose that composite approach for its weight advantage, so the question goes to the core of the design philosophy. The bullish one is that catching the defect on the ground, before flight, is the inspection regime doing its job. On that view, a corrected layup process is a bounded fix, not a redesign.

Either way, waiting costs money. Per The Motley Fool, Rocket Lab's free cash flow over the trailing twelve months was negative $371 million. That came against $769 million of revenue over the same period. In other words, the company burned roughly half a dollar of cash for every dollar it booked. Every slip extends the period Rocket Lab has to finance before Neutron can convert its backlog, including Space Development Agency awards, into flown revenue. How Rocket Lab funds that runway is an open question. Nothing in our sources says a raise is coming. So what: the gap between order book and realized revenue just got wider, and a cash-burning business is financing it.

Briefly, Blue Origin completes this column. New Glenn hasn't flown since a May 28th hotfire test destroyed major parts of Launch Complex 36 in Florida. Blue Origin has asked the FAA to raise its annual launch allowance from twelve to fifty. It says its new infrastructure is designed to support, in its words, "multiple flights per week by the end of the decade." That is the right ask for a reusable vehicle, because reusability economics depend on flight rate amortizing fixed cost. But it is a request, not an approval, from a vehicle that is not flying. Return to flight is targeted near year-end, and there is no confirmed date. A cadence allowance is not cadence.

Put Neutron and New Glenn together, and Western constellation demand keeps defaulting to one provider. As we noted on October 23rd, trade press reported that AST's BlueBirds seventeen through nineteen are slated for a Falcon 9 in November. AST is renting rides from its direct-to-cell competitor.

So who does have fielded capacity? SpaceX, and here the story inverts. On October 21st, this show said it had not located a primary SEC filing for SpaceX's third quarter. It confirms net income of $4.4 billion, against a $1.1 billion loss a year earlier.

The filing adds adjusted EBITDA of $8.8 billion. That is a non-GAAP measure, and it is roughly four times the year-ago figure. The segment mix you've already heard also holds up. Super Intelligence, the AI and cloud-compute segment, at $7.6 billion, is larger than Connectivity, meaning Starlink, at $5.2 billion. That is a rebound off a weak prior quarter, not acceleration. The filing as we have it gives no cause, so we won't speculate on which missions drove it.

The irony is worth landing. The one Western company with launch capacity fielded at scale is not growing on launch. Its growth is coming from AI infrastructure and connectivity. The service every rival in this briefing is trying to build, or rent, is SpaceX's slowest-growing line.

Pulling it together. What's verified and inflecting in this sector is SpaceX's scale, and it is inflecting away from the rocket business that defines it in most people's minds. AST's service, Neutron, and New Glenn are each still a plan dressed as a deployment. The open questions are whether AST's satellite count catches up with its partner list before that end-2027 coverage marker, and how Rocket Lab funds the path to Neutron's first flight. The catalysts on the calendar are Blue Origin's targeted return to flight near year-end, Neutron's early-2027 debut, and AST's end-2027 coverage expectation.
