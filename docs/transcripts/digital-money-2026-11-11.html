Digital Money, Crypto, and Market Structure

Good morning, it's Wednesday, November 11th, 2026, and this is your digital money briefing.

For most of the past week, this desk has carried one open question. Did the Bitcoin flushes actually clear the leverage in the system, or just mask it? We now have a reading, and the answer is no. The book didn't clear. In coin terms, it built.

Start with price, and only briefly. Bitcoin set an intraday high near $80,834 on November 5th. It then slid to about $73,500 before bouncing. By our arithmetic, that's roughly a 9 percent drawdown. The new information is underneath that move. K33 now puts perpetual futures open interest at 353,000 Bitcoin, a two-year extreme.

Read that number carefully. It's counted in coins, not dollars. Venues disagree sharply on the dollar level, so treat it as a percentile reading, not a precise size. And a two-year high in coin terms during a decline doesn't prove traders piled in fresh size. It may simply mean longs held on rather than closing out. Either way, positions did not come off as price fell. So the fuel for another forced-selling leg is still sitting in the system. Because the book is crowded, the next move could be outsized relative to whatever spot flow triggers it, in either direction.

If the leverage stayed, the next question is what it's correlated to. K33 measured Bitcoin's 30-day correlation with the Nasdaq at 0.75. That ranks in the 99th percentile of readings going back to 2020. On Monday, this desk said it had no correlation statistic to quote, and called the Nasdaq link co-movement, not measurement. Now it's a measurement. One limit is worth flagging. A 30-day window is dominated by a handful of big shared-direction days. A single shock session can push the print to an extreme. So it tells you how the last month behaved, not that the regime is permanent.

The same week produced a second data point that complicates the easy read. Gold dropped to about $4,054 an ounce on November 6th, as stocks and Bitcoin sank. That's gold breaking its own safe-haven script, at exactly the moment Bitcoin's digital-gold pitch needed a partner. When gold, Bitcoin, and equities all fall together, it looks less like Bitcoin is secretly tech beta. It looks more like holders who share one risk budget, cutting everything in it at once. That's a statement about who holds the asset, not what the asset is.

For allocators, though, the practical effect is the same. A top-percentile equity correlation, with gold failing alongside it, undercuts the uncorrelated-sleeve pitch that justified much of the spot-ETF buying. If the holder base runs on a shared risk budget, the next equity shock transmits straight into Bitcoin. It arrives through ETF redemptions and perp liquidations at the same time.

Tuesday then added a specific catalyst. Yesterday we noted the October CPI print would land after our recording. It has now landed. Per Bitcoin.com's same-day report, consumer prices rose 0.4 percent month over month, hotter than expected. Bitcoin fell below $73,000 that day, and the same report puts the intraday low at $72,787 after the release. That causal link comes from a single source, so hold it loosely. But the timing lines up. The latest leg down came on a macro print, not on anything inside crypto's own plumbing.

Now the regulated wrapper, which still isn't supplying a patient buyer. US spot Bitcoin ETFs recorded $201 million in net outflows on November 9th. On Monday we asked whether the selling would carry into a second week. It did. That makes the outflow a streak, not a one-off.

If neither the ETF wrapper nor the futures book is offering steady demand, the remaining candidate is the corporate treasury cohort. And its biggest name had a more defensive week than yesterday's headline suggested.

Yesterday we reported that Strategy kept buying, adding 1,036 Bitcoin funded by common-stock sales. What's new is where the rest of that money went. Strategy sold $283.8 million of common stock that week, in net proceeds. Of that, $188.3 million went to buying back its STRC preferred. That's more than twice what it spent on Bitcoin. By our arithmetic, roughly two-thirds of the equity raised went to retiring preferred. Under 30 percent went to Bitcoin.

There's a genuinely bullish reading of that. By our arithmetic, the buyback price works out to roughly par, so Strategy wasn't scooping up distressed paper. Retiring preferred cuts a fixed dividend obligation that sits senior to the common. In a drawdown, that de-risks the capital structure. The blended position isn't deep underwater either. Dividing the filing's total cost by its total coins gives an average near $75,450. Against a tape in the low-to-mid seventy thousands, that's within a few percent of breakeven. Only the recent tranches, like last week's near $79,000, are clearly under water.

The less charitable reading runs the other way. The preferred channel was the engine of Strategy's accumulation model. Now that channel is being retired with common-equity proceeds. That means common stock issuance, not fresh Bitcoin demand, is what's carrying the structure. For MSTR holders, that weakens the Bitcoin-per-share accretion case. The common stock itself becomes what finances the balance sheet, and that turns reflexive if the shares weaken. The data can't yet settle which reading is right. A pending index-eligibility review at MSCI hangs over all of it, with no outcome confirmed.

Strategy is defending its balance sheet. Circle, the other public bellwether for structural crypto demand, is defending its income statement. On November 8th, this desk described a two-sided squeeze on Circle's core revenue, from both rates and float. The third-quarter detail shows what filled the gap. Circle reported $865 million of revenue, up 17 percent. But on the reporting we have, $182 million of that came from selling its new ARC token. Strip that out, and by our arithmetic, core revenue fell roughly 8 percent year over year.

Usage tells the opposite story. Coverage of the quarter put USDC's on-chain transaction volume at $13.9 trillion, and CoinDesk described that volume as soaring. So throughput is surging while the issuer's core economics shrink. That's a real decoupling between how much the dollar token gets used and how much Circle earns from it. And on-chain volume is a throughput measure, not proof of real-world payments demand.

The bull reading on Circle deserves its due. Monetizing the Arc network through a token sale is exactly the diversification away from float income that the squeeze demands. That's arguably strategy, not window-dressing. The open question is whether it recurs. A presale is one-time by nature, unless more tranches follow. So Circle's stock starts to look less like a rate-sensitive float business and more like a network-issuance business. That means less recurring earnings, until proven otherwise.

So here's the strongest bull case, built only from what we can verify. The latest leg down came on an imported macro print, not a crypto-specific break. Strategy's blended cost sits within a few percent of spot. Retiring preferred strengthens its capital structure. USDC throughput is surging. And Circle is actively diversifying away from float income. All of that is real. It just isn't a bull case the leverage data confirms yet. The book stayed crowded, and gold didn't hedge it either.

Three things to watch. First, whether perpetual open interest finally comes down on the next leg, or keeps holding. Second, whether spot ETF flows turn positive at all this week. Third, Strategy's next weekly filing, and whether proceeds keep flowing to the preferred rather than to Bitcoin.

The thesis in one line. The leverage didn't clear, it held, and the two companies most identified with structural crypto demand spent last week defending their own models rather than adding to the bid.
