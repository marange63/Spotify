Good morning, it's Thursday, October 15th, and this is your private equity briefing. Two take-privates this show flagged as unsigned talks in recent days are now signed deals, and both tell you more about who is engineering liquidity than about whether buyout pricing has actually re-rated.

Start with the lead. GTCR and Elliott Investment Management are taking CCC Intelligent Solutions private at an enterprise value of roughly six point one billion dollars. CCC is a vertical-software provider to the auto insurance and claims industry. Shareholders get seven dollars seventy-five cents a share in cash. This show flagged GTCR and Elliott in advanced, unsigned talks on Sunday, October 11th, and had no update on Tuesday. Now it's signed. There's no financing condition, which means the sponsor group carries the funding risk rather than keeping an out. Signed is not closed, though, and nothing has changed hands yet.

Here's where it gets more interesting than the headline. CCC's merger release quotes the premium at forty-four percent to the closing price on July 9th. Against the thirty-day average to that date, it's fifty-nine percent. July 9th sounds like a neutral reference point, but it isn't arbitrary. The release itself defines it as the last trading day before media speculation about a sale. That speculation was a Bloomberg report, published the next day, that Elliott had built a large stake while CCC explored a sale. CCC jumped ten percent on that report alone, closing at five dollars ninety-two cents. So the forty-four percent is measured against a price from before the market even knew Elliott was in the stock.

Now set that against where the stock actually traded more recently. On Sunday, we reported that CCC closed at seven dollars and two cents after the talks leaked. That was the close on October 9th, not the last close before signing, so it's not a clean premium base. But it tells you the seven seventy-five offer sits only modestly above where the market had already priced the deal. The forty-four percent headline is mostly a measure of how far the stock had run on speculation, not of how much the buyers paid up.

That matters because on Sunday this show argued, as our own analysis, that an activist already holding a large stake has an information and blocking edge. That edge can discourage rival bids and hold the premium down rather than up. The signed terms don't prove that argument, and they don't refute it either. We still don't know the final pre-announcement price, and we don't know whether anyone else bid. Call it unresolved, with the headline premium tilting the evidence toward caution rather than celebration.

On financing, the merger release names J.P. Morgan as lead arranger of the committed financing and lead financial advisor to GTCR. That's a genuine upgrade on knowing only that there's no financing condition, because it names who's underwriting the risk. Be careful with the read-through, though. A committed package for best-in-class vertical software cash flows doesn't prove the large-cap buyout debt market is broadly open. It may only prove that this kind of asset is financeable when others aren't. What we still don't have is the size of Elliott's stake or the split between debt and equity. The original Bloomberg report said the stake size couldn't be learned, and the merger release doesn't disclose it either. So don't take a view on leverage here.

The so-what on CCC: this is a sponsor-plus-activist entry, not a clean open auction. Elliott went from building a stake in a public company to sitting inside the buyer group. On our read, that's a template other activists can copy when a public multiple lags what a sponsor consortium will pay, and public minority holders are the ones who lose the competitive tension.

Same channel, other side of the trade now. Bumble is going private, bought by Ithaca Holdings in an all-cash deal valuing the company at one point three billion dollars, or six dollars and five cents a share for Class A stock. This show flagged reported Bumble talks on Tuesday; it's now signed. The premium is a startling hundred and thirty-eight percent against the unaffected close on October 9th. Against the thirty-day volume-weighted average to that same date, it drops to a hundred and nineteen percent. That nineteen-point gap is the tell. Bumble's stock was still sliding inside the final month, so the spot price was below its own recent average. A premium this size says far more about how depressed the starting point was than about a buyer paying up for growth.

The ownership story here is Blackstone. Affiliates of Blackstone, a long-standing Bumble shareholder, approved the transaction by written consent, together with founder Whitney Wolfe Herd. That consent replaces a shareholder vote. KKR Capital Markets, meanwhile, is advising the buyer, Ithaca, on financing. So one sponsor's legacy stake is being sold, another sponsor's capital-markets arm is advising the buyer, and minority holders get no vote at all. I want to be careful here. We don't know Blackstone's cost basis or stake size, so there's no basis to call this a gain or a loss for the firm. What we can say is that this is liquidity Blackstone engineered through a take-private. It's not liquidity the public market supplied through an IPO or a block trade. And signed isn't cash. Nothing here shows up as a distribution to anyone until the Bumble deal actually closes.

The same insider-on-both-sides pattern runs through secondaries too. Coller EQT is the secondaries platform EQT absorbed when it completed its takeover of Coller Capital at the end of August. It has now co-led a single-asset continuation vehicle called the VDK Partnership Fund. The vehicle backs EMK Capital's continued ownership of VDK, a Dutch technical-installation group. Existing EMK fund investors get the choice to sell or roll over, and VDK gets fresh capital for European add-on acquisitions. Deal size and pricing to net asset value weren't disclosed. The claim that this is Coller EQT's first GP-led lead since the takeover comes from a newsletter report, not an EQT or Coller release, so treat it as reported rather than confirmed.

Recall that on Sunday, Coller EQT's refusal of Lightspeed's twenty-five percent carry was the only visible instance of buyer discipline we'd found. Now that same disciplined buyer sits inside one of the largest multi-strategy managers, and it's putting capital to work right away. The so-what, and this is our read rather than reported fact: buyer discipline is a weaker check on the continuation-vehicle market when the same large manager increasingly sits on more than one side of these deals.

Let's bring this together. Everything today is the private equity cycle clearing through engineered channels. There are take-privates with the activist or the seller already inside the deal, and continuation vehicles led by a secondaries buyer that now belongs to a sponsor. None of it is cash in a limited partner's hands yet. The distribution gap this show keeps coming back to is still the binding constraint.

The forward catalyst is Blackstone's third-quarter report, due in the coming days. Blackstone's own intra-quarter update put realized performance revenue plus realized principal investment income at more than three hundred fifty million dollars. That covers July 1st through September 22nd only, and about ninety percent of it is realized performance revenue. It explicitly excludes anything after September 22nd, so it's a floor for the quarter, not the quarter. And it's firmwide, not private equity alone. When the full number comes out, measure it against that floor. A figure well above it would mean late-quarter exits actually landed in cash, which supports a thaw. A figure barely above it would say the exit window is still running mostly on engineered deals like today's.

Watch three things from here: whether the CCC and Bumble deals actually close, how far Blackstone's full quarter clears that three-hundred-fifty-million-dollar floor, and whether any sponsor exits a public position this quarter without an insider sitting on both sides of the table.

That's the briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
