Good morning, it's Saturday, November 7th, and this is your Capital Markets Radar.

Here's the thesis up front. Thursday's rout had started to price a Fed that skips its December hike. Friday's jobs report took that cushion away. So the AI unwind now has to clear through positioning on its own. And oil is tilting the macro risk toward stagflation, not rescue.

Start with Thursday. The Nasdaq Composite fell five and a half percent, per CNBC. Reuters called it the index's deepest one-day drop since April 2025. The trigger most outlets cited was regulatory, after Texas's governor-elect called for halting data-center construction. But that headline landed on a trade hedge funds were already cutting. Goldman's prime-brokerage data showed them selling tech at the fastest pace in at least a decade back in mid-October. So what: Thursday was a political spark on a crowded book, and the size came from the book.

Then Friday's data arrived, and it points the opposite way. The Bureau of Labor Statistics reports that nonfarm payrolls rose 165,000 in October. Reuters-polled economists had expected about 65,000. Unemployment fell to 4.1 percent. Keep in mind this is a Fed that already hiked a quarter-point in September.

Then there's the detail that complicates the easy skeptical read. BLS also revised August and September up by a combined 84,000. So this isn't one surprising month against a weak trend. The two months before it were stronger than first reported, too. So what: this is not the fragile economy Thursday's tape was pricing.

Here's the collision. Thursday's rout had pulled December hike odds down to about 70 percent, per Reuters, from over 80 percent a week earlier. By Friday afternoon, after the print, CME-derived odds of a quarter-point December hike stood at 77.7 percent. Those are snapshots from different sources and different hours, so read the direction, not the decimal. Either way, the data isn't validating the policy put the rout began to price.

Give the competing interpretation its due, though. A labor market still hiring at this pace suggests Thursday was a valuation and positioning event, not a growth shock. That's bad news for anyone hoping the Fed rescues the AI trade. It isn't bad news for earnings. The market may be over-reading the rout as a growth signal. We've also seen no public comment from any Fed official on the rout or the print. So every read of the reaction function here is market-implied, not official.

There's a second-order cost if the hike does come. Policy would be tightening again after September's hike, straight into an equity-led tightening of financial conditions. That's pro-cyclical. It also raises the cost of capital for this week's new AI debt, from Oracle's raise to Meta's bond deal. So what: the data didn't ask for rescue, and the hike case now has to stand on its own through December.

So did markets believe the print? The loudest answer came from the dollar. The dollar index rose about three-quarters of a percent on Friday, to 102.26, per GV Wire. Bonds moved far less. The same outlet has the 10-year Treasury yield up just 1.6 basis points, to 4.85 percent. Other outlets put Friday's level slightly higher, so treat it as approximate. Either way, that's a muted long-end move for a payrolls beat of about 100,000 jobs. We have no sourced 2-year figure, and the front end is where a hike repricing would show first. A dollar that firms into an equity selloff tightens global dollar funding. It's also a headwind for gold and for emerging-market carry. So what: the currency market priced the hawkish print, and the long end barely flinched.

The missing confirmation is US stocks. Early coverage pointed to a partial rebound on Friday, but we don't have a sourced close or a sourced size for the bounce. A bounce straight through a hawkish print would say the market sees Thursday as positioning. A fresh slide would say it's mourning the missing put. Until that close is confirmed, treat the equity verdict as open.

The second hawkish input landed in the same twenty-four hours, from energy. Brent's war premium drains on one headline and refills on the next, and right now it's refilling. Oil rallied on renewed Iran uncertainty, per DTN. That followed reports from Iran's Revolutionary Guard of tankers set on fire near unauthorized routes through the Strait of Hormuz. Treat that as reported, not independently confirmed. Eurasia Group doubled its odds of a prolonged Iran conflict, to 30 percent. It says escalation could push Brent back toward 110 to 125 dollars a barrel. That's a conditional scenario, not a base-case forecast.

Now the other side of the oil ledger. JPMorgan says the market ran a surplus of 1.4 million barrels a day in October, wider than in September. A risk premium stacked on a stated surplus is fragile. Oil has already round-tripped that exact setup once in the past two weeks. But this time it lands alongside a hawkish payrolls print, and ahead of an unreleased October CPI. That strengthens the hawks' inflation case. It's a stagflation-adjacent setup, now arriving from the inflation side. So what: neither of this week's two surprises points toward the Fed easing up.

So if neither the data nor the Fed is going to clear this market, positioning has to. Goldman estimates that tech-focused equity long-short funds fell more than 4 percent on Thursday alone. Reuters reports the selloff wiped out much of their gains for the year. Goldman's Brian Garrett, as relayed by Reuters, says trading desks are still selling tech. He expects systematic CTA strategies to generate new short signals. He says fund flows and systematic selling have yet to clear. He calls that the main near-term risk for US stocks.

Notice how far the positioning risk has flipped. Earlier this week, the worry was that crowded hedges loaded the upside with squeeze risk. Now the sell side's flagged risk is mechanical downside. Two cautions before you trade that. First, "poised to generate" short signals is Garrett's conditional forecast, not selling that has happened. Second, estimates earlier this week had trend-following equity positioning already deeply net short. If that's still true, there's less mechanical selling left than the next-leg narrative implies. So what: another leg down would be mechanical follow-through, not new information, and it may be smaller than feared.

Why does the market's plumbing matter more than usual this time? Start with scale. Opening Bell Daily ranks Thursday's VIX jump as its third-largest daily gain on record. And the shock didn't stay in New York. South Korea's Kospi fell almost 9 percent at Friday's open, which triggered a trading halt. That shows the crowded AI and memory-chip trade is a global positioning book, not a US one. Contagion now runs through foreign markets with circuit breakers and different liquidity. So what: this unwind can't be treated as a US-hours event.

Then look at who's actually exposed. Bloomberg puts about 610 billion dollars of US pension fund holdings in tech, roughly 44 percent of what it measured. That implies a base of only about 1.4 trillion dollars, a subset of pension assets, not the whole system. And "tied to tech" isn't the same claim as "exposed to AI capex." Bloomberg also finds the S&P 500 has added nearly 32 trillion dollars since ChatGPT's release. More than half of that came from just ten AI-linked companies. That 32 trillion is gains already booked, which is exactly what's at risk now. So what: the AI trade's risk doesn't sit only on hedge-fund books. A meaningful slice of it sits on retirement balance sheets.

That concentration question has a live price on it right now, in Anthropic's IPO. A disclosure first: Anthropic makes the model behind this show. On Kalshi, the odds of an Anthropic filing by November 30th fell from 84 percent to 60 percent on Thursday afternoon. Treat that as a thin, reflexive prediction market, not an underwriter's book. Reports relayed by Benzinga and Reuters suggest the target valuation could be cut. Those reports rest on unnamed sourcing, and there's no confirmed change to the roadshow timeline. The read-through still matters. A materially lower private AI valuation would pressure the marks that venture and sovereign portfolios carry on labs like OpenAI and xAI. It would also chill the 2027 IPO pipeline, even as it marginally eases near-term equity supply. A lower price could also simply be a healthier clearing level, not a verdict against AI. So what: the first real-time price on whether the AI reset is durable will come from this book.

Three things to watch into next week. First, October CPI lands Tuesday, November 10th, and it hasn't been released. It meets hike pricing that re-firmed after the jobs print. Second, whether Goldman's CTA short signals actually fire, or the already-short trend-following base caps the follow-through. Third, credit. We still don't have a clean, dated read on how high-yield and investment-grade spreads moved through Thursday's session. That gap needs closing before anyone calls this contained.
