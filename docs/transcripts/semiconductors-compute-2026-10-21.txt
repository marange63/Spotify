Good morning. It's Wednesday, October 21st, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

Here's the thesis for today. Last week, Seoul's equity market priced memory as a cyclical peak. The price evidence that has arrived since then points the other way. Forecasters are raising their contract-price calls. Samsung is asking roughly triple today's HBM pricing for next year's product. And the shortage is now showing up in the oldest, least AI-related memory on the market. That matters because it shifts more of the AI buildout's scarcity rent toward the memory makers. It also pushes the cost of that rent onto everyone who buys memory, not just the hyperscalers.

Start with the forecast revision, because it's the freshest data point and it tests an open question from Sunday's episode. J.P. Morgan raised its fourth-quarter DRAM contract-price forecast to a 10 to 15 percent increase, up from 3 to 8 percent. It lifted its NAND call to 15 to 20 percent, up from zero to 5. That comes from Money Today's report on the bank's note. Notice that the new DRAM range matches what TrendForce had already pencilled in back in August. In other words, a major bank just caught up to where the price trackers already were.

On Sunday we said a three-to-four-times forward earnings multiple in Seoul could mean one of two things. Either memory earnings are about to fall, or consensus estimates simply hadn't caught up with prices. This is the first sell-side revision since then, and it leans toward the second reading. Keep two cautions in view. One bank's forecast is not an observed contract price, and it doesn't settle the debate on its own. And even the raised call implies a much slower pace than the second-quarter surge we described on Sunday. A bigger, later peak is still a peak. But for now, the direction of the surprise is up, not down.

The sharpest version of that pricing power sits at the top of the memory stack, in HBM4. Samsung has proposed a price in the mid-four-dollar range per gigabit for 2027 supply. Today's HBM3E sells for roughly a dollar fifty. That's close to a tripling. The negotiation is with Nvidia and Broadcom. As of an early-October report, it had entered its final stage on price. So this is a live ask, not a signed contract.

Independent analysts are in the same neighborhood. KB Securities expects average HBM selling prices to rise 100 to 150 percent year over year in 2027. TrendForce separately projects an average HBM price increase of 121 percent. So treat Samsung's number as a real negotiating position, with analyst support behind the magnitude. Two caveats still apply. Nothing is signed. And HBM4 carries more silicon and base-die logic than HBM3E, so part of that gap reflects a different product, not pure pricing power.

For scale, look at what memory is already earning. Samsung's preliminary third-quarter operating profit came in at 107.4 trillion won, up more than 780 percent from a year earlier, and memory drove it. The full segment breakdown isn't out yet. SK hynix is the bigger HBM supplier, with half of second-quarter HBM revenue according to Counterpoint. FnGuide consensus has SK hynix earning roughly 77 trillion won in third-quarter operating profit. That implies an operating margin near 78 percent. Those are estimates, not results. The report is expected in the final days of October, and sources disagree on the exact date.

Here's what that means for the accelerator bill of materials. If HBM4 pricing lands anywhere near Samsung's ask, more of each accelerator's cost flows to the memory makers. Nvidia, AMD, and the custom-silicon programs then face a choice. They can absorb the higher input cost in gross margin, or pass it on to hyperscalers and labs. Mark that as inference, since none of them has said which. And be precise about the competitive dimension. What the memory makers are gaining here is pricing power and control over capacity allocation. None of this evidence says anything new about memory technology leadership.

And the pull on capacity doesn't stop at HBM. Digitimes reported last week that DDR4 contract prices are now rising faster than DDR5. DDR4 is the oldest mainstream DRAM standard still in volume production, so that reverses the normal pattern. No reliable magnitude has surfaced yet, so take the direction, not a number. The likely mechanism is capacity allocation. Fabs are moving wafers toward HBM, and that squeezes the legacy DRAM that PCs, phones, and enterprise servers depend on. There's an alternative explanation worth naming. Some of this could be ordinary end-of-life supply cuts on an aging standard. Even so, the direction fits the mechanism we flagged on October 15th. The squeeze on old memory is being set by how fabs allocate wafers, not by any surge in demand for the old product.

That forces a correction. On Sunday we suggested that slower contract-price inflation would offer some relief to hyperscalers and device makers. Both the J.P. Morgan revision and the DDR4 move argue against that relief holding. PC, smartphone, and server makers with no AI exposure are likely to keep absorbing rising memory costs into 2027. We don't yet have a quoted price or an OEM statement to size that squeeze, so hold it as inference.

If memory is gaining pricing power, it's worth checking who isn't. As we covered on October 15th, TSMC's gross margin fell 1.3 points sequentially to 66.4 percent on 2-nanometer dilution. Management expects the overseas-fab drag to widen to 3 to 4 points over the next few years. One detail from that call we hadn't aired: Arizona Fab 2 is now tracked for volume production in the third quarter of 2027, slightly ahead of schedule. That pulls part of the reshoring cost a little closer.

Treat the rent-shifting framing as a working inference, not settled fact. TSMC's margin dip is largely a timing effect from a node ramp, and those dips have historically recovered. It isn't clear evidence that memory is taking AI profit away from TSMC. Still, the contrast is worth holding. The foundry is absorbing margin pressure from its own ramps, while the memory makers are pushing prices up.

The other rent-setter in this market is Washington. For context, IDC data from mid-October put Nvidia's share of China's AI chip market at 34 percent. What's new is a report on Tuesday that the administration has loosened limits on some advanced Nvidia chips to China, in exchange for a revenue share to the US government. A White House official has disputed that framing. No Commerce Department or BIS notice confirms any change. So this is a contested claim, not a policy fact.

Last Friday we called China's AI ceiling a policy ceiling, set in Washington. A policy ceiling can move, and this report is the first test of that. But even a finalized revenue-share license would likely cover specific chips, not the whole market. And it wouldn't erase the ground Huawei and other domestic rivals have already taken. Until a notice is actually published, nothing here has changed.

A short watch list for the coming week. First, SK hynix's third-quarter report, the first reported memory quarter since the Seoul rout. If forecasts keep rising while multiples stay compressed, either the stocks re-rate or the market is pricing new Chinese DRAM supply and HBM normalization in 2027. SK hynix's numbers are the cleanest test of which. Second, Samsung's full third-quarter breakdown, including its foundry segment. Third, any actual Commerce or BIS filing on China chip licensing. And fourth, whether a second research house follows J.P. Morgan upward. If one does, the estimates-were-lagging reading gets stronger.

That's the briefing. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
