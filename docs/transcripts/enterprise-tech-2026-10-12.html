Good morning. It's Monday, October 12th, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

One disclosure before we start, because it bears directly on the lead story: Anthropic makes the model behind this show. Here's today's thesis. Microsoft is buying less Claude for its own staff while selling more of it to customers, and that tells you where leverage in enterprise AI is moving. It's moving toward whoever owns the channel, not whoever owns the best model.

Start with the lead. Microsoft has cut its projected internal spending on Anthropic's Claude by more than a third. That's down from an annualized peak projection of over a billion dollars earlier this year. Meta has cut the number of employees using Claude Code roughly in half, from about sixty thousand earlier this year to thirty thousand. All of this traces to a single report from The Information, which is paywalled. We're relying on outlets that relayed it, chiefly PYMNTS and Electronics Weekly.

Here is what's on the record. A Microsoft spokesperson said the company has been steering staff toward its own GitHub Copilot coding tool, while still letting engineers choose other models. Meta did not comment. We found no Anthropic statement in any of the coverage, so Anthropic simply hasn't been quoted on this.

Now the twist that keeps this from being a simple story about Claude losing ground. According to the report, Microsoft's lower internal spending has been offset by higher Claude use from its customers. Its total payments to Anthropic have reportedly stayed roughly flat. That offset applies to Microsoft only, not to Meta.

So there are two readings. One is a demand signal against Claude. The other is cost-driven vertical integration. The evidence favors the second. Microsoft has its own in-house models and a deep OpenAI relationship. It has every incentive to push internal workflows toward tools it controls. Meanwhile, customers buying through Microsoft's platforms are reportedly using more Claude, not less.

Meta adds two wrinkles. First, despite halving its Claude Code users, Meta reportedly still spent over a hundred and five million dollars on Claude Code in one recent four-week window. Annualized, that pace would top Microsoft's entire billion-dollar peak projection. So the seat cut is not a proportional usage cut. The remaining Meta users may simply be using Claude Code far more intensively. Second, the motive may not be only cost. As the tech outlet The Decoder put it, Meta has little reason to keep feeding Anthropic when it wants to sell competing products. That's analysis, not a Meta statement, but it points to a competitive motive alongside the cost one.

This extends a pattern we've followed all week, as enterprise platforms route work across model vendors. Atlassian's Rovo was routing each task across vendors, and Salesforce was shifting some Agentforce traffic off Google's Gemini models. Yesterday we covered Anthropic's own lever: when a customer's commitment runs out, pricing snaps back to list. Today, two of Anthropic's biggest buyers are shrinking that commitment from the inside. A model vendor's pricing power erodes first with its most sophisticated customers, the ones with in-house alternatives.

The takeaway: seats and logos are reversible, and fast, even at the most sophisticated buyers. That's exactly why vendor seat counts are weak evidence of durable adoption, no matter which lab is reporting them.

Now, the same week Microsoft trimmed its own Claude use, it shipped the gate that AI agents now pass through on Windows. Microsoft Execution Containers, or MXC, reached general availability on October 7th. It's a policy layer. A developer declares exactly what an agent can access: which files, which network destinations, which processes, and which parts of the interface. The agent can't exceed that explicit grant. Windows 365 support is generally available too. This comes mainly from Neowin's account of Microsoft's Windows Experience blog post.

Launch partners include OpenAI's Codex and GitHub Copilot, along with Replit, LM Studio, and NVIDIA's OpenShell. Anthropic's Claude Code is among the agents adding support later, rather than at launch. Don't over-read that as a snub. The source gives no reason for the sequencing. Codex and Copilot going first may simply reflect Microsoft's existing relationships.

What matters more is how MXC differs from the other agent control layers announced recently. SAP's Joule Work, OpenAI's Marketplace, and ServiceNow's Action Fabric all arrived as announcements with no adoption data. MXC is actually generally available, with named integrations. It still has no usage data. Nobody has counted how many enterprises are enforcing these policies, so this is a capability, not proof of deployment.

But the strategic point stands. Whoever controls endpoint containment influences which third-party agents IT departments are willing to approve. That makes integration timing with Microsoft's control plane a distribution variable for agent vendors. And that lever sits apart from which model performs best on a benchmark.

Which brings us back to the lead. When Microsoft's customer resale offsets its internal Claude cut, whose revenue is that, and how is it counted? Start with OpenAI. It told its investors that its annualized revenue is approaching fifty billion dollars. Keep in mind that's an investor disclosure, not a filed or audited number. OpenAI's own investors have tried to line that number up directly against Anthropic's annualized revenue. That comparison doesn't hold, because the two labs count partner sales differently.

Here is the difference. OpenAI books only its share of sales made through partners. Anthropic counts gross revenue from cloud partners, including Amazon's AWS and Google Cloud. So OpenAI's headline is a net number, and Anthropic's headline is a gross number. Set them side by side, and Anthropic's figure looks larger relative to OpenAI's than end-customer demand alone would justify.

The enterprise angle is what matters here. Every dollar of Claude resold through a hyperscaler marketplace can lift Anthropic's headline, while the hyperscaler keeps the customer relationship and its cut. We could not verify from a primary source whether Claude sold through Microsoft is booked gross the way the AWS and Google Cloud sales reportedly are. So we won't extend the claim. But the open question is the point. Ahead of prospective IPOs from both labs, any revenue comparison needs a stated basis. Gross or net isn't a technicality. It changes what the number means.

Application incumbents are running the same play through their own bundles. Salesforce has replaced its Enterprise, Unlimited, and Agentforce 1 editions with three tiers: Core, Advanced, and Max. Existing customers keep current pricing until renewal. Agentforce users can upgrade to Max at no extra cost.

What's new is the pricing architecture. Salesforce is monetizing agentic AI through its own bundle and its own renewal cycle, not through per-model pricing it doesn't control. One customer claim is worth noting, labelled as a Salesforce-published figure rather than an independent one. Hibbett's AI agent reportedly handles ninety percent of core shopper journeys after going live in six weeks. That's one hand-picked customer, not a platform average. The real economics test arrives at renewal, when a free upgrade meets whatever Salesforce decides the AI-inclusive tier is worth.

So where does that leave the picture? Platform position is capturing the leverage in enterprise AI faster than any single model's quality is. That means the reseller, the router, the endpoint gate, and the bundle. And headline adoption numbers need a stated basis before you trust them. Three things to watch. First, whether Microsoft, Meta, or Anthropic goes on record beyond what's been reported. Second, whether Claude Code actually ships MXC support. Third, whether either frontier lab discloses a clear revenue basis before it goes public.
