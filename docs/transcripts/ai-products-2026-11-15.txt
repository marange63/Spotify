Good morning, it's Sunday, November 15th, 2026, and this is your AI Products briefing. One standing disclosure before we start: Anthropic makes the model behind this show, and one of Anthropic's products comes up later in this episode.

Here's the thread running through this week's launches. Who owns the surface decides where the agents can go. Google pushed its newest model by default into the places it already controls. Amazon's existing ban on AI shopping agents shows the same logic from the other side: the store decides which agent gets in. And on the enterprise side, two vendors moved their agents onto the customer's own infrastructure, instead of asking the customer to come to theirs. Three different plays, one underlying logic.

Start with Google. On November 12th, Google shipped Gemini 4 Hydrogen, which it positions as the everyday workhorse for agents and coding. The headline isn't really the model. It's the distribution. Google's own blog put it plainly: "Starting today, Hydrogen is rolling out globally across our developer, enterprise, and consumer surfaces." Per that announcement, the rollout spans the Gemini app, Google Search, AI Studio, Android Studio, the Gemini API, Gemini for business, and Gemini in Sheets. On Search, Google's Search team went further: "Hydrogen is now available globally in Google Search for all users." So in Search, this is universal, not a paid-tier test.

Here's our inference from that breadth. No standalone model vendor can match that kind of same-day placement, however good its model is. And once Hydrogen sits inside Search and the Gemini app, a lot of simple research and coding-adjacent work can happen right in the search bar. The user never makes a model choice at all. That puts pressure on point tools that sell on the same tasks.

The pricing is the other concrete detail. Google says Hydrogen is "priced flat across its entire 1 million-token context window, at $1.50 per million input tokens and $7.50 per million output tokens." As we read it, flat across the full window means no long-context surcharge. That matters for agents that reload large codebases or big document sets. A builder can budget a job without worrying that crossing some context threshold triggers a price jump. What Google's post doesn't settle is how that price compares with the older Flash rate it replaces. So we won't call it a cut or a hike.

On capability, Google cites a Terminal-Bench score that jumps from 18.2 percent to 57 percent. Read that carefully. It's Google comparing Hydrogen to its own previous cheap tier, Gemini Flash, not to any rival. The honest takeaway isn't that Hydrogen beats everyone. It's that the old Flash tier wasn't really usable for agentic terminal work, and this one is. That's a real jump. But it's a within-family comparison, it's self-reported, and we found no independent evaluation we could verify.

There's also a catch in Google's speed claim. Google describes Hydrogen as "finishing tasks 2.5 times faster at its high default setting." Higher thinking settings generally mean more output tokens per task. And output is the expensive half of that price sheet. List price per token isn't the same thing as cost per completed job, and Google hasn't published that number. Our inference is that a flat, published volume price will push rival fast tiers to compete on cost per agentic task rather than benchmark rank. But that contest can't be scored until someone publishes the per-task number.

One more thread. Google is filling in a tier ladder in public. On November 10th, Google made the larger Gemini 4 Carbon generally available at an introductory price. Hydrogen followed on November 12th, filling the volume slot below it with its own published price. Meanwhile, as of our last read, OpenAI's newest image models and Anthropic's office tools still had no public price. So what: Google is pricing each new tier out loud as it ships, and that transparency is itself becoming a competitive signal.

Owning the surface cuts both ways, and the clearest counter-example sits in retail. OpenAI's ChatGPT Wallet lets its shopping agents, which OpenAI calls dots, complete purchases on a user's behalf. When this show covered Wallet yesterday, no source had named which merchants those agents can actually buy from. The first real signal on breadth is a negative one. TechCrunch reports that OpenAI "aims to expand to more retailers." The same report adds: "Amazon, the largest retailer to ban AI agents, is steering users toward its own agent, Alexa." That ban isn't a new move against OpenAI. It's a standing policy aimed at AI agents in general. But it likely means the biggest retailer with such a ban sits outside Wallet's reach. That's the Google story from the other direction. Whoever owns the checkout decides which agent gets to transact there.

Now scale that against OpenAI's broader numbers. CNBC reports that ChatGPT "has more than 1.2 billion weekly active users and nearly 100 million paying users as of the end of Q3." That paying-user figure counts every ChatGPT subscription, not Wallet users. Wallet is limited to US Pro subscribers, a subset of that base. So the headline figure overstates Wallet's addressable audience today. There's still no transaction volume and no merchant list. Our inference on where this goes: if other large retailers follow Amazon, agentic commerce splits into walled gardens. An assistant's checkout reach would then be set by merchant deals, not model quality. So what: Wallet's usefulness to a shopper depends on which stores let it in the door, and the biggest retailer with an agent ban doesn't.

In the enterprise, vendors are taking the opposite route. Instead of asking customers onto their surface, they're moving onto the customer's. Again, Anthropic makes the model behind this show. On November 12th, Anthropic's platform release notes said simply, "We've launched self-hosted Claude Managed Agents." That's genuinely new. The practical promise, on our reading of the label, is that a company can run these agents inside its own environment, next to its own systems, rather than sending those workloads to a shared cloud. That's the setup a regulated buyer wary of shared clouds would want. But the launch is also genuinely thin. There's no disclosed pricing and no named customer. The notes don't say whether self-hosted means the agent runtime, the orchestration layer, or something more. Nothing in that line says Anthropic is putting model weights on-premises. We'd rather say plainly that the scope is undisclosed than guess at it. And there's a tension worth naming. Anthropic's meeting assistant for Google Meet, the Claude Meetings beta, reportedly excludes organizations on zero data retention and on Anthropic's HIPAA-ready configuration. So Anthropic's story for regulated buyers is uneven across its own product line, not uniformly solved.

GitHub is working the same angle from the developer-tools side. Support for GitHub Enterprise Server accounts went generally available in the Copilot app this past week. And Copilot's code-review settings can now be managed through the REST and GraphQL APIs. Call this what it is: incremental plumbing, not a capability leap. It also comes with a real gap. Cloud sessions still aren't available for Enterprise Server accounts. So on-premise customers get a reduced feature set, not parity with cloud users.

Put Anthropic and GitHub together and the pattern holds. When the buyer controls the infrastructure, the agent has to go to the buyer. Right now, that move is arriving without finished pricing or finished features. Here's our inference on who benefits. Running agents inside a company's own environment shifts the operational burden onto its IT team. That means uptime, upgrades, and evaluation. That's exactly the kind of gap systems integrators get paid to fill, even as the platforms absorb more of the workflow itself.

So, three surfaces, three outcomes. Google used the surfaces it owns to put a new agentic model everywhere at once. Amazon's standing ban keeps outside agents away from its checkout. And two enterprise vendors are pushing their agents onto surfaces they don't own, with the fine print still unwritten. Three things to watch from here. First, whether Google or an independent tester publishes a cost-per-task number for Hydrogen, not just a list price. Second, whether any retailer besides Amazon opens, or closes, its checkout to ChatGPT's agents. Third, whether Anthropic says what self-hosted actually includes, and names who's running it.
