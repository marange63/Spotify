Good morning. It's September 2nd, and today's briefing starts with a price that didn't move, and a bill that fell anyway.

Yesterday, Anthropic didn't touch the sticker price on its top model. Claude Fable 5.1 launched on September 1st, generally available across Amazon Web Services, Google Cloud, and Microsoft Azure, at the exact same headline rate as its predecessor: ten dollars per million input tokens, fifty dollars per million output tokens. And yet Anthropic says a highly agentic workload running on Fable 5.1 can cost up to roughly 45% less than it did on Fable 5. Nothing on the rate card changed. So where did the savings come from?

They came from the meter, not the rate. Anthropic cut its cache-read price 75%, down to twenty-five cents per million tokens. Cache reads are what a long agentic session pays, over and over, every time it re-references a big stable chunk of context it already loaded, a codebase, a long document, a running conversation history. Anthropic itself estimates this cuts typical workload cost around 25%, and highly agentic workload cost up to about 45%. Those are Anthropic's own modeled numbers, on workloads Anthropic chose, so treat "up to" as doing real work in that sentence.

Here's the mechanic worth understanding, because it decides who actually captures that savings. A session only reaches the cheap twenty-five-cent read rate after first paying to write to the cache, and that write isn't free either. Anthropic's standard pricing documentation shows a five-minute cache write costs twelve dollars fifty per million tokens, and a one-hour write costs twenty dollars per million, both unchanged from before. So a session only benefits if it re-reads that written context repeatedly, inside a five-minute or one-hour window. A coding agent polling the same repo every few seconds captures the discount many times over. A workflow that revisits a document once an hour or less barely captures it at all. This is a discount on repetition, not on reasoning, and output tokens are still fifty dollars per million, completely untouched. The headline number, in other words, belongs to a narrow class of heavy, fast, repetitive agent workloads, not to AI spending broadly.

That has a sharper implication for the companies building on top of Claude than the press release suggests. This is a margin expansion that depends on architecture, not on volume. A team that already engineered around a long, stable system prompt and a cached repo captures the cut immediately, with no code change and no contract renegotiation. A team that streams fresh context on every turn captures close to nothing. So a single published price change reshuffles relative margins inside the coding-agent category rather than lifting the whole category evenly. That is analysis rather than a disclosed figure, because no vendor here has published a customer-level bill either way.

One useful data point on durability: Anthropic's own pricing page lists that twenty-five-cent cache rate as standard, ongoing pricing, with no expiry attached, in the same format as every other permanent line on the card. That's worth noting because the same page does attach an explicit end date to something else. Sonnet 5 is Anthropic's cheaper mid-tier model, and its rate of two dollars per million input tokens and ten dollars per million output was introductory only, through the end of August. Anthropic has now confirmed that the September 1st increase, to three dollars and fifteen dollars respectively, will not happen. So in two straight days, the effective price of running an Anthropic agent fell twice, by two different mechanisms. A canceled increase on one tier, and an uncatalogued discount buried in the caching mechanics on another. Anthropic hasn't stated a rationale for either move. What that pattern most plausibly signals is that competitive pressure on inference pricing is now real enough that a lab will absorb margin quietly, through the parts of the bill buyers don't typically shop on, rather than compete loudly on the number everyone compares.

Alongside Fable 5.1, Anthropic released Mythos 5.1, the same underlying capability with its safety filters loosened, available only to vetted cybersecurity-defender and life-sciences participants through a trusted-access program. Anthropic says its newest cybersecurity safeguards block 60% fewer false positives, its own measured figure, with no test set disclosed. Worth flagging as a packaging pattern to watch: rather than one product at one access tier, the same capability now ships twice, gated by vetting instead of by price. If other labs copy that pattern, access to the more capable configuration gets allocated by who is already inside an approved program, not by who is willing to pay for it. And you can see what that gate is for in the showcase Anthropic paired with the launch: protein binders it says bind ten times more tightly than the best prior designs. That is precisely the life-sciences work the vetted tier exists to control access to. It is also Anthropic's own chosen example, not reproduced outside the company, so file it as a capability demonstration rather than a product with economics.

Now, that cache-read lever only exists because a text-heavy agent session can reuse the same context repeatedly. Here's a workload that never had that option. OpenAI's Sora API, including Sora 2 and Sora 2 Pro, is scheduled to go dark on September 24th, with account data deleted after that date. That finishes a wind-down that began when Sora's consumer app closed back on April 26th. The reported economics explain why: roughly a million dollars a day in compute cost, against about two-point-one million dollars in total lifetime revenue. Those figures are reported, not disclosed by OpenAI itself, though they are widely corroborated. Take the ratio as directionally sourced, not audited.

The point stands anyway. You cannot cache your way out of rendering new video frames the way you can cache a repeated block of text, so the same discounting lever that just took nearly half the cost out of an agentic text workload had nothing to work with here. For once, the load-bearing number in this brief cuts against the company it describes rather than flattering it. The likely path forward is that standalone generative video stops being a freestanding product with its own budget, and becomes a subsidized feature living inside a larger platform. Google's Veo, Kling, and Seedance inherit the displaced demand. None of them has published its own unit economics either, so the category's viability remains unmeasured, not disproven.

A price cut only matters at the speed a developer can actually act on it, and that speed just went up. GitHub's Copilot in VS Code, in an August 31st changelog, added the ability to switch model providers mid-session. A developer can choose between an Anthropic subscription and a Copilot subscription without ending the active session. The same release lets you continue an agent session started in another application, and introduces a portable "Agent Plugins 1.0" standard for agent customizations that travel with you across that switch. No adoption numbers are disclosed, so we don't know how many developers actually use this yet. And a plugin standard published by one vendor is a proposal, not an ecosystem, until someone else implements it. But the direction matters. If switching providers becomes a toggle rather than a migration, then holding price on the strength of lock-in stops working, and discounting becomes the fastest lever a lab has left to compete with.

So the shape of the week is this. Pricing has migrated off the rate card and into mechanics most buyers don't yet know to negotiate over: cache lifetimes, write costs, expiry dates. Meanwhile the cost of switching providers keeps falling. For an enterprise buyer, that means a rate-card comparison across vendors no longer predicts the bill, and procurement has to start pricing terms it has never had a line for. The one disclosure that would settle whether today's cut is structural or just generous for now is whether that twenty-five-cent cache-read rate ever gets an expiry date attached. Nothing on Anthropic's own pricing page says it will.

That's the briefing for September 2nd. Talk to you tomorrow.
