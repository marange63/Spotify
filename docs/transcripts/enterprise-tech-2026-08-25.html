Cautious Optimism Briefings

Enterprise Technology & Software

Transcript · August 25, 2026

Good morning, it's Tuesday, August 25th, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

Start with what a company just gave away for free. Google has transferred its Agent2Agent protocol, known as A2A, into the Agentic AI Foundation, a Linux Foundation-directed standards body. That places A2A alongside Anthropic's Model Context Protocol under one neutral governance roof. MCP is the spec agents use to talk to tools and data. A2A is the spec agents use to talk to other agents. Putting both under the same foundation means the two halves of agent communication are now jointly governed, rather than split across two companies' roadmaps. The foundation has grown fast. It counts more than 250 members today, up from 49 founding members less than a year ago, with AWS, Microsoft, OpenAI, Anthropic, Cloudflare, Bloomberg and Block in the platinum tier alongside Google.

Here's why that matters more than the announcement suggests. Nobody donates an asset they think they can charge for. When the company that built a piece of the agent stack hands it to a body it doesn't control, that's revealed preference. Google is telling you the agent-to-agent messaging layer isn't defensible, so better to take credit for openness now than lose a standards fight later.

Hold that next to where this briefing was yesterday. Yesterday we covered Stripe agreeing to buy OpenRouter, the layer that decides which model provider gets paid for each enterprise inference call, at a price Bloomberg reported above seven billion dollars and that Stripe has not confirmed. The argument was that when the model becomes a substitutable input, the router becomes the platform. Today the same class of vendor gave the messaging layer away. Those are two halves of one judgment about which layers can be owned and which cannot.

For enterprises building multi-vendor agent fleets, the giveaway is good news in a narrow, specific way. A shared, jointly-governed protocol lowers the odds that an agent-orchestration investment needs a rewrite when the ecosystem consolidates. The tempting analogy is Kubernetes, which standardized container orchestration after early proprietary alternatives faded. Treat that as a hypothesis, not a finding.

Because this is a governance event, not a deployment event. No production-adoption number for A2A came with the announcement, and that absence is the tell, not a footnote. The 250-plus member count and the platinum-tier list come from coverage of the foundation's own announcement, not an independent audit. There is no public breakdown of how many of those members are contributing code versus sitting on a roster. MCP already has a real tooling head start, and nothing here tells you whether A2A closes that gap or simply sits next to a more mature protocol. Donating a spec is cheap, reputationally useful, and costs Google nothing if adoption never arrives. So the honest read is that the plumbing layer is being commoditized on purpose, by mutual agreement among the biggest vendors. Which makes the interesting question: what are those same vendors still willing to pay for?

One answer showed up the week before, at IBM. On August 11th, IBM and Together AI signed a multi-year, 240 million dollar agreement to stand up a dedicated Nvidia HGX B300 cluster on IBM Cloud, with availability expected in the first quarter of 2027. That figure and that date come straight from IBM's own press release. But read the release closely, because it complicates the tidy version of this story. IBM's own wording is that Together AI plans to use the environment to deliver inference services for open-source models, expanding its AI Native Cloud platform for enterprise customers. That describes Together AI as the operating tenant. Together AI is the party that will run the workloads on that cluster and sell them onward to its own customers.

So this isn't IBM buying GPUs for its own account the way a hyperscaler does. It reads more like IBM Cloud winning a nine-figure hosting contract, with a neocloud-native AI company as the anchor tenant. That is still a real proof point, just a different one. IBM Cloud can win infrastructure business on straight commercial terms against the neoclouds, rather than needing to bundle capacity into a consulting engagement to move it. It also changes the risk picture. A committed anchor tenant means the exposure sits with the build-out rather than with an idle asset on IBM's balance sheet. And be clear about what this is: a multi-year commitment is not recognized revenue. IBM has disclosed no revenue contribution and no utilization assumption.

Be precise about the rest of what isn't known. Neither the primary release nor the coverage around it discloses a contract term in years, a GPU count, or a wattage figure, so treat the cluster's physical scale as large and nothing more precise. Separately, IBM has a partnership with OpenAI to embed GPT-5.6 and Codex into its consulting practice, announced two days later with no disclosed contract value attached. That one is a distribution deal, not a capacity one. The through-line across both moves is the same. Whether IBM is the buyer or, more likely here, the landlord, the compute layer is where the money changes hands. Nobody is donating GPUs to a foundation.

That tension only matters if agent fleets are actually scaling fast enough to need either the free protocol or the paid capacity. Salesforce's own platform telemetry, combined with a survey of 4,689 respondents fielded in May, gives a live read on that. Take the source seriously before the number. This is Salesforce reporting on Salesforce's own product, and no comparable third-party dataset exists this cycle to check it against. The anchor figure is deployment time. The average time to stand up a new agent on Agentforce fell from about four days to 1.9 days, a 53 percent reduction. Salesforce's data also shows each agent doing more once it exists, with average skills per agent rising from two to six over the same window. And in retail, businesses that deployed agents on digital-commerce channels saw four times the online sales growth of those that didn't.

Now the caution. Every number there is a mean with no published median, and a handful of heavy Agentforce users can carry an average. The four-times retail claim is a correlation: the retailers sophisticated enough to run agent fleets were probably already growing faster online than their peers. But directionally, the deployment-time figure is the one that carries the argument, and it should worry anyone modeling systems-integrator revenue. When standing up an agent takes two days instead of four, the cost center stops being building the agent. It becomes making dozens of them work together.

That reshapes the integrator's business rather than shrinking it. The first engagement gets shorter and cheaper, which is worse. What replaces it is fleet operations, governance and cost management, which is recurring rather than project-based, and that is the better half of the trade — provided the integrator can staff and price it. The firms with a problem are the ones still selling implementation by the milestone.

It also tells you where vendor differentiation goes next. If agent-to-agent messaging is neutral and free, nobody gets to charge for the message. What they can still charge for is identity — which agent is allowed to act as whom — plus permission scoping, audit trails, and the cost controls that stop a fleet of agents running up an unbounded inference bill. Those are the layers where a customer can still be billed and still be locked in. That is structurally good for independent platforms that can credibly claim neutrality. It is structurally bad for any vendor whose agent strategy assumed a proprietary messaging format would hold customers in place.

Put the week together and the pattern is a line, not a coincidence. The layer everyone agrees to share just got cheaper and more neutral. The layers nobody is giving away — GPU capacity, hosting contracts, the routing decision — keep attracting nine-figure checks. If you want to know where enterprise AI margin will actually live in two years, don't watch the protocol fights. Watch who is still writing checks for the pipes underneath them. What would prove this wrong is either A2A adoption materializing at scale, which nobody has evidence of yet, or a major vendor turning around and shipping a proprietary agent-messaging format anyway, betting that lock-in beats standardization after all.

That's the briefing. Have a good day.
