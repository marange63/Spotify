Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Credit & the Corporate Credit Cycle

Transcript · August 10, 2026

Good morning, it's Monday, August 10th, 2026. This is your private credit briefing.

Here's the thesis. Private credit is becoming the financier of last resort for the AI capex boom at exactly the moment its own existing book is showing its first real cracks.

Start with the tipping point. Apollo's Torsten Slok reports that orders for hyperscaler bonds covered nearly five times the amount offered back in February. By July, that coverage had slipped below two times. Public bond investors are backing away from hyperscaler paper even as the borrowing need explodes. Meanwhile Goldman Sachs reports hyperscaler leverage ratios have doubled, from 0.9 times to 1.8 times, in roughly six months. Morgan Stanley now projects that private credit will supply about 800 billion dollars of data-center financing through 2028. That's the number to hold onto — not the headline trillion-and-a-half figure floating around, which is a funding-gap estimate, not a debt forecast. Total AI-related debt issuance is tracking toward 570 billion dollars globally this year on Morgan Stanley's math, with 489 billion already priced by late July, according to Goldman's Amanda Lynam. Compare that to just 121 billion dollars issued by the five largest hyperscalers in all of last year. And the structural template for how this debt gets packaged is already here: Meta's Hyperion data-center project raised about 27.3 billion dollars of project-level debt through a private securities offering, an off-balance-sheet vehicle that keeps the leverage off Meta's own balance sheet.

We tracked this trend last week at the deal level — CoreWeave's repriced GPU loan, the Proofpoint financing. This is that demand migrating wholesale. The risk isn't disappearing, it's moving into mark-to-model vehicles — BDCs, insurance balance sheets, SPVs — precisely as the leverage underneath it doubles. So the losses from any AI-capex overbuild, if they come, get marked slowly and privately instead of repriced daily in public bond markets. That's a genuine delay in price discovery, not a resolved risk. And note the linkage doesn't run only one way. Public and private tranches sit in the same capital structures, so if hyperscaler leverage keeps climbing, it can eventually bleed back into investment-grade bond spreads even on the deals private credit absorbs. The two markets are not as walled off as the migration story implies.

That risk is arriving just as the book already on the shelf is showing strain. Blue Owl's business development company, ticker OBDC, cut its base dividend from 37 cents to 31 cents per share, as adjusted net investment income fell to 31 cents from 36 cents a year earlier. We flagged this exact setup a few days ago: NAV erosion against a flat dividend is how you get to a payout cut, and this is that prediction landing. Underneath it, a single name did real damage — OBDC's Loparex Group second-lien loans, roughly 130.6 million dollars of them, got marked down to five cents on the dollar this quarter, from sixty-three cents the quarter before. That's a fifty-eight-point collapse in three months on paper that was performing until it wasn't. To be fair, OBDC's non-accruals moved in opposite directions depending on how you measure them — up to 2.8% of the portfolio at cost, but down to 0.8% at fair value — so this isn't a clean deterioration story, it's a lumpy one. Ares Capital, meanwhile, is pricing new senior loan commitments 20 basis points wider than last quarter with upfront fees up 50 basis points, which management attributes to thinner wealth-channel competition. Take that explanation as Ares's own framing, not an independently verified market statistic. So what: this is the first confirmed payout crack in the cycle, not proof of a sector-wide wave, but it's exactly the kind of event the "benign because untested" bulls need to explain away.

Now the mechanism that could make the remaining book worse without a single default. Direct lenders and the broadly syndicated loan market are refinancing each other's deals at a record clip. PitchBook LCD counted 34.1 billion dollars of direct-lender loans refinanced into the syndicated market in 2025, the highest since LCD started tracking it in 2022 and up 18% from 2024. Direct lenders took the other side too, refinancing 36.9 billion dollars of syndicated loans over the same period, also a record. By deal count the two markets are roughly tied, each capturing about 40 borrowers from the other last year. I tried to run this down further — whether banks are systematically winning back the highest-quality, cheapest-to-refinance credits — and couldn't confirm it. A widely cited figure claiming private-credit borrowers refinance into bank markets three times more often than the reverse traces back to a paywalled Bloomberg piece I couldn't verify verbatim, so treat that ratio as directionally plausible, not confirmed. What is confirmed is the volume: both channels are competing hard for the same names.

That competition has a price of its own, before any credit even sours. When banks and direct lenders bid against each other for the same borrower, the all-in yield compresses on both sides. And the yield premium over public markets is the entire pitch private credit makes to allocators for locking up their capital and accepting mark-to-model valuations. Erode that premium and you're asking investors to take illiquidity and opacity for less and less extra return. So there are two distinct threats in this refinancing war. First, the return edge narrows for everyone. Second, if banks really are skimming the best credits, average quality left sitting in BDC portfolios can quietly deteriorate even while headline non-accrual rates stay flat. That's a selection-effect risk, distinct from an actual cycle turn, and it's a different failure mode than the market is watching for.

Last, the live test case for what happens when a private credit actually gets tested. First Brands, the auto-parts company whose bankruptcy exposed roughly 2.3 billion dollars in fabricated receivables, has moved toward a settlement in which most entities liquidate under Chapter 7 while one entity pursues a litigation trust for creditors. Senior secured obligations across the estate total about 7.5 billion dollars, and the company's 1.1 billion dollar rescue loan is quoted near 16 cents on the dollar. A litigation consultant estimates roughly 2 billion dollars might eventually come back through lawsuits, over the next two and a half years — an estimate, not a booked recovery, and the plan itself isn't confirmed by the court yet. So what: when a private credit finally gets tested by reality instead of a model, the mark doesn't drift, it falls off a cliff.

Pull it together and the credit cycle right now looks like this: new risk flowing in at the top through AI-capex financing, quality potentially leaking out the side through bank refinancing competition, and the one name that actually got tested this cycle printing pennies on the dollar. None of it has been through a real downturn yet. Watch the rest of BDC earnings season for more dividend cuts and non-accrual migration, watch whether AI-debt terms hold as issuance keeps climbing, and watch the First Brands docket for whether that settlement actually gets confirmed.
