Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Equity & the Deal Economy

Transcript · September 6, 2026

Good morning, it's Sunday, September 6th. This is your private equity briefing.

For most of this week we've argued that private-equity assets only get priced when whoever controls the sale decides to sell. The exit, on that view, is the one moment of real price discovery in this asset class. This week the pattern broke, and it broke on the buy side, in London.

Start with the aggregate, because that's the part that's new. Bloomberg's compiled data show M&A targeting UK companies is up more than two hundred fifty percent this year, to roughly one hundred fifty billion dollars. Be precise about what the hundred-fifty-billion figure is. It counts all buyers, strategics included. It is not a sponsor-only or take-private-only number. But the sponsor share of it is unmistakable in a single week. Veritas Capital beat CVC Capital Partners to buy heat-treatment specialist Bodycote, raising its offer to nine hundred forty pence a share. That's a thirty-four-and-a-half percent premium to where Bodycote traded before takeover speculation started in May, and about one point eight five billion pounds including debt. We covered that auction on its own on Friday. In the same window, buyout group Epiris offered roughly one billion pounds for telecoms services provider Gamma Communications, and Norway's DNO agreed to buy Capricorn Energy for about two hundred ninety two million pounds. One auction became a tape.

Here's why that matters more than any of the headline numbers. A contested bid, with two sponsors chasing the same target, is the one transaction type in private equity where an outside party actually sets the price. And it happens on the way in, not on the way out. That is genuine competitive tension, which the exit side of this business has been starved of for years.

But be careful what the premium tells you. The premium is thirty-four and a half percent against the pre-speculation May price. Against the twelve-month average it is forty-two and a half percent. That gap tells you the stock had already partly re-rated before Veritas's winning bid landed. The premium is measured against a moving target, not a clean baseline. And the offer document itself gives you no entry multiple. It discloses an equity value and an enterprise value, but never pairs either against an EBITDA figure, so you cannot tell whether entry discipline is holding. The informative fact here isn't the size of the premium. It's that a second sponsor was willing to be in the room at all. Read that as a financing-conditions signal for the sub-two-billion-pound tier, and nothing more.

Two things to flag before we move on. First, this is signed, not closed. Bodycote isn't expected to complete until the first quarter of 2027, so none of it turns into cash for any limited partner this year. Second, on CVC. Under UK takeover rules CVC faced a put-up-or-shut-up deadline of five p.m. on September 2, to announce a firm offer, confirm it would not bid, or secure a Takeover Panel extension. That deadline has passed without public confirmation either way, so don't treat "still evaluating" as current status.

Now the turn, because a take-private is a purchase, not an exit. Every one of these deals moves a company off a public register and onto a fund's books. S&P Global counted one thousand five hundred four private equity and venture capital exits globally in the first half of this year, down six percent from one thousand six hundred one a year earlier. And when exit value did show up in 2025, seventy-eight percent of it sat in a handful of mega-exits, which means the middle of the market barely moved. So the buying wave doesn't drain the backlog. It adds to it.

There's a second-order cost sitting underneath that. Every sterling take-private shrinks the London listed universe a little further. That universe is the set of public comparables against which private marks get sanity-checked. The deal wave is quietly removing the yardstick the marks are measured with.

And the backlog number is itself a mess. McKinsey, cited secondhand through S&P Global rather than read from the report, puts unsold private-equity-backed companies at roughly sixteen thousand. This show used a figure closer to thirty-two thousand on Friday, sourced differently. That is not a rounding difference. That is a factor-of-two disagreement on the single number the entire distribution debate rests on. The honest read is that the industry does not know how big its own unsold inventory is. Whatever the true number, it is large and it is not shrinking.

That inventory has to clear somewhere, and increasingly it clears into vehicles the manager itself controls. GP-led continuation vehicle volume hit a record one hundred eight billion dollars in 2025, more than double the prior year, and continuation vehicles are now estimated to account for at least a fifth of private-market distributions this year. Sit with what that implies. A growing share of the cash going back to limited partners comes from a transaction where the same manager sits on both sides and effectively sets the exit price. That is liquidity. It is not independent price discovery.

Follow the money forward, because the managers are already guiding this outcome themselves. These are second-quarter prints, roughly six weeks old, so use them for the guidance rather than as news. Blackstone told investors that realizations, meaning cash actually coming back to limited partners, are expected to slow sequentially in the third quarter, before improving later this year and into next. That is the seller of the exit trade forecasting a slower quarter against its own book, which makes it more credible, not less. Carlyle's in-carry ratio, the share of performance-fee-eligible assets currently generating carried interest, fell to seventy-nine percent from eighty-four percent a year earlier, which Carlyle attributed to recent realizations working through the system. Not every manager agrees. TPG posted roughly fourteen billion dollars of realizations year to date, up twenty-eight percent on the first half of last year. So take Blackstone's guide as one firm's signal, not an industry verdict.

But note what sits behind that guide. Blackstone alone carries two hundred twenty-eight billion dollars of dry powder. That is capital ready to fund exactly the kind of bids we just described in London, even while the firm's own cash back to investors is guided lower. The fee-generating machine and the distribution machine are now running on two different clocks.

One brief item closes the loop on scale. Silver Lake is reportedly in talks to take Workday private, a company with a market value around forty-three billion dollars. Nothing is confirmed, neither party has commented, and the reporting itself says a deal may never happen. Note too that forty-three billion is a market value, not an agreed price. But it is the real test of this cycle's appetite. The UK deals clear the bar because financing at a couple of billion pounds works. Whether that same bidding confidence stretches to a forty-billion-dollar enterprise-software target is a different question entirely, and Workday is the live experiment.

So where does the cycle actually stand? The bid is real. Sponsors are competing and paying up for scarce public targets, and that is genuine price discovery, something this business has lacked. The exit is not real, at least not yet. Exit counts are falling, the backlog is large and contested even in its own measurement, and the largest manager in the industry is telling you itself to expect a slower third quarter. Buying and selling are not the same market right now, and conflating them is the mistake to avoid. Watch three things from here. Whether CVC's post-deadline position surfaces publicly. Whether the Epiris bid for Gamma Communications firms into a recommended offer. And how third-quarter realizations print against Blackstone's own guidance in late October.

That's the briefing. Talk to you next time.
