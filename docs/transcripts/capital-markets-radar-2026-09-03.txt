Good morning, it's Thursday, September 3rd, and this is Capital Markets Radar.

The first hard data since Jackson Hole landed yesterday, and it landed soft. Kevin Warsh's hawkish pivot two weeks ago repriced the Fed from cuts to a live hike. He built that case on inflation, not labor: PCE running at 3.7% over twelve months, against a hotter 4.1% six-month pace, with breadth accelerating rather than fading. What the speech never had was a labor-market data point to test it against. August ADP private payrolls supplied one. The print was plus 38,000, below the roughly 48,000 economists expected, and down from a July figure that was itself revised up to 46,000. That revision matters. The slowdown is not an artifact of a since-corrected low base. It is real, and ADP calls it the slowest pace of hiring since January.

This is the branch we flagged on September 1st. That day, this program named the scenario nobody was being paid to hold: an energy-led inflation impulse and a softening labor market arriving at the same time. We said then we had no read on the labor side. Now we have the first piece of it, and it cut against the hawkish case. One nuance from the internals keeps that case alive, though. Job-stayer pay growth held steady, at 3% on base pay and 4.4% gross, roughly in line with recent months. So this looks like an employer pullback in hiring volume, not a collapse in wages. The softening shows up in who gets hired, not in what they're paid. That complicates Warsh's timing without touching his breadth argument.

Anchor that against rates. The ten-year hit 4.818% intraday yesterday, its highest print since November 2023, before easing to 4.79% by the close. The two-year had jumped 11 basis points to 4.34% on the day of the speech itself. That was the biggest Jackson Hole speech-day move in at least twenty years. Hike odds on CME's FedWatch roughly doubled, from about 35% before Warsh spoke to the high 50s and above since. Treat that as a range, not a number. It is market pricing of a genuinely undecided outcome, and it moves by the hour. So here is the tension. The hawkish case was built on tone and on oil, and the first real data print since the speech pushed the other way. But be careful. ADP is not payrolls. One 38,000 reading sits inside the error bars of a noisy series, and the number that actually counts arrives tomorrow, with August nonfarm payrolls. A soft ADP does not settle the hike question either. Warsh's stated case rests on inflation breadth, not labor slack. So a weak jobs number does not obviously bind him.

If that branch is now live, the place it should show up first is credit. This program put that exact test on the record twice, on August 29th and again this week: if bond volatility woke up, high yield would widen first. We now have a score, and we lost the call. The ICE BofA US High Yield index option-adjusted spread stood at 2.65% on September 1st. That is a fresh, post-Warsh print, and it is no wider than where spreads sat before the speech. Credit did not widen into the sharpest hawkish repricing of the year. A program that puts tests on the record has to report the ones it fails, and this one failed.

The VIX tells the same story from a different market. It closed at 16.34 yesterday, up from a 2026 low of 14.13 late last week, but nowhere near a level that reflects a coin-flip Fed decision two weeks out. Give the fair counter before drawing the inference. 62% of the ICE BofA Global High Yield index is now rated BB, up from just 39% in 2007. Part of this tightness is compositional. A materially better-rated index earns a tighter spread on its own. But that does not fully close the gap. So here is the point that survives. Two independent markets for tail protection are both priced for calm into a decision that is genuinely live. If it breaks the wrong way, the first move wider is a mechanical de-rating of a high-quality index, not a default cycle. That makes it faster and more indiscriminate than the fundamentals would justify.

Nothing is paying for that tail because the inflation leg of this story still looks like a supply shock, not demand. So check the supply. Brent crude is up roughly 20% over the past month, on renewed US-Iran hostilities around a reported strike late last week. Copper printed $6.52 a pound yesterday. It was pushed up by a sulfuric-acid shortage tied to the same Middle East escalation, because acid refiners need it to process copper concentrate. Front-loaded buying ahead of possible US tariffs on unprocessed copper added to the squeeze. So this is an AI-buildout input-cost story as much as an energy story. The same grid and data-center capex plans the market has been pricing all week now face a tighter, pricier copper supply chain, layered on top of a rising discount rate.

There is one falsifier to name rather than resolve. Unconfirmed reporting suggests Brent may be easing toward the mid-$90s as Strait of Hormuz transit resumes. We cannot confirm that print, so treat it as a developing report, not a fact. But if it holds, and the ten-year stays near its highs anyway, that tells you the yield move was about policy, not energy. This program named that exact test on September 1st, and it may be triggering right now. We are watching our own falsifier, not quietly dropping it.

One complex has been asked this stagflation question repeatedly this week, and it keeps giving the same answer. Crypto. US spot Bitcoin ETFs pulled in $1.92 billion in the week ending August 21st, their largest weekly haul since early October. A combined $1.75 billion followed across bitcoin and ether funds the week after. Bitcoin itself sat near $77,100 yesterday. A single-day ETF outflow earlier in the week had been read here as a crack in that structural bid. The weekly totals say that read was wrong. Daily-resolution flow data is simply the wrong lens for judging a structural bid, and that is the transferable lesson.

But name the caution too. MicroStrategy ended a ten-week buying pause, adding 4,603 coins at an average price of $80,318, funded by an equity raise rather than debt. That took its holdings to 845,050 bitcoin. The newest tranche was bought above yesterday's spot price, so the marginal corporate buyer is already underwater on it. And because it was funded with equity, that bid expands when the stock is strong and shrinks exactly when coins get cheap. That makes it a pro-cyclical absorber, not a stabilizer, in a real drawdown. The flow currently soaking up macro selling is the flow least likely to show up when it is actually needed.

Gold makes the same point about reading one session too hard. Yesterday this program reported gold at a two-week low and called the cross-asset dissent collapsed. This morning's preliminary reading has it back near $4,428 an ounce, up about 0.9% on the day. That capitulation lasted about a session. But one green day does not rebuild a de-dollarization thesis, and the durable fact is still the drawdown from January's record of $5,595. Treat the bounce as noise for now.

The calendar decides this, not the narrative. Payrolls tomorrow, PPI on the 10th, CPI on the 11th, the Fed decision on the 16th. None of it is known yet. If tomorrow's payroll print comes in firm, the stagflation branch closes, the hawkish case gets its confirmation, and most of what you just heard needs rewriting by Friday.
