Good morning, it's August 11th, 2026, and this is your private credit briefing.

All week this show has carried one open question: are business development company marks lagging reality, or are they conservative first-lien marking that just looks scary next to public spreads? This week gave us the first real answer, and it came from an actual sale, not a model.

BlackRock's TCP Capital, a publicly traded BDC known as TCPC, sold ninety-five percent of its interest in a continuation vehicle holding roughly five hundred twenty-three million dollars of loans to seventy-eight portfolio companies, to funds managed by Pantheon. That's about forty-eight percent of TCPC's pre-transaction debt portfolio, and it priced at ninety-five percent of the fund's own December 31st fair value. TCPC retained a direct interest in most of those same companies too — on average it only transferred about two-thirds of each position, and it kept a five percent stake in the vehicle itself, with its own advisor still managing it, unpaid. So this wasn't TCPC exiting the book. It was TCPC bringing in outside capital to delever while staying exposed to the same names.

The headline number is the net asset value hit: TCPC expects NAV to fall about ten-point-four percent, or sixty-eight cents a share, off the June 30th NAV of six-fifty-eight. Here's the caveat that matters and that I won't paper over: that ten-point-four percent NAV cut is measured off the June 30th NAV, while the sale price is fixed at ninety-five percent of the December 31st fair value. Nobody has disclosed what the pool was actually marked at in June versus December. So we cannot cleanly say the market just proved TCPC's book was overstated by ten-point-four percent — we can say a sponsor accepted a real cash discount to move distressed assets off its balance sheet, and the NAV math that resulted was a double-digit cut. That is directionally the same story — marks eroding when tested — just not a laboratory-clean number.

What this transaction does buy TCPC is breathing room. Leverage, which chairman Phil Tseng said had "stayed north of one-point-four times in recent quarters" and "really inhibited our ability to reposition the portfolio," is expected to fall to roughly zero-point-four times, below zero-point-three after an announced paydown. This is TCPC's third markdown of 2026 — book value fell nineteen percent in January and another five percent in May — and it comes while both the Manhattan U.S. Attorney's office and the SEC are examining how the fund sets its valuations. The stock actually jumped on the news, its largest intraday gain since 2024, but shares remain down almost thirty percent this year. That's a deleveraging relief rally, not vindication of the book. And for context on the book quality behind all of it: at June 30th, before this sale closed, TCPC's non-accruals sat at seven-point-four percent of the portfolio at cost, though only one-point-six percent at fair value, since those loans are already written down. Deleveraging is not the same as a cleaned-up portfolio.

Now here's why TCPC isn't a one-off. The same week, a second mega-manager stepped in to prop up its own listed vehicle. FS KKR Capital, KKR's flagship BDC, reported a GAAP loss of thirteen cents a share, with net asset value down two-point-eight percent to eighteen-thirty, driven by marks against specific names including PRG, ATX, and Wittur. Non-accruals did improve sequentially, to seven-point-one percent of the portfolio at cost from eight-point-one percent — but worth flagging that a sequential improvement like that can reflect restructuring or amend-and-extend treatment rather than genuine healing, and the numbers alone don't tell us which. The portfolio is also shrinking: one-point-three billion dollars of sales and repayments against only five hundred ninety million of new originations. And in the same quarter, a KKR subsidiary completed a hundred fifty million dollar tender offer for FSK shares, while FSK issued a hundred fifty million dollars of convertible preferred stock to that same KKR subsidiary. We don't know the pricing terms, so I won't call it distressed financing. But the fact of it — a sponsor injecting its own balance sheet capital into its listed BDC — is the signal. Two mega-managers, same week, both moving capital to defend vehicles that carry their own name. That's the story shifting from eroding marks to active intervention.

There's a second pressure building underneath both of these, and it's about who's even left in the direct-lending book. Bloomberg's Credit Weekly reported that Wall Street banks refinanced nine deals worth four-and-a-half billion dollars out of the private loan market in the second quarter, against only three deals worth one-point-three billion moving the other direction, from syndicated loans into direct lending. Named examples: insurance underwriter Fidelis Partnership working with Morgan Stanley to refinance about two billion dollars of direct loans, and KKR-backed Accuris pricing a seven-hundred-million-dollar syndicated loan to take out its direct lenders. That's a real reversal of the flow that defined the 2021-to-2024 direct-lending land grab, and the mechanism to watch is adverse selection: the borrowers with access to the syndicated market are, by definition, the higher-quality credits, and they're the ones leaving first. What stays behind in direct lenders' books is disproportionately the names that couldn't get a bank deal — which means flat headline non-accruals across the sector may be masking a quietly worsening average credit quality, not confirming a benign cycle. One caveat on the numbers: they come from a secondary report of Bloomberg's Credit Weekly, and there's no prior-quarter comparison, so read this as a Q2 data point consistent with a reversal, not a confirmed inflection.

One more data point on where capital is actually rotating. Blue Owl's credit assets under management slipped sequentially, to a hundred fifty-eight-point-one billion dollars from a hundred fifty-nine-point-two billion at the end of the first quarter, and fresh capital raised fell to seven-point-six billion dollars from twelve-point-one billion a year earlier. At the same time, Blue Owl is targeting a first close later this year for its next digital-infrastructure flagship fund, with a ten-billion-dollar fundraising goal aimed squarely at data-center financing. Put simply, generalist corporate direct lending is contracting at the margin while manager and investor appetite pivots hard toward the AI capex buildout — the same rotation this show has tracked from the top down in the syndicated and structured markets, now visible from the bottom up inside a direct lender's own AUM mix.

So where does the cycle actually stand? Stress that was visible in eroding marks a week ago is now visible in sponsors spending their own capital to defend listed vehicles, and in one case, in a real transaction that put an actual price — not a model — on a distressed book. None of this is a downturn yet; non-accruals at the largest managers remain in the low single digits to high single digits depending on how you measure them, and fundraising into the asset class hasn't stopped. The unresolved risk is valuation opacity: nobody outside these firms can yet see whether TCPC's ninety-five-cent sale price was a one-off distressed discount or the first data point in a repricing that other levered BDCs will be forced to match. Watch the rest of BDC earnings season for more sponsor support or dividend cuts, watch whether the bank-refinancing reversal holds into the third quarter, and watch for any outcome from the SEC and U.S. Attorney's inquiry into TCPC's valuations. That's your near-term scorecard.
