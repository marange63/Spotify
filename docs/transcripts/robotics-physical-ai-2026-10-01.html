Cautious Optimism Briefings

Robotics & Physical AI

Transcript · October 1, 2026

Good morning. It's October 1st, and here's where embodied AI actually stands.

Two numbers define this week. A new count puts global humanoid shipments at nearly twenty-five thousand units in six months, with almost four in five of them Chinese. In the same week, the secondary market marked a robot-brain company, Skild AI, at nearly seventeen billion dollars. Bodies are being counted in China. Brains are being priced in the West. Neither one, as you'll hear, is proof of a paying customer.

Start with the count. Research firm IDC says China shipped more than nineteen thousand humanoid robots in the first half of 2026. That's seventy-seven point nine percent of global shipments. Global volume reached nearly twenty-five thousand units, up four hundred thirty-two percent year over year. And IDC has raised its 2030 forecast to more than seven hundred fifty thousand units worldwide. Four days ago this desk asked whether the IFR's first humanoid tally would give us an independent yardstick for vendor claims. Now there's a second counter, and that matters precisely because it isn't a vendor's own number.

But run our own arithmetic underneath it, and the picture gets less triumphant. A four hundred thirty-two percent jump implies a first-half 2025 base of only around forty-seven hundred units. That's tiny-base growth, not a mature market compounding. Annualize the current half-year and you get roughly fifty thousand units a year. To hit IDC's seven-hundred-fifty-thousand forecast by 2030, the industry needs something like a fifteen-fold increase in four years. That's not impossible. But it's a forecast from a firm whose number just went up, not an order book.

There's also a sourcing caveat worth stating plainly. This coverage is secondary, relayed by TechNode from IDC's report. We have not seen IDC's primary release or its definition of a shipment. That gap matters. Earlier this month, a different source on this beat put Chinese vendors behind more than ninety-seven percent of first-half humanoid shipments. IDC now says seventy-eight. The yardsticks are multiplying faster than the definitions are being disclosed.

And a shipment is not a deployment. A deployment, in turn, is not a paying customer. All week this desk has found China's humanoid demand weighted toward research institutes, education, and data-collection buyers. Commercial end users have been the minority in everything we've seen. There is a fair competing reading. China's share may reflect a genuine cost and supply-chain advantage, not just subsidized demand. The count alone can't separate the two. Either way, the listing test we covered yesterday points the same direction. Beijing's securities regulator is reportedly using informal window guidance to demand real revenue and owned core technology before humanoid makers can list. That test asks for exactly the revenue this shipment count can't show.

One more wrinkle. If seventy-eight percent of volume is Chinese, the rest of the world shipped only a few thousand units in a half-year. That raises the question of whether captive builds, like Tesla's Optimus program, sit inside the count at all. Supply-chain reporting from early September claims Tesla ordered parts for roughly fifteen thousand Optimus units this year. The same reporting has the ramp heading toward about twenty-five hundred a week by year-end, beyond the late-September checkpoint we've flagged before. Tesla has not confirmed any of this. Parts ordered are not robots built, and robots sent to Tesla's internal training program are not sales. But if those rates are real and counted as shipments, the non-China share in IDC's next count should jump. Tesla's October earnings call is the first chance to get a primary number instead of a trade-press guess.

So the count tells you who is building bodies. It does not tell you who is paying to use them.

If China owns the body count, follow the Western money. It isn't going to bodies. It's going to brains. For a week this desk has said nothing at the model layer cleared our bar. Today the model layer moves, but on price, not on proof.

Start with Skild AI. Its January Series C was one point four billion dollars, led by SoftBank, at a valuation of more than fourteen billion. As of September 28th, an investor-tracking site puts Skild's Forge price at sixteen point nine billion dollars. Be clear about what that is. A Forge price reflects secondary-market trading. It's sentiment, not a priced new round.

The same aggregator reports that Nvidia and Samsung put a combined thirty-five million dollars into Skild in the last days of September. Treat that framing with care. TechCrunch's account of the January round already names Nvidia as an investor, alongside Macquarie Group and 1789 Capital. So Nvidia's stake in Skild is eight months old, not new. Samsung's participation hasn't surfaced in any tier-one account. Treat Nvidia as a known Skild investor, and Samsung as unconfirmed.

Physical Intelligence, the robot foundation-model lab, is the purest case of price running ahead of product. Back in March, TechCrunch reported, following Bloomberg, that the company was in talks to raise about one billion dollars at a valuation exceeding eleven billion. That would more than double the five point six billion dollar mark it had set just four months before. The company has no commercial product and no revenue. Co-founder Lachy Groom told TechCrunch on the record that it has no timeline for commercialization at all. Our sources don't say whether that round closed, or at what price.

Set those marks against the Chinese listing test, which wants revenue alongside owned brain technology. Physical Intelligence has the brain and none of the revenue, yet it carries one of the richest valuations in the sector. There's a bull case worth stating fairly. A cross-embodiment robot brain could rationally be worth more than any single body maker. That holds if the model layer captures the margin while hardware commoditizes under Chinese volume. Our inference from that scenario is uncomfortable for Western humanoid integrators. They'd be squeezed between cheaper Chinese bodies below and richly valued brain vendors above. The bear case is just as plain. These marks are running well ahead of any disclosed deployment. If they correct, pre-revenue brain labs get hit first.

So the richest marks in physical AI sit on the layer with the least revenue.

Where, then, is anyone actually paying at scale today? Not in humanoids. It's in autonomous driving. This isn't this week's news, but it's the right benchmark. As of early September, Waymo was delivering more than five hundred thousand paid rides per week across fourteen U.S. cities. Its fleet has grown past four thousand vehicles. That figure is relayed by TechCrunch, and it says nothing about per-ride margins. But it corrects something this desk said yesterday. We called a defense budget the one unambiguous paying customer in physical AI. It isn't. Half a million paid rides, repeated every week, is a recurring paying run-rate. A contract ceiling is only a maximum. China's Pony AI is chasing the same prize internationally. Its stated goal is more than thirty-five hundred robotaxis across twenty-plus cities by year-end, a target rather than an achieved fleet. The pattern holds either way. The physical-AI business with real paid volume today is a narrow, heavily engineered autonomy stack, not a general-purpose robot brain.

Pull the threads together. What's genuinely inflecting is Chinese humanoid shipment volume, and paid robotaxi rides at real scale. What's still unproven is paid humanoid demand at any volume. Also unproven is whether robot-brain valuations track anything but capital enthusiasm. There are four catalysts to watch. First, Tesla's October earnings call, which could finally give us a primary Optimus number. Second, whether any robot-brain lab prices a primary round, rather than a secondary trade, at these levels. Third, IDC's next half-year count, and whether it arrives with a methodology. And fourth, whether Beijing's listing criteria ever become a published rule instead of reported guidance.

That's the briefing. Talk tomorrow.
