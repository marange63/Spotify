Cautious Optimism Briefings

Strategic Power, Energy, and Institutional Change

Transcript · August 11, 2026

Good morning. It's Tuesday, August 11th, and this is your strategic power briefing.

We start in the Persian Gulf, where the Strait of Hormuz is now in its 163rd day of de facto closure. IMF PortWatch's most recent count, taken August 2nd, found just two vessels transiting a route that normally carries seventy-three ships a day. Reuters' more current read puts it at six vessels — still a rounding error against that seventy-three baseline. War-risk insurance for a single VLCC passage has climbed to roughly ten million dollars, about thirty times pre-crisis levels. That is the price the market is charging simply to attempt the transit, regardless of whether anything actually gets hit.

On Sunday, something did. Houthi forces struck Saudi Aramco's Jazan refinery on the Red Sea with a drone — their spokesman called the strike "precise" — and it's tempting to read that as a single, contained incident, since Aramco's chief, Amin Nasser, says the attacks have had "no material operational or financial impact," beyond "some production interruptions." But follow-up reporting complicates that framing considerably. Jazan, a four-hundred-thousand-barrel-a-day refinery, was already shut down on July 27th after an earlier Houthi missile-and-drone strike — two weeks before Sunday's attack made headlines. Sunday's strike reportedly landed just days before a planned restart, extending an outage that was already underway rather than opening a fresh, one-day incident. So the honest picture is closer to three weeks of lost Jazan throughput, not a single Sunday event that Aramco shrugged off. Nobody has published a confirmed barrels-lost figure — that's still missing — but the capacity at risk, four hundred thousand barrels a day, is now confirmed, and it's product that normally serves Indian Ocean and African buyers.

Layer that onto Riyadh's other problem: the Red Sea was supposed to be Saudi Arabia's hedge, the reroute around a closed Hormuz. Now it's the route under direct attack too. That's two chokepoints compromised at once, and it's why crude has broken sharply higher — reporting puts Brent somewhere in the high eighties to just under ninety dollars a barrel, up in the mid-to-high single digits day over day, though exact prints vary by source and shouldn't be taken to the decimal.

On the diplomatic track, treat the "reopening" talk as theater, not relief. Iran and Oman have discussed a sixty-day arrangement letting vessels use a route near Iranian waters. But Iran's own deputy foreign minister, Kazem Gharibabadi, has cautioned that this arrangement would not by itself reopen the strait — Tehran wants the US to meet further conditions first. So this is a war-risk-premium and route-fragility story, not a confirmed-volumes story, and it's not a story that's ending soon. The so-what: sustained elevated crude, even without further escalation, is a fresh cost impulse landing right as the Fed weighs a September rate cut — cheaper money arriving alongside pricier energy.

There's a longer-tail effect worth banking too. Two chokepoints under fire at once pushes Asian and US buyers to keep diversifying away from Gulf barrels toward US LNG and crude, which quietly reinforces the dollar's grip on energy settlement. And a closure grinding into its sixth month is exactly the kind of slow, structural stress that tends to keep a bid under gold and Bitcoin as geopolitical hedges. Neither is a headline today, but both are the durable read underneath the price spike.

That same logic — geography and adversaries delivering leverage in real time — shows up one input over, in rare earths. The neodymium-praseodymium benchmark, the alloy behind every high-performance magnet in EVs, wind turbines, and defense systems, jumped to roughly one hundred thirty-three dollars a kilogram as of July 1st, up twenty-one percent from June's benchmark of about one hundred ten dollars, a new high for the year. Chinese domestic pricing from the China Rare Earth Industry Association corroborates the move, in the same range. Notably, nobody had to announce a new export control to get this. But be careful with the causal story: the move could reflect Western restocking or speculative positioning as much as deliberate Chinese supply discipline, and the reporting doesn't cleanly separate them. What we can say is that the existing licensing regime plus a harder enforcement posture appears to be doing the work without a fresh policy headline — leverage exercised through signaling.

Here's the wrinkle worth flagging: this price is now sitting above the one hundred ten dollar floor that MP Materials and the US government set contractually just over a year ago, backed by federal loans and Department of Energy grants. With the market price clearing that floor on its own, the floor becomes moot for now — Western magnet economics are working without the subsidy having to bite. That's a genuine bright spot. The flip side is that higher magnet input costs are a near-term margin headwind for EV makers, wind-turbine builders, and defense primes that depend on those same magnets for guidance systems and motors.

So put those two stories side by side: a kinetic shock closing physical chokepoints in the Gulf, and a tightening rare-earth market, both delivering real leverage this week without a single new law needing to pass anywhere. Washington's answer, by contrast, is still mostly announced dollars. On August 6th, the administration signed a fifteen percent tariff on polysilicon and its derivatives — the solar-panel precursor material — citing that the US share of global polysilicon production capacity has collapsed from fifty percent in 2005 to under two percent by 2024. That remedy doesn't take effect for one hundred twenty days, which hands importers a four-month window to front-load shipments before it bites, and it raises the cost of solar inputs at precisely the moment data-center power demand argues for building more generation, not less.

Two days later, on August 8th, the administration rolled out roughly two billion dollars in critical-minerals mining investments plus a further one hundred eighty million for workforce development, and the Export-Import Bank separately announced fifty-eight million dollars across three rare-earths deals. President Trump framed it plainly: the goal is to make sure America is "never again reliant on hostile foreign nations for the resources our country needs," and to reclaim the country's "rightful place as the minerals superpower." Fair ambition, but scale the numbers honestly — this is billions in deal-by-deal capital against a Chinese rare-earth and magnet supply chain built over two decades of state-directed investment. A hundred-twenty-day tariff runway and a two-billion-dollar mining round are announcements with real dollars attached, not yet operating mines, fabs, or refining capacity.

So here's where the ledger sits tonight. In the Gulf and in rare earths, adversaries and geography are delivering strategic leverage in real time — a drone strike that's kept a major refinery down for the better part of three weeks, and a rare-earth price that jumped a fifth in a month without anyone announcing anything new. Washington's counter-move, for now, remains mostly on paper: tariffs that don't bite for four months, and mining dollars that still have to become operating capacity before the next round of Chinese supply pressure arrives. Watch whether that gap closes, or widens.

That's your briefing for August 11th. Stay sharp out there.
