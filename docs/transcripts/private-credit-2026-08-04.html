Good morning, it's Tuesday, August 4th, 2026. Let's look at the credit machine from the bottom up.

Second-quarter earnings season for the direct lenders is landing, and it's telling two different stories depending on where you sit in the capital structure. At the manager level, the fee engine has never run hotter. KKR reported fee-related earnings per share of one dollar thirty-two, up thirty-four percent year over year, on management fees of one point two billion dollars, up twenty-six percent. Last-twelve-month fundraising hit a record one hundred thirty-three billion dollars, and KKR's credit assets alone have gone from eighty billion to three hundred billion since the Global Atlantic acquisition. Blue Owl told a similar story: seventy-five percent of the equity capital it raised over the past year came from strategies outside core direct lending, and it's now sitting on thirty-one billion dollars of committed capital that isn't paying fees yet — worth roughly three hundred eighty million dollars in future annual management fees once it turns on. That's a fee base compounding almost independent of what any single loan is doing.

Now walk down to the actual loan books, and the picture softens. Ares Capital's non-accruals at amortized cost rose to two point four percent of the portfolio, up from two point one percent last quarter. Net asset value per share fell to nineteen dollars thirty-five cents, down from nineteen fifty-nine last quarter and nineteen ninety a year ago. Core earnings per share held at forty-seven cents — flat versus last quarter, but that flatness is masking a decline from fifty cents a year earlier, and it's sitting on top of that NAV erosion. Portfolio leverage ticked up too, to five point eight times from five point seven. Ares is still calling this manageable — non-accruals remain below its roughly three percent historical average — but the direction is the point, not the level.

That's the story worth naming plainly: general-partner economics are decoupling from the economics of the people actually bearing the credit risk. The fee-earner is increasingly insulated from the loan book. Some of that is legitimate diversification — Blue Owl's shift toward non-direct-lending fundraising is a real business-mix change, not just a trick of accounting. But some of it is a classic late-cycle pattern, where the asset manager keeps compounding on assets under management while the vehicle underneath quietly erodes. Worth watching whether that gap widens further as more BDCs report.

So why is capital moving at the margin? Follow it to the retail channel, because that's where the tightening pressure is actually originating. In the first quarter of 2026, investors across the non-traded BDC landscape requested thirteen point nine billion dollars in redemptions. Sponsors honored seven point four billion. More than four point six billion dollars was trapped behind gates — the first quarter in which pro-rata gating has occurred across that entire product type. Blackstone gated redemptions on its flagship BCRED fund, a seventy-nine billion dollar vehicle, on June 4th. Reporting on Blue Owl's OTIC interval fund put redemption requests at forty point seven percent of assets, with only twelve cents on the dollar actually paid out. Those fund-level figures come from a securities-law publication rather than the funds' own filings, so treat the precise percentages as directional rather than audited — but the direction is unmistakable, and it lines up with what Golub Capital told its own investors: capital is exiting direct lending through retail-vehicle redemptions, and that outflow is shifting bargaining power from borrowers back to lenders.

That's a genuinely different tightening mechanism than a credit-quality story. It's liquidity-driven. Redemption pressure forces funds to sell their most liquid, best-marked assets to meet the requests they do honor, which adversely selects the assets left behind toward the harder-to-sell, lower-quality names. And it lands directly on the growth story every major manager is chasing right now — pushing private credit into four-oh-one-k and wealth-channel accounts. A fund that gates its existing retail investors is a hard story to sell to the next batch of retail investors.

Which brings us to the question underneath all of this: is the benign default data even real? Proskauer's Private Credit Default Index, covering seven hundred sixteen loans and one hundred ninety-five point six billion dollars of original principal, put the second-quarter default rate at two point five one percent, down from two point seven three percent in the first quarter. On its face, that's a resilience story, and Proskauer frames it that way. But break it out by borrower size and the picture bifurcates exactly like the BDC data did: companies with twenty-five to forty-nine point nine million dollars of EBITDA — the traditional lower-middle-market sweet spot for direct lending — saw defaults worsen to three point four percent from three point one. The smallest and largest cohorts improved. The stress is concentrating precisely where direct lending does most of its business.

And there's a harder question behind even that number: what counts as a default when liability management exercises have become the default tool for avoiding one? Restructuring practitioners describe majority lender groups now using increasingly coercive co-op structures to force minority holdouts to capitulate faster. A recent Oxford University study of eighty-nine of these liability management transactions found that more than eighty percent defaulted again within thirty-six months. Moody's data, as reported, shows excluded lenders recovering just fourteen cents on the dollar in these liability-management-driven cases, versus fifty-seven cents for senior lenders in ordinary non-LME bankruptcies. Both figures come through trade-press summaries rather than the primary studies themselves, so hold them as directional evidence — but directionally, they say the same thing: today's low default rate is partly an artifact of delay. Amend, extend, and restructure again in three years. That means the real stock of impaired credit, and the restructuring pipeline for 2027 and 2028, is larger than the headline number implies.

One more thread worth naming before we close. Private credit is now the primary financier of the AI data-center buildout — the Financial Stability Board found AI accounted for more than a third of private credit deals in 2025, up from seventeen percent over the prior five years. The same manager franchises writing that data-center debt — Blue Owl, Blackstone, Apollo — are also, through their legacy software books, exposed to AI's disruptive effect on the borrowers they've already lent to. J.P. Morgan's asset management arm estimates twenty-five to thirty-five percent of private credit portfolios carry some degree of AI-disruption risk. It's a hedge that exists across a manager's platform but not necessarily inside any single BDC's marks.

So where does that leave the cycle. Benign on the surface, deteriorating at the lower-middle-market margin, and flattered by tools — amend-and-extend, PIK, liability management — that delay recognition rather than resolve it. The binding risk right now isn't spread levels; it's liquidity mismatch in the retail vehicles and valuation marks that haven't been tested by a real downturn.
