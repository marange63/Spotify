Enterprise Technology & Software

Good morning. It's September 7th, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

One name from last week's security earnings cluster hasn't been examined on this show yet. Zscaler reported on September 3rd and guided fiscal 2027 growth down, and we spent yesterday on that. CrowdStrike has come up here three mornings running. Okta is the one we haven't looked at directly. It turns out to be the sharpest version of a pattern this beat has been tracking for over a week.

Start with what Okta actually reported. Revenue for its fiscal second quarter was $805 million, up 11 percent year over year. That is the slowest growth of any security vendor that reported this cycle. Its subscription backlog, what the company reports as RPO, was $4.858 billion, up 17 percent. That gap is the single piece of evidence pointing toward acceleration. And a backlog number carries contract-duration effects, so it is not a clean read on demand.

The guidance undercuts even that. Okta guided next quarter's revenue to a range of $813 to $817 million. Outside analysts reading that range compute it as roughly 10 percent growth. That is a step down from the 11 percent just delivered, not a step up. The company also guided its current subscription backlog, the portion due inside twelve months, to $2.59 to $2.6 billion. That implies growth of about 11 to 12 percent, well below the 17 percent total book it just reported. Those are two different measures, so hold the comparison loosely. But both guided numbers point the same direction. On the company's own outlook, the forward book is not accelerating. Okta guided into a business that is slowing.

Here is what happened next. Okta's stock popped nearly 29 percent on the print, in CNBC's own words. That is an 11 percent grower, guiding to something closer to 10, re-rating violently in a single session. The stated reason, from chief executive Todd McKinnon, was AI agents. As he put it, every agent needs a trusted identity and clear controls over what it can access and do. Investors bought that framing hard.

Now listen to what management said about the size of the business behind it. McKinnon described dozens of deals in the quarter, including several million-dollar-plus deals. That is a count and a per-deal threshold. There is no aggregate dollar figure anywhere in the release or on the call. Chief financial officer Brett Tighe was blunter. Still immaterial, he said. Still very small. We're very early innings. Asked directly about materiality, he said that for fiscal '27 they don't think it's going to be material, though there is a real possibility it matters in the long run. McKinnon said the same thing in different words. It's too small to show up in the numbers right now. That is not analyst skepticism. That is management telling investors not to model this yet, and the market bid the stock up anyway.

So what is that move actually pricing? Not current revenue, which management says doesn't include it. Not an accelerating forward book, since the guide points the other way. What is left is optionality. It is a bet that agent identity becomes a durable, separately priced budget line, and that Okta owns it before Microsoft's Entra or Amazon's IAM bundle the same function in for free. That is a legitimate bet. On anything disclosed this week, it is also entirely unverifiable.

There is a competing interpretation worth naming, and it is the one this beat put to the security vendors yesterday. Agent identity may not be new budget at all. It may be existing identity and access management spend, relabelled. The same seats and the same renewals, sold under a newer name to a board that will fund anything with the word agent on it. Nothing Okta disclosed separates the two. One disclosure would settle it: agent-identity contract value reported as its own line, split between net-new logos and expansions inside existing accounts. Until some vendor publishes that, dozens of deals is equally compatible with a new category and a re-priced old one. Enterprise buyers should contract accordingly. The vendor's own finance chief says this category is pre-material, which argues for term flexibility rather than locking multi-year pricing into a product whose shape is not yet set.

This is also the fifth time in barely a week that this show has had to discount a vendor-defined adoption number. Workday's agent customers. Salesforce's six thousand paying Agentforce customers. Snowflake's CoCo account count. Zoom's AI adoption growth. Every one of those at least counted something. Okta's metric is not even a count of activations. It is a handful of deals with no dollar total attached, and it drew the largest market reaction of the whole cohort.

Now, if identity is being repriced as the control point for enterprise AI, something has to be making it that. The clearest evidence arrived the same week, from Salesforce and Anthropic. The two launched a partnership branded Claudeforce, and the flagship product is called Salesforce in Claude. It is a plugin with 37 prebuilt sales skills that, in the companies' own language, let sellers and agents reason over live revenue context, automate pipeline updates, and take governed action right from Claude. Read that plainly. Salesforce is letting sellers do their pipeline work inside a competitor's chat window, without ever opening the Salesforce application, as long as Salesforce still controls what that agent may see and do.

That is the mechanism. When the interface moves outside the application, the enforcement point stops being the software and becomes identity. It is also the flight from the per-seat meter this beat traced a week ago, now arriving at the vendor's own front door. If the work happens inside Claude, Salesforce is not billing a seat. It is billing governed access to data and actions. That is precisely the budget line the market just paid up for at Okta.

Two caveats belong on it. Salesforce in Claude is live with a handful of pilot customers, with an open beta targeted for later this month. There is no production usage and no revenue evidence behind it yet, only a shipping product where a week ago there was a passing mention that Salesforce had picked a single default model. And naming Claude the default reasoning engine inside Agentforce reverses Salesforce's model-agnostic stance and concentrates dependency on one external lab. Whether Salesforce is hedging its own disintermediation or accelerating it is not answerable from a press release.

Here is what the market is not paying for. Figma reported back on August 5th. That is a month old, and it is the cleanest counter-example available. Revenue grew 48 percent year over year, to $370 million, the third straight quarter of accelerating growth. More than 80 percent of Figma's larger paid customers used AI credits weekly, and more than half used the Figma agent weekly. That is disclosed weekly production usage, not a pilot count. Net dollar retention on accounts above ten thousand dollars of recurring revenue ran 136 percent. The stock fell anyway. Reporting at the time put the after-hours decline at around 16 percent, because the next quarter's guide implied growth slowing to roughly 36 percent.

Be fair to the other side of that. Going from 48 percent growth to a guide near 36 is a real deceleration, whatever the AI metrics say. And weekly credit usage measures engagement with a bundled feature, not incremental revenue. It is fully consistent with AI working as a retention tool rather than a growth engine.

Put the two side by side and the finding is not about either company. Okta re-rated on a business its own management calls immaterial. Figma sold off while documenting real weekly AI usage across most of its largest accounts. The market is not rewarding proven AI adoption right now. It is rewarding the version of the story where the number is still too small to check, and pricing off the growth rate wherever the number is big enough to be measured. There is a perverse incentive in that for every vendor watching. Deal counts without dollars got paid this week. Audited usage metrics did not. Expect more of exactly the disclosure this beat keeps discounting. The test is Okta's third quarter. If those agentic deals still carry no dollar figure by then, the 29 percent bought a narrative rather than a business.

That's the briefing. Have a good day.
