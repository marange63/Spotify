Good morning, it's September 1st, and here's your capital markets radar.

On Monday, US forces struck Iranian assets in the Strait of Hormuz after detecting preparations to mine the waterway. Iran retaliated against US-linked facilities, and Brent crude briefly traded above ninety dollars a barrel, with WTI above eighty-five. Last Thursday we said the Iran premium was sitting on positioning, not physical disruption, and that a credible transit threat would snap it back. It did. But be precise about what actually came back into the price. This is a threat premium re-entering, not evidence that a barrel of oil stopped moving. The mine-laying preparations were struck before they could be executed, which is the opposite of flows being interrupted. Grant the concession this beat made last week: demand-side caution was doing real work in the earlier selloff, so part of this move is a low base rather than a pure new shock. The conflict itself is fluid; the market reaction is the settled fact here, not the military picture.

Here's why this isn't purely an energy story. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh built his Jackson Hole case, delivered August 28th, on breadth rather than on a single core number. The speech itself is old news on this beat, but one figure in it has gone unused and it carries today's spine. Fifty-four percent of the PCE basket showed price increases above three percent over the trailing year. A chair arguing from breadth has much less room to wave off an energy shock as a one-off relative-price move than a chair arguing from a narrow core reading does. So an oil spike lands differently on this Fed than it would have a month ago. That is the mechanism connecting the Strait of Hormuz to the September policy debate, not just sentiment. The counter is worth naming. A chair who blames the central bank for sixty-five months of elevated inflation has a reputational stake in hawkishness, and that is not the same thing as an economic case for it.

Which is exactly why duration got sold on two fronts at once. The ten-year Treasury yield topped four-point-seven-five percent on Monday, its highest level since January 2025, per Bloomberg. Two forces hit the long end in the same session, and they do not argue for the same shape.

A hawkish Fed repricing argues for the front end moving, which flattens the curve. An oil-driven inflation-risk premium argues for term premium building at the long end, which steepens it. We can't tell you which force is dominating, because the two-year and thirty-year prints around the speech aren't clean enough to stand behind. What we can tell you is that the market is trading two incompatible impulses as a single trade, and that is the thing most likely to be wrong here.

The data that would settle it hasn't landed. August payrolls print September 4th, PPI on the 10th, CPI on the 11th, all ahead of the September FOMC meeting. None of that is in hand, so treat any hike-probability number quoted this week as a moving target rather than a settled read. And hold on to the branch nobody is being paid to hold. If the Fed tightens into a labour market that weakens on that payrolls print, this stops being a hawkish soft landing and becomes a policy-mistake trade. That is a scenario, not a forecast. We have no information about the payrolls number. But it is the branch where an energy-led inflation impulse and a softening labour market arrive together, and neither credit spreads nor equity volatility is currently paying anyone to hold it.

Now to who actually pays for higher-for-longer. Private credit. Yesterday this program reported BDC bond deals clearing at multiples of target and called funding conditions easier, not tighter. Here is the other side of that ledger. Fitch's trailing default rate for US private credit hit a record six percent in April and has held at or above that level every month since, reaching six-point-one percent through July across thirteen hundred tracked borrowers. Those figures reach us through outlets citing Fitch rather than a Fitch release we retrieved ourselves, so hold the decimal loosely and the direction firmly. Now the retail funnel. Fundraising for non-traded business development companies fell to two billion dollars in the second quarter, an eighty-two percent drop from eleven billion a year earlier. That is the retail-facing channel only. Institutional private credit funds are a separate pool and are not in that number.

So the wholesale funding window is open while the retail equity funnel has essentially shut, at the exact moment defaults sit at a record. That moves the binding constraint. It is no longer the cost of these vehicles' liabilities, which is demonstrably cheap. It is the supply of new equity underneath them and the quality of the loans already on the books. There is an adverse-selection wrinkle on top of that. Banks have been winning stronger borrowers back from direct lenders through refinancing, on trade reporting rather than a measured share shift. But if it is happening at scale, the best credits are leaving the private lenders' books, which can make the headline default rate look better even as what remains gets riskier. Pair that with payment-in-kind usage near ten percent of BDC loans, which this beat covered yesterday, and the headline statistic becomes less informative precisely as the underlying risk rises. That default rate is elevated, not distressed. Watch the fundraising number and the default rate together, not the rate alone.

Now to what refused to confirm the hawkish story, and start with a call this program got wrong. On August 29th we said gold's structural leg would hold up better than a pure rates asset through the Warsh speech. It didn't. Gold fell roughly three percent, essentially in line with bitcoin. Three sessions later gold closed August 31st at four thousand five hundred ninety-one dollars, back above the level it broke down from, on a single daily recap, so hold the exact figure loosely. And it is not a record. A higher one was set earlier this year. The structural bid did reassert, just on a longer horizon than one session suggested. The plainest explanation is the Hormuz risk premium and continued diversification demand rather than the standard rate relationship. Which is also the caution. Gold and oil rising on the same headline is one geopolitical trade expressed twice, not an independent verdict on the Fed. De-escalate Hormuz and both legs unwind together.

The dollar tells a similar story. The dollar index closed August 31st at 99.44 and finished lower for a second straight month, but the monthly move was only two-tenths of a percent, which is close to noise. Month-end prints are contaminated by rebalancing flows, and this index is two-thirds euro and yen, so it can be telling you about Europe and Japan rather than about the Fed. The signal is the absence of a rally, not the magnitude of the decline. A dollar that will not rally through a hawkish chair, a bond selloff and an energy shock all at once means this hawkish episode is tightening global dollar-funding conditions far less than a textbook one would. That is why offshore credit and emerging-market stress signals have not appeared, and why the damage so far is confined to duration.

And bitcoin closed higher, up about half a percent, on a day with oil up more than three percent and the ten-year at a twenty-month high. Half a percent is noise on its own. But it puts bitcoin in the same basket as gold and the dollar: three assets that should have confirmed the hawkish bond move and didn't.

So which side is right, the bond market or everything else? Either Treasuries are over-reading a chair's tone as a policy decision that has not been made, or the currency and hard-asset complex is under-reading a genuine tightening turn. The evidence narrowly favours the first, for one reason: the bond move was built entirely on rhetoric plus a threat premium, and neither has yet been tested by a single data print. But unresolved is the honest label, and the useful conclusion is to position for volatility around the calendar rather than for a settled direction.

Three things to watch into next week. Whether Brent holds the ninety-dollar handle or retraces toward the sub-eighty-eight area it traded last Thursday on a de-escalation headline. Whether the yield move keeps behaving like a mixed policy-and-energy trade or separates into a cleaner curve shape. And whether non-traded BDC fundraising stabilises or keeps falling alongside a rising default rate. Here is the falsifier for today's framing. If Brent retraces sharply while the ten-year holds its highs, that says the yield move was about policy after all, not energy, and everything you just heard needs rewriting.

That's the radar for today. Talk to you next time.
