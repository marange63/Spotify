Capital Markets Cross-Asset Radar

Transcript · October 10, 2026

Good morning. It's Saturday, October 10th, and this is your Capital Markets Radar.

Two tests cleared this week, and the market is reading them very differently. The bond market's biggest test just passed. Underneath it, the oil story changed from a priced risk into barrels that are measurably missing. Let's take them in order.

Start with the long end. Since Monday, this desk has argued that duration buyers want more compensation before they show up. On Thursday, October 8th, the Treasury put that to a direct test with a thirty-year reopening. It cleared at 5.618 percent. By TFTC's auction tally, that is the highest thirty-year auction yield since August 2000. The obvious read is that buyers balked. They didn't. Bid-to-cover came in at 2.54, per Helious, above the six-auction average of 2.41.

The breakdown of who bought tells the same story. Indirect bidders, the bucket that includes foreign investors, took 72.3 percent of the supply against a 69.1 percent average. Direct bidders came in a touch above their own average as well. Primary dealers, the buyers of last resort, took just 6.8 percent, against a usual share of about 10. In plain terms, dealers barely had to step in. End demand cleared this auction on its own.

So the long end is clearing at a price. It is not failing. The opposite over-read is just as weak, though. Strong demand at these levels does not mean thirty-year yields have topped. One reopening tells you buyers will take duration at five-point-six percent. It does not tell you they won't ask for more next time.

There's a currency wrinkle worth flagging. That above-average foreign bid came with the dollar index near its fifty-two-week high of 102.54, set October 5th. As a mechanism, a strong dollar tends to make Treasuries less attractive to foreign buyers once hedging is paid for. That they leaned in anyway suggests the yield, not the currency, is doing the work. That's an inference, not a measurement, but it says foreign demand for duration is holding.

Then the second-order piece. A long bond near five-point-six percent is the benchmark that long-dated borrowers price off. That means infrastructure debt, power build-outs, AI data-center financing, and private credit. A thirty-year that clears at a price, rather than failing to clear, still raises the hurdle for every dollar of long-dated borrowing behind it. So what: the long end isn't breaking, it's repricing everything downstream of it.

So why would a bond already yielding five-point-six percent still not look cheap? Look at the barrels. On Thursday, this desk said the oil premium was pricing the fragility of the Hormuz route, not barrels actually lost. That read needs updating. CNBC reports that about 9.5 million barrels a day exited Hormuz in the week ended Tuesday. That's roughly 30 percent below pre-war levels. At the same time, a Gulf of Mexico hurricane forced producers to shut in about 1.3 million barrels a day of US output.

Those are two different losses on two different clocks. Hurricane shut-ins are transitory and typically come back within days to weeks. The Hormuz shortfall is a war variable with no restoration date. So the market may over-read the combined headline if Gulf output returns quickly. Prices show where the loss actually sits. Per CNBC, Brent traded at $105.02 a barrel on October 8th, while WTI rose 3.6 percent to settle at $91.49. That gap of about thirteen and a half dollars says the shock is concentrated in seaborne crude, not US inland supply. It also matters for margins. Diesel prices are reportedly near record highs, which makes freight and industrial names a margin risk heading into third-quarter earnings.

This is no longer just a risk premium. It is a measurable quantity shock, landing days before the September CPI print on Wednesday. Now recall the Fed's own September projections. Sixteen of eighteen FOMC participants pencilled in at least one more hike before year-end. That was a projection of intent in mid-September, not a decision, and the October 27th-28th meeting is still open. But an energy shock that shows up as lost barrels, rather than priced fear, is exactly the input that would validate those hawks. It is also the channel most likely to finally move breakevens, which this desk has watched stay flat through the whole selloff. The CPI print hasn't happened, so assume no outcome. So what: oil has turned from a price-of-risk story into a quantity story, and Wednesday is its first test.

Here's the strange part. The equity tape barely noticed. The S&P 500 touched an intraday record on October 6th, besting its August 13th high. It gave some of that back in Thursday's AI-driven wobble. Then it closed Friday at 7,811.54, up 0.59 percent on the day. The VIX sat at 14.84, per Cboe. That's below the 15.41 close right after Thursday's selloff, even as the oil losses became measurable.

You can read that two ways. Either the AI-capex earnings story is genuinely offsetting a hiking Fed and an oil shock. Or this is late-cycle complacency, with tail protection priced cheaply into a live war and an open FOMC. A low VIX can also simply mean the market sees each shock as idiosyncratic. The numbers on hand can't settle it, so both readings stay live.

What can be settled, partly, is where the stress is showing up. It's not in the index. Yesterday this desk said to watch whether high-yield spreads catch up to Thursday's equity move. Thursday's credit print gives a partial answer. The broad ICE BofA high-yield spread, via FRED, sat at 3.15 percent on October 8th. That's still tighter than the 3.24 percent of a week earlier. But CCC-and-lower credit, the bottom of the junk stack, widened to about 12.5 percent that same day. That's dispersion, not contagion. The weakest issuers feel funding costs and margin pressure first, while the broad index stays calm. One day of CCC widening doesn't make a trend, so treat it as a tell, not a verdict.

Private credit adds a caveat rather than a headline. The last read on the Private Performing Credit Index put the average loan spread at 576 basis points on June 30th. That's more than three months stale. The lag itself is the point. A market that reprices this slowly could be building stress well before it ever shows up in a print.

The speculative edge tells a similar story. Ether broke below $2,500, trading near $2,493 on Friday morning, per Fortune. Meanwhile, US spot bitcoin ETFs have logged about $510 million of outflows over five sessions. On Monday, this desk floated a rotation read inside crypto, with the marginal dollar shifting toward ether. Ether falling alongside bitcoin outflows rules that out. This looks like the whole speculative sleeve de-risking at once. The cause is still inference, whether leverage, a basis unwind, or macro. But the sleeve is behaving as if it prices a tighter Fed path than the S&P does. So what: the stress is pricing at the edges, not the core.

Three dated tests line up fast from here. JPMorgan and Goldman Sachs report Tuesday, October 13th, before the open. Consensus has Goldman earning about $15.18 a share, up roughly 24 percent year over year. That's an estimate, not a result. A strong trading and banking quarter would reinforce the bet that financial conditions are loose enough to underwrite this tape. A credit-cost miss would be the first hard data point validating the CCC stress. September CPI follows on Wednesday the 14th, and the Fed decides October 27th-28th. The one thing to watch across all three: whether the CCC stress and the oil quantity shock reach broad high yield and breakevens, or stay contained at the edges while the core keeps shrugging.
