Good morning. This is Cautious Optimism Briefings, the Capital Markets Radar, for Tuesday, October 6, 2026.

Let's start with the long end. On Treasury's daily par yield curve, the ten-year closed Monday at 5.31 percent. That is up from 5.24 percent on October first. It is the highest ten-year close this desk has tracked in this selloff, and the thirty-year rose with it. Monday had no major data release that we can verify, so there was no headline catalyst to point to.

This desk has argued for two weeks that the long end will not fall on calming news. Monday extends that run.

A correction first. Our October third episode misread Treasury's table, and Friday's ten-year close was 5.28 percent, not 5.17. The direction stands, because both long tenors still rose on the jobs report. And the test we set that day, that the long end holds above Thursday's closes into CPI, is so far passing.

But the level is not the most important thing in the table. The shape is, and the shape forces us to revisit our own story.

So look at the whole curve, not just the long end. From October first to October fifth, the two-year rose six basis points, to 4.84 percent. The ten-year rose seven basis points. Three-month bills rose five. That is close to a parallel shift. The gap between the two-year and the ten-year barely moved, from about 46 basis points to about 47, on our arithmetic from Treasury's tables.

Why does that matter? For most of this run, we argued that term premium was driving the selloff, and that the policy path was not the problem. A term-premium shock should steepen the curve. This one did not steepen. It lifted the front end and the back end together.

Treasury's real yield curve sharpens the picture. Over those same sessions, the five-year real yield rose five basis points. The ten-year real yield rose seven, to 2.95 percent on Monday. Those are the same moves the nominal yields made. That means implied inflation compensation was flat, at about 2.36 percent at both tenors. That breakeven is our arithmetic from two Treasury tables, not a Treasury figure, and it covers only three sessions. But it rules out inflation as the driver of this leg. Whatever is pushing yields up, it is real rates.

So what does that mean for the desk's own thesis? Two of our reads weaken. The first is the claim that term premium, not the policy path, is doing the work. The second is our line that the long end was not trading the October meeting. Bills and the two-year kept rising after Friday's payroll miss. On our read, the market is pricing a tighter policy path, not simply a hike pushed further out by a weak jobs number.

There is a competing read, and we should give it its full weight. Term premium can lift the front end too. Real yields carry term premium of their own. And three sessions is a small sample. A parallel move over three days does not prove the policy path is driving the bond market. It is consistent with that story, and it is hard to square with a pure supply story.

Which shock it is decides what the Fed can do about it. If this is the policy path repricing, a hawkish Fed confirms it, and the front end carries the pain. If it is term premium, no Fed action fixes the long end. A parallel shift cuts both ways. It means the Fed is not boxed in purely by forces it cannot control. But it also means the curve is offering no growth-scare escape, because the front end did not rally on weak data. The so-what is simple. This week, a jobs miss did not make duration a hedge.

One oddity deserves a flag. The twenty-year closed Monday at 5.70 percent, above the thirty-year at 5.66. So the long bond is not the weakest point on the curve. If heavy long-bond supply were the whole story, you would expect the thirty-year to lead. We do not have a quoted explanation for the kink, so we will not invent one.

If the front end is moving too, the pain shows up in funding first. When three-month bills and the two-year rise with the ten-year, there is no steepening carry pickup. A levered holder earns a yield that rises at the same pace as its funding cost. That is the worst configuration for levered duration and for basis-style carry. This is mechanism, not measurement. The takeaway is that carry trades get no cushion from this curve.

The same arithmetic hits anything priced off the ten-year. On October fifth, we said a deferred hike buys stocks time, not a lower discount rate. Monday's close updates that line. The curve is not even buying time. A ten-year at a new high for this selloff, with no help from the front end, means valuation support has to come from earnings. Higher real discount rates compress long-duration multiples first. And a parallel shift adds a second channel that a pure long-end selloff would not. Floating-rate borrowers reprice off the front end, so their interest bill rises right away. That means the pressure is not confined to long-duration growth names. Leveraged, short-funded balance sheets feel it too, which narrows the places to hide inside equities.

Now the real assets, where the flat breakeven matters most. A rise in real yields with no rise in inflation compensation gives inflation hedges nothing to work with. Gold pays no coupon, so it competes directly with a higher real yield. Uranium and power infrastructure carry long-dated cash flows, so they reprice off the same real discount rate. And TIPS sold off alongside nominal Treasuries, so inflation-linked bonds did not hedge this week's move.

The same lens applies to the dollar and to crypto. We have no verified prices for gold, the dollar, or crypto across these sessions, so what follows is mechanism only. For the dollar, a real-rate move that reaches the front end is the textbook source of support, because short-rate differentials do the work. So the dollar is a useful cross-check on our read. A firm dollar would fit the policy-path story. A soft one would argue that something else, like term premium, is doing more of the lifting. For bitcoin and ether, a higher real discount rate is a headwind with no inflation-hedge offset this week. And for stablecoins, every rise in bill yields widens the carry a holder gives up by sitting in a non-yielding dollar token. That strengthens the case for yield-bearing tokenized cash.

The same logic reaches private markets. In private credit, higher front-end rates are what test model-based marks. So watch redemption requests at semi-liquid funds. If the cost of carry stays high, that is where stress would surface first.

Now the over-read and the under-read. The first over-read is the level headline. Secondary coverage frames the ten-year as at its highest since 2002. We could not confirm that on Treasury's closing series, and the level tells you less than the shape. The second over-read is the payroll miss. A weak print made a near-term hike look less likely in the narrative, but the bond market did not act on that. The under-read is how far the front end has moved with the long end. That makes the October 28 FOMC more live than the payroll story suggested. We should not assume the Fed gets a clean path to ease into a term-premium shock. The curve has not offered one.

What would settle this? Two dated events matter. September CPI is due October 14 at 8:30 Eastern. The FOMC decides October 28 at 2 p.m. Eastern. Neither has been released as of this recording, so we can only set up the test. If the two-to-ten spread steepens on a soft inflation input, the term-premium read gets its vindication. If the curve keeps moving in parallel, the policy path is the driver. Then the Fed has more say over this bond market than we have been giving it credit for. Watch breakevens too. A hot print that finally lifts them would be the first inflation component this selloff has shown.
