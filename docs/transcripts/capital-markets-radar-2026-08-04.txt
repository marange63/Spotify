Good morning, it's Tuesday, August 4th. Here's your capital markets radar.

All week we've been tracking one vise: oil spiking on the Iran conflict, feeding a hawkish Fed, feeding an eighteen-month high on the long end. Over the weekend that vise started to loosen. President Trump said early Monday that a planned strike on Iran had been called off, and markets reacted immediately. West Texas Intermediate fell five point nine percent to seventy-nine dollars and sixty-nine cents a barrel, a three-week low. The ten-year Treasury yield eased five basis points to four point six nine percent, and the thirty-year fell the same five basis points to five twenty-three, both retreating from their recent eighteen-month highs. Equities followed, with the S&P 500 up one point three percent to seventy-five eighty-four. And fed funds futures moved with everything else: September hike odds fell to roughly sixty-three percent, down from about eighty percent just before last week's FOMC meeting. That's a genuine cross-asset round-trip, and it happened in under a week.

But here's where you need to slow down, because the clean version of this story — strike called off, negotiations resumed, crisis over — is not what actually happened. Trump also claimed direct US-Iran talks would begin Monday afternoon. Iran's government publicly disputed that. The Washington Post's own headline on this captures it: "Iran rejects Trump's claim that talks will restart, adding to weekend of whiplash." Unnamed US officials told CBS News the same thing — no new negotiations were actually planned. So what you have on the ground is a stand-down of an imminent strike, not a confirmed diplomatic breakthrough. The risk premium came out of oil and rates because the market is pricing the absence of an attack this week, not the presence of a deal. That's a real de-escalation, but it's a thinner one than the price action implies, and it can reprice on the next headline out of Tehran just as fast as it unwound.

That distinction matters directly for the Fed case. New Chair Kevin Warsh, who took office in May after the most divisive confirmation vote in Fed history, laid out his own precondition for this back on July 29: the conflict, he said, "has to find a resolution and can't reaccelerate." Read literally, against Iran's denial that talks are resuming, that bar has not been cleared. And it's worth remembering how hawkish the backdrop was to begin with. The July 29th FOMC voted nine to three to hold rates at three and a half to three and three-quarters percent — the most divided vote since September 2016, with regional presidents Hammack, Kashkari and Logan all dissenting in favor of a hike on tariff and energy-inflation grounds. None of the three, and neither Warsh, has said anything on the record since the weekend's news. So the sixty-three percent hike probability you're seeing in futures is the market inferring a softer Fed reaction function. It is not the Fed telling you that. Don't confuse futures repricing for forward guidance.

And if you needed a reason not to call this a clean dovish pivot, the ISM Manufacturing print gave you one Monday. The headline hit fifty-five point six for July, the best reading since May of 2022, and the employment component rose three point one points into expansion — the first time that's happened in thirty-three months. That is real-economy data running hot at exactly the moment the market wants to tell you inflation risk is fading. It sits awkwardly against ADP's weekly pulse, which showed private hiring slowing to around fifteen thousand jobs a week, a fifth straight weekly deceleration. Those two series aren't directly comparable — one's a diffusion index, the other a preliminary weekly estimate — so call it a genuine tension rather than a hard contradiction. But it means the hawkish case is still alive, and the July jobs report landing this Friday, August 7th, is what actually resolves it. Until then, you're trading a repricing, not a confirmed regime change.

Now, the credit and volatility tape gives you a way to judge whether all this Fed anxiety was ever fully believed in the first place. High-yield spreads sat at two hundred eighty-four basis points as of July 30th — historically tight — right through the most hawkish FOMC vote in a decade. The VIX closed at fifteen ninety-nine on Monday, near the low end of its trailing-month range. In other words, while the rates market was flagging real hike risk, credit and equity volatility barely blinked. The weekend's de-escalation partially validates that calm — if oil stays down and the Fed narrative keeps softening, spreads and vol were right not to move. But the validation is conditional. If Tehran's denial turns into a real breakdown and oil snaps back, the gap between how nervous rates markets got and how relaxed credit and vol stayed becomes the trade to fade. Watch that spread, not the headline.

The currency and metals tape is telling a consistent, if quieter, story. The dollar index slipped to about ninety-nine point eight, a seven-week low, with foreign investors continuing to trim dollar positioning — consistent with the market pricing less Fed tightening. Gold, meanwhile, is cooling rather than spiking: it traded around four thousand thirty-eight dollars an ounce Monday, down about three percent over the past month and well off January's record high of fifty-five ninety-seven, though still up nearly twenty percent year over year. Read together, that's not a fresh safe-haven bid and it's not a de-dollarization crisis — it's the January risk premium in both assets normalizing in the same direction as the rest of this repricing.

So where does that leave you positioning into the rest of the week. The lower long end is a real, if partial, reprieve for duration-heavy trades that got hurt by last week's hawkish FOMC — worth watching whether that shows up in the capex-heavy names that took the biggest earnings hit. But the core tension is unresolved: Iran hasn't confirmed talks, Warsh's own precondition for easing isn't clearly met, and the dissenters haven't recanted. Friday's jobs report is the actual catalyst. A hot print revalidates the hawks and re-tightens the vise across oil, yields and the dollar. A soft one confirms the round-trip you saw this week. Trade the repricing, but don't mistake it for a settled outcome.
