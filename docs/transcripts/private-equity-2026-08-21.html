Private Equity & the Deal Economy

Transcript · August 21, 2026

Good morning, it's Friday, August 21st, and this is your private equity briefing.

For two days running, this briefing has argued that the industry was generating fees and deal activity without actually returning cash to limited partners. On Wednesday the frame was financialization. On Thursday it was that the thaw talk had run well ahead of the cash. The second-quarter earnings season from the largest managers is the strongest challenge to that read yet, so let's take it seriously before we decide how much of it to believe.

Start with Blackstone. Fee-related earnings climbed twenty-two percent to one point seven eight billion dollars in the quarter, and distributable earnings rose twenty-six percent to a dollar fifty-two a share. Assets under management hit a record near one point three five trillion dollars. But the number that actually speaks to the exit question is realizations: thirty-one point eight billion dollars monetized in the quarter, including three public market debuts.

KKR's version of the same story is even sharper. Fee-related earnings per share hit a dollar thirty-two, up thirty-four percent year over year, and the firm posted a record eight hundred and forty-eight million dollars of realized performance income. Co-CEO Scott Nuttall didn't hedge about it. He called it KKR's largest monetization quarter ever, and he said it specifically to push back on market pessimism about the exit environment.

So here's the turn, and it starts with a distinction the headlines flatten. Fee-related earnings and realized performance income are not the same line. Fee-related earnings is an annuity. It grows with assets under management whether or not a single portfolio company gets sold. Realized performance income is the only line that actually answers whether the exit window is open. Hold Blackstone's two figures side by side and you can see how differently those lines are moving. Fee-related earnings were one point seven eight billion dollars. Net realizations in the same quarter were four hundred and fourteen million, up twenty-seven percent year over year. The growth rate is real. The scale tells you which engine is carrying the firm.

One more note of caution on the KKR record. A firm whose assets under management are also at a record should mechanically be capable of a record monetization quarter. The level is close to what compounding alone would predict. What would settle the argument is the rate of realization against invested capital, and KKR did not frame it that way.

Now look at what Blackstone says it actually sold. On the earnings call, the firm attributed the quarter's net realizations to dispositions including a data center sale and multiple energy portfolio realizations. It went further and said plainly that the most significant driver of its results continues to be its large-scale investments in artificial-intelligence-related areas: data centers, energy and power, and the frontier AI companies themselves. Blackstone does not break out how much of the thirty-one point eight billion is AI-adjacent versus ordinary corporate buyout, so this is direction, not a number. But it's the firm's own language, not our inference. What reopened is a bid for one category of asset. That is not an exit window.

Set that against the industry-wide picture, and the contrast is stark. MSCI's private capital monitor puts distributions at roughly six percent of buyout assets under management, against a ten-year average near fourteen. That figure is a year stale, running through June of last year, and a deeper search this week found nothing more current. What it did find is corroboration of the direction: McKinsey's private markets work has distributions running below fifteen percent of net asset value for four straight years, with rolling five-year buyout distributions-to-paid-in at the lowest level on record. The backlog behind that is real too. Trade reporting citing Bain and Company puts the industry's unexited inventory at roughly thirty-one thousand companies worth three point seven trillion dollars, carried past normal hold periods. So the honest verdict is this: the cash is real, but it is arriving at the top of the industry, and disproportionately from the one asset category currently in favor. That is not the same thing as the backlog clearing.

Now to the allocator side. If that cash were showing up broadly, the largest public pension in the country is where we'd expect to see it. CalPERS reported a preliminary fourteen point eight percent total return for the fiscal year ended June thirtieth, its best in five years, with funded status rising to eighty-five percent from seventy-nine. Private equity was the standout line inside that, at a preliminary seventeen percent, ahead of private debt and real assets.

Here's the turn on that one. Seventeen percent is a mark, not cash distributed, and CalPERS labels it preliminary. It also trailed public equity's twenty-four point one percent in the same fiscal year. For an asset class sold on an illiquidity premium, underperforming a daily-priced, liquid alternative by seven points is not the pitch. What the print does do is remove the denominator effect as an excuse. A pension whose total assets just grew almost fifteen percent is not being forced to trim private equity for allocation-math reasons. So any continued caution from limited partners here is a judgment about managers and about liquidity, not about arithmetic.

That brings us to the mechanism tying both of those together: who is actually getting the capital. On PitchBook data reported by the trade press, funds above five billion dollars now capture roughly forty-five percent of all capital raised globally, despite being under five percent of funds by number. The ten largest funds closed through mid-April took sixty point two billion dollars between them, sixty-one percent of everything raised in that window. Aggregate first-half fundraising dollars were up nine percent year over year, but on fewer funds closing, not more. That is not a broad recovery. It is concentration wearing a recovery's clothes.

And it is the same names showing up on both sides of the ledger. KKR's record hundred and thirty-three billion dollar trailing-twelve-month fundraising haul sits in the same quarter as its record monetizations, and Ares reported a record fundraising quarter of its own. The managers who can sell are the managers who can raise, and they are the managers limited partners are choosing to fund. That loop reinforces itself, which means a fundraising recovery of this shape deepens the two-tier structure rather than relieving it.

There is a second-order consequence for anyone who owns the manager rather than the fund. If listed alternative managers are increasingly valued on fee-related earnings while carry realizations stay cyclical and concentrated, their equity decouples from the health of the buyout cycle itself. You can be right on the stock and wrong on the asset class. The market is quietly treating those as the same call.

One last data point on where the top of the market is heading. Electronic Arts' fifty-five billion dollar take-private officially closed on August fourth, the largest leveraged buyout in history. Treat that as a reference point rather than fresh news. What's notable is the ownership. The Saudi Public Investment Fund holds ninety-three point four percent, with Silver Lake at just five point five. The marginal dollar in the largest buyout ever was not limited-partner capital at all. It was a sovereign fund with no fund life, no redemption clock and no distribution obligation, which is exactly the kind of buyer that can transact while fund-life-constrained sponsors are the forced sellers.

So where does that leave the cycle. The exit machine has restarted, but only at the very top, and disproportionately for assets tied to the AI infrastructure buildout. That is a real concession to the read we've been running, and a real risk for everyone else. It means private equity's one working exit channel is now correlated to the AI capex cycle. If AI infrastructure valuations reprice, that channel closes. The falsifier to watch is straightforward: realization strength showing up at mid-market managers, or a current-period industry distribution rate that actually climbs back toward its long-run average, rather than another quarter of the same five names.

That's the briefing for today. Enjoy the rest of your Friday.
