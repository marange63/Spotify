Strategic Power, Energy, and Institutional Change

Good morning. It's Saturday, August 1st, and this is your strategic power briefing.

The Senate just moved to weaponize tariffs against Russia's two biggest customers. On July 28th, senators voted 86 to 12 to advance the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act, named for the bill's chief architect, who died shortly before the vote. The procedural motion was held hours after Graham's funeral. The bill authorizes President Trump to impose tariffs of up to 200 percent on the largest buyers of Russian oil and gas — read: China and India — plus expanded sanctions on Russian banks, oligarchs, and the shadow tanker fleet. The lone Republican voting no was Rand Paul. That near-unanimity, arriving on the emotional current of a colleague's death, changes the political calculus for the administration. It raises the cost of quietly shelving the authority the way prior sanctions bills have often ended up sitting unused.

Be precise about what this is and isn't. It's a Senate procedural vote, not enacted law. The House timeline and the White House's actual willingness to trigger 200 percent tariffs, versus holding them as leverage, are both open questions. And this lands on top of an already-rising tariff wall, not a clean slate. The average effective US tariff rate for 2026 is running near 6.6 percent, the highest since 1969, and a fresh round of 50 percent tariffs on Canadian goods under Section 338 is set to take effect August 19th. So the sanctions bill is one more front in a widening trade conflict, and every front adds to the same inflationary pressure the Fed is already fighting.

The bill also lands alongside a second, more physical blow to Russian oil revenue. On the night of July 31st, Ukraine's General Staff confirmed a strike on the Lukoil-Volgogradneftepererabotka refinery, one of Russia's largest, with roughly 15 million tonnes a year of capacity, producing gasoline, diesel, and jet fuel for the Russian military. It's a recurring target, and the fire it triggered adds to a pattern of Ukrainian strikes degrading Russian domestic fuel output. So the squeeze on Moscow is now two-pronged: a legislative tariff threat aimed at Russia's crude customers, and physical destruction of the refining capacity that turns that crude into usable fuel. That second prong bites independently of Western politics. Every refinery offline widens Urals discounts and tightens the Kremlin's war-financing math without waiting on a House vote.

The risk, though, cuts both ways. Two-hundred-percent tariffs on Chinese and Indian purchases, if actually imposed, would be a serious escalation against Washington's two largest trading counterparties, not just against Moscow. Faced with that, both could shift toward Gulf and US crude, or lean harder into yuan and rouble settlement workarounds that chip away at the dollar's role in energy trade. In other words, a tool aimed at Russian revenue could accelerate the very de-dollarization that hard-money and Bitcoin narratives have been anticipating for years.

That tariff-as-weapon logic cuts both ways in another sense too. Economic warfare invites retaliation, and China holds the sharpest counter-lever of all — rare earths.

Here's where you should be careful, because the consensus story is running ahead of the facts. China's rare-earth export-control regime has been under a partial diplomatic truce since November 2025, one that's due to expire November 27th. The widely repeated claim is that Beijing is tightening the screws even through that pause, via a rule requiring a license for any foreign product containing just 0.1 percent Chinese-origin rare-earth content. On a closer look at China's own notices, that isn't what's happening right now. That specific extraterritorial rule — the 0.1 percent content trigger, along with a related technology-transfer provision — is currently suspended. It doesn't snap back automatically until November 10th, absent a fresh order from Beijing's commerce ministry. So the rare-earth chokehold is not actively squeezing Western magnet, EV, or wind manufacturers through that mechanism today.

What is real, even during the suspension, is narrower. Beijing's commerce ministry has formalized public reporting of export-control violations, and it's applying tightened, case-by-case licensing scrutiny — but specifically to advanced-computing semiconductors and AI systems with potential military applications, not to the broader rare-earth supply chain. So the leverage China is actually exercising right now is concentrated on chips and AI hardware, not on the magnet inputs that feed EV motors and wind turbines. That distinction matters for how you price the risk. The magnet chokehold is a loaded weapon, not a live one. The real question is whether Beijing lets that November 10th suspension lapse, which would reopen the broader rare-earth lever with six weeks still to run on the wider diplomatic truce. That's the date to track — a latent escalation, not a squeeze in force today. And if it does lapse, the second-order effect is a hard floor under allied diversification spending, from Lynas to newer domestic magnet players, plus a fresh cost drag on EV and wind capex at the exact moment utilities are already straining under new load.

Which brings us home. All of this — sanctions, tariffs, export licensing — is a contest over who controls the physical inputs that make an economy or a war machine run. And that contest runs straight back into the base layer at home: the US power grid the AI buildout depends on.

This week added two new data points to the PJM story this feed has tracked all week. First, on July 25th, a single fallen power line caused about 3.1 gigawatts of data-center load — roughly 3 percent of PJM's total demand at that moment — to disconnect in about 30 seconds, according to PJM's own data. That's the first real-world reliability event to validate what had been, until now, a curtailment framework on paper. It shows just how much concentrated, correlated load AI data centers now represent on a single grid, and how fast that load can vanish or spike.

Second, FERC has now declared the existing large-load interconnection tariffs used across the major grid operators, PJM included, "presumptively unjust." Grid operators have until August 17th to defend those tariffs or replace them. That's a regulatory clock, not a curtailment in force today, but it points the same direction as the rest of this week's coverage: regulators pushing to make new large loads — mostly data centers — pay their own way rather than spreading the cost across existing ratepayers. So the reliability risk exposed by that July 25th disconnect is turning into a cost-allocation fight. And the outcome reshapes the economics of the whole buildout. If hyperscalers have to internalize their own reliability cost, the advantage tilts toward independent power producers, gas-turbine makers, and small modular reactor developers, and away from pure grid-tied cloud capex. It also nudges new compute toward grids with more headroom — parts of Texas and the Southeast — rather than PJM's congested queue.

Step back, and the pattern across all three stories is the same. Power, in every sense of the word, runs through control of physical inputs — oil and refining capacity, rare earths and licensing regimes, electrons and grid interconnection queues. Markets have mostly priced these as background risk. This week argued they're becoming the binding constraint.

So watch three dates. The House timeline on the Graham sanctions bill, since a Senate supermajority doesn't guarantee enactment. November 10th, when China's rare-earth extraterritorial rules are set to resume unless Beijing extends the suspension. And August 17th, when FERC's deadline forces grid operators to show their hand on who pays for the AI buildout's power.
