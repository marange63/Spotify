Good morning. It's Sunday, November 22nd, and this is your private credit briefing.

This week the real prices on private-credit risk came from a data-center bond and a stalled loan, not from the funds themselves. Together they say credit is discriminating by who stands behind the risk. It is not repricing the asset class as a whole.

Start with the open question we've been sitting on. ING's auction of roughly five hundred million dollars of data-center loans, including debt tied to a New Mexico Stargate site, still has no public clearing result. But a price on data-center credit did surface this week. It came from Blue Owl.

Per Bloomberg, Blue Owl is preparing a five hundred and thirteen million dollar commercial mortgage securitization. It finances an Amazon-leased data center in Northern Virginia, and Citigroup is running the deal. The senior bonds are expected to be rated triple-A by Fitch and Kroll. Initial price talk on them is about one hundred ninety-five basis points over the benchmark. That level hasn't printed. Every account as of the latest reporting still calls it guidance. So treat it as where the deal is being shown, not where it clears.

Here's the reframe that matters more than the number. This isn't a price on data-center risk broadly. It's a price on Amazon's lease. A triple-A on a single-tenant, single-asset building is a judgment that the tenant keeps paying rent. It is not a judgment on the economics of running a data center. Fed Governor Michael Barr made a related point in a speech this desk covered on Thursday. He said hyperscalers and chipmakers may absorb the first losses in these structures. This deal is the bond-market version of that idea. So when you hear one ninety-five for triple-A paper, you're hearing the price of Amazon's credit wrapped in a mortgage.

And that number isn't obviously cheap. Trepp tracks senior triple-A, fixed-rate, single-asset data-center deals. That spread stood at one hundred eighty-two basis points over Treasuries in the week ending October 23rd, after widening twenty-one basis points that week. Blue Owl's guidance sits a touch above that. The benchmarks may not match exactly, so call it in line with the going rate. This is not investors chasing data-center risk at any price.

If the deal does price near guidance, there's a second-order effect, and it's this desk's inference. Securitization becomes a repeatable takeout for private managers' data-center purchases. Their capital recycles into the next deal. And the concentration in hyperscaler leases moves off private balance sheets and into rated bond portfolios.

One caution on that recycling story. As reported from Blue Owl's own SEC filing, Citibank and Wells Fargo lent about five hundred twelve million dollars against the building when Blue Owl's trust bought it on November 10th. That's almost exactly the size of the bond deal, so the bonds may simply refinance the bank loan. But the filing doesn't say so, and it doesn't mention a bond sale at all.

Now a deal with no reported Amazon behind it. A four hundred eighty-eight million dollar loan for a Malaysian data center, backed by Actis, was delayed while being marketed to other lenders. Bloomberg called that unusual for this market. The reporting gives no reason, and it doesn't tie the project to an investment-grade tenant. The pause could be idiosyncratic to one sponsor, structure, or jurisdiction. Or it could be an early sign that appetite thins without a hyperscaler name on the paper. One stalled loan can't settle that. But set it beside Blue Owl. Paper with Amazon on the lease is finding a securitization bid. Paper without that visible support just stalled mid-syndication. Appetite looks conditional, not universal.

So where does the risk the banks sell actually go? ING's sale is part of a wider bank push to move data-center risk to private credit firms, asset managers, and insurers. But the risk isn't leaving the banking system. The Fed's November Financial Stability Report puts large bank holding companies at close to one trillion dollars of exposure to that same complex. That covers tech firms, data-center developers, private credit vehicles, and utilities. It's exposure across four borrower types, not drawn loans, and not a loss estimate. The same report counts two point eight trillion dollars of bank commitments to other financial institutions. This desk covered that figure on Wednesday. Put the two together and the pattern holds. The risk changes form, from a direct loan to a line against a fund. It doesn't leave the banks.

A two-tier funding picture follows, and again this is inference. Assets with an investment-grade tenant get public-market funding at securitization spreads. Projects without one lean harder on private lenders and bank lines. That pushes the least-protected slice toward business development companies, life insurers, and other private debt vehicles. That's where the Fed has said the full exposure is hardest to measure.

Which raises the next question. How is the risk that lands in private books actually being marked? Yesterday's episode covered Golub Capital BDC. Its net asset value fell twenty-five cents a share, to fourteen dollars. Most of that drop came from markdowns on positions already on nonaccrual. Management pinned the loss on a small number of credits it had already flagged as problems. The open question was whether markdowns like that stay inside known problem names, or show up across peers. Several peers had already reported their third quarter earlier this month. Their prints point to dispersion, not uniform damage.

Start with Goldman Sachs BDC. On its earnings call, non-accruals were four point two percent of the portfolio at fair value. At amortized cost, they were seven point three percent. Both rose over the quarter. That gap says this manager is carrying its problem loans well below what it paid for them. Arguably, that's the honest end of the distribution.

Kayne Anderson BDC shows the leading indicator. On its call, payment-in-kind income rose to four point nine percent of total interest income, from four point five. Rising PIK tends to show up before a loan formally goes non-accrual. So Kayne Anderson's PIK line is the one to watch into the fourth quarter.

Crescent Capital BDC reported no new non-accruals, and its non-accrual rate dipped slightly. But that dip came largely from selling a small legacy investment, not from loans curing.

So the dispersion is only partly visible. We can see the managers already marking hard. We can't see the ones whose marks haven't moved, and a handful of prints won't identify them. Golub's peers neither confirm nor refute its known-names defense.

One more data point shows where losses land first. KBRA's third-quarter middle-market data, out earlier this month, has lower-middle-market borrowers defaulting at five point eight percent by count. Its full portfolio runs at three point six percent. Higher defaults among the smallest borrowers are a long-standing pattern, not a new turn. But that's where direct lenders have the least diversification and the least pricing power.

Pull it together. On the broader cycle, this week's news doesn't change the read from earlier in the week. Banks' syndicated market took back the top of the lending market last quarter, and realized payment defaults haven't turned. Within private credit, this week looks like discrimination, not a wholesale repricing. Capital will fund Amazon's rent at roughly the going triple-A spread. A Malaysian loan with no such name attached stalled. And BDC marks are splitting between managers taking the loss and managers whose books we can't yet read. The biggest unresolved risk is unprotected AI-buildout exposure and small borrowers, sitting at model marks in books that haven't been cut.

Here's what would confirm or refute that read. First, Blue Owl's final pricing against that one ninety-five guidance. Second, the ING result, whenever it surfaces. Third, fourth-quarter BDC marks. If PIK keeps rising at some managers while others have already cut, discounts to NAV should sort by manager rather than move as a block. And further out, the Fed's private credit survey results, due in the first quarter.

That's the lending machine for November 22nd. Stay cautiously optimistic.
