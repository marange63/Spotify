Good morning. It's Friday, November 13th, 2026, and this is your Strategic Power briefing. On Thursday, the price of money and the price of energy security rose together, and the AI-industrial build is caught between them.

Start with rates, because everything downstream reads off them. On November 12th, the ten-year Treasury yield rose more than 13 basis points to 5.125 percent, according to CNBC.

The shape of that move matters more than the level. The two-year rose even more, by over 15 basis points. The thirty-year rose less, by 10. So the front end led. That's the market pricing a hiking Fed, not a deficit panic or a term-premium revolt. If this were fiscal stress, the long end would be leading. It isn't.

This desk flagged October CPI as a catalyst on Monday and Tuesday, and declined to guess at it. Here's how it resolved. Headline CPI actually slowed, rising 0.4 percent for the month after 0.6 percent in September. Core held at 0.3 percent for a third straight month. So inflation did not accelerate. What changed is the Fed's tolerance for it.

Fed Governor Waller said a 50 basis point hike "should be considered" at the December meeting. Markets are taking the direction, not the magnitude. Futures fully price a December hike, but give a half point only about 13 percent odds. Goldman Sachs went a step further and added a March hike to its forecast. The debate has moved from whether this cycle starts to how long it runs.

And the Fed is doing this on a partially blind dashboard. The Bureau of Labor Statistics never collected October 2025 data during last year's shutdown. So there is no year-over-year inflation rate for this print. The upshot: the rate-cutting cycle that strategic infrastructure financing assumed is off the table, at least for now.

And the inflation the Fed is hiking against just got a fresh input from the Gulf.

Brent crude jumped 3.3 percent on Thursday, according to market reports. The trigger was a claim. Yemen's Houthis said they had attacked a pumping station on Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline, an oil facility at Yanbu, and an Aramco building in Riyadh. That move broke a slide that had been running on Ukraine and Iran peace-talk optimism.

Now be precise about what's confirmed. Per Reuters, Saudi Arabia did not immediately respond to a request for comment. The Saudi-led coalition said it intercepted and destroyed two drones, one in the Riyadh region and one in the Eastern Province. That is a claim about stopping an attack. It is not an admission that any target was hit. No credible report so far shows a single barrel lost. So treat Thursday as a returning risk premium, not a confirmed supply disruption.

The choice of target is the telling part. The East-West line is Saudi Arabia's route around the Strait of Hormuz. In early October, Riyadh said flows on it had reached 5.8 million barrels a day, against a maximum of 7 million. So the line has room to spare, and a hit on one pumping station would matter less than a Hormuz closure. But the Houthis aimed at the insurance policy, not the main exposure. The bypass itself is now a declared target.

The cushion behind that insurance may also be thinner than it looks. OPEC+ raised October output by 2.04 million barrels a day, led by Saudi Arabia, according to FXStreet. That's capacity being spent pumping into a falling market. We couldn't find a reliable current spare-capacity figure, so treat this as our inference. But barrels already being pumped can't also be held in reserve.

The peace premium that had been pushing oil down looks thin too. At the second day of Russia-Ukraine talks in Abu Dhabi, US envoy Jared Kushner said Washington is working toward what he called the war's largest prisoner exchange. Europe will join the next round. But there's no ceasefire, and territory remains the sticking point. That's a humanitarian step, not a structural one. Meanwhile, this desk has now tracked energy infrastructure as the target in three theaters in five days: Kyiv's refinery campaign, Moscow's substation strikes, and now the Gulf.

So what does a Fed hiking into a live, if unconfirmed, supply risk actually do? It restrains demand. It cannot restrain a pipeline. Our inference: utilities, independent power producers, and infrastructure funds get hit from the discount-rate side and the input-cost side at once. And a Gulf premium feeds the headline inflation the Fed is already leaning against, which argues for a longer cycle, not a shorter one.

Higher money and pricier energy land on the same balance sheet: the AI power build.

On Tuesday, this desk inferred that the Senate permitting bill would push more data-center developers to self-supply with gas, rather than build less. BloombergNEF has now put a number on that drift. Announced on-site gas plants for data centers could add roughly 10 percent to the US power sector's 2025 emissions. One caution. Those are announced plants, and announced gigawatts have been discounted almost every time someone audits them.

The longer-run scale is bigger still. BNEF estimates global data-center electricity demand could quadruple by 2035, reaching about 7 percent of global power consumption. That's a projection that sets the scale, not a near-term constraint.

The newest wrinkle is in Texas. ERCOT's chief executive asked state lawmakers for authority to make data centers cut usage before a grid emergency is declared, not just during one. That builds on Senate Bill 6, passed in 2025, which already requires large-load customers to curtail with 24 hours' notice. Be clear about what this is. It's a legislative ask, not a power ERCOT holds. Senate Bill 6 remains the real instrument today. Still, the direction is plain. The gatekeepers on AI power are moving from pricing access toward rationing it.

The second-order effect follows, and it's our inference. Routing around the grid with self-supplied gas turns hyperscalers into fuel-price takers. It privatizes reliability risk while socializing the emissions. That favors gas producers, pipelines, and fast-deploy turbine suppliers over merchant generators that live on grid scarcity. Keep the fuel benchmarks separate, though. That gas prices off Henry Hub, not Brent. So Thursday's oil spike doesn't flow through to data-center power costs one for one.

The rate side bites unevenly. Debt-funded builders now face a higher financing hurdle and a less certain grid at the same time. Balance-sheet hyperscalers can absorb both far more easily. So the grid is pushing data centers off it just as the money to build their own power got dearer.

Markets set the price of money and energy. The one input Washington actually licenses is chips, and this week that lever's weak point turned out to be its own enforcer.

Axios reported, citing unnamed sources, that Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick resisted other administration officials' efforts to restrict companies suspected of diverting Nvidia AI chips to China. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent reportedly challenged Lutnick on November 5th. The issue was Commerce's failure to act on Megaspeed, the Singapore-based firm suspected of funneling Nvidia chips into China.

Then, on Thursday, the top trade-committee Democrats, Ron Wyden and Richard Neal, opened a formal investigation. Its target is what they call the administration's "scheme to approve and tax exports" of Nvidia's AI chips to Chinese companies. The inquiry also covers Nvidia's 26 million dollars in donations toward Trump's projects. That's an allegation under investigation, not a finding of a quid pro quo.

Here's the mechanism worth sitting with. In August 2025, Nvidia and AMD agreed to share 15 percent of their China chip revenue with the US government to secure export licenses. So the department approving those licenses is the same one reportedly slowing the crackdown on diversion. And those approvals come with a revenue share for Washington. That's an incentive problem layered on an enforcement gap. It's our inference, not a finding.

It also has a clock. This desk reported Monday that Democrats won both chambers. If that majority inherits this probe in January, a letter could become an investigation with subpoena power. Nvidia's China channel would then be a standing oversight target into 2027, a governance overhang stacked on top of next week's earnings. An export control is only as strong as the official willing to enforce it. Right now, the reporting says that official was the one slowing it down.

A short watch list. October producer prices are due this morning, and we have no figure to report. Nvidia reports earnings on November 17th, the day before the briefing House chairmen have requested from Jensen Huang. The Fed decides on December 9th. And watch for any Saudi or Aramco statement that confirms or denies damage from this week's claimed strikes. Right now, silence is doing a lot of the market's work.

That's Strategic Power for November 13th. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
