Good morning, this is The Throughline for Tuesday, October 6th, 2026.

Today's headline: the ten-year Treasury closed Monday at 5.31 percent, the highest close the Radar desk has tracked in this selloff, and the front end rose right alongside it.

Only two briefings shipped today, so each gets a little more room than usual. We'll start with the macro backdrop, then move to the AI and compute stack, where Enterprise Technology has two deals.

From Capital Markets Cross-Asset Radar, the key point is not the level but the shape. From October first to October fifth, the two-year rose six basis points and the ten-year rose seven. Three-month bills rose five. That is close to a parallel shift, and the gap between the two-year and the ten-year barely moved. A term-premium shock should steepen the curve. This one did not.

Treasury's real yield curve sharpens that picture. The ten-year real yield rose the same seven basis points, to 2.95 percent. So on the desk's own arithmetic, inflation compensation was flat, and real rates are doing the work. That forces the desk to revisit its own story. For two weeks it argued that term premium, not the policy path, drove the selloff. Now it reads the market as pricing a tighter policy path. The desk gives the competing read its due. Term premium can lift the front end too, and three sessions is a small sample. But the so-what is plain. Friday's jobs miss did not make duration a hedge.

The practical result is that carry trades get no cushion from this curve. A levered holder earns a yield that rises at the same pace as its funding cost. Floating-rate borrowers reprice off the front end right away. So the pressure reaches leveraged, short-funded balance sheets, not just long-duration growth names. Inflation hedges get nothing to work with either. A real-rate move with flat breakevens works against gold, which pays no coupon. And TIPS sold off alongside nominal Treasuries.

Two housekeeping notes from the Radar. First, it corrects its own October third episode. Friday's ten-year close was 5.28 percent, not 5.17. Second, it flags an oddity it will not explain. The twenty-year closed above the thirty-year, which argues against a pure long-bond supply story. Two dated events will settle the bigger argument, and neither has happened yet. September CPI is due October 14, and the FOMC decides October 28. If the curve steepens on a soft print, the term-premium read wins. If it keeps moving in parallel, the policy path is the driver.

Now from rates to the software deals that have to clear at those rates. The desk is Enterprise Technology, and its theme is that durable value sits in the system of record that AI and agents write into.

Its lead is Schneider Electric's agreement to buy PTC, the design and product lifecycle software maker, announced October 5. PTC shareholders receive 205 dollars a share in cash, a 42.3 percent premium to the last close. The implied enterprise value is 23.7 billion dollars. Schneider prices the deal at 21 times PTC's 2027 estimated adjusted EBITA, and 13 times with full run-rate synergies. So the price looks sensible only if the synergies arrive.

The synergy mix is the real tell. The cost savings, 250 million euros within three years, are modest and within Schneider's control. The bigger line, 800 million euros of revenue synergies from cross-selling, depends on customers. So the first question is whether Schneider's hardware customers will buy PTC's software at scale. Press reports put the financing at roughly three euros of debt for every one of equity. Schneider expects to keep its Category A ratings, but that is the company's own view, and no rating agency has spoken yet. Closing is not expected until the third quarter of 2027. That leaves roughly a year for rivals like Siemens and Dassault Systèmes to work on PTC's customer base.

The desk's bigger signal is who the buyer is. Schneider is an operational technology incumbent, not a financial sponsor. Software assets are clearing when a buyer with an operating reason to own them shows up, while financial buyers sit on the sidelines.

The second item in that briefing goes one layer down, to the database an agent creates. Supabase, the open-source Postgres platform, announced 150 million dollars of new funding on October 2, in a round led by GIC. It also agreed to acquire Turso. Supabase says about 70 percent of its new databases are now created by agents or AI-driven tools. Those are company-stated figures, and neither the round valuation nor the Turso price was disclosed. The skeptical read is that an agent-created database may be free-tier scaffolding or a throwaway test. Creation counts are not paying databases.

The desk's sharper point is about Turso. Turso is built to run very large numbers of isolated, low-cost databases. Supabase says it adds 4 million databases a month. At that scale, cost per database becomes the economics that matter. The desk infers that database pricing could shift toward metering per database or per tenant. That makes the managed-database price list the next place to watch.

Enterprise Technology ties its two deals together. In both, the asset is the system of record, and the unproven number is monetization. At Schneider, it is the cross-sell revenue. At Supabase, it is paying, retained databases.

One change versus recent days is worth marking. On Monday, this show relayed the Radar's line that a deferred rate hike buys stocks time, not a lower discount rate. Today the Radar updates that line. The curve is not even buying time. A ten-year at a new high for this selloff, with no help from the front end, means valuation support has to come from earnings. That is the thread to carry into the October 14 CPI print.
