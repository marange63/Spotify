Cautious Optimism Briefings

Capital Markets Cross-Asset Radar

Transcript · October 23, 2026

Good morning, it's Friday, October 23rd, 2026. Here's the Capital Markets Radar.

The headline from Thursday's session: the bond market had its biggest one-day rally since March of 2023, and the ten-year Treasury yield is back under 5 percent.

Start by owning yesterday's call. We framed the long end as a global term-premium selloff, with the dollar rising right alongside yields. One session later, that frame did not survive.

Here is the move. The ten-year yield fell 19.3 basis points to 4.953 percent, per the Globe and Mail's tally. The two-year fell nearly as much, down 18.5 basis points to 4.638 percent. The thirty-year lagged a little, but it fell hard too.

That near-parallel shift is the signature of a flight to quality. A term-premium unwind would be led by the long end, with the front end sitting still. Instead the whole curve moved together, so don't let anyone sell you a steepening or flattening narrative off this session.

Now put it against the arc. The ten-year peaked at about 5.36 percent on October 7th, its highest since April of 2002. In barely two weeks, the long end went from pricing a global duration problem to pricing fear.

The dollar was the other half of yesterday's frame, and that broke too. The dollar index sat around 101.8 on Thursday, per Trading Economics, little changed from earlier in the week. So yields collapsed and the dollar barely moved. The rate differential swung hard against the dollar, and a safe-haven bid roughly offset it. The so-what: the story we told yesterday, the world's duration problem showing up in the reserve currency, is on hold. One session is not enough to say what replaces it.

So what caused the fear? Two shocks landed at once. The first was an AI-capex scare. The Nasdaq Composite fell about 2.6 percent, and Meta dropped roughly 7 percent on its earnings.

The second was an oil shock. Brent crude pushed above 111 dollars a barrel, and WTI moved back above 100. Attacks on shipping started it. Then came reporting, via secondhand summaries of a gated New York Times story, that the US and Israel have drafted plans for renewed strikes on Iran after the midterms. Treat that as reported plans, not confirmed policy.

Neither of those shocks is disinflationary. Oil at these levels argues the other way on inflation. So this is a risk-off rally sitting on an unresolved inflation input. It is not a verdict that the inflation fight is over.

One honest gap in the data. We could not get a clean, sourced split between real yields and breakevens for Thursday. If the drop was mostly real yields, that's a growth scare, and growth scares reverse when equities stabilize. If breakevens fell while oil rose, the market is betting the oil spike is transitory. Hold that as an open question, not a settled one.

Note that the two-year fell almost as much as the ten-year. That means the market was repricing the Fed's path itself, not just term premium further out the curve.

That repricing shows up directly in Fed odds. Per CME FedWatch, the market-implied probability of a quarter-point hike at the October 28th meeting has fallen to 7.2 percent. That's the end of a month-long walk-down this desk has tracked since CPI day. To be precise, that meeting has not happened. This is where pricing stands, not a forecast of the outcome.

Yesterday we said the October hold was priced and the risk in the language skewed hawkish. Thursday sharpened that asymmetry. With almost no October hike risk left in the price, the Fed has little dovish surprise to deliver at this meeting. The real surprise risk is a Fed that declines to validate the rally the bond market just threw itself into. Anyone chasing duration here is effectively short two catalysts. One is a firm message from the Chair's press conference. The other is an oil premium that unwinds and lets equities recover.

Now, back to what started the equity leg: Meta. Meta posted its first negative free cash flow in over a decade, negative 2.12 billion dollars for the quarter. At the same time, it raised full-year capex guidance to a range of 135 to 145 billion dollars.

This extends a credit ladder we've been climbing all week. It started with CoreWeave's financing stress. It moved up to Oracle's credit pressure and wider BBB spreads. Now a hyperscaler's own cash flow has gone negative. So the question has shifted. It's no longer just who finances the neoclouds. It's whether the biggest buyers can keep funding this buildout out of their own cash flow.

There's a genuine bull case. The quarter carried a large one-off litigation charge. Revenue still grew strongly, so this is not a demand failure. The bear case is simpler. The guide went up again, so the cash drain is the plan, not an accident. My read: the market may be over-reading one quarter's sign change on free cash flow and under-reading the guide. The guide is the part that compounds.

Positioning adds fuel either way. Goldman's prime brokerage data, for the week through October 15th, show hedge funds had cut net leverage to a one-year low of 48.6 percent. But tech still made up 23.5 percent of Goldman's global prime book, in the 90th percentile of the past five years.

So the book went into the Meta print less levered on net, but still heavily concentrated. And lower net leverage is not lower gross leverage, so don't read it as a clean book. That cuts both ways. There's room to de-gross further if the selling continues. There's also fuel for a sharp snapback if the next hyperscaler print reassures. Meta's reaction is not proof the AI trade is over.

The point to bank here is about discount rates. Thursday handed long-duration tech a lower discount rate, and it didn't help. The market was marking down near-term cash flows faster than it was marking down the rate applied to them. Rate relief alone won't rescue the AI capex trade.

That brings us to the other shock, and the asset that refused to play along. Oil spiked, and gold broke down. Spot gold fell to 3,986.74 dollars in Asian trade this morning, per FXStreet. That's a three-month low, and its first print below 4,000 since July.

This week we described gold's weakness as consistent with rising real yields. That explanation just failed. Yields fell hard on Thursday, which should help a non-yielding asset. Gold fell anyway.

There are two competing reads. One is liquidation: funds selling a liquid winner to meet margin or to de-gross elsewhere. That ties straight back to the Goldman positioning data. The other is that the market is quietly discounting the Iran story. The data we have can't settle it, so hold both.

And there's a reason to take the second read seriously. On Thursday, President Trump said on Truth Social that Iran is making up fake hits and mines to drive up oil before the midterms. He argued that more oil now moves through the Strait of Hormuz than before the conflict began. That's an on-the-record challenge to the shipping-attack narrative feeding the oil bid. Note its scope, though. It addresses the Hormuz attack claims, not the separate report of post-midterm strike plans. And it's a political claim, not verified flow data.

It still matters, because it gives the oil risk premium a named catalyst to unwind. If that happens, it hits both legs of Thursday's move. Oil comes down. And the flight-to-quality bid under Treasuries loses urgency, so yields can back up. Oil is both the threat to the bond rally and the likeliest trigger of its unwind.

Briefly, the digital side. Bitcoin opened Thursday near 84,000 dollars and slid to about 81,800 by early morning. Yesterday we called the spot-led, leverage-light bid fragile, and it gave back its gain within a day. It's now back near the level where the average spot ETF holder roughly breaks even, which is the line to watch. Most of that drop came before the cash session, so read it as an overnight oil move, not a reaction to the Treasury rally.

Now put bitcoin and gold together. Neither caught a haven bid on Thursday. That tells you this was a liquidity and de-risking day across the board, not a rotation into hedges.

So, what to watch. First, whether the ten-year holds below 5 percent into the October 28th Fed decision, which is still pending. Second, whether oil's risk premium holds or unwinds as the dispute over the Hormuz attacks plays out. Third, whether the next hyperscaler print validates or undercuts the message Meta just sent on cash flow. And one carry-over from yesterday: Moody's scheduled review of France is due today, with no outcome as of this recording.

That's the Capital Markets Radar for October 23rd. Stay cautiously optimistic.
