Good morning. It's Wednesday, August 5th, and here's where capital markets stand.

The story of the week is a currency intervention, and it's rare enough that we need to start there. Japan and the United States have confirmed a coordinated, multi-day operation to buy yen, after the currency slid toward a forty-year low. Bank of Japan data point to Japan alone spending roughly thirty-four billion dollars on Friday, August 1st, building on intervention the day before. On the US side, the New York Fed sold euros from its own reserves, not newly created dollars, to buy yen on the Treasury's behalf. That detail matters: it's a way to defend the yen without forcing Japan to dump Treasuries outright to raise dollars, which would have hit the long end of the US curve directly.

What actually pushed the two governments to act together was dollar-yen trading around 163 to 164, a level that had cleared a forty-year low earlier in July before intervention pulled it back sharply, down toward the high 150s. And here's the part worth sitting with: Japan's Ministry of Finance put out its own statement on this, and it's narrower than the market narrative. Finance Minister Katayama said the action countered, quote, excessive volatility and disorderly movements in the Japanese yen, under last September's joint finance-ministers statement. That's it. No mention of US Treasuries, no mention of bond-market stability. The idea that this was really about heading off forced Japanese selling of Treasuries -- and a further spike in yields -- is a credible analyst read, from Wolf Street, not something either government has actually said. So treat the systemic-spillover story as informed inference sitting on top of a much narrower official rationale. And treat the intervention's durability as genuinely unresolved too -- nobody official has said whether the operation is being sterilized or represents a real policy shift, and that's the difference between a durable floor and a one-off smoothing trade.

There is one clean second-order read here, though. Coordinated intervention on this scale, sitting on top of a hawkish Fed, does narrow the odds of another disorderly yen carry-trade unwind of the kind that jolted cross-asset volatility earlier this year. A currency the authorities are actively defending is a less inviting short. So on the margin, this is a modest de-risking for global volatility, even as it complicates the currency picture.

Now, why does this land the way it does. A hawkish Fed should support the dollar, not require a rescue operation for someone else's currency. Remember the backdrop: the Fed held rates at 3.5 to 3.75 percent on July 29th with a 9-3 vote, three regional presidents pushing for a hike, and the ten-year jumped over seven basis points that day while the thirty-year topped 5.2 percent, the highest since 2007. That's a genuinely hawkish signal. And yet the dollar index just closed its worst week in three months, near 100.3, gapping down to an intraday low near 99.29 before paring some of the loss. A hawkish Fed and a weakening dollar breaking down together is not the standard playbook. Read one way, it's positioning unwinding -- crowded dollar longs getting flushed. Read another way, it's the market quietly pricing that the Fed can't actually sustain this stance into a softening labor market. Either way, if the dollar stays soft while real yields rise, that's a combination that can support commodities and emerging-market currencies even as the usual higher-rates, stronger-dollar trade breaks down. Worth watching, not yet resolved.

Equities, meanwhile, are behaving as if none of this matters. The S&P 500 closed Tuesday at a record 7,737, up 1.79 percent, its first record close in two months. The Dow broke 54,000 for the first time, up more than 900 points. The Nasdaq surged 2.59 percent and is up almost 9 percent since a July 29th low. The single biggest driver was Palantir. Its second-quarter revenue rose 92.8 percent year over year to 1.94 billion dollars, US commercial revenue jumped 149 percent to 764 million, and the company posted a Rule-of-40 score of 155 percent. Palantir also raised full-year guidance to a range of 8.15 to 8.16 billion dollars, up from 7.65 to 7.66 billion, and well past the Street's prior estimate of 7.69 billion. So what does that tell you: AI enterprise demand is converting into real, guided-up revenue, not just capex promises, and that's strong enough evidence to offset a real headwind. That headwind is the discount rate. A record index sitting on top of 5-plus percent thirty-year yields compresses the equity risk premium toward nothing, and it does that at the same moment credit spreads sit near cycle tights, pricing almost no stress at all. If long yields keep climbing from here, this rally has less room to shrug it off. And worth flagging: this move is being carried heavily by one marquee name and a geopolitical tailwind, not confirmed broad participation.

That geopolitical tailwind is the Strait of Hormuz, and it's the same variable moving oil. Crude fell more than 5 percent, below 76 dollars a barrel, on hopes of renewed US-Iran diplomacy, then climbed back toward 81.80 as that uncertainty persisted. Treasury Secretary Bessent said a deal to reopen the Strait could come within a day or two, with Qatar circulating a de-escalation proposal. But the price action is the tell here, not the headline: oil round-tripping straight back above 80 dollars says the market is pricing continued uncertainty, not resolution. That means the equity rally and the oil move are both leaning on the same unresolved outcome -- a real Hormuz setback would hit both legs of this trade at once, through energy costs and through risk appetite simultaneously.

One market is conspicuously not joining this rally: crypto. Bitcoin is trading around 63,500 dollars, still down 44 percent from its October 2025 all-time high near 128,200. A big piece of that lag traces to the CLARITY Act, the digital-asset market-structure bill. It's cleared the House and the Senate Banking Committee, but it still hasn't reached the Senate floor, and reporting genuinely conflicts on whether it even gets a vote before Friday's recess. What isn't in dispute is the market's own read: Polymarket's odds on the bill becoming law this year have collapsed to roughly 28 percent, down from an 82 percent peak back in February. So what that tells you is crypto's underperformance against a record-high equity tape isn't a broad risk-off signal -- it's a specific regulatory overhang, and it's getting worse, not better, as this legislation stalls heading into the August recess.

Step back and here's the shape of the week: a hawkish Fed, a weak dollar needing intervention, record equities on one blockbuster earnings print, and crypto stuck behind a stalled bill. That configuration holds together only as long as two things stay unresolved -- Friday's July jobs report, which will test whether the Fed can actually sustain this hawkish stance, and an actual, confirmed Hormuz deal rather than another day-or-two promise. Watch both closely.
