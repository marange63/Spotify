Cautious Optimism Briefings

Frontier AI Lab Competition

Transcript · September 24, 2026

Good morning. It's Thursday, September 24th, and this is your frontier AI labs briefing.

Start with a correction. Yesterday this show named OpenAI's September 29th DevDay as the dated test of whether OpenAI would answer Anthropic's Opus 5.5 on price. OpenAI had already answered, on the same day Opus 5.5 shipped. On September 22nd, OpenAI cut API pricing for GPT-6 Sol and Luna and called the cut permanent, not promotional. We missed it. The price war is now two-sided, and it moved faster than the calendar we set for it.

Look at how differently the two labs cut. GPT-6 Sol now runs two dollars per million input tokens and ten dollars per million output tokens. That's down from four and twenty for the GPT-5.6 Sol it replaces, according to pricing reported by VentureBeat. GPT-6 Luna, the small efficiency-tier model, dropped to ten cents input and fifty cents output, from twenty cents and a dollar twenty. Call it half off or better, across the board.

Anthropic's Opus 5.5, as we covered yesterday, took a different route. It cut list price twenty percent, to four dollars in and twenty dollars out. Its steepest cut went to cache reads, down sixty percent to twenty cents per million. So these are two different theories of where the invoice gets decided. OpenAI is betting the fight is won on the sticker price of every token. Anthropic is betting it's won on the context an agent re-reads over and over, the cache-heavy pattern of long coding and tool-use sessions.

The phrase "permanent rather than promotional" is itself a competitive signal. It tells enterprise buyers not to wait for the price to snap back, and to budget against the new floor now. That's a company pricing for share and locking in the floor, not chasing a headline. What neither lab has disclosed is what these cuts do to gross margin. OpenAI's per-token revenue on its volume tier just halved, and unless serving cost fell as fast, that is the number a funding round or a prospectus will have to answer.

On OpenAI's reliability claim, be careful. OpenAI says GPT-6 Sol makes roughly half as many mistakes as GPT-5.6 Sol. But that comes from an unpublished internal evaluation, built from de-identified ChatGPT conversations where users had flagged factual errors. Nobody outside OpenAI can check it. And OpenAI's own wording is that Sol is "approaching" Astra-level reliability, not that it has arrived. Treat it as a vendor claim, not a measurement.

Now the outside grade this show asked for yesterday, and some of the coverage has it wrong. The Register reported that Artificial Analysis has Opus 5.5 tied with GPT-6 Astra. Artificial Analysis's own comparison page says otherwise. It puts Opus 5.5 at 58 on its aggregate Intelligence Index, and GPT-6 Astra at 53. That's a five-point lead, not a tie. Artificial Analysis's own write-up calls Opus 5.5's score the highest it has measured, by several points.

That 53 for Astra is the same figure this show reported on Sunday and again on Tuesday. So there's no sign the index was rescaled this time. Opus 5.5 is simply the new high score.

There is a genuine tie, but only on one component. On Terminal-Bench 4.0, the agentic coding test, Artificial Analysis describes Opus 5.5 as level with Astra. That is the outside check on a claim Anthropic made at launch, that Opus 5.5 matches Astra on that benchmark. The parity held up. Anthropic's cost-per-task claim on that test has not yet been checked.

So the honest version of this story has layers. On the one independent aggregate available, Anthropic's cheaper Opus tier now leads OpenAI's flagship outright. That's stronger than Anthropic's own line, that Opus 5.5 performs at the level of Fable 5.1 on most work. But one aggregate index doesn't settle real enterprise agentic workloads. And it doesn't settle the comparison that actually sets enterprise unit economics, which is Sol against Opus 5.5, the two workhorse tiers both labs just repriced. Nobody outside either lab has run that matchup. So the so-what is this: Anthropic now owns the leaderboard headline, and OpenAI owns the cheaper sticker, and the matchup that decides where agentic coding traffic gets routed is still unmeasured.

The pressure from below hasn't gone away either. Two weeks ago, DeepSeek's open-weight V4.1 Flash claimed benchmark wins over GPT-5.6 Sol and Claude Opus 5. Those are DeepSeek's own numbers, and both of those comparators were superseded on September 22nd. So DeepSeek's claim of beating the US labs went stale in twelve days. The more telling comparison now is on price. DeepSeek's off-peak rate is fifteen cents per million input tokens on a cache miss, and sixty cents out. GPT-6 Luna now lists at ten cents in and fifty cents out, slightly under that. Two caveats matter. Luna is a small efficiency-tier model, not a capability peer of a 552-billion-parameter model. And DeepSeek's off-peak rates double at peak. Still, the direction is clear. The US labs' cheapest tiers are now pricing at the floor a Chinese open-weight lab set.

Cheaper tokens only matter if there's somewhere to run them, and that's the next constraint. CNBC reported last week that both OpenAI and Anthropic are exploring smaller data center deployments, roughly twenty to thirty megawatts each. Anthropic has sounded out sites in the UK and the Nordics, and OpenAI in the Nordics. The goal is capacity that comes online sooner than their gigawatt-scale projects. Those deals are explored, not signed, and no dollar figure is attached. A few tens of megawatts is tiny next to gigawatt commitments, so read this as a signal of urgency, not a material capacity addition.

The reason for the urgency is timing. Anthropic's Nscale lease in West Virginia, which we covered on Sunday, doesn't come online until the end of 2027. OpenAI says it has passed its original ten-gigawatt Stargate commitment and has since added three gigawatts in Georgia and eight in Ohio. But those are development commitments, not energized capacity. So both labs just told enterprise buyers to budget against a permanently lower price, while their own capacity mostly arrives next year.

That raises an open question this show can't answer yet. Are these cuts funded by real efficiency gains, or will they end up rationed through rate limits once demand catches the discount? Nothing disclosed separates the two. It also makes compute access its own competitive race, distinct from model quality. The winner there is whoever energizes capacity fastest, and labs that lease third-party capacity are exposed to someone else's construction schedule.

One more lever before we close: distribution. On Tuesday, this show noted that xAI's Grok 4.7 trails the leaders on the independent index and is priced into the cost lane. Also on September 22nd, xAI turned on Grok Bot inside Tesla vehicles. Drivers can delegate multi-step tasks by voice, like food orders, restaurant bookings and calendar management, but only with a SuperGrok Heavy subscription. There's no adoption data yet, and the premium gate limits reach. The point is that a lab losing on the leaderboard can still turn a car fleet it already reaches into a channel for its agent that nobody else has.

So, three things to watch. First, DevDay on September 29th is no longer the test of whether OpenAI competes on price, because it already has. It's now the test of whether OpenAI competes on tooling and lock-in, Codex against Claude Code, the layer that matters once price and capability converge. Second, an independent head-to-head between GPT-6 Sol and Opus 5.5 at matched effort settings, the pairing that actually prices enterprise workloads. Third, the first sign of rate limits or capacity rationing on these newly cheap tiers, which would tell us the cuts were a bet made ahead of supply.

That's your briefing. Have a good Thursday.
