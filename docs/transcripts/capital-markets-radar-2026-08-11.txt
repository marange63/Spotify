Good morning, it's Tuesday, August 11th, and this is your Capital Markets Radar.

Start with the story that actually matters this week: rates just round-tripped a huge move in ten days, and that tells you more about the market's conviction than any single data point did. On August 1st, July nonfarm payrolls landed at negative 23,000 against a consensus call for a gain of 83,000 — a genuinely ugly miss. Markets read it as decisively dovish. The ten-year Treasury yield dropped to 4.641%, the S&P 500 put in its best week since the spring Iran-ceasefire rally, up 3.59% with tech leading at plus 7.22%, and gold surged 7.13% in a week, its largest single-week gain since the peak of the Iran conflict. Expectations for a September rate hike, which had been a real possibility heading into the report, fell hard on that print.

Then oil took the story back. By August 10th, talks to reopen the Strait of Hormuz to full shipping broke down — Iran's Foreign Ministry said the U.S. naval blockade has to end first before normal transit resumes. WTI jumped about 5% to $82.13 a barrel and Brent rose a similar amount to $87.72. That repriced the inflation outlook fast: the ten-year yield climbed back above 4.7%, to 4.736% on the live quote by August 11th — its highest level since January — and expectations for a September hike partially recovered from their post-jobs-report low. I want to be precise about the size of that swing, because the temptation is to call it a round trip from zero to fifty-fifty — that overstates it. The betting had real hike odds priced in before the jobs report too; the miss knocked them down, and oil has now clawed some but not all of that move back. The honest read is a real reversal in direction, not a return from despair to conviction.

So what does that mean. It means the curve right now is less a labor-market read than a geopolitical-oil bet, and that bet can move again on the next Hormuz headline. And notice the cross-current it sets up. Growth data is soft — the July jobs number was outright negative — but oil is pushing the inflation side back up at the same time. That is the stagflationary squeeze, and it is the one combination that leaves a rate-sensitive, long-duration equity market nowhere comfortable to hide. You cannot lean on rate cuts to rescue weak growth if energy is reviving the inflation print. So watch oil, not the next jobs print, for where September expectations go from here.

Now, why did the dovish trade fade so fast in the first place. The answer is sitting one level up, at the Fed itself. This was the first FOMC meeting under new Chair Kevin Warsh, confirmed by the Senate 54 to 45 back in May — the most divisive confirmation vote in the Fed's history. On July 29th, the committee held rates at 3.50 to 3.75%, but three members — Beth Hammack, Neel Kashkari, and Lorie Logan — dissented in favor of hiking immediately by a quarter point. That's an unusually hawkish dissent pattern for a meeting that ended in a hold. The statement itself said inflation remains elevated relative to the 2% goal, in part reflecting supply shocks, including energy.

Put those two things together and you get the reaction function that explains this week: a Fed leadership that already has three voices pushing to hike, sitting on a statement that names energy as a supply-shock inflation risk, right as energy prices reaccelerate. That's a committee primed to fade a soft data point faster than markets are used to from the Powell era. So when oil moved, it didn't just push commodity and inflation-breakeven traders around — it pushed the market's read of what Warsh's Fed does next. The dovish trade off the jobs report was always going to be fragile against that backdrop; now we've seen how fragile.

That same policy-divergence theme is showing up loudly in currencies. Japanese and U.S. authorities ran a record intervention in the yen over the past week. USD/JPY had slid from above 162.80 to a low near 155.35 over five trading days, and Bank of Japan data showed Tokyo spending about ¥5.33 trillion on one day's operation, after a reported record ¥8.45 trillion the day before that. That's the kind of single-day intervention size that only shows up when a policy gap has become acute. And notably, the pair has already drifted back up past the 157 to 158 area since — intervention can cap the move, but it can't repair the underlying policy gap between a Bank of Japan still on hold and a Fed that may be leaning toward hikes.

So what. If Warsh's Fed genuinely tilts hawkish while the BOJ stays accommodative, that policy gap widens, not narrows, and the dollar side of that trade stays live. Carry trades funded in yen have a history of unwinding messily when that gap reopens after intervention — that's the tail risk to watch into the September 16th FOMC, not just for FX desks but for anyone running leveraged positions funded in yen.

Let's close on the one market that isn't pricing any of this stress at all: credit. Private credit continues to deteriorate by the numbers. Fitch put the private credit default rate at a record 6% through the second quarter, and at Blue Owl Capital's flagship fund, the defaulted-loan share hit 2.8%, its highest in at least five years. Redemption requests at the top non-traded BDCs averaged 13% of assets in the first quarter and 14% in the second, forcing most managers to activate gates. And there's a new data point worth banking: Boston Fed research published this month found the share of BDC loans structured as payment-in-kind — meaning borrowers paying interest with more debt instead of cash — rose from about 5.4% in early 2022 to 9.8% in the first quarter of this year. That's a classic late-cycle marker of borrowers who can't service cash interest anymore.

None of that is showing up in public high-yield spreads. The high-yield index sits around 270 to 280 basis points over Treasuries, in the richest decile of its history against a long-run median near 450 basis points. Public credit investors are pricing something close to perfection while a parallel, less visible corner of the credit market is quietly building up leverage and payment-in-kind structures. Either that divergence means public investors genuinely see private credit's problems as contained and idiosyncratic, or public high-yield is underpricing a risk the private market is already flashing. Given everything else moving this week — a Fed reaction function that's gotten less forgiving, and an oil shock that can revive inflation risk on short notice — betting on "contained" looks like the more exposed position.

So the thread through today: conviction on the Fed path is thin, the curve is trading oil headlines as much as labor data, currency markets are already showing the strain of that policy divergence, and credit is the one place still refusing to price any of it. The thing to watch from here isn't the next single data print — it's whether the Hormuz premium holds into September, and whether rate volatility keeps round-tripping the way it just did.

That's the Capital Markets Radar for August 11th. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll see you next time.
