Good morning, it's Friday, August 7th, and this is Cautious Optimism Briefings.

Taiwan spent this week doing something it has never done before: physically rehearsing what happens to its economy in the opening hours of a war. The Han Kuang Exercise, Taiwan's largest annual war game, is running August 5th through the 14th, and for the first time it includes a simulated wartime relocation of weapons production. The target is the Armaments Bureau's 202nd Arsenal, along with private-sector contractors that handle whole-system assembly. The idea is to test whether Taiwan can keep producing weapons if front-line and coastal industrial hubs take a first strike. The Ministry of National Defense's own language is that the drills simulate an emergency mobilization order under which the 202nd Arsenal and civilian manufacturers would relocate production lines and keep output running. Taiwan's public broadcaster separately confirmed that on August 6th, the 202nd Plant actually drilled two named procedures: production-line relocation and production conversion. So this was a conducted exercise, not just a plan on paper.

Here's the caveat that matters most, and the research could not close it even after a dedicated second pass: every source, in English and in Chinese, pairs the word "simulate" with "relocate." No statement from the defense ministry confirms whether actual production equipment was physically trucked to a rear-area site, or whether this was a command-post and tabletop exercise testing the mobilization order itself. Both readings survive. That ambiguity is the story as much as the drill is — Taiwan is telling the public it tested this, without telling the public how physically real the test was. And nobody has named which private contractors were involved; the sourcing only ever says "civilian manufacturers."

The second half of the drill is more concrete. Starting this week, the military is deliberately degrading mobile internet speeds for roughly seventy-three percent of Taiwan's population, in staged regional blackouts — central Taiwan on August 10th, northern Taiwan on August 13th — leaving only voice calls and text messages available. One detail closes a gap from earlier reporting: southern Taiwan is exempted, and the reason is explicit. The south's air-raid drills run at ten in the morning, when the Taiwan Stock Exchange is open, and a mobile-data slowdown at that hour could disrupt real-time stock trading conducted on phones. Taipei is willing to degrade its own civilian communications as a wartime test, but not willing to touch the stock exchange while it's live. That's a small detail, but it tells you a lot about which parts of the economy Taiwan treats as untouchable even in rehearsal.

There's a wider signal in the drill itself. A government deliberately throttling its own citizens' mobile data on a published schedule, just to see what survives, is a civil-defense template other frontline states can copy. South Korea and the Baltics face the same first-hour question about whether their civilian communications hold when the network is jammed or saturated. Taiwan is effectively writing that playbook in public, and its allies will be reading the results as closely as Beijing is.

None of this proves Taiwan's defense-industrial base can actually survive a first strike — the exercise runs through August 14th and there are no results yet, only design and intent. But the direction matters. This is Taipei moving past rhetoric about resolve and into physically quantifying its own fragility, for the first time, in public. If the exercise exposes real gaps, expect louder arguments for allied backup fabrication and reshoring outside Taiwan — the same silicon-shield erosion this show has tracked all week through TSMC's Arizona buildout and an unprosecutable chip-smuggling case.

From a defense-industrial base being tested under simulated fire, to the power base that AI and defense production actually run on: Texas. ERCOT's own chief executive, Pablo Vegas, told the state Senate's Business and Commerce Committee on July 29th that statewide electricity demand is forecast to reach roughly 175,000 megawatts by 2032 — nearly double where the grid stands today. And that record is fresh. ERCOT's grid hit an all-time hourly peak of 91.1 gigawatts on July 22nd, six percent above the prior high set back in 2023. Vegas is telling lawmakers to plan for roughly double even that, in six years. Now the caveat that changes how much weight to put on it: that's Vegas's testimony, not a confirmed published ERCOT forecast. He attributed the surge to a wave of new data centers, population growth, and industrial projects. On the delivery side, ERCOT added 16,000 megawatts of new generation last year, mostly solar and batteries; solar capacity more than doubled between 2023 and 2025, and battery storage more than tripled. Vegas's own warning to the committee was blunt: without a faster build-out, Texas runs into more frequent scarcity events.

Here's the skeptic's point, and it's the same one this show has made about Texas all week: at the grid's record evening peak, only thirty-two percent of the power came from solar. Forty-eight percent came from natural gas. A doubling target built mostly on solar-and-battery additions still leans on gas for firm capacity when the sun goes down. And this is the same grid whose interconnection queue this show walked through on Tuesday — roughly 474 gigawatts of requests against a peak barely above ninety. Most of that queue is padded, speculative filings. Announced generation and interconnection queues are not delivered, dispatchable megawatts. And the clock is real: federal regulators gave the grid operators until August 17th to reform how they connect large loads like data centers, a deadline this show flagged Thursday that is still unresolved. For markets, the read cuts both ways: a real doubling is bullish for ERCOT-exposed independent power producers, gas-turbine makers, and battery suppliers; a stalled one leaves most of that queue as stranded optimism. If Texas actually executes this doubling on a six-year timeline, it becomes the strongest real-world rebuttal to the argument that grids can't keep pace with AI demand. If it doesn't, this becomes the same announcement-versus-delivered-capacity gap this show has flagged for four straight days now — just measured in gigawatts instead of legislation.

That gas dependence is also why oil prices belong in the same briefing. Brent crude is trading near $83.64 a barrel as of August 6th, up roughly sixteen percent over the past month and about twenty-four percent year over year. The specific driver is not established — this is not a fresh sanctions story, whatever recycled headlines suggest — so treat the move as real but the cause as unconfirmed. J.P. Morgan's own research still forecasts Brent averaging $86 in the third quarter, easing to $80 in the fourth, and $78 by year-end, which tells you the bank reads today's level as a spike, not a new floor. But as long as ERCOT's firm capacity rides on gas, grid economics and the Federal Reserve's own inflation math stay tied to whatever oil does next.

The throughline this week: the physical base of power got stress-tested, twice, and in both cases the delivered capacity is still unproven. Taiwan rehearsed surviving a first strike without confirming how physically real that rehearsal was. Texas's own grid operator conceded it must nearly double capacity in six years while still leaning on gas to keep the lights on tonight. Watch both for results, not announcements.
