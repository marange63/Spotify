Cautious Optimism Briefings

Strategic Power, Energy, and Institutional Change

Transcript · August 12, 2026

Good morning. It's Wednesday, August 12th, and this is your strategic power briefing.

The Federal Reserve's independence is under direct political attack again, and this time the target is the Board itself, not just the reaction function. In a letter dated August 6th, the White House told Fed Governor Lisa Cook that President Trump is, quote, "considering removing" her, and gave her until August 26th to respond to a mortgage-fraud allegation that's been circulating for a year. This is round two. Trump fired Cook last August, a district court blocked it, and this past June the Supreme Court ruled five-to-four that she could keep her seat while her lawsuit proceeds. But Chief Justice Roberts included a footnote saying nothing stops Trump from, quote, "trying again," provided she gets proper notice and a chance to contest it. That's exactly what this letter is: a second attempt, engineered to survive the legal test the Court just laid out. Cook's attorney, Abbe Lowell, says the allegations are as baseless now as they were a year ago, and calls the move an attempt to interfere with Fed independence. Be precise about what this is and isn't. Cook has not been removed. She has three weeks to respond, and the far more likely near-term outcome is another injunction fight, not a vacant seat. But the stakes are real: this is the clearest test yet of whether the executive branch can reshape the Board's composition, and the Board's composition sets the reaction function that underwrites Treasury market credibility and dollar reserve status.

And the precedent reaches past the Fed. A second Supreme Court round on for-cause removal wouldn't just settle one governor's job. It would redefine the firing protections that shield every independent agency head across the government. It's also the kind of slow erosion that, at the margin, gives foreign central banks one more reason to keep diversifying reserves away from Treasuries. That's not a headline move. It's the direction that matters.

Here's why the timing matters. Today's July CPI report, out this morning from the Bureau of Labor Statistics, showed core inflation — which strips out food and energy — rising three-point-one percent year over year, up from two-point-nine percent in June. That's the largest monthly core gain since January, at three-tenths of a percent. Headline inflation held at two-point-seven percent, matching June, so the story isn't the headline, it's underneath it. Tariff pass-through is now visible in the data: footwear prices jumped one-point-four percent in a single month, the sharpest footwear increase since April of 2021, with furniture and bedding up nine-tenths of a percent and apparel also rising. That's real cost migrating from tariffs into consumer prices.

Notice where the pass-through is landing — footwear, furniture, apparel. Those are discretionary goods, and that concentration is the signature of tariff cost, not broad demand. It also marks the category most exposed if tariff rates climb further or new sectoral duties land. So this print doesn't just feed the next FOMC meeting. It feeds TIPS breakevens and the inflation-expectation side of the debasement trade. And it's landing against a labor market that's now soft: July payrolls fell twenty-three thousand, and the prior two months were revised down a combined one-hundred-three thousand. So the Fed is boxed in — cooling jobs argue for cuts, reaccelerating core inflation argues against them — and that dual-mandate tension is exactly the moment where a politically reshaped Board would matter most. If a governor seen as more compliant replaces Cook, the market read won't be about one seat, it'll be about whether the Fed can still credibly resist pressure to cut into rising inflation. That's the kind of doubt that shows up in term premium on long Treasuries and reinforces the gold and Bitcoin debasement trade. But I'd stress: no confirmed market reaction to either the Cook letter or today's print has been reported yet. That's analysis, not observed price action.

Now pivot from the monetary fight to the industrial one, because this week also tested Washington's other lever: building capacity outside China's control. USA Rare Earth reported second-quarter results Monday confirming a roughly two-point-eight-billion-dollar definitive deal to acquire Brazil's Serra Verde Group — the only scaled non-Asian producer of all four magnetic rare earths — targeted to close in the third quarter. The company also has definitive agreements with the Commerce Department unlocking up to one-point-six billion dollars under the CHIPS Act: up to two-hundred-seventy-seven million in direct federal funding and up to one-point-three billion in loan capacity. Here's the caveat that matters: that money is disbursed against milestones, not handed over up front, and Serra Verde hasn't closed yet. This is the industrial-policy test in miniature — signed agreements are not producing metal. If Serra Verde does close, it would hand a US-listed company control of scaled non-China magnet supply, a real dent in Beijing's leverage over defense and EV production. But right now it's a commitment on paper, layered on top of the roughly three-billion-dollar minerals package the administration touted earlier this week.

And the structure here is a template. If the milestones actually get hit, the same milestone-gated CHIPS financing can be pointed at scandium, graphite, copper — the rest of the critical-minerals map. That's the positive read-through for other North American magnet plays, names like MP Materials and Niron, and for the private-credit funds financing defense-adjacent supply. But the whole template rests on that one word: if. Milestones can slip as easily as they can be met. Watch execution, not announcements.

That same paper-versus-delivered test runs through sanctions on Russia. The Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control has a wind-down license for Lukoil-related transactions — General License 131H — that expires at one minute past midnight Eastern on August 22nd. Any refiner in India, China, or Turkey still working through Lukoil-linked crude has to have separate OFAC authorization in hand by then. Separately, the EU agreed its 21st sanctions package on July 23rd, targeting finance, energy, and military-industrial sectors and closing third-party evasion loopholes. History here is a caution: after Washington's October 2025 designations on Rosneft and Lukoil, buyers largely routed around them through smaller intermediaries rather than actually curtailing purchases. August 22nd is the next real-world test of whether sanctions produce delivered supply cuts or just paperwork.

And there's a deeper pattern in the sanctions story. The formal instruments face fraying political will in Europe — the 21st package landed amid open concern that collective resolve is starting to weaken. The more durable pressure on Russian revenue may be the slower channel that doesn't need a vote: the physical attrition of Russia's own refining and export infrastructure. Paper leverage needs consensus to renew. The kinetic channel does not. That contrast is worth holding onto as the August 22nd cliff approaches.

So the ledger for this week: America's monetary credibility is being contested from inside, its industrial counterweight to China remains mostly milestone-gated promises rather than operating capacity, and its sanctions leverage on Russia faces its next hard test in ten days. The common thread is the gap between announced power and delivered power. A reshaped Board is not yet a captured Fed, a signed CHIPS agreement is not yet refined metal, and a license expiring is not yet a barrel that stops moving. Watch August 22nd for Lukoil, and August 26th for Cook's response — two dates that will tell you whether this week's pressure produces durable change or just more paper.

That's the briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
