Cautious Optimism Briefings

Enterprise Technology & Software

Transcript · September 5, 2026

Good morning. It's Saturday, September 5th, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

Start with Hewlett Packard Enterprise, which reported its fiscal third quarter on September 2nd, and which gives us the clearest read yet on where the AI infrastructure cycle is actually binding. Revenue was 12.2 billion dollars, up 34 percent from a year earlier, and came in above the high end of the company's own guidance. Non-GAAP gross margin hit a record 40.1 percent. On the surface, that's an unambiguously strong print. One housekeeping note before we build on it: HPE's own release could not be retrieved directly for this briefing, so every figure and quote here comes through secondary coverage of the release and the earnings call. Treat them as reported rather than as read off the filing.

Here's the turn. HPE's management said on the call that order momentum is running three and a half times faster than revenue growth, and that supply availability, not customer demand, is the primary constraint. They named the specific bottleneck: DDR5 memory and NAND flash shortages, which they expect to persist into fiscal 2027. In their words, supply constraints are limiting the company's ability to convert strong customer demand into revenue, particularly in networking and AI systems. That's a company telling you, on the record, that the problem isn't selling AI hardware. It's building it. This is the same story this beat has now traced across the stack this week, from record memory pricing in the semiconductor briefing to Dell's backlog of AI servers it has booked but not yet shipped, which led this show on Tuesday. The shortage has climbed from a component price into a vendor's forward guidance.

And that guidance is the headline worth sitting with. HPE guided full fiscal 2026 revenue growth of 34 to 37 percent, and then guided fiscal 2027 down to 13 to 17 percent. Both are guides, not results. The easy read is demand normalization, that the AI server surge cools off after a 2026 peak. But that is not what the company itself is saying. Management is explicitly framing next year's growth ceiling as a supply problem, not a demand one, and is pursuing multi-year supply agreements to fix it. Hold that at arm's length, because a supply story is also the more flattering story for a management team guiding growth down by more than half. Still, if it is the accurate read, the revenue isn't lost, it's deferred, and the order book is worth more than the guide implies.

What would tell the two apart? Watch the order line, not the revenue line. If orders keep growing while shipped revenue lags, that is a conversion problem and the backlog is money in escrow. If order growth itself rolls over next quarter, the supply framing was cover for softening demand. Those two readings imply opposite valuations for the whole AI server cohort, and one quarter of order data will separate them.

Note too that this isn't a margin story, at least not yet. Gross margin was a record 40.1 percent in the same quarter the company is flagging a component shortage. And HPE put no number on the cost side at all. It quantified no basis-point hit to gross margin from memory and NAND inflation, no dollar figure, no stated fiscal 2027 cost expectation. That absence is worth naming rather than filling in. The constraint being described today is on volume, not on unit economics. Keep those two facts separate: a company can be squeezed on how much it can ship while still printing its best margin ever. For an enterprise buyer, the practical implication is timing. If the vendor closest to the shortage says it persists into fiscal 2027, the pricing power sits with the memory suppliers and the delivery risk sits with you. That argues for locking hardware delivery slots and price protection into contracts now, rather than discovering the queue at renewal.

So if a physical-input bottleneck is now shaping how vendors guide 2027, the natural question is which vendors don't carry that bill. That split showed up in cybersecurity on Friday, when this show covered Palo Alto Networks: hosting and memory costs pressing directly on its gross margin, and next year's platform growth guided down sharply as a result. Now look at CrowdStrike, which sits on the other side of that line entirely.

CrowdStrike's fiscal second quarter, reported August 31st, raised its full-year net-new annual recurring revenue growth guidance by 630 basis points, to 34 percent at the midpoint. Be precise about the metric. That's not a revenue guide and not an ending-ARR guide. It's specifically the growth rate of new business added each year, and it's a large upward revision. The quarter itself produced a company record 332.8 million dollars of net new ARR. Accounts that have adopted Falcon Flex, the bundled licensing model spanning identity, cloud and endpoint modules, now carry 2.29 billion dollars of ending ARR, up 101 percent year over year, though that figure is flattered by a small prior-year base and by existing renewals converting into the Flex envelope, which the release does not separate out.

So in the same fortnight, two security platforms guided fiscal 2027 in opposite directions, and the divergence is the story rather than either print alone. But keep the comparison honest. CrowdStrike discloses no cost-to-serve or hosting-cost figure at all. The contrast with a vendor citing memory and hosting costs is currently a difference in what gets disclosed, not a proven difference in underlying economics. The company also reports no separate figure for how much of this growth comes from its Charlotte AI product, so the platform-consolidation story and the AI story are bundled in a way the numbers don't let you unbundle. There is a consolidation implication here too. The vendor guiding growth down while its own input costs rise has a more expensive currency for buying its way to a platform than the one raising guidance. Expect the acquisition initiative in security to follow the guidance direction.

Now the far end of that spectrum: a vendor whose AI story carries essentially no infrastructure bill of its own. Zoom reported its fiscal second quarter on September 1st. Total revenue was 1.28 billion dollars, up 4.9 percent, the slowest growth in this earnings cluster. But underneath that headline, enterprise revenue, the managed-account side of the business, was 787.5 million dollars, up 7.8 percent year over year. That is the fastest enterprise growth in three years, and enterprise is now 62 percent of the company. Online, meaning self-serve and small business, grew 0.6 percent.

Alongside that acceleration, paid adoption of Zoom's AI Companion rose 184 percent year over year, and its My Notes feature passed 1.5 million licensed users within months of launch. This is the quarter Zoom began charging for AI features it had given away free for eleven straight quarters, and the enterprise segment is where that bet is showing up.

Two caveats matter. First, 184 percent is an adoption count, the same activation metric this beat has already discounted elsewhere this week, and Zoom hasn't disclosed what dollar revenue that adoption generates. Second, the split itself is the real finding. Enterprise accelerating while self-serve is flat says the AI upsell is working as a renewal-negotiation lever inside accounts with a dedicated sales team, not as a product-led growth engine that pulls in new self-serve buyers. That generalizes: any vendor whose revenue is weighted to self-serve should expect converting a free AI feature into a paid tier to produce churn defense, not expansion. And total company growth is still under 5 percent, so a fast-growing enterprise segment is currently carrying a business that in aggregate is barely moving. The one hard number in Zoom's favor is cash. Full-year free cash flow guidance was raised to a range of 1.78 to 1.82 billion dollars.

Pull it together. This week's fiscal 2027 guidance isn't sorting enterprise vendors by how big an AI number they can put on a slide. It's sorting them by how much memory, NAND, or hosting capacity sits inside their own cost of goods sold. HPE, closest to the physical constraint, guided down sharply. CrowdStrike, disclosing none of that exposure, guided up. And Zoom, whose AI product is a pure software feature attach with no infrastructure bill of its own, found its fastest enterprise growth in three years exactly where a sales team could turn a free feature into a paid one. The thing to watch next is whether HPE's fiscal 2027 range moves once supply, rather than demand, becomes the variable the market is actually pricing.

That's the briefing. Have a good weekend.
