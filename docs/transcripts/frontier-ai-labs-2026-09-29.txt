Good morning, it's Tuesday, September 29th, and this is your frontier AI labs briefing.

This week the frontier AI contest isn't about who has the best model. It's about who controls access: access to another lab's outputs, access to silicon, and access to the capital that pays for compute. Beijing moved on the first, Alibaba is building around the second, and SoftBank just showed the price of the third. In between, Anthropic is hardening the door everyone else was allegedly walking through.

Start with the lead. China's Cyberspace Administration has opened a probe into DeepSeek and Moonshot, two of its own AI champions. The trigger was a September 10th threat-intelligence report from Anthropic. It alleges both companies covertly routed huge volumes of queries to Claude, in what Anthropic calls illicit distillation. These are Anthropic's own numbers, and nobody has independently verified them. By Anthropic's count, Moonshot ran more than 23 million exchanges with Claude through 5,380 fraudulent accounts. DeepSeek ran more than 12.1 million exchanges in a single 14-day window in July. Regulators first summoned seven companies, then narrowed the probe to DeepSeek and Moonshot.

Now the skeptical turn, because the obvious reading is probably wrong. The obvious reading is that Beijing has confirmed the charge, and that Chinese models borrowed their efficiency edge from Claude. But look at what Beijing is actually worried about. According to reporting from Hong Kong's The Standard, the fear is that routing queries through Anthropic may have leaked sensitive military, policing and corporate information to the US. That is a data-security case under China's own laws. It is not an intellectual-property case on Anthropic's behalf.

There's a tell in who got dropped. Alibaba was one of the seven labs named in Anthropic's report. Secondary summaries of that report describe Alibaba's campaign as by far the largest. We could not confirm that figure against Anthropic's original document, so hold it loosely. But if it's right, a regulator validating the distillation complaint would start with Alibaba. Instead, Alibaba sits outside the narrowed probe. So Beijing appears to be policing which data leaves the country, on its own criteria. It is not endorsing a US lab's grievance.

That leaves the distillation question itself genuinely open. One reading is that these labs' cost and capability edge is partly borrowed. Tens of millions of exchanges could matter a lot for post-training, even if they matter little for pretraining. The other reading is that the harvesting was a marginal shortcut layered on top of real efficiency work. Nothing in hand measures which is true. Nor is there any finding yet. Regulators have only sent officials to interview executives and staff, and neither company has responded publicly.

Hold one contrast next to this. OpenAI and Anthropic are reportedly negotiating a pact to let each lab probe the other's commercial models for vulnerabilities, with guarantees that neither keeps the other's data. It isn't signed. But it is sanctioned, logged access between rivals. What DeepSeek and Moonshot are accused of is the opposite: covert access through fraudulent accounts.

Here's the second-order effect, and it's an inference. If Beijing tightens controls on cross-border use of US model APIs, that becomes a second one-sided veto. On September 25th this show reported Beijing, not Washington, as the side declining licensed Nvidia H200s. The same logic may now reach model access. For Chinese labs, that would cut off a cheap source of post-training signal just as they're pushed onto domestic chips. That raises their near-term cost of keeping pace. It also compounds a problem we flagged on the 24th, when DeepSeek's benchmark wins over US models went stale within twelve days. And for US labs, detecting and publicizing distillation becomes both a commercial moat and a policy argument. Expect more threat-intel disclosures aimed at competitors. So the takeaway: API access to a frontier model is now a governed, contested resource, not just a product you sell.

If Beijing is closing off both borrowed chips and borrowed outputs, the alternative is to build the whole stack at home. Alibaba laid out exactly that plan last week at its Apsara conference. Its chip unit, T-Head, unveiled the Zhenwu V900. Alibaba says it delivers three times the compute of its previous chip, the M890. That is Alibaba's own figure, measured against its own prior part, with no independent benchmark. Alibaba hasn't disclosed absolute performance or who fabs the chip. So a three-x generational jump can't be converted into parity with an H200 or a Blackwell-class part.

On models, Alibaba confirmed Qwen 4 is in training. Its roadmap has Qwen 4.5 and Qwen 5 scaling to 5 to 10 trillion parameters. Those are roadmap targets, not shipped models. And parameter count tells you less than it used to, given how hard Chinese labs have leaned on efficiency. Announcing scale is also positioning, aimed at domestic policymakers and investors as much as at engineers. The so-what: this is the route that needs neither Nvidia licenses nor US model outputs, but for now it's a self-sufficiency claim, not evidence of parity.

Now back to the lab at the center of the original allegation. Anthropic shipped again yesterday. According to 9to5Mac's report of the announcement, Sonnet 5.5 keeps Sonnet 5's pricing exactly where it was. That's two dollars per million input tokens and ten dollars per million output. Anthropic says it runs more than 30 percent faster, though it gave no workload and nobody has measured latency independently. The more interesting detail is safety. Sonnet 5.5 is the first Sonnet model to launch with cyber safeguards and fallbacks similar to those on Anthropic's most capable models.

That continues a pattern. On September 23rd this show noted that Opus 5.5, priced at four dollars in and twenty out, deployed under safeguards similar to the flagship Fable line's. Now the safeguard floor has dropped another tier, into Anthropic's highest-volume product. In competitive terms, Anthropic is using safety posture to protect distribution and enterprise trust, not only price. The mid tier is exactly where scaled API abuse is cheapest to run. Is that a deliberate answer to the distillation campaigns? Anthropic hasn't said so, and its stated safeguards are about cyber misuse, so treat that link as our inference. There's also a margin question. Holding price flat while going 30 percent faster only helps Anthropic if the speedup comes from cheaper serving. Otherwise Anthropic is absorbing the cost. Bottom line: Anthropic is pushing trust down into the tier where distribution is actually won.

Access to models and chips is one constraint. Access to money is the other, and OpenAI's biggest outside backer just showed what that costs. SoftBank's junk-bond deal was priced last week and is scheduled to settle today. It's ten billion dollars plus one billion euros of senior unsecured notes, together more than eleven billion dollars. If it closes at that size, it would be the largest non-financial corporate bond deal ever from Asia-Pacific and Japan. That tops the 10.93 billion dollars 7-Eleven raised in 2021. Bloomberg frames the deal as funding SoftBank's OpenAI bet, though no source says how much of the proceeds goes to OpenAI.

Once its third tranche completes, SoftBank's cumulative OpenAI investment reaches about 64.6 billion dollars, for roughly a 13 percent stake. Do the arithmetic and you get a cost basis near 500 billion dollars. That blends tranches bought at different prices, so it's a cost basis, not a valuation of OpenAI.

The structural point is where the leverage sits. It sits at SoftBank, not at OpenAI. OpenAI gets the cash with its own balance sheet untouched. SoftBank's creditors and shareholders carry the markdown risk. And this isn't the first leveraged leg. On September 24th, our private equity briefing reported Apollo in talks to expand a loan against SoftBank's Vision Fund 2 portfolio. SoftBank also has a margin loan against its OpenAI stake that requires cash if OpenAI's preferred shares fall substantially. So OpenAI's next funding-round price now feeds straight into a levered holder's credit. That raises the stakes on OpenAI's monetization pace, just after it made permanent price cuts to its workhorse models on September 22nd. The so-what: OpenAI's capital advantage is increasingly financed one step removed, on someone else's levered balance sheet.

So tie the levers together. Beijing is policing who sees Chinese data, not settling whether Chinese models borrowed their edge. Alibaba is hardening its stack on silicon, and Anthropic on safeguards. And OpenAI's war chest is being filled with junk debt at its largest backer. Three things to watch. First, any actual finding from China's regulator, or a response from DeepSeek or Moonshot. Second, an independent measurement of the Zhenwu V900, or a Qwen 4 release. Third, OpenAI's DevDay, which happens today.

That's the briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
