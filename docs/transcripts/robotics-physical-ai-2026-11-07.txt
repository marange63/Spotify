Good morning. It's Saturday, November 7th, and this is your robotics and physical AI briefing.

Today's thread is who owns the robot. Two of America's most-watched humanoid makers are converging on renting rather than selling. And the first public filing from one of them shows who has actually been paying for that model.

Start with Elon Musk, speaking Friday at the Baron Investment Conference. Asked about Optimus economics, he said: "I think probably rent them." Read that narrowly. He's not announcing a business model. He's handicapping one, at a conference, not in a filing or a shareholder letter. On cost, the only verbatim line he gave was this: "Yes, initially I'd say about the same, but then over time it would be much less." The one secondary account paraphrases the question as whether Optimus would be cheaper to build than a car. No outlet has the interviewer's exact words. Musk gave no dollar figure and no rental price. The thirty-thousand-falling-to-twenty-thousand-dollar path in the headlines is the reporting outlet's framing, not his. So there is no payback period to compute, and anyone quoting one is guessing.

Still, the direction matters, and the mechanism is worth spelling out. If Tesla rents rather than sells, it keeps the robot on its own balance sheet. The capex, the depreciation, the maintenance and the obsolescence risk all stay with Tesla instead of leaving with a hardware sale. Every design revision then strands or forces a retrofit on units Tesla still owns, not units a customer already paid for. Revenue arrives as a slow rental stream rather than a recognized sale, and that is harder for outsiders to read. A rented fleet also implies Tesla needs a field service organization to keep it running, a capability Optimus has never needed.

That fits a pattern this desk has tracked all week. On Monday, it was a Fremont production line with no reservation window, after Musk answered "No" to a December date. On Thursday, it was a third-quarter 10-Q with no Optimus volumes. Now it's a rental signal with no price attached. Our inference is that each step defers the one number investors could actually check, which is a unit sale. The competing reading is less cynical. Renting is simply what you do when a product is still changing fast and customers won't underwrite its residual value. That's a story about immaturity, not financial genius. Either way, Optimus is turning into a fleet-financing business. It will be judged on utilization, uptime and residual value, not sticker price.

Renting is already how America's first listed pure-play humanoid company makes its money. And as of this week, we can finally see who's paying. Agility Robotics, the maker of Digit, began trading on Nasdaq as AGLT on Thursday, November 5th, after closing its SPAC merger. On Friday this desk said it had no read on the trade. Now there is one. The stock opened at $11.26, against the $10 deal price. That's a single opening print, on a float thinned by redemptions. Treat it as a data point, not a verdict on humanoid economics.

The real payload is in the filing. Agility's 2025 revenue was $1.78 million, against a net loss of $138.1 million. Roughly 64 percent of that revenue, about $1.13 million, came from related parties. That's the filed version of a pattern this desk flagged on Wednesday and Thursday. Skild's model was running inside Nvidia's own Houston plant. Xiaomi's humanoid was working in Xiaomi's own factory. Now a listed company has put a number on it.

There are two honest readings. Investor-customers are often the normal first buyers in a brand-new industrial category, and strategic backers do run real facilities. The filing, as reported, doesn't name which related parties make up the 64 percent, so we can't say how strategic they are. But the bearish reading holds too. Arm's-length demand for humanoids, from a buyer with no equity stake, remains largely unproven.

It gets sharper. Agility's headline Digit 5 order book, more than $300 million, has anchored every bullish case on this stock. According to Humanoids Daily's read of the filing, it is a single contract. That contract covers 1,000 Digit 5 units on a three-year Robots-as-a-Service term. The customer is identified only as a related party, and it isn't named. The orders are also subject to milestones, and Digit 5 deliveries aren't expected to begin until 2027. So the backlog meant to prove outside demand is itself a related-party contract.

That same contract gives us the closest thing yet to a humanoid rental price. Divide $300 million by 1,000 robots over three years, and you get roughly $100,000 per robot per year. That's a little over $8,000 a month. It's our arithmetic, not a disclosed price, and it assumes the full value is rent and every milestone is met. It lands close to the roughly $8,500-a-month figure this desk cited on Wednesday. Our follow-up research indicates that figure is Agility's own illustrative price from investor materials, not a disclosed contract rate. Take that as a correction. No paying customer has actually disclosed a humanoid rental rate.

On the operating side, one data point is new this week. At GXO's original Flowery Branch, Georgia site, Digit has now moved more than 100,000 totes. That's a real count, but it's cumulative. It comes with no duration, no uptime figure, no intervention rate and no robot count. It tells you the work has been done many times, not how reliably. GXO is extending Digit to sites in North Carolina and Tennessee starting early next year, again with no robot count or financial terms disclosed. Uptime and intervention data are still missing for every humanoid deployment this desk has covered.

For scale, take one benchmark from outside humanoids. Intuitive Surgical is the clearest case of a robotics platform at durable volume. In its September quarter, reported in October, it booked $3.05 billion of revenue. Agility's revenue for all of 2025 was $1.78 million. That's not a knock on Agility. It's the distance between a robot platform at recurring scale and a humanoid sector still at the start of that curve.

One more Agility term deserves attention. The deal carries a 180-day lock-up on existing Agility and sponsor shares, subject to early release based on stock-price performance. By our count, 180 days from the debut runs to early May. That clause gives Agility a mechanical incentive to generate good news over the next six months. That's an inference from the structure, not evidence of intent. But weigh near-term Agility announcements with it in mind.

Which brings the two stories together on who carries the risk. Tesla is signaling it will keep Optimus on its own books. Agility already runs that way, and its largest contract so far is with a related party. On Thursday, this desk contrasted Walmart, locked into its Symbotic commitment as the customer, with Shield AI matching a Navy contract with its own money. Humanoids now sit on the Shield AI side of that line. The vendor and its related parties are underwriting the robot, not an arm's-length buyer.

Pulling it together. What's genuinely inflecting is the business model. The leading humanoid vendors are converging on fleet ownership rather than unit sales, and for the first time there's a public equity price attached to that model. What's still unproven is everything downstream: arm's-length demand, an actual rental price for Optimus, and any reliability metric for a fielded humanoid. The model and data layer stayed quiet again, with no fresh primary release from Nvidia's GR00T team, Physical Intelligence or Skild AI. Capital structure and disclosure, not model capability, set this week's pace.

Three catalysts to watch. First, China's suspension of its expanded rare-earth export controls lapses this coming Tuesday, November 10th, and the outcome is unknown. Under a rental model, any rise in magnet input costs would land on the vendor's fleet economics rather than a customer's purchase price. That's inference. Second, any disclosed Optimus rental price, which would finally make payback computable. Third, Agility's first quarterly filing as a public company, where the related-party split matters as much as the revenue total.
