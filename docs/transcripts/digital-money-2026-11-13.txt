Good morning. It's Friday, November 13th, and this is your digital money briefing.

On Wednesday this desk said bitcoin's leverage hadn't cleared, and made that the thing to watch. The newest data doesn't resolve it cleanly. One big venue shows leverage coming down. The market-wide measure shows the opposite. So let's take the pieces in order, then follow the money to the spot wrapper, the stablecoin float, the Treasury market, and Washington.

Start with Binance. Its bitcoin open interest fell from ten point six billion dollars to eight point eight billion in less than a month. That's a seventeen percent drop. It came alongside the exchange's highest monthly liquidation totals since 2021. On its face, that looks like forced positions finally getting flushed. But the report behind it gives only dollar figures, not coin counts. Its only price context is bitcoin near seventy-three and a half thousand dollars, down almost nine percent on the week. So part of that seventeen percent could simply be the same positions being worth fewer dollars. Bitcoin lost about ten percent over the past thirty days. The windows don't match exactly, but by our rough arithmetic, price alone could explain more than half of Binance's drop. Bank the narrow version. One venue's dollar book shrank, and its liquidations hit a 2021-era high.

Now the complication, and it comes from K33. K33's Vetle Lunde tracks aggregate perpetual futures open interest across venues, counted in coins rather than dollars. As of Tuesday it sat around three hundred fifty-three thousand bitcoin. That's the two-year extreme this desk cited on Wednesday. Open interest was higher on only one point two percent of trading days in that span. The new detail is the trend underneath it. Over the trailing thirty days, that coin-denominated open interest rose twelve percent, while price fell ten. Traders added exposure into the decline. That's the opposite of a cleared book. So the honest read is that deleveraging is partial and venue-specific, not a market-wide reset.

Funding is the one gauge leaning the other way. Per CoinDesk, it ran at about eight-tenths of a basis point per eight-hour period, just under the standard one basis point. That argues nobody is aggressively building new longs. But a book that's merely less eager to add longs is not a book that has been cleared.

The options market isn't treating any of this as an all-clear either. On Deribit, puts ran at one and a half times call volume for expiries from November 12th through the 27th. Twenty-five delta puts were priced four to five volatility points above matching calls. There are two ways to read that. It's a hedged book, less prone to a disorderly long cascade. It's also a market where nobody wants to be outright long. Either way, traders are paying up for downside protection into year-end.

Which leaves the question of who's actually buying. The spot ETF wrapper says: not yet. US spot bitcoin ETFs logged five straight sessions of net outflows through Wednesday, about one point four four billion dollars in total. Wednesday alone lost about three hundred million. That's roughly triple Tuesday's ninety-five million, on Farside and SoSoValue tallies that get revised and don't always agree. BlackRock's IBIT was about two hundred seventy million of Wednesday's total, roughly nine-tenths of it. We don't yet have Thursday's flows, so we can't say whether the streak reached six.

One caution on reading that concentration. When a single fund is nine-tenths of the outflow, that fits rebalancing or a handful of large holders as well as a broad institutional exit. So don't hear it as institutions leaving en masse. The macro backdrop doesn't help, though. As we covered earlier this week, the hot October inflation print pushed CME-implied odds of a December hike to around ninety percent. Our inference is that this raises the cost of waiting in a wrapper that pays no yield, because cash looks set to pay more for longer. The upshot is simple. Even where leverage eased, the wrapper that was supposed to be a permanent bid is still a net seller. A cleaner corner of the book is not the same as a buyer showing up.

The other door crypto uses to pull in cash tells a similar story. Yesterday we covered the headline: stablecoin supply down fourteen point two billion dollars over thirty days, the sharpest drop since 2022. The new piece is the timing of the 2022 precedent, as The Block laid it out. In June of 2022, the thirty-day contraction reached nearly thirty billion dollars. That came about five months before bitcoin's November 2022 low, near fifteen thousand five hundred dollars. That's one case, not a template. Today's drop is about half that size in dollar terms. And 2022 was driven by a credit collapse. But in that precedent, a shrinking float was an early warning, not a bottom signal. Glassnode names the signal to watch instead. A return to stablecoin supply growth, it says, would be the first sign that new money is coming in. Nothing in the data shows that yet. So watch the direction of the float, not the size of the drop.

That float is also where crypto touches the Treasury market, and this part is our inference. Glassnode's reading is that a shrinking supply means holders are redeeming stablecoins for dollars. Issuers meet those redemptions by running down reserves, and those reserves sit largely in short bills and repo. So a fourteen billion dollar contraction means roughly that much less issuer demand at the front end. For scale, Tether alone reported about a hundred thirty-two billion dollars of Treasury bills at its third-quarter attestation, as we noted on Sunday. Even if every dollar of the drop came out of bills, that's about a tenth of one issuer's book. So this is a trim to front-end demand, not a funding event. But the channel policymakers have cited as a new source of Treasury demand is, for now, running in reverse.

The same float reaches listed equities, and Coinbase is the clearest case. As we covered on Sunday, the stablecoin business Coinbase bulls call its durable, non-trading line sits on the USDC float. A shrinking float is a headwind for that line. We have no fresh figure to size it, so bank the direction only.

If the market is waiting on a buyer, Washington has been waiting on a vote, and that wait just changed venue. The White House sent four nominations to the Senate this week, its first batch for the SEC and CFTC in about a year. Three of them matter most here. Start with the CFTC pair. Nathan Anonick was a lead negotiator on the Agriculture Committee's portion of the CLARITY Act. Matthew Rowland also worked on the bill. On the SEC side, Steven Levine was nominated to succeed Hester Peirce, who led the agency's crypto task force.

Here's our inference, not a reported fact. The market-structure bill has been stalled in the Senate since September. So parts of its architecture could now arrive through the commissions instead, staffed in part by people who helped write it. Keep that in proportion. Working on a bill is not a commitment to implement it by rule. And commission rulemaking is slower and more open to litigation than a statute. These are also nominations, not confirmations. The CFTC picks go to Senate Agriculture, the SEC picks go to Senate Banking, and there's no timeline yet. Peirce's exit is the real open question for the SEC's light-touch posture. None of the nominees has stated crypto views in the reporting we have, so continuity and reversal are equally unproven. For exchanges and custodians, the practical upshot is that policy risk has moved from a floor vote to committee calendars and confirmation hearings.

Now the strongest bullish case, built only from what the data shows. Funding is below baseline. Binance's dollar leverage is down sharply, and its liquidations hit a 2021-era high. The options book is hedged. Together that means fewer forced sellers in that corner of the market, and lower odds of a disorderly cascade from here. What that case still lacks is a break in the ETF outflow streak. It lacks any return to stablecoin supply growth. And per K33, it lacks any sign that leverage has come down market-wide, rather than at one exchange. That last gap matters, because a crowded aggregate book can still amplify the next move in either direction.

Three things to watch into next week. First, whether the ETF outflow streak, and IBIT's redemptions specifically, finally break. Second, whether stablecoin supply turns back up, the signal Glassnode itself names, and with it issuer demand for bills. Third, how quickly Senate Agriculture and Banking schedule hearings for the four nominees.

So the headline isn't that crypto's leverage problem is solved. The picture is split. One exchange eased, the market as a whole didn't, the options market is still paying for protection, and the wrapper that was supposed to be a permanent bid is still walking out the door.

That's the digital money briefing for November 13th. Have a good weekend.
