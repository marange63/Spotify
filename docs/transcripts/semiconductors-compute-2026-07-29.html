Good morning, it's Wednesday, July 29th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

We start with the climax of a week the memory market will not forget. On Tuesday, July 28th, the Kospi fell 10.8 percent in a single session, its worst day in years. Samsung Electronics dropped 13.4 percent. SK Hynix dropped 14.7 percent. Neither move happened in isolation. SK Hynix is now down 47 percent from its end-of-June level, and Samsung is down 38 percent, after a string of earlier double-digit sessions this month. Reporting attributes Tuesday's move to a retail-leverage unwind rather than any single fundamental catalyst. That framing matters: this is journalists' interpretation of flow, not a confirmed structural cause, and it should be treated that way. What makes it strange is the trigger. On July 25th, Nvidia announced a milestone-based supply agreement with SK Hynix for HBM3E and HBM4, worth up to 500 billion dollars. When it landed, the market called it a bullish trophy. Four days later, some of those same investors are reading the deal as evidence that the AI buildout is devouring capital rather than validating it. That is a framing flip inside four trading sessions, and it tells you more about positioning than about memory economics.

Because the adjudicator landed today. SK Hynix reported its second-quarter results on July 29th, Korea time, and the headline numbers are records: revenue of 79.3 trillion won, operating profit of 60.5 trillion won, a 76 percent operating margin. Revenue is up 257 percent year over year, and operating profit up 557 percent. And yet multiple Korean and US outlets are describing this as a miss against the top end of analyst consensus. Hold both of those facts at once. A record-profit quarter that still misses what the Street had priced in is a genuine, if narrow, fundamental data point. It is not proof the leverage-unwind story is wrong, but not proof it's right either. It suggests the supercycle's growth rate, not its level, may be decelerating relative to expectations, which is a much more nuanced story than either "memory glut" or "nothing to see here." On the more encouraging side for bulls, SK Hynix confirmed that HBM4 mass shipments began in the second quarter, with a ramp-up planned for the second half of the year. That removes one specific bear-case risk the market had been pricing in: a feared slip into the third quarter. The company also described DRAM and NAND pricing as up significantly quarter over quarter, though it did not disclose a precise percentage. So treat any specific ASP growth figure you see elsewhere as an estimate, not a confirmed number.

Now, what the tape is fading matters as much as what it's pricing. Micron, the one major memory maker that has actually reported clean results this cycle, posted its fifth straight record quarter in June: revenue of 41.46 billion dollars, up from 23.86 billion the prior quarter and just 9.3 billion a year earlier. It guided its next quarter to 50 billion dollars, plus or minus a billion, at roughly 86 percent gross margin. That is guidance, not a booked result, but guidance moving up, not down. Micron says its full calendar-2026 HBM supply, including HBM4, is already fully priced and volume-committed. Put that next to a Korean equity market that just erased tens of billions in market value in two sessions, and the dispersion is the story. This looks like a derating driven by leverage and positioning, not a demand break confirmed by the two companies that actually make the product. The genuine risk in that dispersion is circular. If the derating persists, it raises the cost of capital Samsung and SK Hynix need to fund the very HBM4 and next-generation capacity expansions that would eventually ease the shortage. A panic about AI capital intensity could, perversely, slow the supply response that resolves it.

That dispersion isn't confined to memory. The same session that crushed Korean chip stocks also split the broader AI-accelerator complex for the first time this year. On Tuesday, AMD fell 9 percent to 453.37 dollars, Marvell fell 7 percent to 175.52, Arm fell 7 percent to 246.37, and Intel fell 6 percent to 85.82 — all on zero company-specific news. Nvidia, meanwhile, rose half a percent to 197.58, and Broadcom slipped just half a percent to 381.30. Year to date, remember, AMD is still up 112 percent, Intel up 133 percent, and Arm up 122 percent, against Nvidia up only 6 percent and Broadcom up 10 percent. So this single session partially unwound a much larger outperformance in the so-called satellite names. Reporting frames this as investors consolidating exposure into the clearest AI infrastructure leaders and cutting the crowded, higher-multiple names ahead of a heavy earnings slate. That test arrives tonight: Microsoft, Meta, and Arm itself all report after today's close. If hyperscaler capex guidance disappoints, expect the leader-versus-satellite gap to widen further. If it beats, the satellite names could reverse sharply. Either way, the market is now discriminating on execution certainty within a theme that traded as one undifferentiated basket for most of the year — a genuine pattern break worth watching past today.

Now step away from the equity tape entirely, because the physical constraint on how fast any of this ships hasn't moved. TSMC's second quarter, reported July 16th, was a record: 40.2 billion dollars in revenue, up 36 percent year over year, at a 67.7 percent gross margin, with third-quarter guidance of 44.6 to 45.8 billion and full-year growth guidance raised above 40 percent. But the binding limit on AI accelerator supply is no longer leading-edge wafers. It's advanced packaging. CoWoS lead times are now running 52 to 78 weeks, meaning an order placed today may not deliver until mid-2028 in many cases, and TSMC's own CEO has confirmed CoWoS capacity is sold out through 2026. Some reporting suggests that bottleneck is migrating further upstream into substrate materials, though those specific figures come from a single source and shouldn't be treated as confirmed. The safe claim is simply that packaging, not wafer output, is now the gate. That's reinforced from the other direction by ASML, which is close to fully booked on EUV lithography orders for 2027 already, adding roughly 30 percent more EUV capacity next year on top of that. Lithography visibility stretches two-plus years out. Packaging does not. That asymmetry means Nvidia, AMD, and hyperscaler custom silicon can only ship as fast as CoWoS capacity allows, regardless of how much wafer supply improves — and it hands unusual pricing leverage to the packaging and substrate layer of the supply chain, a part of the stack that historically had none.

So here's where that leaves the week's central tension. A historic Korean equity crash, driven by leverage more than fundamentals according to the reporting, collided today with a record-but-consensus-missing SK Hynix print, a confirmed on-schedule HBM4 ramp, and a Micron guide that keeps climbing. None of that is a demand break. What's real and unchanging underneath all of it is that advanced packaging, not memory sentiment and not wafer capacity, is what actually caps AI infrastructure growth right now. Watch Samsung's own results tomorrow, July 30th, for the DRAM average selling price trajectory and HBM4 ramp guidance — that's the number that will tell you whether this was a leverage story or the start of something structural.
