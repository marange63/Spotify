Cautious Optimism Briefings

Strategic Power, Energy, and Institutional Change

Transcript · October 28, 2026

Good morning. It's Wednesday, October 28th, 2026.

Today's thread is simple. The contest over strategic infrastructure has moved from building capacity to allocating it. A grid operator, a group of hyperscalers, and a private satellite constellation are each making allocation decisions that used to sit with public policy.

Start with the grid. On Friday the 23rd, this desk read FERC's October 9th order on PJM's data-center plan. FERC approved a registry of large loads but rejected the tools to manage them. We said cost allocation was the fight coming next. PJM has now put a number on it.

The pressure behind it is real. Wholesale electricity costs across PJM, the nation's largest regional power market, surged 50.3 percent in the first half of this year. PJM's answer was previewed to stakeholders on October 22nd. It is a one-time backstop procurement for about six point eight gigawatts of new supply. That size is not arbitrary. PJM's last capacity auction came up just over sixty-eight hundred megawatts short of what the grid needs for reliability in 2028 and 2029. The backstop is built to fill exactly that hole.

The design has four pieces worth holding onto. The auction is pay-as-bid. The weighted average price is capped at five hundred fifty-five dollars per megawatt-day. New supply has to be online by June 1st, 2032. And contracts can run as long as fifteen years.

Then comes the part that makes this a cost-allocation fight. Costs would follow each zone's share of large-load growth from 2026 into 2029, based on firm service agreements. In plain terms, the zones signing up the most data-center demand would carry the most cost. One nuance matters here. That formula allocates cost between zones. The sources don't spell out how the bill gets split inside a zone. So the easy headline, that hyperscalers will now pay, runs ahead of the actual design.

Two more caveats. First, this is a stakeholder preview, not a filed or approved tariff. PJM has said it will file a revised plan with FERC by Thursday the 29th. If that filing doesn't satisfy the commission, FERC has a separate docket open to impose its own version. So the final design could end up in the regulator's hands rather than PJM's.

Second, a 2032 online date means this backstop finances the next decade of firm capacity. It does nothing for the bills households and businesses pay in 2026, 2027, and 2028. Ratepayers keep carrying today's shock while this fixes tomorrow's.

It's also worth getting the attribution right, because the data-center story is easy to overstate. PJM's independent market monitor, Monitoring Analytics, puts data centers at thirty-eight percent of the sixteen point four billion dollars in charges from the latest capacity auction. Across the last four auctions combined, the monitor's figure is forty-six percent. Note what those numbers measure. They are capacity charges, one component of the wholesale bill, not the whole fifty-percent surge. And they sit below some higher shares that have circulated without a clear source. So on the monitor's own numbers, data-center load is a large plurality of the problem. It is not nearly all of it.

There's a real debate buried in the price cap. One reading is that capping the price and locking in long contracts suppresses the scarcity signal merchant generators need. That would blunt investment right when the price spike was supposed to pull it in. The competing reading is that a cap paired with a fifteen-year contract is a de-risking trade. It swaps volatile spot upside for contracted cash flow, and that can make new plants easier to finance. On the current record, neither reading can be settled. What I'd say, as inference, is that independent power producers and infrastructure funds winning this auction would hold something closer to utility-like cash flow. That means lower upside and lower risk, and it changes how those assets get valued.

There's a second-order effect on siting too, again as inference. If PJM's cost-allocation template survives FERC, large loads have a reason to avoid allocated grid costs. That favors behind-the-meter generation and regions with slack capacity. And it makes owners of existing firm generation and grid interconnections the holders of the scarce asset. The bottom line on PJM: this decides who pays for the 2030s grid, not who pays this year's bill.

Which leads to the second story. If new firm supply doesn't arrive until 2032, the power that exists today is the prize. Hyperscalers are already locking it up. Amazon, Google, Meta, and Microsoft have together contracted about eight and a half gigawatts from operating, restarted, and uprated US nuclear reactors. Listen to that description carefully: operating, restarted, uprated. That's existing iron, not new construction.

So the sharper read is not that nuclear is scaling for AI. It's that firm power is being reallocated. Output that served the general grid is moving into private offtake agreements. My inference is that this can tighten supply for other customers in the same regions. That's the same who-pays question PJM is wrestling with, one layer upstream.

Markets reacted Tuesday with a rally in nuclear-adjacent names. NuScale rose about seven percent to five dollars and sixty-four cents. Oklo rose about six percent to twenty-seven dollars and thirty-nine cents. One day's move is not a re-rating, and it says little about construction execution risk. The takeaway is that existing firm generation is now the scarce asset. Private contracts are pulling it out of the shared pool everyone else draws from.

The same allocation problem shows up in orbit, and here there is no regulator in the middle at all. Elon Musk said over the weekend that SpaceX restricted Starlink service to Ukraine because its military use violated the service's terms. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky says normal operating speeds have since returned. Nobody has confirmed exactly what activity triggered the restriction, or how long it lasted. So treat Musk's characterization as his claim, contested by Kyiv, not settled fact.

What is measurable is the concentration. By one estimate, Starlink accounts for roughly seventy-five percent of all active maneuverable satellites in orbit. Three-quarters of the maneuverable satellites in the sky sit under one company's terms of service. And that company keeps turning up across this show's desks. On Tuesday, the Frontier Labs desk reported Anthropic's filing disclosing large compute commitments to SpaceX. So SpaceX is now a node in both AI compute and military communications.

Kyiv's stated alternatives are thin. Ukraine has its own constellation with a single usable satellite, and a letter of intent with Nokia and ICEYE. Those are intent and one satellite, not substitute capacity.

The second-order effect is about direction of pressure, not a measured outcome. Single-vendor satellite dependence becomes a risk that defense planners have to price. That applies to European sovereign-space budgets. It applies just as much to Taiwan contingency planning, where command-and-control on a commercial link is a vulnerability. The point to bank: connectivity has become a contestable node of military power that sits outside any government's direct control.

Pull the three together. A grid operator is designing who pays for the next decade of electrons. Hyperscalers are contracting away the existing nuclear fleet's firm output. And a single private company's terms of service just functioned as a military allocation decision. In each case, a function that used to run through public policy now runs through a private contract or a stakeholder process.

On the watch list. PJM's revised filing at FERC is due Thursday. China's rare-earth export control suspension lapses November 10th, a date worth keeping on the calendar. And today's Fed decision is still pending as this is recorded. It is the financing backdrop for capped, long-tenor power contracts and nuclear offtake deals alike, and nothing about its outcome should be assumed.
