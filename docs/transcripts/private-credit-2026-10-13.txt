Good morning, it's Tuesday, October 13th, and this is the private credit briefing.

Here's the thesis for today. The AI buildout is outgrowing bank balance sheets, and banks are now trying to hand that risk to non-banks just as the market marks the anchor tenant's credit down. And the books that would receive that risk already carry stress, mostly in the form of PIK and extensions rather than hard defaults.

Start with the lead. Four banks, JPMorgan, Morgan Stanley, SMBC, and MUFG, have spent more than six months trying to distribute thirty-eight billion dollars of construction debt. All of it is tied to a single Oracle-leased data-center project spanning Texas and Wisconsin. Every detail here is relayed secondhand from Financial Times reporting, so treat it as reported, not filed. The reason the paper is stuck, per that reporting, is that banks are "bumping up against internal limits on how much AI-linked exposure" they can hold. The banks have been exploring significant risk transfers. And some lenders are now willing to sell pieces to non-bank buyers at a discount, though no price has been reported for this package. So this isn't origination slowing down. It's banks trying to shed risk they have already underwritten, to free capacity for the next AI loan.

One voice from the buy side frames the scale. Man Group's co-head of credit risk sharing called the deal sizes "out of scale to anything we've thought about, ever." Weigh that accordingly. He runs a risk-transfer business that benefits from exactly this flow, and nothing reported says Man Group has bought any of it.

Oracle is the name that ties this together. In recent weeks, Oracle served a force-majeure notice on Blue Owl's Project Jupiter data center in New Mexico. Last week, Oracle was reported to be in talks with Apollo and Goldman Sachs over a chip financing vehicle. Now it is the single tenant behind thirty-eight billion dollars of construction debt that four banks can't move. The same name keeps showing up as a stressed counterparty and as a fresh credit ask.

The market's pricing reflects that discomfort. Oracle's five-year credit default swap climbed to around a hundred and fifty-one basis points in early October. That's a level last seen in 2009, according to Bloomberg market data. S&P had already cut Oracle to triple-B-minus, one notch above junk, back in July.

Now the skeptical read. A hundred and fifty-one basis points is elevated for Oracle, but it is not a distressed level. Some of that widening may reflect lenders hedging the very exposure they're trying to sell. That's our inference, not a reported fact. And this is construction debt secured by the asset and the lease. The holder's exposure is to build-out and lease performance, not simply to Oracle's unsecured balance sheet. Significant risk transfers are also routine bank capital management, not a distress signal on their own.

So don't read six months of stalled distribution as proof that buyers reject Oracle credit. At thirty-eight billion dollars for one tenant, the package can exceed every buyer's single-name limit without anyone doubting the credit at all. The banks' own limits point the same way. The evidence fits a concentration problem at least as well as a credit verdict. Separating the two is the real analytical work here.

What the transfer doesn't do is take the risk out of the system. It moves it to insurers, credit funds, and risk-sharing investors. And their marks are not set by the CDS market.

That's where valuation comes in. We do have one real market price for Oracle-linked paper, just not for this package. About eighteen billion dollars of loans tied to an Oracle-leased data center in New Mexico were quoted at eighty-nine to ninety-one cents on the dollar in mid-September. Those quotes came from syndicate banks including Santander and Jefferies, again per the FT. The reporting doesn't name that New Mexico project. It may well be Project Jupiter, the Blue Owl site where Oracle later served its force-majeure notice, but we can't confirm that. Either way, it is a separate financing from the thirty-eight billion dollar Texas and Wisconsin package.

So ninety cents is not a clean comparable. It may carry project-specific stress that the Texas and Wisconsin debt doesn't share. But it is the only secondary price we have on Oracle-leased construction paper. If pieces of the thirty-eight billion dollar package land in private-credit or insurance books at a model mark, that mark will have to justify any gap above ninety. That's the place where private valuations could lag public reality.

This is the concentration problem the industry's own research has been warning about, now showing up in one deal. According to a Carlyle white paper, the industry may need to provide roughly a trillion dollars to finance AI computing infrastructure. That's more than half of current private credit assets under management. Carlyle's co-president, Mark Jenkins, put the counterparty problem plainly: "just seven or eight top-tier counterparties make up most of the underlying financings." Carlyle is a competitor positioning itself as the disciplined lender, so its warning isn't disinterested. But the Oracle package is that abstract warning made concrete: one tenant, four banks, thirty-eight billion dollars.

Contrast that with how an ordinary credit gets financed. Blackstone Credit and Insurance just led a one billion dollar financing backing the combination of Analogic with parts of Leidos' security business. Blackstone's credit arm sat as joint lead arranger alongside HPS, Bank of America, and Wells Fargo. No pricing or structure was disclosed, and it's one deal, not a trend. But it shows the first route by which risk crosses from banks to non-banks: shared at origination. The Oracle package shows the second route. Banks take the whole thing, then try to shed it afterward. Hyperscale single-tenant risk is moving through the worse of the two routes.

So what shape are the books in that would receive this risk? Fitch's trailing-twelve-month private credit default rate hit a record six point three percent through August. That's the freshest reading available, but it's already about a month old. And these figures come via secondary reporting of Fitch's release, not the primary report.

The composition matters more than the headline. Interest deferrals and PIK substitutions made up forty-seven percent of trailing-year default events. Stressed maturity extensions were another forty-one percent. Uncured payment failures were just eight percent. Bankruptcies and restructurings that handed control to lenders were only four percent. So close to nine in ten of Fitch's defaults are loans being deferred or extended, not loans actually failing. Losses are being deferred, not realized. That leaves a record default rate sitting on top of very little recovery data.

Two cautions on that read. Fitch counts PIK substitution under stress as a default, and narrower trackers don't, so this is the high-end construction of the number. And Fitch's quarterly series splits these events differently, so don't read this as a confirmed shift from extensions toward PIK. Still, PIK and deferral is now the largest bucket in Fitch's count. That matters for manager economics. Incentive fees on accrued PIK income can keep paying the manager in cash while the underlying loss stays unrecognized.

The stress isn't evenly spread, either. Healthcare providers had the most unique defaulters, eighteen, and industrial and manufacturing credits sat at a nine point nine percent default rate. A year earlier, industrials were at five point two percent. Technology software was the lowest major sector, at zero point six percent. That cuts against the week's AI-disruption-of-software narrative as a near-term default driver. The caveat is that sponsor support and PIK toggles may be keeping software credits out of the count, rather than proving genuine health. Either way, a diversified direct-lending book with roughly ten percent default rates at one end and under one percent at the other can't be fairly priced off one blended spread. It misprices both ends.

Pulling this together. The private credit cycle isn't turning so much as it's being asked to absorb risk faster than price discovery can keep up. Realized losses are still being pushed out through PIK and extensions. Meanwhile, the AI construction risk banks can't hold is looking for a new home. The biggest unresolved risk is that single-tenant AI construction exposure migrates out of banks and into vehicles that mark to a model. The only secondary price we have on Oracle-leased construction paper sits around ninety cents. That print may carry project-specific stress, but it's the only outside test a model mark faces so far. One scope note on that verdict. Leveraged loans, high yield, and CLOs gave us no fresh, verifiable credit-cycle signal this week, so today's read rests on private credit.

Watch four things from here. First, the next monthly Fitch default reading. Second, Ares Capital's third-quarter earnings later this month, alongside the alternative managers' own results. That's the first hard non-accrual and PIK read since this transfer story broke. Third, Apollo's first asset-level loan prices, due October 30th. Watch whether its marks on AI and data-center paper sit anywhere near that ninety-cent secondary print. Fourth, whatever price eventually gets put on pieces of that thirty-eight billion dollar package once a buyer actually shows up.
