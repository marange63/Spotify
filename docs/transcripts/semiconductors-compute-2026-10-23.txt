Good morning. It's Friday, October 23rd, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

The theme today is simple to state and uncomfortable in its implications. Vendor balance sheets are becoming the AI buildout's financing layer. Scarcity is being converted into contractual commitments, paid for in advance. And that is happening while the equity tape has spent more than a week pricing financing risk into this sector. Today's evidence runs the other way from that selloff. No supplier we have tracked has cut a forecast. They are committing more capacity, not less.

Start with the lead. At GTC Berlin on October 21st, NVIDIA announced coordinated capacity-expansion commitments with six manufacturing partners. They are Amkor, IBIDEN, King Yuan Electronics, Samsung Electro-Mechanics, Siliconware Precision Industries, and Unimicron. Jensen Huang was explicit about why: "Capacity is the constraint." Not demand.

The layers he named tell you where the real bottleneck sits now. Substrate capacity is set to rise two and a half times over three years. Amkor's Korean interposer wafer capacity is targeted to grow more than 300 percent by the end of 2028. Total semiconductor test capacity is meant to double from 2026 to 2027. Part of that rests on King Yuan Electronics' new US test facilities, backed by a 1.1 billion dollar capacity-reservation advance and targeting high-volume production in April 2027. And SPIL's optical-engine capacity for co-packaged optics is targeted to rise 15-fold by mid-2027, then double again by the end of 2028. Each of those numbers describes a different physical choke point: substrates, interposers, test, and optics. None of them is a wafer. The binding constraints NVIDIA chose to fund sit past the fab, in the back end of the chain.

That funding detail is the real story. Yesterday on this show, we described NVIDIA as the allocator, deciding who gets the scarce parts. This week it became the underwriter, putting cash down to enlarge the parts themselves. Amkor's own announcement, as reported by Investing.com, adds a second dollar figure. It discloses a new 1.3 billion dollar agreement with NVIDIA for the Korea expansion. Amkor says that brings total multi-year commitments between the two companies to 3 billion dollars, including a previous agreement in Arizona. That's partner-side evidence that at least this piece is new money layered onto an existing deal, not an old announcement in new packaging. So supplier capacity risk now sits, in part, on NVIDIA's own balance sheet.

But keep the skepticism proportional to the hype. These are targets and reservation commitments, not shipments or qualified output. Every multiple is measured against an undisclosed base. A 15-fold jump in optical engines could easily start from close to zero, which makes it a statement of roadmap intent, not volume. And the landing dates mostly fall in 2027 and 2028. King Yuan's US test sites target April 2027. SPIL's optics land by mid-2027. Amkor's interposers arrive by the end of 2028. This does little for 2026 supply, so near-term tightness persists. It's a bet on demand two years out, paid for now.

There's also a more cynical read worth holding alongside the earnest one. A coordinated six-partner announcement at a flagship NVIDIA conference, during a sector drawdown, also functions as demand signaling to investors. The evidence favors the genuine-constraint story, especially with Amkor putting its own dollar figure on the table. But both can be true at once.

The packaging piece has a second reading. TSMC's management maintains that CoWoS "remains the number one choice for customers." At the same time, NVIDIA is scaling Amkor's interposer capacity by more than 300 percent. Is that a complement to CoWoS, filling capacity TSMC can't scale fast enough? Or is it a hedge against TSMC's packaging dominance? The public record doesn't resolve it. Amkor's Korea deal is described only as interposer wafer capacity for NVIDIA platforms, and nobody has said on the record whether it feeds TSMC's flow. So treat packaging capability as its own competitive axis, separate from who leads in transistor manufacturing. The tell to watch is whether Korean interposer output ships under a TSMC flow or around it.

There's a slower-moving implication for the suppliers themselves, and it is inference. If these reservation advances hold, the six partners all gain multi-year revenue visibility. Markets have historically not credited that kind of visibility to commodity subcontractors. That's the re-rating case. These firms start trading less like cyclical assemblers and more like contracted AI-infrastructure suppliers. That holds on the dimension of production capacity, though it changes nothing about who leads on underlying chip technology.

The optics buildout carries its own second-order read, also inference. A 15-fold scale-up in co-packaged optical engines puts that technology squarely on NVIDIA's near-term roadmap. That matters for how Broadcom and Marvell compete on AI networking silicon in 2027 and beyond, and for the pluggable-optics vendors on the other side of that transition.

The same prepay-for-scarcity pattern shows up one tier higher in the chain. ASML's long-term customer prepayments jumped 5.7 billion euros in a single quarter, to 9.3 billion euros. CEO Christophe Fouquet said some customers are now expressing interest in placing orders for 2029 and even 2030. On this show on October 17th, we called ASML's claim that 2027 and 2028 supply won't meet demand a seller's claim with no bookings figure behind it. The prepayment jump is the first real cash evidence behind that claim.

The caveats matter here too. Prepayments replaced the bookings metric ASML stopped disclosing, so this isn't a like-for-like read. ASML doesn't say which customers paid, so the money could be concentrated in one or two buyers. And "expressing interest" in 2029 and 2030 slots is not the same as a placed order. Still, the point lands. Customers, not just ASML, are now putting cash behind the shortfall.

Now the money moving in the other direction. NVIDIA and ASML's customers are pushing cash upstream to secure supply. Broadcom is reportedly pushing cash downstream, to its buyer. The Chinese outlet 36Kr reported this week that Broadcom is in early-stage talks to arrange financing tied to OpenAI's custom chip program. That is a single source, the talks are early, and no figure has been confirmed. Treat it as reporting, not fact.

What is on the record is the scale of the custom-silicon business that financing would feed. Marvell, at its October 6th investor day, raised its fiscal 2029 custom-silicon revenue target to more than 12 billion dollars. That's a target, not revenue. It came alongside an expanded Google deal reportedly covering inference accelerators and controllers tied to Google's TPU ecosystem.

Put together, the pattern is that chip vendors may now be financing both ends of the AI compute chain: their suppliers, and potentially their customers. Yesterday's Enterprise Technology briefing carried a similar structure. A TD Cowen analyst suggested NVIDIA may be helping Oracle secure financing, which neither company has confirmed. That's worth remembering the next time a buyer's reported AI demand looks unusually strong. Some of that demand may be the vendor's own balance sheet talking to itself.

Which brings us to the synthesis. Scarcity rents and credit exposure now sit on the same balance sheets. This show has tracked stress in neocloud credit since October 17th. If downstream financing conditions tighten, the capacity NVIDIA has prepaid for doesn't vanish. NVIDIA still holds the commitment, whether or not the demand behind it materializes on schedule. In the terms this show uses, the supply side is still telling a compute-constrained story. The financing side is where economics-constrained demand would show up first. That risk transfer is the thing to watch, not the headline multiples.

On the watch list. SK hynix reports third-quarter results on October 28th. Samsung's full divisional breakdown and Intel's third-quarter earnings land October 29th. AMD reports November 3rd. Rubin cloud availability is the next checkpoint on NVIDIA's own roadmap. And any partner filing that puts a hard number on these NVIDIA-backed commitments will be the next real test of how much of this buildout is genuinely incremental.

That's the briefing for October 23rd. Have a good one.
