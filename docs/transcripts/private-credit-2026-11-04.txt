Good morning, it's Wednesday, November 4th, and this is your private credit briefing.

This week the strain in private credit showed up in the money behind the loans, not in the loans themselves. Insurance float took a rate hit at Apollo. Retail money ran into a queue at Blackstone. And the loan books underneath still report as broadly benign.

Start with Apollo. Yesterday it was a catalyst we couldn't read, because no figures had landed. Now they have. Apollo originated a record $114 billion in the third quarter. Of that, $98 billion was debt, and management said on the call that more than 80 percent of the debt was investment grade, averaging BBB-plus. Note the scope. The investment-grade tilt applies to the debt, not to the whole headline. But it tells you where this growth lives. It's a different neighborhood from the stressed middle-market sponsor book.

Now the split screen. Fee-related earnings hit a record $802 million, up 23 percent from a year ago. Yet Apollo posted a GAAP net loss to common stockholders of about $1.06 billion, against a profit a year earlier. The swing sits in Retirement Services, which is Athene. Athene's investment-related gains-and-losses line flipped to a loss of about $3.4 billion, from a gain of roughly $3 billion the quarter before.

Be careful about what that loss is. Press reporting ties it to unrealized, rate-driven marks. Apollo's call, as transcribed, didn't address the $3.4 billion directly, so hold that attribution as reporting, not management's words. But nothing in the record calls it a credit loss, and nobody should. Here's the structural point. Much of an insurer's asset book flows through GAAP at market value. The private loans that same capital funds are marked to model. So a rate move hits earnings at once, while credit deterioration in the loans may not show up for quarters.

And the float itself isn't leaving. Athene still took in $19 billion of inflows in the quarter. So the fee engine and the risk-bearing capital are different things, and Apollo just printed a record on one and a loss on the other. One quarter can't tell you whether that GAAP volatility recurs. If it does, my inference is that markets may stop treating insurance float as truly permanent capital. That would raise the cost of the whole annuity-funded lending model.

Apollo is originating at a record pace. The rest of the direct-lending complex is not keeping up. Ares Management raised more than $31 billion of gross commitments in the third quarter, over half of it from outside its Credit Group. But management said U.S. direct-lending deployment slowed on muted M&A. They call the fourth-quarter pipeline record-level, but that's their forecast, not a fact. It's also the same cyclical story Blue Owl told last Friday, when its direct-lending book didn't grow on net. Two managers telling the same story isn't independent confirmation. On credit, Ares' direct-lending non-accruals actually improved slightly, to 1.8 percent from 1.9. So the deployment gap is specific to M&A-driven middle-market lending, not the asset class broadly. Capital is flowing to whoever has an origination channel that doesn't depend on sponsor deals. Right now, that's Apollo's investment-grade machine.

If insurance float is one funding source behind this machine, retail money is the other. And it had its own test this week. Blackstone's flagship non-traded BDC, BCRED, furnished an 8-K on Tuesday with hard redemption data. Third-quarter repurchase requests totaled $4.3 billion. But that includes $2.3 billion of second-quarter requests the fund never filled. By my own subtraction, fresh third-quarter requests were about $2 billion. That's not a disclosed number, but it suggests new demand may be easing.

The harder number is fulfillment. For investors who sought liquidity across those two quarters, BCRED met about 75 percent of requested capital within roughly 90 days. The filing gives no prior-period rate, so that's a level, not a trend. Still, it complicates the easing read we gave you last week, from Stanger's tender data and Blue Owl's flagship credit fund. This is a backlog, not a run. But it's a backlog carried across quarters at the category's flagship vehicle. That's the liquidity mismatch, measured.

Two more BCRED numbers pull in opposite directions. Class I NAV sits at $23.51 a share, against $25 at inception. That's about 6 percent of erosion over the fund's life. The filing attributes the recent pressure to spread widening and "select older-vintage investments." That's the fund grading its own homework, so take it as a data point, not a verdict. Meanwhile, PIK income fell to 5.9 percent of total investment income, from 7.8 percent at the end of last year. That's a genuine improvement in income quality, and it deserves credit. So income quality is improving while marks on older loans soften. Those are two different stories about the same book, and which one wins matters more than either number.

That older-vintage language lines up with Moody's. In the CNBC appearance we cited yesterday, Moody's Marc Pinto located the stress in older, speculative-grade private-credit loans, not in the new AI-linked wave. Earlier Moody's research adds a sharper point. It put the default probability of private, non-traded BDCs at about 0.92 percent as of this spring. That's nearly double the roughly 0.49 percent for public BDCs. That research is months old, so treat it as background, not this week's data. The takeaway: the stress sits in the back book, and Moody's models the non-traded vehicles, the ones with the retail queues, as the riskier ones.

Non-accrual rates are the narrowest lens on all of this, and today that lens gets sharper. BCRED's filing puts non-accruals at 2.7 percent of cost, but only 1.7 percent at fair value. The fund says that's below the industry's long-term average. Maybe so. But it's the same shape as the gap we flagged at Ares Capital, the listed BDC, last Thursday. A full point between cost and mark is the valuation question in miniature.

And that metric is now being tested in court. A Mayer Brown client update this month describes amended complaints against BDC advisers. They allege restructurings were used to move positions off non-accrual status, making credit quality look better than it was. One case cites a Justice Department investigation into a BDC's valuation practices. No adviser or fund is named in what's public, and nothing ties these cases to any manager in today's briefing. These are allegations in a law-firm summary, not findings. Still, the metric every manager is leaning on this earnings season is the one now being litigated.

Last beat, on where new money is going. BCRED's filing also discloses a $5 billion debt financing for Waymo in October, co-led by an affiliate of the fund. It priced at SOFR plus 525 basis points. Yesterday we cited Chicago Atlantic's middle-market range of SOFR plus 475 to 525. So Waymo priced at the wide end of middle-market spreads, on a borrower that looks nothing like a middle-market credit. The filing gives price and size, not structure, so this is inference. My read is that private lenders are extracting middle-market pricing on AI and robotics capex that sits outside the rated bond market, because they can. It extends last week's arc, when banks sought insurance on AI debt and some credit insurers said they were nearly full on data-center exposure. When bank and insurer capacity is tight, private credit sets the price on the buildout.

So where does the cycle actually stand? Yesterday I called it a grind with a timer attached. Today's update is about where the stress is sitting. Reported credit metrics are stable to modestly improving, and non-accruals and PIK share are not blowing out. The stress has moved to two places instead. One is the funding side, where insurance float absorbs rate volatility and retail vehicles absorb liquidity demand they can't fully meet on time. The other is the back book, where older vintages carry pressure that newer paper hasn't yet been tested against.

The biggest unresolved risk is what the queues hide. Redemption queues mean fewer forced sales. And fewer forced sales mean private marks keep going untested by a real liquidation price.

Watch three catalysts. Blue Owl's listed BDC was slated to report today, and there are no figures in hand as of this research. KKR reports Monday, November 9th, the next test of an insurance-funded platform, this time Global Atlantic, against the Athene question. And the rest of the BDC earnings wave will show whether BCRED's cost-versus-fair-value gap and its 75 percent fulfillment rate are an outlier or the new normal.

That's the briefing. Stay cautious, stay optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
