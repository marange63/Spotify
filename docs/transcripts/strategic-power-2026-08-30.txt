Good morning. It's Sunday, August 30th, and this is Cautious Optimism Briefings.

Today's story is about who actually controls a price. Start with the long end of the Treasury curve. On August nineteenth, Treasury surprised the market by at least doubling the size of its longer-dated bond buybacks. The operations go from two billion dollars to four billion dollars each, they run from September ninth through November fourth, and they target the ten-to-twenty-year and twenty-to-thirty-year sectors. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent went further the next day. He said the operation could be more than four billion dollars per issue, and he argued that current yields don't reflect fundamentals because liquidity in the thirty-year is weak. Then, on August twenty-fourth, reporting attributed to two senior Treasury officials said Bessent could tap the Treasury General Account, built up to around nine hundred fifty billion dollars, to fund still more buybacks.

Here's the problem. The tape already delivered its verdict. On the announcement, the thirty-year bond fell nine basis points, to 5.196 percent. Shortly after, it was back up more than five basis points, to 5.248 percent. That is higher than before the intervention. A four-billion-dollar operation is small next to the stock of long debt it is meant to support, and the bounce says the market noticed.

Two more things worth saying plainly. First, no on-the-record Treasury document links the General Account to buyback funding. Treasury's own August quarterly refunding materials do project the account near that nine-hundred-fifty-billion level at quarter's end, but purely as a routine cash-management forecast, with no stated tie to the bond program. The ammunition story lives only in anonymous sourcing. Second, even if that story is real, buying long bonds with cash raised by issuing bills is a maturity swap, not debt reduction. It changes the government's funding profile, not what it owes. And it changes it in one direction: shorter and more rate-sensitive. Every bill that replaces a thirty-year bond has to be rolled again within months, at whatever the front end costs then. That is the trade Treasury is making. Lower long yields today, more rollover risk later.

The market's own read on all this showed up in gold and Bitcoin, which have been trading as a single anti-dollar position. Bitcoin's thirty-day correlation to gold climbed to plus zero point eight one, while its correlation to the dollar index dropped to minus zero point eight six. Two assets, one trade: short the dollar, short fiscal credibility.

That debasement trade then broke within days, and it broke on a completely different lever. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh turned hawkish at Jackson Hole on August twenty-eighth. He warned that while this summer's PCE and CPI readings were better than expected, they do not tell him that underlying trends have meaningfully improved. Market-implied odds of a September rate hike, a hike and not a cut, jumped to fifty-seven percent, from thirty-five percent the day before. The thirty-year yield rose again, to 5.21 percent. Gold fell about three percent from its August peak. Bitcoin was rejected just under eighty-two thousand dollars intraday and corrected the following day. No scheduled macro release landed in that window. No payrolls, no CPI, no Fed decision. One speech did all of it.

So the same trade that is supposed to be pricing currency debasement unwound in hours. That is twice this month: once on a Treasury announcement, once on a Fed speech. It tells you this is a positioning trade riding rate expectations, not a settled verdict on monetary credibility. And the honest takeaway on the long end is this. It is not being managed by Washington. It is being tolerated by the market, and a hawkish Fed comment or a weak auction moves it more than an official intervention can.

That five-percent-plus thirty-year yield matters well beyond Treasury markets. It is the financing cost for exactly the kind of long-dated, debt-heavy capital the AI buildout depends on. Which brings us to data centers. A widely circulated estimate says thirty to fifty percent of the U.S. data-center capacity scheduled to come online in 2026 could be delayed. The claimed cause is not chips and not financing. It is shortages of transformers and switchgear. That is the headline, and it does not hold up well under scrutiny.

SemiAnalysis pushed back directly, and their objection is about the denominator. Hyperscaler self-build forecasts for the end of 2026 have moved only about one percent over six months, and colocation forecasts under five percent. A lot of what is being counted as canceled was never a real order. In their phrase, speculative announcements dying in a county commission hearing remove zero orders from anyone's books. So adjudicate rather than repeat the scary number. The defensible fact is the construction ratio itself, roughly sixteen gigawatts scheduled for 2026 operations against about five gigawatts actually under construction. Add to that a small number of specific, physically gated projects, like the STACK Infrastructure and Oracle site now pushed to 2029 on gas-pipeline permitting.

That is a narrower story than half of AI capacity being at risk. It is also a truer one, and it matters more, because it says the constraint has moved down the stack. Not appropriations, not capital, not even chips anymore. Transformers, switchgear, and permits. Things money cannot simply accelerate. The trade follows from that. Value shifts away from silicon and toward the companies making grid-adjacent hardware, and toward power that is already interconnected. Behind-the-meter generation, meaning gas turbines and fuel cells sited on the property, becomes the rational hedge against years-long interconnection queues. And if hyperscaler capacity really does slip toward 2027 and 2028, that eases near-term power-price pressure on grids like PJM and ERCOT while concentrating it later. Good for utilities holding capacity today. Bad for the assumption that AI power demand shows up on schedule.

Now the one input price that can override both of those stories: energy, and specifically the Strait of Hormuz. Six months into the campaign against Iran's nuclear program, which began February twenty-eighth, vessel traffic through the strait remains, in the latest assessment, significantly reduced. The UK Maritime Trade Organization rates it a severe risk zone, with at least seventy attacks on shipping recorded since the war started. CENTCOM says eighty-two commercial ships have been redirected around blockaded Iranian ports. Iran's own president says the country's oil exports have dropped nearly thirty-five percent because of sanctions. And Treasury separately hit a UAE bank it says processed roughly one point eight billion dollars for a hundred and three companies tied to Iranian sanctions evasion.

Two cautions on that. Treat the thirty-five percent export figure as Iran's own political framing as much as an independent market read. It builds a case for sanctions relief as much as it concedes damage. And treat this whole item as thinly sourced, because it rests on a single live-updates page. I am also not going to quote you an oil price, because the research behind this briefing did not confirm one. But the pattern across six months is unambiguous. Strikes and blockade have suppressed a chokepoint that carries roughly a fifth of global seaborne oil. They have not reopened it. That is durable disruption, not restored leverage, and it means a geopolitical risk premium stays embedded in crude regardless of what OPEC does.

Here is how the three pieces close the loop. A chokepoint that stays closed keeps a risk premium in energy prices. That premium is one more reason the long end refuses to fall no matter what Treasury buys back. And the long end is exactly what prices the AI buildout this beat has been counting all week. Two things worth watching from here. Whether Treasury ever puts the General Account claim on the record. And whether data-center capacity actually under construction starts closing the gap to what has been scheduled.

That's the briefing. Have a good Sunday.
