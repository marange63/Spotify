Good morning. It's Sunday, August 2nd, 2026, and this is your private credit briefing.

This week we spent three days on Ares Capital's non-accrual creep and the gap between private marks and public credit indices. With KKR and Blackstone now in, the Q2 alt-credit earnings season is complete, and it reframes the question. The sharper signal isn't credit quality at the big managers — it's a bifurcation in where the money is coming from. Institutional and insurance-funded capital is compounding at record rates. The retail and wealth-channel vehicles built to be the industry's next growth leg are wobbling, at the exact same time.

Start with the institutional leg, because it's unambiguous. Ares Management raised a record $36 billion in the second quarter, $23.7 billion of it for credit strategies, pushing firm AUM up 17% year over year to $671.3 billion. KKR's credit book has gone from roughly $80 billion to $300 billion in a few years, and the annual management fees attached to that business have more than tripled, to about $1.2 billion. Blackstone posted distributable earnings of $1.52 a share, up 26% year over year and comfortably ahead of the $1.35 consensus, on record firm AUM of $1.35 trillion — up 11% year over year on $68.3 billion of quarterly inflows. None of that is retail money. It's insurance balance sheets, pensions, and institutional allocators writing bigger checks into credit strategies than ever before. So the institutional funding engine for private credit isn't just intact — it's accelerating.

Now the other pole. Blue Owl's wealth-channel inflows nearly halved, to $1.7 billion from $4.4 billion a year earlier, and total new capital raised for its credit business fell to $7.6 billion from $12.1 billion — a three-year low. Credit AUM actually shrank quarter over quarter, to $158.1 billion from $159.2 billion, and direct lending has drifted down to 35% of Blue Owl's assets from roughly half two years ago. Blackstone's flagship retail vehicle, BCRED, tells the same story with more precision. Its own earnings call disclosed gross sales of $1 billion in the quarter, but repurchase requests exceeded the fund's 5% quarterly redemption cap, and only about half of those requests were actually fulfilled — producing $1.2 billion of net outflows despite that billion dollars of fresh money coming in the door. That's the redemption gate working exactly as liquidity-mismatch critics warned it would: capped, binding, and still leaving investors who wanted out unable to get out in full.

Which brings us to the skeptical beat. Both Blue Owl and Blackstone used their calls to say redemption pressure is easing. Blue Owl's CFO said 90% of one flagship fund's investors didn't request a single dollar of redemption in the quarter. Blackstone's president, Jon Gray, reaffirmed that BCRED redemptions are down materially heading into the third quarter. And here's the detail worth sitting with. He attributed that improvement specifically to reduced negative media and analyst commentary about private credit, not to any change in the underlying fund economics. Meanwhile the same calls disclosed a multi-year low in credit fundraising and, at Blue Owl, an outright quarter-over-quarter decline in credit AUM. A relief narrative about sentiment, sitting on top of a fundraising engine that's visibly slowing, is not the same thing as a resolved liquidity problem. So track the fulfillment rate on redemption requests, not the net-outflow headline. That 50% figure at BCRED is the number that tells you whether the gate is actually binding.

Now, is any of this a credit-quality story yet? Not at the big managers, not really. Ares Capital's non-accruals ticked up to 2.4% of cost from 2.1% in the first quarter, but that's still below its roughly 3% historical average, and borrower EBITDA growth across the portfolio ran around 8% on a trailing basis. KKR's management pushed back hard on software-sector worry, noting software is only 5 to 6% of its credit book. Be precise about what that is and isn't: there's no confirmed write-down behind the software concern, only an unconfirmed framing from secondary reporting, so treat it as an open question rather than a fact. So for now this reads as a liquidity and structural story — semi-liquid vehicle design meeting a cap on how fast investors can get their money back — not a deterioration in underlying loan performance.

That distinction is the whole game, because the two poles differ in their liquidity terms, not just their size. Insurance and pension money funds long-dated, often locked-up liabilities, so it can't line up at a redemption gate the way monthly-liquidity retail money can. A drawdown that would spark a run in a wealth-channel vehicle barely registers against an annuity book. So the pivot toward institutional capital isn't only slower growth — it's a structurally more stable funding base, one that can't force the manager to sell into a weak market to meet redemptions.

But there's a base-rate counterweight worth a couple of sentences. Sector-wide data across the twelve largest publicly traded BDCs show non-accruals rising broadly, by both borrower count and cost basis, with PIK loans getting marked down faster than the rest of the book. That's Q1 data reported in July, directional rather than precise, but it says the deterioration underneath the surface is not confined to any one manager. The big names' marks still look benign. The sector base rate says credit quality is drifting the wrong way at the margin. Those two things can both be true right now — the question is whether they converge. If Ares-style non-accrual creep keeps compounding quarter over quarter, the liquidity story and the credit-quality story stop being separable.

One more thread on the funding side. Ares Capital just launched the BDC sector's first commercial paper program, which management says could cut funding costs by 50 to 100 basis points. If that scales across peer BDCs, it lowers the industry's cost of capital broadly — good for equity holders in the near term — but it also compresses the spread cushion BDCs currently earn over their own funding costs. Cheaper liabilities are good until they erode the very spread compensation that's supposed to be paying investors for credit risk. Worth watching whether Blue Owl, FS KKR, or the other large BDCs follow Ares into the commercial paper market.

So where does the cycle actually stand? Institutional and insurance-funded private credit keeps compounding at records, and that funding base looks structurally sound. The retail and wealth-channel vehicles that were supposed to be the industry's next growth leg are showing simultaneous redemption pressure and a multi-year fundraising slowdown, and the "it's easing" language from managers is running ahead of what the fund-level mechanics actually show. If this persists, expect the growth narrative to pivot quietly away from 401(k) and wealth-channel retailization and back toward the slower, stickier capital of insurers and pensions. The biggest unresolved risk isn't a default wave — non-accruals are still near historical norms at the majors. It's whether liquidity stress in the retail vehicles eventually forces enough selling or gating friction to start showing up in the marks themselves. The near-term catalyst is Apollo's print on August 4th, the last of the majors to report. It will tell us whether its retail vehicle, Apollo Debt Solutions, is following Blue Owl and Blackstone's pattern or breaking from it.

That's your private credit briefing. Talk to you next time.
