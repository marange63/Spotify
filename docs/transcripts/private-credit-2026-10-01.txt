Good morning. It's October 1st. Here's the state of the credit machine.

The default count says this cycle is benign. The courtrooms are saying something different. A low default rate tells you how often lenders lose money. It says nothing about how much they lose when they do. This week brought two live restructurings that speak to that second question, and they sit at opposite ends of the range.

One note on scope first. The private-credit tape itself was quiet this week. We found no new BDC results, no fresh fund close, and no new non-accrual print. So today's read on private lending comes through the bankruptcy courts, and through what those cases imply for the marks that private books will have to carry.

Start with the consensus view, because you need it as the baseline. Deutsche Bank's model-derived cumulative default rate for US CLOs stood at 1.1 percent through September 1st. That's about half the 2.5 percent rate at the same point last year. PitchBook, reporting the numbers, puts CLO portfolio defaults at their lowest level in four years. Deutsche calls the backdrop "still-constructive" for leveraged-loan borrowers.

Hold that number loosely. It's one bank's model, and it's a year-to-date cumulative figure, so don't line it up against trailing-twelve-month default rates, which run on a different basis. It also can't tell you how many weak credits were refinanced, extended, or moved out of portfolios before they could default. Earlier this week we argued that deferrals and distressed exchanges are carrying much of this cycle's stress, outside a simple default count. So take the four-year low as a statement about frequency. Yesterday's filing shows severity.

That filing is Leslie's. The pool-supply retailer filed prearranged Chapter 11 cases on September 30th. It came in with a restructuring support agreement from lenders holding about 81 percent of its term loan debt. The filing put roughly 757 million dollars of term loans into default, alongside 50 million of asset-based borrowings.

Now the size of the cut. Leslie's own press release, filed with its 8-K, says the plan delivers "a reduction of approximately $685 million or 90% of the Company's outstanding funded debt." As reported, that reduction comes from converting the debt into equity. Existing shareholders are wiped out, and the lender group takes control. The company also closed 76 underperforming stores immediately, and more than 900 stay open.

Then the new money. Leslie's lined up 90 million dollars of new-money debtor-in-possession financing. Alongside it sits a 225 million dollar asset-based DIP facility. And the same release discloses a 60 million dollar equity financing, "fully backstopped by certain of the RSA parties." Read that as defensive capital. Lenders are writing fresh checks to protect a position they've just agreed to cut by about nine-tenths.

Here's the analysis that matters. Converting a term loan into equity doesn't book a recovery. It defers one. The lenders now own a pool-supply retailer, a seasonal, consumer-discretionary business. They have to earn their return through operating performance, not a cash payout. Any fund carrying that post-emergence stake will carry it at a model mark.

That is exactly the kind of mark the SEC staff took aim at this week. The Commission's chief accountant and the head of its investment-management division issued a statement on fair value for private assets. It's framed as a reminder of existing obligations, not a new rule. But it is pointed. It says a lack of timely information "does not relieve management of its responsibility to estimate fair value." And it says auditors "should not accept less than persuasive evidence." So a lender-owned stake in a reorganized retailer, with no trading price, is precisely where that evidentiary bar will bite.

Second, this was not a maturity-wall failure. Leslie's refinanced its term loan in March 2021 into an 810 million dollar facility. That pushed the maturity from August 2023 out to March 2028. The loan shrank modestly from there to the roughly 757 million now in default. And we found no record of a liability-management deal or covenant amendment in between. So the company still had about a year and a half of runway when it filed. That points to an operating and leverage problem, not a financing-calendar problem.

Now, who takes the hit. On Sunday, we looked at Loparex, the borrower whose second-lien loan in Blue Owl's flagship BDC saw its private mark collapse. We said that if Loparex filed, it would become a hard recovery datapoint for direct-lending structures. It hasn't filed. Leslie's gives us a hard datapoint anyway, but with a catch. We don't know who holds the paper. Neither the company's filing nor the available trade coverage says whether this term loan sits mostly in CLOs or with direct lenders and BDCs. That matters, because the answer decides which part of the system absorbs the loss. If it's CLO paper, the loss lands in the same market that just posted a four-year-low default rate. That would quietly undercut the benign headline. If it's direct-lending exposure, it's a hard recovery print for private books. Until someone discloses the holder list, treat it as unresolved. And one caution: a single retailer is an example of an outcome, not proof of a trend. A consensual case shows how lenders settle a loss. It doesn't tell you what the loss actually is.

That's the orderly version. Here's the disorderly one. Trinseo, the specialty-materials company, filed a prepackaged Chapter 11 back in May. The aim was to eliminate about 2 billion dollars of debt and cut roughly 140 million dollars of annual interest. That plan has not been confirmed on schedule, because rival creditor groups are disputing the restructuring. On September 28th, a Texas bankruptcy court appointed Robert Drain as mediator.

Trade-press reports say the plan is supported by senior lenders including Apollo Global Management. That's worth sitting with. Apollo is among the platforms raising wealth-channel money for direct lending. In Trinseo, it is also a workout counterparty in a contested restructuring. Whatever Apollo and the other lenders recover in court will eventually feed into what their vehicles report.

What the creditor groups are fighting over isn't established in the available reporting. It could be priority, valuation, or something else, and we won't guess. Be fair about mediation, too. It's a routine way to avoid a contested confirmation fight, and it may resolve quickly. But roughly four months after a prepack filing, this case is in mediation rather than confirmation. The takeaway is that in this cycle, the recovery depends on the courtroom as much as on the collateral.

Put Leslie's and Trinseo side by side and you get the range of outcomes this cycle produces. Leslie's lenders lined up early, with 81 percent backing the deal on the day it filed. Trinseo's lenders are still fighting months later, spending time and professional fees while a mediator works. Recovery assumptions are often anchored on smooth, consensual prepacks. If more cases drift toward the Trinseo pattern, those assumptions are too high. The process itself becomes a cost that erodes recovery, independent of the collateral. That's an implication, not yet a measured result.

So where does that leave the cycle? Defaults remain rare by the headline count, and CLO portfolios look historically clean. But severity is being settled case by case in restructuring courts. A default-rate model doesn't capture that. Meanwhile, lenders are accumulating equity stakes through these conversions. Those stakes sit on private marks that the SEC now says must rest on persuasive evidence. The biggest unresolved risk isn't the default rate. It's whether recovery assumptions built on that benign default rate survive a cycle where losses arrive as debt-for-equity swaps and contested mediations, not clean charge-offs.

Watch four things. First, the Trinseo mediation, which will show whether contested cases are becoming the norm or staying the exception. Second, Leslie's plan milestones, the first read on what that lender-owned equity is actually worth. Third, third-quarter BDC filings in November, which will show whether this is an isolated consumer-retail stumble or the first of several mid-market credits running out of room. Fourth, the result of Blue Owl Credit Income Corp's tender, which closed yesterday. Third-quarter requests at the biggest non-traded private-credit funds have run from 10 percent to over 16 percent of shares, against a customary 5 percent quarterly limit. If Blue Owl's number lands in that range, the liquidity mismatch in retail vehicles is still the binding constraint, whatever the default count says.

That's the state of the lending machine. Have a good day.
