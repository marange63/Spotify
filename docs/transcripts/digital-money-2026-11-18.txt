Good morning. It's Wednesday, November 18th, and this is your digital money briefing.

Three questions have been open on this desk. Did the leveraged long book actually shrink after the weekend flush? Did Monday's ETF session break the outflow streak? And how is Strategy funding its next round of bitcoin buying? All three got answers in the last day. Together they sketch one picture. The forced sellers are mostly gone, but nobody has shown up to replace them as a buyer.

Start with leverage. A week ago, on November 11th, this desk cited K33 putting perpetual open interest at a two-year high in coin terms. Now CryptoQuant puts bitcoin derivatives open interest at about 20.5 billion dollars, its lowest level since late 2024. That is a real move. It fits a week of long-heavy liquidations clearing out levered positions.

But apply the caution this desk used on Binance last Friday. CryptoQuant's figure is counted in dollars, not coins. And bitcoin has fallen from the low seventies to around 64,200 dollars on Tuesday morning, per CoinDesk. So part of that decline is positions that are still open, simply worth fewer dollars. Nobody has published a post-flush coin-denominated reading to compare against K33's high. Trackers also disagree on the dollar level itself, and others put it materially higher. So trust the direction more than the level. The honest claim is materially less dollar leverage, by the only measure available. It is not that the levered book has been purged.

The practical upshot cuts both ways. Less leverage means less fuel for another cascade like the weekend's. That's the good news. It also means less fuel for the short squeeze this desk floated on Monday. Lower amplification, in either direction. So bitcoin's next move depends more on who buys spot than on who is levered.

Fewer forced sellers is not the same thing as a buyer, though, and the ETF tape makes that point. Yesterday this desk couldn't tell you whether Monday's session broke the outflow streak. It did, barely. Per CoinDesk, US spot bitcoin ETFs took in a net 7.9 million dollars on Monday. That ended a run of seven straight days of outflows. Then Tuesday swung right back, with a net outflow of 239.9 million dollars on November 17th.

BlackRock's IBIT accounted for about 209.5 million of that. By our arithmetic, that's close to nine-tenths of the day's total. As this desk noted last Friday, that kind of concentration in one fund fits rebalancing by a few large holders as well as a broad institutional exit. Either way, the streak broke for exactly one session before resuming. Call it a stutter, not a reversal. The wrapper, in short, still isn't the bid.

If the ETFs aren't buying, the other structural buyer just filed its weekly disclosure, and that's the more interesting story. Per Bitcoin Magazine's reporting on the filing, Strategy added 485 bitcoin this week for 33.4 million dollars. It sold new common shares to fund that. But it also spent 228 million dollars buying back its own STRC preferred stock. By our arithmetic, that's nearly seven times what it spent on bitcoin.

Now set that against the prior week, which this desk covered on November 11th. That week's preferred buyback was 188.3 million dollars, a bit more than twice the bitcoin spend. So in one week, the ratio went from roughly two to one to nearly seven to one. That is an escalation, not a steady state.

The coin side moved the other way. Strategy's purchase shrank by more than half, from 1,036 bitcoin the prior week to 485 this week. Against reported holdings of roughly 850,000 bitcoin, this week's buy barely registers. The largest corporate buyer is still buying, but in rounding-error size.

Give the bull reading its due. By our arithmetic, this week's coins cost about 68,900 dollars each. That's well below the roughly 75,450 dollar blended cost this desk cited on November 11th. So buying here does lower Strategy's average, if only marginally. And retiring preferred stock cuts a fixed dividend obligation that sits senior to the common. In a drawdown, that is a genuine de-risking move.

But the trend is the story. The largest corporate bitcoin treasury is increasingly spending its equity raises defending its capital structure rather than adding coins. Strategy hasn't said why it's prioritizing the preferred, so treat the motive as our inference. The mechanism, though, is not inference. The common stock now finances both the preferred defense and whatever bitcoin buying still happens. And that common is the asset falling fastest. MSTR shares fell 29.1 percent over the past month, faster than bitcoin itself declined. A falling common makes every future raise more expensive, whichever purpose it serves.

That's why the date this desk led with yesterday matters here. Strategy's deletion from MSCI's indexes takes effect at the close on November 30th. That is passive selling, blind to fundamentals. It lands on the one instrument carrying the whole structure. Our inference is that the corporate bid could weaken further, just as the levered bid has already gone. So Strategy is still a buyer, but a shrinking one, and its own funding channel is under pressure.

If neither the ETF wrapper nor the biggest treasury company is adding much, check the cash sitting on-chain. DefiLlama's dashboard shows Tether's USDT at about 175.2 billion dollars, against Circle's USDC at 69.3 billion. By our arithmetic, Tether is roughly two and a half times Circle's size. DefiLlama's snapshot puts the whole float at roughly 284 billion dollars. But we don't have a same-source prior reading. So we can't tell you whether the float has turned back to growth, the signal Glassnode named last week. That turn remains unconfirmed.

Tether's lead is worth a beat. It holds that position despite weaker US regulatory alignment than Circle. That undercuts any clean story that compliance alone is winning market share. The regulatory catalyst, at least, is dated. The OCC's final GENIUS Act rule for payment stablecoins takes effect on January 18th, 2027, alongside the Act itself. The Fed's parallel rule, per the reporting we have, is still only a proposal. Our inference is that a hard deadline pulls bank and fintech stablecoin announcements forward into year-end. The skeptical counterweight is adoption. A Conference of State Bank Supervisors survey found that 49 percent of community bank respondents had no plans to enter the stablecoin business.

That float is also where crypto touches the Treasury market. Stablecoin issuers hold their reserves largely in short bills. So a float that isn't demonstrably growing adds no incremental front-end demand through this channel. That's our inference, and it's the same read-through this desk drew last Friday. The yield-bearing side of the on-chain dollar is growing, though. Franklin Templeton's tokenized funds added about 532.4 million dollars in market cap over thirty days. That includes 151.8 million in the week ending November 2nd.

That fits the logic this desk has run all week. Holding a zero-yield stablecoin costs more when cash is priced to pay more for longer. But it's one issuer's gain. Set against Tether's 175 billion alone, by our arithmetic it's well under half a percent. So it's consistent with a rotation toward yield-bearing tokens, not evidence of one. And it's nowhere near the scale that moves the bill market.

Weigh it together, and the bull case is real but narrow. There are fewer forced sellers, by the only leverage measure available. Strategy is still buying, below its own blended cost. And retiring preferred genuinely de-risks its balance sheet. What's missing is any evidence of fresh demand. The ETFs managed one inflow session, not a trend. The stablecoin float shows no confirmed turn. And the biggest corporate buyer's purchases are shrinking, even as its funding instrument falls faster than the asset it buys. That makes bitcoin's floor a demand problem now, not a forced-selling problem. It's a different risk from last week's, and no measure in hand says it's solved.

Three things to watch into next week. First, Strategy's next weekly filing, and whether the buyback-to-bitcoin ratio climbs past seven to one. Second, how MSTR trades into the November 30th MSCI deletion. Third, whether spot ETF flows can string together more than one inflow session. One cross-desk wildcard sits on top of all three. Nvidia's latest results aren't in anything we have yet, so their read-through for crypto risk appetite is still to come.

The flush did its job on the seller side. The question now is whether anyone shows up to buy.

That's the briefing for November 18th. Thanks for listening, and I'll catch you tomorrow.
