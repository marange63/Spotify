Good morning. It's November 20th, 2026, and this is your Cautious Optimism briefing on AI products.

Yesterday's episode told you Google was giving agentic Tasks away free to hundreds of millions of people. Today, Google's own release notes complicate that story. Two days after the free Tasks rollout, Google put its most powerful new capability behind a paywall. That capability is control of your actual desktop. It's the pattern across today's four developments. The paywall on AI agents didn't disappear. It moved up a rung. The free tier gets errands, and everything riskier or costlier sits behind a subscription or a preview label.

Start with that desktop capability. On November 19th, Google shipped computer use in the Gemini app for Mac. In Google's own words, "Gemini can now navigate Mac apps and websites in the background to finish complex tasks." You describe what you need, and, as Google puts it, "Gemini handles the clicking, typing, menus, and forms for you." That's a different kind of agent from a chatbot with plug-ins. It isn't limited to a curated partner list, the way many agentic integrations are. It drives the same interface a human would use. So the agent no longer needs a company to build an integration before it can act.

That cuts against a point this show made yesterday. We argued that the Tasks list of more than 60 connected apps was becoming the new storefront, because a service outside the list risks being invisible to the agent. Computer use partly routes around that list. Any Mac app the user grants access to becomes something Gemini can operate, partner or not. That's our inference. But it means that on the desktop, the integration list matters less than it does on the phone.

Now the gate. Google's blog says computer use is "rolling out in English in the U.S." and is "available to Google AI Plus, Pro and Ultra users." That means paid tiers only, for personal accounts aged eighteen and up. It also requires an Apple Silicon Mac running macOS Sequoia 15.0 or later. It is not part of the free Tasks rollout. And "rolling out" means even paying subscribers may not all have it yet.

Compare what Google widened on the free side the same week. Its release notes say you can now "text Gemini and manage Tasks in Gemini right from Google Messages or iMessage," after a one-time phone verification. You can also "email Gemini at gemini@google.com to kick off Tasks." Both are rolling out in English in the U.S. They sit in the same release-notes entry as the free Tasks rollout. Google doesn't explicitly say these two channels are free, though, so treat that as our read of the placement. Either way, the errand side got more doorways, while the desktop got a price.

The safety posture is the other half of the story. Google says users can "watch every step live, pause at any time, and approve key actions before they happen." It also says "you choose which apps Gemini can access." On November 14th, this show noted that every agent launch that week shipped with a mandatory human checkpoint. Google's desktop agent keeps one. It hasn't been traded away for speed. But Google doesn't list which actions count as key, so the checkpoint's exact scope is still undefined.

What's missing is just as telling. Google publishes no success rate, no reliability data, and no usage numbers for computer use. Desktop automation has a long history of flashy demos that break on contact with real software. So treat this as availability, not proven usefulness. The paid gate is ambiguous too. It could be pricing strategy. It could just as easily reflect the compute cost of running a vision-and-action loop against live screens. Or it could reflect the safety risk of handing an agent the mouse. The sources don't say which. Either way, the takeaway is plain. The free tier runs your errands, and the paid tier gets the keyboard and the mouse.

OpenAI drew the same line this week, but on speed rather than surface. Yesterday we held back GPT-6.2 pricing, because it reached us only through third-party trackers. OpenAI's own developer-community announcement now confirms it. GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol are live in ChatGPT Work and Codex for Plus, Pro, Enterprise, and Edu users. Astra is the model for complex work, and its rate is "reduced by 20% compared to GPT-6 Astra." Sol, the everyday model, "keeps the GPT-6.1 Sol rate." OpenAI's post gives the discount, not the absolute per-token price. So the 20 percent is a relative cut on an undisclosed base. And as this show has argued before, a per-token cut is not the same thing as a per-task cut.

The more interesting move is what OpenAI reserved. Its low-latency serving tier is called Ultrafast. OpenAI says that "for the first time," Ultrafast is coming to subscribers on the plan it calls Pro 200, starting with GPT-6.2 Sol. So the frontier model gets cheaper per token, while fast serving becomes something you pay a premium plan for. Our inference is that the pricing premium is migrating from model quality toward latency. Hold that loosely. OpenAI publishes no latency figure for Ultrafast, so "faster" is the company's label, not a measured gain. And the gating may be about capacity rather than segmentation. Low-latency serving is expensive to run at scale, and OpenAI may simply not be able to offer it broadly yet.

If speed does become the priced axis, power users and coding-heavy teams face a new kind of choice. They'd pick a tier by how long they're willing to wait, not only by which model is smartest. That's our inference too. The landing point for OpenAI is simple. It cut the price of intelligence, and it put a price on speed.

A third version of the same gate shows up somewhere more sensitive: your financial accounts. One standing disclosure first. Anthropic makes the model that powers this show. Anthropic's Personal Finance feature, which links a user's financial accounts to Claude, is already in beta. Per Anthropic's release notes, "It's available on Pro, Max, and Ultra plans, and is available in the US only." So the shape repeats. Free gets the chat, and paid gets access to something sensitive, this time a user's own money. The release notes we reviewed don't say whether Claude can act on those accounts or only answer questions about them, so don't assume either.

In the enterprise, the gate isn't a subscription tier at all. It's a preview label. Salesforce and Microsoft announced Copilotforce at Microsoft's Ignite conference this week. It lets sellers prep for meetings, analyze opportunities, and take actions on live Salesforce customer data from inside Microsoft Copilot. A Teams integration is in pilot. Salesforce's own announcement labels the core capability "Public Preview (Beta)," not general availability. Most of the pieces announced alongside it are preview or pilot as well, and only the Fabric data-federation piece is generally available. Salesforce also cites over 400 million active Teams users. That's Microsoft's reach, not a count of who can use Copilotforce. And the announcement we reviewed carries no price.

The product implication is bigger than the preview tag suggests. Two of the largest enterprise software vendors are letting a rival's interface act on a system of record. The usual move is for each vendor to wall its agent inside its own app. If this works, what's at risk isn't Salesforce's data, which Salesforce still controls. What's at risk is the seat inside Salesforce's own screen, because a seller may never need to open it. That's our inference, and there are two counter-readings. One is defensive. If customers were going to live inside Copilot anyway, Salesforce is better off as the data underneath than being routed around. The other is licensing. If Salesforce keeps charging per user regardless of interface, seat revenue may not move at all. For SaaS incumbents, the takeaway is that their own screen is becoming optional, even if the license isn't yet.

So, four products, one shape. There's a free floor for errands, led by Google's Tasks. Then there are paid tiers or preview labels for anything that touches a desktop, a speed tier, your financial accounts, or a customer record. Three things to watch from here. First, whether Google extends computer use to free users, or publishes any reliability data for it. Second, whether OpenAI discloses Ultrafast's actual latency gain, or extends it from Sol to Astra. Third, whether Copilotforce reaches general availability with a price attached.
