Good morning, this is your Cautious Optimism Briefing for July 29th, 2026.

This week the marginal shifts in strategic power showed up as market prices before they showed up as law. Start with chips. Beijing and Taipei are tightening control over the same TSMC-centered supply chain from opposite ends, in the same week, for opposite reasons. China's Commerce Ministry is consulting Alibaba, ByteDance, and Zhipu on a rule that would bar TSMC and Qualcomm from producing advanced chips based on designs from Chinese firms like Huawei, according to a Financial Times report. The same package goes further than chips. It would also restrict the overseas transfer of the data Chinese firms use to train their AI models, and block foreign users from downloading those models' weights. Regulators say the measures could be folded into the next revision of China's catalogue of technologies restricted from export. So Beijing wants leverage over which foreign fabs can serve its own national champions, and over how far its AI stack travels.

There's a tension inside that move worth naming. Beijing has spent this year letting open-weight models like Kimi and Qwen diffuse abroad as soft power. Walling off model weights and training data cuts the other way. That is the internal contradiction in China's tech strategy: diffuse for influence, or hoard for security. This week it chose hoarding.

Taiwan is moving the other way. Taipei is weighing a stricter export standard, known as N-2, that would only let TSMC ship manufacturing technology two generations behind its home leading edge, tighter than today's one-generation rule. That change is explicitly aimed at TSMC's own Arizona expansion. The first phase of its Fab 21 site there already complies. It's the second phase, which starts three-nanometer-class production in 2027, that would fall foul of the new standard. So Taipei is using statute to cap how fast America's onshoring push can catch up to Taiwan's home fabs. Notice what that does to TSMC itself. The company is now squeezed from both sides: Washington paying billions to pull leading-edge production onshore, and Taipei writing law to keep it at home. The subsidy and the statute point in opposite directions, and TSMC is caught in between.

On July 28th, the market repriced this hard. TSMC shares fell close to 3 percent, and Taiwan's TAIEX index dropped 2,030 points, its third-worst single-day decline on record. Here's the important caveat: coverage of that selloff does not agree on why. A primary Taiwan-press account puts the blame mainly on Federal Reserve rate uncertainty and AI-spending jitters, and it quantifies TSMC's own contribution to the index drop at 556 points, which means most of that 2,030-point fall actually came from other stocks entirely, not TSMC. A separate account instead points to the export-control overhang as the driver. Neither source ranks the two causes against each other, and neither China's rule nor Taiwan's has been finalized, so treat both as proposals under consultation, not law, and treat the crash as a shared verdict on two live risks at once, not proof that export controls alone spooked the market. What's durable here is the state capacity question: two governments are now asserting control over one private company's commercial buildout, from opposite directions, and the market is pricing that risk before either legislature votes.

States are metering one input by decree. Markets are metering another by price, and that shows up in oil. Crude fell to $79.28 a barrel on July 28th, down 4 percent on the day, as renewed diplomacy over the Strait of Hormuz calmed chokepoint fears that had briefly pushed crude toward $100 earlier in the month. But refined products didn't follow crude down. European diesel refining margins hit a record above $60 a barrel after Russia banned diesel exports in response to Ukrainian drone strikes on its refineries. The 3:2:1 crack spread, the standard measure of refining profit, broke its 2022 record and pushed toward $64 in mid-July. US wholesale diesel futures are up 26 percent this month alone. The scarcity this month is in the refinery, not the wellhead. Estimates of how much Russian refining capacity those strikes knocked out range widely, from the IEA's "more than 20 percent" to Ukraine's own claim of over 40 percent, so treat that as a range, not a settled number.

Two policy choices sit on top of that physical scarcity. Meanwhile the EU's newest sanctions package, adopted July 23rd, chose to freeze the price cap on Russian crude at $44.10 a barrel for another year, rather than let a scheduled review lift it toward roughly $58.50 to track higher market prices. That's a deliberate choice to keep squeezing Russian oil revenue rather than follow the market up. The second-order effect is that a below-market cap plus a deepening Russian fuel crisis pushes more Russian barrels onto the shadow fleet, which is exactly the flow the EU's new enforcement powers are meant to catch. And because the crude move rests on Hormuz diplomacy holding, a geopolitical premium is still embedded in the price that could snap back the moment talks wobble. So what matters for inflation watchers is this: the pass-through from this month's energy shock lands first in diesel and shipping costs, not in the headline crude price everyone's watching.

And that diesel pass-through is exactly the inflation the Fed can't yet locate. Today the Federal Reserve held its benchmark rate at 3.5 to 3.75 percent for a fifth straight meeting, under new chair Kevin Warsh, who had already called inflation "too high" without signaling which way he'd move. The Fed's statement described activity as expanding at a solid pace, with inflation still running above the 2 percent goal. This meeting carried no fresh Summary of Economic Projections, so there's no new dot plot to read the rate path from, and no confirmed vote breakdown to tell us how united the committee was. Note the overlap: the same Fed rate-path uncertainty that one account blamed for hitting the TAIEX is now the same uncertainty the Fed itself is holding into, with an oil-driven inflation impulse it can't yet cleanly attribute to either tariffs or the diesel shock. That is the problem heading into the fall. Tariff pass-through and energy pass-through could land in the same CPI prints, and the Fed would struggle to tell them apart or to know which fades.

For markets, a hawkish hold with no cut in sight keeps real rates elevated and puts a floor under the dollar. It also keeps upward pressure on the Treasury term premium, since investors financing a hard-to-forecast inflation demand more to hold duration. That same setup cuts two ways for hard-money assets. Elevated real rates, with no near-term cut in sight, are a headwind for gold and Bitcoin narratives that depend on anticipated easing. But persistent, hard-to-tame energy inflation is exactly the environment that strengthens the underlying case for holding them as a hedge. Both are true at once, and the Fed's silence on the path forward leaves that argument unresolved rather than settled.
