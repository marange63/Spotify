Good morning. It's Tuesday, October 27th, and this is your frontier AI labs briefing.

One standing disclosure before we start. Anthropic makes the model behind this show, and today's lead story is Anthropic's own filing. I'll flag every caveat as we go.

Here's the thesis for today. The frontier labs are becoming each other's infrastructure. Anthropic's new filing shows a compute line running up to one hundred twenty billion dollars to the company that owns its rival xAI. Google, meanwhile, now sells OpenAI's models next to its own. So the competitive question is shifting. It's no longer just who has the best model. It's who controls the layer that rivals cannot do without. And on that layer, compute, not distribution, is where the leverage sits.

Let's start with what's actually new. Anthropic filed its S-1 with the SEC on Monday, October 26th, to list on Nasdaq under the ticker A-N-T. This isn't a leaked draft anymore. It's the public filing itself. Reporting says trading could start, quote, "as soon as November 16." That date is media-reported, not confirmed by Anthropic, and the registration statement isn't effective yet. The revenue and loss figures this desk walked through on Sunday are now on the public record. I won't re-run them.

What is new is the operating line. On Sunday we noted the leaked draft had no operating-loss line at all. The public prospectus reportedly fills that gap, and it shows the underlying business crossing into profit. One report quoting the filing says, "Anthropic made 974 million dollars from running its business in the second quarter, according to the IPO prospectus it filed Monday." The same reporting adds, "Three months earlier, the same line showed a 1.7 billion dollar loss." So the operating business swung from loss to profit in a single quarter. That's a real inflection, but it's one quarter. The reporting doesn't tell us how that line treats stock compensation or compute amortization. And one data point is not a trend. Keep the giant GAAP net loss separate in your head from this operating line. They measure different things, and conflating them misreads the business.

Now, the line that actually changes the competitive map is the compute one. In the filing, Anthropic discloses what it calls the SpaceXAI Arrangements. The verbatim language is: "we agreed to spend an aggregate of up to 120.5 billion dollars over terms extending into 2029." That's a ceiling. The filing's own words are "up to," not a guaranteed spend. Once the full contracted capacity arrives, the run-rate is steep: "approximately 3.1 billion dollars in the aggregate per month once all capacity has been delivered." The filing says it expects that full delivery in December 2026. For scale, Anthropic's earlier May deal with SpaceX already gave it access to, quote, "over 220,000 NVIDIA GPUs."

Here's the catch. The filing doesn't total its own commitments anywhere. So we can't cleanly reconcile this 120.5 billion dollar figure against the roughly 450 billion dollar leaked compute total this desk covered on Sunday. One analyst who worked through the filing's tables concluded the SpaceXAI number sits inside Anthropic's broader disclosed commitments, rather than stacking on top of them. But that's an analyst's own tally, not a sentence the company wrote. Treat it as directionally right, not confirmed.

There's also a cancellation clause worth knowing about. The filing's infrastructure section says, "each agreement may be cancelled by either party upon 90 days' advance written notice." That sentence sits in a general passage, though. It doesn't name SpaceXAI. So applying it to this particular deal is a contextual read, not something the filing states outright. And on orbital compute, Anthropic has only, in its own words, "expressed interest in partnering with SpaceX to develop multiple gigawatts of orbital AI compute capacity." That's interest, not a contract. So what's new here is simple. A major new compute supplier to Anthropic is a company that also owns a direct model rival.

Which raises the question of who actually sits on the other side of that contract. SpaceX acquired xAI outright back in February, in an all-stock deal that reporting said valued, quote, "the combined company at 1.25 trillion dollars." That made xAI a wholly owned SpaceX subsidiary. On Friday, after Tesla cut its capex guidance, this desk said the question of who pays for xAI's compute was open again. The S-1 gives a partial answer. A rival lab, Anthropic, is now contracted to pay up to 120.5 billion dollars into the SpaceX compute stack. That same stack presumably also serves xAI's own training. Nothing in the filing shows money moving between those business lines, so that funding link is an inference, not a disclosed fact. And there's no evidence of misallocation or misconduct here. This is a structural risk, not an allegation.

It also changes the kind of compute risk we've been tracking. Yesterday, the worry was supplier solvency, with CoreWeave's short interest climbing and neoclouds like Nscale still lining up financing. SpaceX is not a financing-dependent neocloud. It's a vertically integrated owner of chips, power, and a rival model. So for this counterparty, the risk shifts from whether the supplier can fund the build to whose interests the supplier serves.

There are two honest readings of this, and the evidence doesn't yet favor one. The bullish read for Anthropic is diversification. SpaceX is a major supplier outside the hyperscalers, which pulls Anthropic away from Google and AWS. Each of those two held roughly 110 billion dollars in the earlier leaked breakdown. More suppliers usually means more negotiating leverage. The bearish read is concentration. Anthropic's capacity now depends on a company controlled by the owner of a direct competitor. That's an incentive risk that simply doesn't exist in Anthropic's dealings with Google or Amazon. And if capacity stays scarce, a lab that depends on a rival's parent may have weaker leverage at renewal. What would settle this is how the cancellation and lock-in terms play out, and whether that December delivery lands on schedule. Watch those, not the headline number.

Compare that with the distribution layer, where this kind of co-opetition is already normal and fairly benign. Google Cloud now resells OpenAI's GPT-6 models alongside its own Gemini and Anthropic's Claude. Google's own framing is that it's, quote, "the only hyperscale cloud provider offering access to Gemini, Claude, and GPT." That's a real distribution move, but it's early. The models are, quote, "entering private preview for selected enterprise customers, with general availability planned in the coming weeks." There's no usage data and no verified pricing, and Microsoft remains OpenAI's primary cloud partner. The point isn't the deal itself. It's the contrast. Shelf space in a cloud marketplace isn't scarce, so selling a rival's model costs Google nothing strategically. Compute capacity is scarce, so selling it to a rival carries real leverage. That's why the SpaceX-Anthropic relationship is structurally different from the Google-OpenAI one, even though both get described as rivals hosting rivals.

That split also answers the winner-take-most question, at least for today. Interchangeable distribution pushes the model layer toward commodity economics. Concentrated compute pushes the other way, toward the few players who own chips and power, like Google and SpaceX with xAI. Today's evidence moves the compute dimension, not the model one.

So here's where the competitive dimensions stand. On compute access, SpaceX and Google both gain leverage as suppliers, while Anthropic gains capacity but takes on new counterparty risk. On capital access and disclosure, Anthropic is now the public one, with OpenAI still private, a contrast we've covered before and won't re-run. On distribution, Google is positioning itself as the neutral supermarket for all three major model families. On raw capability, there's no verified move to report this week.

Three things to watch. First, whether Anthropic's registration goes effective and trading actually starts around November 16th. Second, whether SpaceXAI's capacity lands on its December schedule. Third, whether OpenAI's models on Google Cloud move from private preview to general availability. Distribution is commoditizing. Compute is concentrating. That split is the story to track from here.
