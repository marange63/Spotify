Good morning. It's Saturday, August 29th, and this is Capital Markets Radar.

Three briefings running, this program closed on the same unresolved catalyst. Kevin Warsh had not yet given his first Jackson Hole address as Fed chair. Until he did, we said, the September call was an open question, not a resolved one. He spoke Friday, and it resolved against the lean we had been carrying. Odds of a quarter-point hike at the September meeting, per CME FedWatch, jumped to 59.7 percent by late morning. They had been 35.4 percent the day before, and 39.9 percent a week earlier. No CPI, no PPI, no payrolls, no FOMC decision landed in between. That is a twenty-four point move on tone alone. The honest read is not that the Fed changed its mind. It is that the prior 35 percent was carrying almost no conviction to begin with. Positioning is now being set off a chair's cadence rather than off the inflation series itself.

We also got his actual language, from the Fed's own posting of the remarks, rather than only the press framing of them. Warsh cited the twelve-month change in the PCE price index at 3.7 percent, and a hotter 4.1 percent pace over six months. That is the same headline gauge this program carried on Friday, and it is the level he built the speech around. His condition was explicit. Policymakers must be confident inflation is moving to target, in his words, clearly and at sufficient speed. Otherwise, he said, they have work to do. His text also noted that the July committee chose to await new information in the intermeeting period rather than move immediately. Nowhere in the passage we could retrieve did he name the September meeting at all. So treat this as a shift in tone and confidence framing, not as a pre-commitment to hike. That distinction matters a great deal for how durable this repricing turns out to be.

What obeyed the shift within hours was everything without a yield. Spot gold sold off about 3 percent, breaking below forty-five hundred dollars an ounce. The dollar firmed. Oil eased to $82.82 a barrel, down less than one percent on the day. That last one is the piece that does not fit a clean risk-off script. A hawkish dollar impulse should pressure crude harder than that, or leave it alone entirely. Mild softness instead reads as independent demand caution. And that oil level is an aggregator print rather than exchange data, so hold it loosely. The two-year Treasury yield also jumped on the speech. We are not going to give you a precise level. The secondary reporting on the exact print is internally inconsistent, and we would rather tell you that than hand you a number we cannot stand behind.

Now score ourselves, because two calls settled here. On Wednesday this program argued gold's rally had two separate legs. One was a dollar-and-duration leg, fully exposed to a hawkish Warsh. The other was a reserve-diversification leg that would not sell on a single speech. We predicted gold would therefore give back less than a pure rates asset. It didn't. Gold fell roughly 3 percent, essentially in line with bitcoin's 3.2 percent decline the same day. On a one-day horizon, the flow leg dominated the structural leg completely. That is a statement about time horizon rather than a repudiation of the central-bank buying thesis. But the prediction was wrong on the day, and we are saying so rather than quietly dropping it.

Bitcoin is the cleaner test, because we set that one up in advance. On Tuesday this program called bitcoin a rates proxy, and repeated it Wednesday and again Friday. The framing had never been stress-tested by an actual rates impulse. Going into the speech, spot bitcoin ETFs had strung together at least seven straight days of net inflows. That run included $517 million on August 19th, the largest daily take since early May. Bitcoin touched an intraday high above eighty-one thousand dollars earlier this week before stalling. Institutional demand was building steadily right into the repricing. Then the speech landed, and bitcoin traded down alongside gold rather than as a hedge against it. That confirms the rates-proxy framing rather than breaking it. But the price move is not the real tell. The tell is whether the ETF creation streak breaks in the days ahead, because those flows are the most reversible capital sitting in this trade. We do not have post-Friday flow data. And the inflow figures and Friday's drawdown come from different sources that nobody has reconciled into a single flow-through-Friday number. So this is a test that has been set, not a test that has been scored.

Now turn to what did not move, because that is the more interesting mispricing. As of the week of August 20th, before the speech, high-yield spreads sat around 281 basis points. That is the richest decile of that index's history, against a long-run median near 450. Bond volatility was dormant alongside it, with the MOVE index at 74.7. Both figures are pre-speech, from a single asset-manager commentary, and we have no post-speech print for either. On Wednesday this program defended that tightness as rational rather than complacent, on two specific grounds. Long yields were falling, and the energy impulse was dead. One of those two premises just broke. So we cannot tell you credit failed to react. What we can tell you is that it was priced against a Fed that was still easing, and that world now looks different. The asymmetry is the point. Equity volatility is calm too, with the VIX near 14.51 on Friday evening, even as policy odds moved twenty-four points in a single session. Part of that calm has a benign explanation worth granting: Nvidia's beat landed earlier in the week, and may genuinely be offsetting a higher discount rate in equity pricing rather than ignoring it. But a market this rich and this quiet, heading into a live rate-path surprise, has far more room to move one way than the other. And if bond volatility does wake up, the widening arrives first in the instrument priced richest, which is high yield. It reaches private credit's funding benchmarks and business development company cost of capital second, not first.

Which brings us to what credit is actually underwriting. Nvidia's headline revenue and its guide for the current quarter you have already heard from us this week. What is new, and what we did not give you, is the margin trajectory. Nvidia guided fiscal fourth-quarter gross margin down to a range of 71 to 72 percent, from 75 percent today, citing rising memory costs. That is the first company-confirmed cost signal of this capex cycle. And it lands in the same week the discount rate applied to that entire trade got harder to argue down. Read it in both directions. It is a genuine input-cost squeeze on every AI infrastructure buyer without contracted memory supply, which makes it more painful for the rest of the stack than for Nvidia itself. Nvidia is absorbing three points off a seventy-five point gross margin. A challenger without supply locked up is absorbing it off far less. And the same fact cuts bullish, because rising memory prices are a byproduct of demand, not a sign of demand cracking. So the capex cycle is visibly cost-inflating at the same moment the rate path financing it got tighter. Those two facts now have to be reconciled with each other, not reported side by side.

Here is the calendar into that reconciliation. Payrolls land September 4th, PPI September 10th, CPI September 11th, and the FOMC decides September 16th. Nothing before that payrolls print tests a hike probability near sixty percent that was set entirely on a chair's tone. One more thing belongs in that window. A Fed chair leaning hawkish against an administration that prefers lower rates is live headline risk into the meeting, which argues for those odds staying volatile rather than converging on anything. Until then, watch three things: whether the bitcoin ETF creation streak breaks, whether credit moves off its richest-decile starting point, and whether the calm in equity and bond volatility survives contact with actual data. That is where this resolves.

That's Capital Markets Radar for August 29th. Trade safe, and we'll see you next time.
