Good morning. It's Sunday, August 16th, and this is your frontier AI labs briefing.

The capability leaderboard has been frozen all week, clustered around the low sixties on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index. So the competition keeps sliding down a layer. Earlier in the week it was financing, then talent and distribution. Today it's two layers further down: inference speed, and the price of open-weight coding models. Neither is a capability leapfrog. Both tell you where the real fight is now being fought.

Start with OpenAI. On August 13th, OpenAI and chipmaker Cerebras unveiled a preview tier called Ultrafast, running the flagship GPT-5.6 Sol model at up to 750 output tokens per second. That's roughly fourteen times standard processing speed. The target use cases are the ones where wall-clock time is the constraint, not benchmark score: incident response, live customer support, real-time trading tools. That's the point. For voice agents and trading desks, a model that scores well on a leaderboard but takes three seconds to answer is useless. A model that answers instantly, even at similar quality, is a different product. So this opens a new competitive axis: latency, sold separately from intelligence.

Follow that axis one step further, because it changes who gets paid. If a durable low-latency tier holds, the inference market fragments by workload. Latency-sensitive traffic, voice and trading and real-time agents, becomes a separately-priced product line from ordinary batch inference. And it runs on different silicon. The reason Ultrafast exists at all is Cerebras, whose wafer-scale parts are built for exactly this speed regime rather than for throughput. So this is also a quiet bid to carve out a defensible niche for specialty chips alongside Nvidia's GPUs and Google's TPUs. The caveat matters just as much as the headline, though. OpenAI hasn't disclosed pricing or a general-availability date. This is a preview, not a shipped, priced product. So treat it as a demonstration of what's technically possible rather than a market move yet, and watch for a price before you underwrite it.

Now the other side of the ledger: open-weight pricing. China's Z.ai released GLM-5.3 on August 14th and billed it as the top open-weight coding model available. Worth checking that claim against Z.ai's own numbers, because it only half holds up. On CyberGym, a benchmark for finding security vulnerabilities, GLM-5.3 does lead, scoring 84.5%. But on Z.ai's own preferred yardstick, its Code Bench, GLM-5.3 scores 34.5%, still behind Claude Fable 5's 39.5% on that same test. So the model that's marketed as the coding crown trails the US frontier leader on the metric its own maker chose to showcase it. Where GLM-5.3 does win outright is price: its API runs around $1.40 per million input tokens and $4.40 per million output tokens, roughly one-tenth of US frontier rates. Its open weights aren't even public yet; they're targeted for release about two weeks after the announcement. So the honest way to state this gap is capability-adjusted price, not raw capability. Chinese open-weight models aren't catching up on quality yet. They're compressing the price you pay for a given level of quality. That's a different, and for now more durable, story than a simple narrative of China overtaking the West.

That gap is only visible because someone checked the harder number. And that keeps being the lesson on this show. Take xAI's Grok 4.6, which rolled out this past week priced at two dollars in and six dollars out per million tokens, with Elon Musk citing a 1753 Elo score. That figure is self-reported and hasn't been verified on any public leaderboard, so treat it as marketing until an independent eval confirms it. It's the same pattern as GLM-5.3's coding-crown claim, and the same pattern behind the unconfirmed Chinese benchmark numbers we flagged earlier this week: a vendor-chosen number that looks great until you check it against a harder, independently-verified one. The practical takeaway for an enterprise buyer is dull but important. As every lab cites its own preferred benchmark, the independent evaluators, Artificial Analysis and the public arenas, are becoming the only load-bearing signal. The marketing number tells you what a lab wants you to believe. The third-party number tells you what to budget against.

That price compression shows up more broadly in a genuinely crossed pricing map this week. DeepSeek's peak-hour price increase, first flagged on August 6th, took effect today, August 16th. The company disclosed the actual mechanism on August 13th: a new dynamic pricing scheme that raises rates by more than four times during peak hours, while off-peak rates stay at half that peak level. Concretely, DeepSeek's V4-Pro model goes from 87 cents to $3.96 per million tokens at peak times. Now, the easy read is that DeepSeek is retreating from cheap pricing. But its own framing is demand-based load balancing, and that framing actually looks accurate. If it were simply repricing toward profitability, it wouldn't hold off-peak rates near the old level. It's holding off-peak. So this looks more like congestion pricing from a capacity-constrained provider than a price-war reversal, and that distinction matters: the cheap floor is still there when the servers aren't busy.

Meanwhile, US labs have been cutting into their own commodity tiers, which is the mirror image. OpenAI cut GPT-5.6 Luna pricing by 80% on July 30th, down to 20 cents input and $1.20 output per million tokens, and cut the mid-tier Terra by 20%, to $2 and $12. Notably, the flagship Sol tier was left untouched at $5 and $30. So OpenAI is defending its top tier's pricing power while racing the cheap end toward cost. That is the whole strategy in one price sheet. Anthropic's posture cuts against a tidy story, too. Its Sonnet 5 pricing is actually reverting higher, from a promotional $2 and $10 per million tokens back to $3 and $15 on September 1st. That's a promo expiring, not a fresh price cut, so it doesn't fit neatly on either side of a China-versus-US pricing fight. The point of walking through these is that the pricing map no longer points one direction.

Put it all together and the picture is a market splitting in two. Frontier tiers, Sol, Sonnet 5, Claude Fable 5, are holding or raising price because there's no substitute for them yet. Commodity tiers, Luna, Terra, DeepSeek's off-peak rate, are being competed toward marginal cost because there is a substitute. And notice how the US labs are choosing to defend that commodity end. Not by matching China on price, which is a losing game at one-tenth the rate, but by attacking their own cost curve, so that a cheap tier can still carry margin. Latency tiers and efficiency both serve that same goal. So the durable competitive question isn't who has the highest score. It's who can hold price at the top and defend margin at the bottom at the same time.

So the scoreboard that mattered a month ago, whose model scores highest, isn't moving. The scoreboards that are moving now are latency and price tier, and those are the ones enterprise buyers should actually be underwriting their AI budgets against.
