Good morning, it's September 8th, and this is your robotics and physical AI briefing.

For the past week this show has kept returning to one argument: the scarce asset in physical AI is permission. Driver-out status, a city approval, a certification — those are things an outside authority grants, and we said whoever collects them wins the ground. Today that argument runs into its own limit. The operator with the most permission in American autonomous driving turns out to have the least control over where its cars are actually built.

Start with the fact that is new to this beat. Waymo has cumulatively imported more than 3,200 Zeekr-built vehicles, the model it calls the Ojai, from China. For scale only, that sits inside a national commercial fleet reported past 4,000 vehicles across fourteen metros. Treat that fleet number as context, not news — it is a press aggregation Waymo itself has never published.

The cost line is where it gets interesting. A thirty-eight thousand dollar Ojai chassis lands at roughly seventy-eight thousand once a 102.5 percent tariff is applied. Waymo then adds about twenty thousand dollars of American-made autonomy hardware on top. Read the behavior here rather than the tariff rate. A company with the best regulatory position in the industry chose to pay an import tax that more than doubles its chassis cost, and then kept doing it, thirty-two hundred times over. That tells you the Chinese cost advantage is large enough to survive being doubled.

Now the step that explains the policy. Before an Ojai reaches American roads it is stripped of its Chinese sensors and its Chinese compute. That is because the Commerce Department's connected-vehicle rule forbids Chinese hardware and software from collecting driving data on US soil. Waymo installs its own sensing and its own compute here afterward. So the line is not drawn at the factory. It is drawn at the data layer. The body can be Chinese. The eyes and the brain have to be American. That is a much narrower rule than it sounds, and it is the one Waymo is threading.

Two cautions before anyone builds a model on this. First, nobody has published a verified, apples-to-apples cost comparing an Ojai to the Jaguar or Hyundai platforms it runs alongside. So "still cheaper after tariff" is an inference from revealed behavior, not a disclosure. I went looking for that comparison specifically and could not source one. Second, do not divide the cumulative import figure into the fleet count and call it a share. Those two numbers come from different dates and different sources, and nobody publishes a current chassis breakdown of the operating fleet.

The politics have moved past commentary, and that is the part that actually changed. At a Senate hearing, Senator Bernie Moreno challenged Waymo's chief safety officer directly. He told him, quote, "it seems like you're getting in bed with China." Then he asked whether the cars Waymo wants to bring in are Chinese automobiles owned by Chinese companies. Waymo's on-record answer was that those vehicles have, quote, "no smarts, no connectivity," and that the autonomy system goes in here in America with no data shared back.

There is a legislative version of that pressure too. Moreno and Senator Elissa Slotkin are reported to have advanced bipartisan legislation out of committee that would target where a connected vehicle is manufactured, rather than what hardware gets installed in it later. Treat that bill as reported and not verified — I could not obtain its text or its current status from a primary record. But note the shape of the threat anyway. A tariff is a cost. A country-of-manufacture ban is not something a strip-and-refit workaround can satisfy.

So the so-what on the lead: permission is necessary, and it is not sufficient. Waymo holds the friendliest regulatory posture in the industry and is still the most supply-chain-exposed operator on the board. Push that read across to humanoids, whose actuators, magnets and cells run through the same geography, and the position is worse, not better. And set it against something this show covered five days ago. In late July the FCC added foreign-produced humanoids, quadrupeds and other mobile robots to its Covered List — a step that blocks the equipment authorization a new model needs before it can be imported, marketed or sold here. Take that as reported at the time, not as a rule text I have verified this week. But the contrast is striking. Same government, opposite instinct: squeeze the foreign-built robot out of the market, and permit the Chinese robotaxi body, so long as the sensing and the data stay American.

If the best-capitalized autonomy fleet in America cannot cheaply source a body outside China, look at what China is building — and, more tellingly, at what it chose to measure. Beijing closed the second World Humanoid Robot Games late last month: five days, 2,056 robots, 666 teams. The headline was Tiangong Ultra, from the state-linked Beijing Humanoid Robot Innovation Center, running the hundred meters in 8.64 seconds. That is faster than Usain Bolt's human world record of 9.58.

Handle that record the way it deserves. It is organizer-timed, on a prepared straight course, and the sources name no independent athletics or robotics body that verified it. A sprint like that is lower-body control, actuator power density and battery discharge. It tells you nothing about the long tail of unstructured work. The sources also do not say whether the winning runs were fully autonomous or task-tuned for that specific course. State that absence plainly. It is not an accusation, it is a gap in the disclosure.

The more investable scoreboard is the medal table, and it went the other way. Shanghai-based AGIBOT topped it with forty-six medals in total, and it won them in dexterous manipulation and scenario-based tasks rather than on the track. That is the same AGIBOT this show scrutinized earlier this week over its Longcheer inspection-line accuracy claim. Two appearances in one week, and both point the same direction: bounded, structured manipulation work, not general-purpose autonomy. A state-linked lab optimized a marketable sprint. A commercially funded one optimized the tasks that resemble a job. Reading the stopwatch as evidence of general capability is reading the wrong scoreboard, and it is the same benchmark inflation this beat has flagged all week in self-reported reliability and shipment numbers.

One corner of this sector has neither a foreign supply chain nor a self-graded number, and that is defense. The Army has awarded a combined 192 million dollars to move the TITAN mobile ground station from prototype into production — 127 million to Palantir, 65 million to Anduril. The exact date of the award was not pinned down in the sources reviewed, so take it as this month rather than this week. What makes it matter is the character of the number. A customer with no incentive to flatter its vendors put a real dollar figure behind an actual prototype-to-production transition. That is precisely the transition this sector claims constantly and documents almost never.

Then look at which side of that split got the bigger number. Two thirds of the award went to Palantir, the software and data layer, and one third to Anduril. That is the same shape Shield AI showed in June, when its Hivemind autonomy software won an Air Force production contract for the Collaborative Combat Aircraft program while flying aboard an Anduril airframe it does not build. The TITAN award is this month's confirmation of the pattern: production dollars are landing on the autonomy and software layer rather than on whoever bends the metal, and that is a problem for the hardware primes. Keep the scale honest, though. 192 million dollars is a rounding error against the private valuations attached to this category. And Anduril's often-cited twenty billion dollar Lattice figure is a contract ceiling running through 2036, not booked revenue.

Pull the threads together. The scarce asset is not just permission, it is provenance, and provenance is unevenly distributed. Waymo has the permission and the exposed bill of materials. China has the manufacturing scale and is still choosing to showcase locomotion over autonomy. Defense is the one corner where the money and the supply chain are both domestic and both documented. Three things would settle this: whether that Moreno-Slotkin bill actually reaches a floor vote, whether NHTSA's audit of Tesla's Cybercab self-certification produces a finding, and whether AGIBOT's manipulation wins ever get tested outside a scored competition.

That's the briefing. Talk tomorrow.
