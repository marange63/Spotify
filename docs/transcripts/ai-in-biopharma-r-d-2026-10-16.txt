Good morning. It's Friday, October 16th, 2026, and this is your briefing on AI in biopharma R&D.

All week, the deal flow has priced AI drug discovery as options on future molecules. Today we look at a contract that puts a hard price on the model on one side and the data on the other. It's a September filing, not new paper. But it reads differently now that Recursion has priced a two-hundred-million-dollar equity offering this week, because the filing shows where some of Recursion's cash is committed to go.

Start with Recursion and Tempus. In September, Recursion filed an 8-K amending its relationship with Tempus, the real-world-data and AI platform. That filing did two things. First, Recursion licensed its RNA foundation model, TxFM, to Tempus. The price is twelve million dollars over two years, paid in two six-million-dollar installments. The license is non-exclusive, non-sublicensable, non-transferable, and limited to the field of oncology.

Second, running the other direction, Recursion's existing data-license agreement with Tempus was reset. The term was extended from five years to six. Recursion now pays Tempus fourteen million dollars a year in the later years of the deal, committed. And Recursion's right to terminate the deal for convenience was eliminated.

Now set that against another deal from this week. AstraZeneca signed a collaboration with insitro. insitro runs its Virtual Human AI platform to find disease-driving biology and advance targets that AstraZeneca selects. AstraZeneca gets exclusive worldwide rights to the resulting programs. A key open question is who keeps the biology data insitro generates along the way. The Recursion and Tempus contract shows what that kind of data access is worth when it actually changes hands. The trained biology model licenses narrowly, by field, for about six million dollars a year. The data access flowing the other way commands fourteen million a year, committed, with the exit ramp removed. On its face, that's a real signal about where pricing power sits in this stack.

It's also a template for the IP question that hangs over every sponsor building its own models. Recursion monetized its model without giving up control of it. Tempus gets a use right that it can't pass on, can't sublicense, and can only apply in one therapeutic area. Watch whether that structure, non-exclusive and field-limited, becomes the default way biology foundation models get commercialized.

But be careful with the clean version of the price comparison. These are not equivalent rights. A two-year, oncology-only, non-exclusive model license is a far narrower thing than six years of data access. So the gap between six million and fourteen million partly reflects scope, not just bargaining power.

The primary filing complicates the cash story too. Before the amendment, Recursion's annual license fees could be paid one hundred percent in its own Class A stock, at its own election. After the amendment, at least four million dollars of each fourteen-million payment must be paid in cash. That cash floor is new. But the rest of each payment can still be stock, cash, or a mix, again at Recursion's choice. So the negotiated change isn't a swing from stock to cash. It's that the amount is now fixed and committed, and the exit right is gone.

There's a second piece of evidence, separate from the contract. Tempus's second-quarter results showed its Data and Applications segment generating ninety-three million dollars, up twenty-eight percent year over year. Inside that segment, the Insights data-licensing line grew faster still, at thirty-six percent. That's the data layer growing in its own right, not resting on one Recursion contract. The so-what: in AI drug development right now, the firmest claims on cash belong to whoever owns real-world data at scale. Recursion, fresh off this week's equity raise, is on the paying side of that ledger.

The same data is now being pointed at the decision where most drugs actually die. Tempus is running a collaboration with Bristol Myers Squibb, announced back in May, so this is context rather than fresh news. The two companies are using AI and multimodal real-world records on Tempus's Lens platform. The goal is to optimize trial design and estimate probability of technical and regulatory success, PTRS, across five initial programs. Those span lung, colon, and prostate cancer, and extend into Alzheimer's.

No financial terms have been disclosed. More importantly, no outcome has been disclosed either. There's no measured change in PTRS accuracy, no shortened timeline, and no dollar saved. Among the deals in view, it's the one aimed squarely at the go/no-go call rather than at discovery. That's the right lever. If PTRS modeling ever demonstrably improves those calls, Phase 2 and Phase 3 spend on programs likely to fail falls, and that's the most direct route to better R&D productivity. But that remains conditional. Prospective evidence that real-world-data-driven PTRS beats historical base rates doesn't exist yet. Tempus has also expanded work with Gilead on real-world data and with Daiichi Sankyo on antibody-drug conjugates, but neither comes with disclosed terms. The so-what: real-world data has entered clinical development planning, but it hasn't yet shown it changes outcomes.

If data is the asset everyone's fighting over, someone still has to manufacture it. That's the Danaher story. Danaher announced an AI-driven autonomous lab at its Abcam unit. The lab designs antibody candidates, robots synthesize and test them, and the results feed back into the model in a closed loop. Five Danaher businesses supply the underlying technology: Cytiva, Beckman Coulter Life Sciences, Molecular Devices, Genedata, and Integrated DNA Technologies.

The stated target, once the lab reaches full scale in early 2027, is up to eight times faster reagent discovery and roughly tenfold annual output. Those figures come via secondary coverage of the announcement. They are Danaher's targets for a future state, not measured results today. They also describe research reagents, not therapeutics. A reagent either binds or it doesn't, which is a much easier bar than surviving a clinical trial. That's exactly why closed-loop automation is furthest along here: the output can be checked fast. Separately, on October 14th, Danaher announced a partnership with Nvidia to build AI and accelerated computing into its scientific instruments, with no terms or products disclosed yet.

My inference on the business side: instrument vendors like Danaher are no longer just selling boxes. They're positioning to own the closed design-build-test loop that generates the proprietary data everyone else now wants to buy. For tools-and-instruments names, that's a route from capital-equipment cycles toward recurring data economics.

Meanwhile, public money is still being asked to fund the molecule layer directly. Iambic Therapeutics, an AI-native small-molecule company, began trading on Nasdaq yesterday under the ticker IAM. Reported first-day gains differ by outlet, so we won't put a number on it, and one session is not a verdict anyway. The larger point stands. Investors are funding an early-stage pipeline on platform promise. By contrast, the cash flows we can actually price this week are going to whoever owns the data and the hardware.

So pull the threads together. Clinical success rates move only when real-world-data PTRS modeling or AI-picked targets beat historical base rates in prospective decisions. Nobody has shown that yet. Not Tempus with Bristol Myers Squibb, and not insitro on the targets it's advancing for AstraZeneca. The biggest pending test sits elsewhere. Isomorphic Labs, the Alphabet-backed AlphaFold descendant, is the highest-profile bet that structure-prediction models can carry a designed molecule into humans. As of the latest reporting, it still hasn't disclosed a first-in-human trial, with its own end-of-2026 target about eleven weeks away.

Three questions to watch. Does Tempus or Bristol Myers Squibb disclose any measured PTRS outcome? Does model licensing stay narrow and field-limited, or does someone pay for exclusivity? And does Isomorphic file before year-end? Until one of these capability deals produces a disclosed result, the near-term effect on R&D spend isn't a reduction. It's a reallocation toward data access and the instruments that generate it. The clinical budgets that decide whether any of this works stay right where they were.

That's the briefing for today. Stay cautiously optimistic.
