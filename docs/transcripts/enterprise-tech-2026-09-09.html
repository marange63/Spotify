Good morning. It's September 9th, 2026, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

Across most of this past week, this beat has run the same finding through a security and software cluster. Palo Alto Networks, CrowdStrike, Zscaler, Salesforce, Okta. In every one of them a fast AI or agentic sub-line sat inside a company whose overall growth rate barely moved. One print from the start of this stretch breaks that pattern, and reading it against the week makes it the more interesting number. Broadcom reported fiscal third-quarter results on September 2nd, for the quarter that ended August 2nd. Total revenue was 29.6 billion dollars, up 86 percent year over year, according to the company's own earnings release. That headline number is almost entirely one line item. AI semiconductor revenue hit 16.7 billion dollars, up 221 percent year over year. AI silicon is now more than half of Broadcom's total revenue. So here is the takeaway: this is the threshold at which an AI line stops being an attach number sitting beside a business and becomes the business. Every other vendor on this beat this week had an AI story that was fast but small. Broadcom's is fast and now the majority.

That's the half of the print that gets the headlines. The other half is what an enterprise buyer actually purchases from Broadcom, which is infrastructure software, the segment built around VMware. The intuition about post-acquisition VMware is that Broadcom raised prices and growth slowed. The filings say the opposite. Infrastructure software revenue was 8.75 billion dollars in the quarter, about 30 percent of total revenue, up 29 percent year over year. One quarter earlier, in fiscal Q2, the same segment grew just 9 percent year over year. That is not deceleration. That is the segment's growth rate more than tripling in a single quarter. And a second claim has to go with it. Broadcom guides consolidated revenue and operating margin, not segment growth, so there is no prior segment-level guidance to measure this against. Nobody can honestly call it a beat or a miss. Take the acceleration at face value and nothing more.

What the acceleration does not tell you is why. It could be enterprises re-upping larger multi-year VMware contracts at higher list prices. It could equally be renewal timing bunching revenue into one quarter. Broadcom discloses no customer counts and no unit volumes for the segment, so you cannot separate price from volume out of this print alone. That distinction is the whole migration question. Rising revenue on falling seats means customers are leaving quietly while the ones who remain pay more, and that shows up two years later, not now. The practical advice for an enterprise buyer is the same either way. Know your renewal date, and negotiate multi-year price protection before you are pushed into a single-year true-up.

There is a structural point underneath that, and it is the reason this segment deserves more attention than its 30 percent revenue share suggests. Infrastructure software is the part of Broadcom that runs the customer's datacenter. It is also now the minority of a company whose growth rate is set by a handful of custom-silicon buyers. A standalone virtualization vendor has no choice but to reinvest in that product, because it is the whole company. Broadcom does have a choice. When the aggregate growth rate is decided by a few hyperscaler capex committees, the software franchise gets managed for cash and margin rather than for share. The acceleration this quarter is genuinely good news against that concern, but it does not settle it, because one quarter of revenue growth is not evidence of one dollar of incremental engineering investment.

Which brings us to what Broadcom says it wants that segment to become. On August 31st, at VMware Explore, Broadcom introduced VMware Private AI Cloud and VMware AI Factory. It is pitched as a private-cloud platform that lets enterprises run inference and agentic applications on their own infrastructure rather than a hyperscaler's. Apply this beat's standing ladder — announcement, pilot, production deployment, recognized revenue, durable adoption — and this sits on the very first rung. There is no general-availability date we could confirm from Broadcom's own materials, no named customer, no design partner, and no bookings or deployment figure attached to it anywhere we could reach. That is worth stating precisely, because this show has spent a week discounting vendor-defined adoption counts from Salesforce, Snowflake, Zoom and Okta. Private AI Cloud does not offer a count to discount. It is the stage before the metric we already refuse to credit. What would move it up the ladder is simple. A named enterprise running production inference on it, or a dollar figure on a future earnings call. Until then, treat it as a slide, not a product with customers.

The stakes of that launch are real even without adoption numbers, because Private AI Cloud aims directly at AWS, Azure and Google Cloud for the workloads enterprises are most nervous about sending off-premises. Regulated data. Agentic applications holding credentials into sensitive systems. This is the same on-premises inference question we flagged with Cisco's internal MyAgent deployment on September 6th, where Cisco routes simple queries to lightweight models and keeps most of the infrastructure on its own hardware for cost and data control. Enterprises building agentic AI want a lever to keep some workloads local, and every infrastructure vendor with an installed base is racing to hand them one. Whether meaningful AI workloads actually stay on customer-controlled hardware is the readable signal here. The alternative is that they consolidate into the big three clouds anyway once the managed tooling closes the gap.

That build-versus-buy question showed up elsewhere this week. On September 3rd, Adobe acquired Rilo, an Indian startup whose product lets users build natural-language agentic workflows for things like competitor intelligence and sales-call analysis. This comes through trade reporting rather than an Adobe release, and no deal value was disclosed, so there is no figure to attach. Set it next to Broadcom anyway. Broadcom is trying to build an AI platform on top of an installed base it already owns end to end. Adobe is buying a narrow, already-built agentic capability to bolt onto an existing suite. Neither has published a revenue number for the bet. The question worth tracking is whether standalone agentic-workflow startups survive as independent companies or keep getting absorbed as features inside someone else's platform. So far the incumbents are voting with acquisitions rather than partnerships, which tells you they think the workflow layer is a feature.

Now the risk to Broadcom's own thesis. AI semiconductor revenue growing 221 percent is concentrated in a small number of hyperscaler and AI-lab customers buying custom silicon. If any one of those buyers pauses or reschedules a capital-expenditure plan, Broadcom's blended growth rate reverts toward whatever the infrastructure-software segment does next. Broadcom is guiding fourth-quarter AI semiconductor revenue to accelerate further, to 21.7 billion dollars, up 236 percent year over year. That is guidance, not a result, and it rests on the same small set of buyers staying on schedule.

One thing to watch tomorrow. Oracle reports fiscal first-quarter results on September 10th, the day after this briefing. We have no result to give you, because none exists yet, and no consensus figure is worth repeating as though it were one. But Oracle's print will test exactly the question Broadcom raised. Has an incumbent's AI-linked revenue line grown large enough to move the company's overall growth rate, the way it just did at Broadcom? Or does it still sit off to the side as an attach number, the way it has all week for everyone else on this beat?
