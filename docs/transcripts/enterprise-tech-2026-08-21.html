Cautious Optimism Briefings

Enterprise Technology & Software

Transcript · August 21, 2026

Good morning. It's Friday, August 21st, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

This week the best AI numbers in enterprise tech and the best stock reaction did not belong to the same company. That gap is the story today.

Start with the numbers. Cisco reported fiscal fourth-quarter and full-year 2026 results after the close on Wednesday, August 19th. Revenue for the quarter was 17.3 billion dollars, up 18 percent year over year. Full-year revenue hit a record 63.3 billion dollars, up 12 percent. AI infrastructure orders were 4 billion dollars in the fourth quarter alone. For the full fiscal year, CEO Chuck Robbins told the earnings call that AI infrastructure orders came to, in his words, 9.3 billion dollars, approximately four and a half times the fiscal 2025 total. Networking product orders overall grew 40 percent year over year in the quarter. And the forward guide is not shy either: fiscal 2027 revenue of 72.2 to 73.4 billion dollars, which is roughly 15 percent growth at the midpoint.

Here is why that matters beyond one company's quarter. This is the clearest evidence yet that AI-driven capital spending is not confined to hyperscaler GPU clusters. Cisco does not sell to a handful of frontier labs. It sells to enterprises, and those enterprises are re-architecting their own data-center networking around AI workloads. Forty percent order growth in switching and routing is a read-through to Arista, to HPE and Juniper, and to the optics and cabling supply chain underneath all of them. The AI capex signal just got broader than the cloud capex disclosures everyone has been trading off.

Now hold the order number against the revenue number, because that gap is the actual story. Cisco guided AI infrastructure revenue to roughly 7.5 billion dollars for fiscal 2027. That is up from something on the order of four billion dollars in fiscal 2026, by the company's own framing of the comparison. So the AI business is guided to nearly double. But it is still guided below a single year's order intake, and orders are a bookings-style forward indicator, not recognized revenue. Nothing about a 9.3 billion dollar order book tells you when it bills, at what margin, or whether the customer takes delivery on the original schedule. The conversion lag, not the four-and-a-half-times headline, is the number that decides whether this is a good business.

Here's the turn, and it came from Cisco's own executives. Despite record results, Cisco shares fell in after-hours trading Wednesday night, from a regular-session close of 123.95 dollars to 118.69, a decline of about four percent. That causal read is press interpretation, not company disclosure. But the reason is not hard to find, because the chief financial officer said it out loud. CFO Mark Patterson told the call, quote, "you should expect a slight gross margin headwind as we move through fiscal 2027." The driver he named was, quote, "higher hardware mix and memory costs." Non-GAAP gross margin came in at 66.3 percent for the quarter, down 210 basis points year over year, and Cisco guided the coming quarter to 65 to 66 percent, slightly below where consensus sat. So this was not a bad quarter. It was a great quarter that got sold. The market has moved past rewarding AI order growth on its own, and started pricing these names on whether the margin survives the mix shift.

There is a second-order sting in that for Cisco specifically. The company has spent years telling investors it deserves a higher multiple because more of its revenue is software and subscription. A hardware-heavy AI mix works directly against that story. If investors now treat AI infrastructure revenue as structurally lower margin, then Cisco's biggest growth engine dilutes the very narrative it was built to support. Growth that discounts your own re-rating is an awkward thing to guide to. And because Patterson named memory costs alongside hardware mix, this is not a Cisco-specific problem — it is component-cost inflation landing on every server and networking OEM shipping AI systems this year.

Now the same week, one layer up the stack, the opposite verdict. Bank of America raised price targets across a swath of software names on August 19th and 20th: ServiceNow from 130 to 150 dollars, Snowflake from 330 to 395, Workday from 140 to 205. ServiceNow shares jumped as much as 8.5 percent in a single session on the note. Here's the catch, and it is the bank's own language, not mine. The firm said the increases reflect, quote, "broad-based multiple expansion across software, with no changes to our estimates." That is the exact inverse of Cisco. Cisco delivered harder numbers and got a margin discount. Software delivered no new numbers at all and got a re-rating.

Two things keep that in proportion. First, ServiceNow remains down roughly a third from its peak even after the rally, so this is recovery off a deep discount, not a new high. Second, the stated thesis is the removal of a negative rather than the arrival of a positive: BofA's argument is that, quote, "the proliferation of AI agents might actually increase the need for gatekeepers like ServiceNow." That is fading disruption fear, not proof of new revenue. Worth a callback here. Four days ago this briefing covered Silver Lake circling Workday for a take-private, built on the idea that the public market was over-discounting seat-based software franchises. BofA has now lifted Workday's target to 205 dollars with no change to its estimates. The discount that made the take-private thesis attractive is closing on sentiment alone, before any private buyer had to prove out the cash-flow math. And Snowflake's target moved without any fresh print behind it. Snowflake has not reported. It does not report until September 2nd. So today's Snowflake strength is analyst-driven, not results-driven, and that print now carries more downside asymmetry than it did a week ago, because the price already moved without the numbers moving.

If the software multiple is not being paid for new revenue, where is the cost actually showing up? Look at Databricks. On August 13th — a week old, not today's news — the company closed a 5 billion dollar funding round at a 190 billion dollar valuation, up from 134 billion roughly six months earlier. Databricks disclosed it has surpassed 7 billion dollars in annualized run-rate revenue, growing more than 80 percent year over year. The core data-warehousing line sits at 1.5 billion of that and is still doubling, and the newer Lakebase Postgres product is at a 100 million dollar run-rate. Do the arithmetic on the headline and you get roughly 27 times run-rate revenue, paid privately, in the same week public hardware got marked down on margin. CEO Ali Ghodsi said, by TechCrunch's account, that the company only wanted to raise 1 billion dollars but investor demand hit 15 billion, and he pointed to one reason for taking more: AI is expensive. Treat that demand figure and the IPO commentary as TechCrunch reporting, not confirmed by Databricks directly. The core point stands regardless. A company growing revenue 80 percent still needed outside capital, and the stated reason was compute cost.

That is the connective tissue across all three stories. Inference and hardware cost shows up as Cisco's product margin pressure, as Databricks' funding requirement, and as the one thing software's expanded multiple has not yet been asked to absorb. There is a market-structure consequence too. Every additional private round Databricks absorbs defers the IPO that would be this sector's real price-discovery event. Which leaves Snowflake as the only public comparable being asked to justify a private mark it never had to clear a market for — and Snowflake reports in twelve days.

One brief closing note. Microsoft has begun merging its consumer and enterprise Copilot apps into a single product, and it has added an "AI Action Ledger" to its Purview compliance portal that logs every action an AI agent takes, including the data it touched and the reasoning it applied, as an immutable audit trail. Both of those come from secondary reporting, with no Microsoft newsroom confirmation, and there is no adoption or revenue evidence attached to either. So call it architecture, not execution. But it is the shipped version of the bet BofA is making on ServiceNow: as agents proliferate, someone has to govern them, and that governance is worth paying for. The unification cuts the other way, though. Folding consumer and enterprise Copilot into one surface blurs exactly the boundary corporate IT procurement has been relying on.

Put it together. Yesterday's briefing closed on a pattern: a marketed number sitting above a harder one underneath. This week inverted it. The hard number, Cisco's, got sold. The unchanged one, software's multiple, got bought. September's earnings calendar, starting with Snowflake on the 2nd, is where that gets tested.

That's your briefing. Have a good weekend.
