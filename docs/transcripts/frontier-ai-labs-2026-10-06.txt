Good morning, and welcome to Cautious Optimism Briefings. It's Tuesday, October 6th, 2026, and this is your read on frontier AI lab competition.

Two corrections open today's briefing, and they point in opposite directions. On Saturday we told you Google's Gemini 4 Argon was priced at half of Claude Opus 5.5, and that this put pressure on Anthropic's flagship tier. We also told you the open-weight counterweight from China had gone untested. The first claim was incomplete. The second was wrong. Together they change where the price pressure in this market actually sits.

Start with Argon. Google's own launch post says the price of two dollars per million input tokens and ten dollars per million output tokens is introductory. The same post says that, quoting, "After the introductory period expires, the price of $4 per 1M input tokens and $20 per 1M output tokens will apply." Artificial Analysis confirms it in its own write-up. Standard pricing is currently discounted by half.

Now compare that with Opus 5.5. The Vals Index lists Opus 5.5 at four dollars input and twenty dollars output. That is the same list price. So at standard rates, Google did not undercut Anthropic's flagship. It matched it. What we called a price cut on Saturday was a launch promotion.

Now, who can actually buy Argon today? Almost nobody. Access is still limited to a vetted cohort of security defenders. Google says paid API customers and Google AI Ultra subscribers come next, but it has not said when. No end date for the promotional price has been reported either. Six days after launch, both dates are still missing.

That is why the promotion costs Google so little. With almost no paying traffic getting through, the discount is not winning volume today. My inference is that it is built to make enterprise buyers hesitate before they lock in budgets with Anthropic or OpenAI. That is judgment, not something Google has said.

The practical risk falls on buyers. A team that budgets a pilot at two and ten will face a repricing to four and twenty on a date nobody has disclosed. Budgeting against launch prices is a mistake. The takeaway is that the headline price on Argon is a teaser, and the real price is double it.

Now the complication. Parity per token is not parity per task. The Vals Index reports what it cost each model to run its full benchmark suite. Argon cost fifteen dollars and sixty-eight cents. Opus 5.5 cost thirty-two dollars and fourteen cents. Sonnet 5.5 landed in between. And Argon was priced at the full four and twenty in that table, not the promotional rate.

So even at the standard rate, Argon ran the same tests for roughly half the cost of Opus 5.5. The reason is token efficiency. Argon used far fewer output tokens to finish the work. That means the pressure on Anthropic's flagship may be real after all, but it works through cost per completed task, not through the list price. The list price says parity. The bill may say otherwise.

That brings us to the leaderboards, and the first independent reads on Argon disagree. On the Vals Index, Argon ranks first at 68.9 percent. Sonnet 5.5 follows at 67 percent, with Opus 5.5 a hair behind it. Vals is a live leaderboard, so treat that as a current snapshot rather than a fixed result.

Artificial Analysis tells a different story. Its Intelligence Index scores Argon at 53, matching OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra. Coverage of that index reports Argon trailing the Anthropic models, but treat that ordering as secondhand.

Here is how to read two indices that disagree. Each one weights a different mix of tasks. Neither replaces a model card or a controlled head-to-head on your own workload. Notice also that Argon's lead on Vals is under two points over Sonnet 5.5. That is a narrow margin. The disagreement itself is the finding. Argon is a top-tier contender, but no index has settled that it is the best.

So the top tier looks like this. Google and Anthropic now share a flagship list price of four and twenty. The Vals test costs suggest Google may still win on efficiency. Capability is contested. And access is gated. That is a market where the leaders hold their list prices and compete on efficiency instead of cutting.

If the top tier holds its price, the pressure has to come from somewhere else. On Saturday we said it hadn't shown up. That was wrong. We missed DeepSeek V4.1 Flash.

I'll be careful about the date. Sources conflict on exactly when it released, and it may not be new this week. What matters is that we had not covered it, and what it costs.

DataCamp describes DeepSeek V4.1 Flash as MIT-licensed open weights. It is a sparse mixture-of-experts model that activates only a few percent of its 552 billion parameters per token. Its output price is a dollar twenty per million tokens.

Put that against Argon. Argon's introductory output price is ten dollars, so DeepSeek's is roughly one-eighth of it. Against Argon's standard rate, it is about one-sixteenth. The sparse activation is the mechanism. Activating only a small slice of the model per token keeps serving cost low.

Now the part that matters more than the price. The license is MIT. Once weights are MIT-licensed, DeepSeek does not set the market price. Whoever hosts the weights most cheaply does. A hyperscaler or a neocloud can serve this model without paying any lab a margin. That is how commoditization actually happens. It happens through hosting competition on open weights.

My inference is that routine enterprise workloads move first. That means summarization, extraction, classification, and the high-volume agent loops that don't need frontier reasoning. Inference revenue on those tasks could shift from the labs to the hosts. Nobody has reported that shift yet. It is where the economics point.

Now the skeptical turn. Cheap tokens are not the same as useful tokens. We have no independent capability evidence for this model. Our source is secondary, DataCamp, and we did not retrieve a primary DeepSeek model card. And remember the lesson from Argon. Price per token can mislead. A model that needs more tokens or more retries to finish a task can lose its price advantage. So we are not saying this is a frontier challenge. We are saying it sets a floor under the mid-tier. I would not draw a conclusion about long-horizon agentic work from a parameter count or a price.

So here is the scorecard by dimension. On flagship list pricing, Anthropic held, and Google matched Opus 5.5 rather than undercutting it. OpenAI made no fresh pricing move that we could find this week. On efficiency, Google may have an edge, but that rests on one index's test costs. On mid-tier economics, open weights gained ground. On capability, nothing this week is independently settled. And on access, Argon is still gated.

What does this say about winner-take-most versus commodity? The evidence points to a split market. The top tier is concentrated, with list prices converging and the leaders competing on efficiency and enterprise trust. The commodity layer is real, but it sits one tier below the frontier, and open weights set its floor. For capital markets, my read is that this splits the inference economics. The labs keep pricing power on flagship tokens. The cloud and neocloud hosts capture the margin on mid-tier volume, and they compete it down. That favors whoever owns the cheapest serving capacity, not whoever trained the model.

Three things to watch. First, Google's end date for the introductory rate, and a general availability date for paid API and Ultra. Second, a primary DeepSeek model card or an independent evaluation of V4.1 Flash. Third, whether any Western lab cuts its mid-tier list price in response, instead of reaching for temporary promotions.
