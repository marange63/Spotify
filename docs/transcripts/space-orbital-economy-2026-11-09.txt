Good morning. It's Monday, November 9th, and this is your space and orbital economy briefing.

This week the sector is splitting more sharply. On one side are commitments you can check against a calendar. On the other are plans that contradict their own clocks. Today gives us both.

Start with Starship Flight 15. Over the past few days, we've called its planned ship catch unconfirmed, closer to stated intent than a settled flight plan. Now SpaceX's own announcement says the flight "is planned to be the first to return both Starship and Super Heavy to the launch site for catch." One caveat on sourcing. That announcement reaches us relayed by a company-watcher on X, not from a SpaceX mission page we've read directly. If that relay is accurate, the dual catch has moved from a maybe to SpaceX's stated plan.

The target is Friday the 13th, in the early evening, Eastern time. It is a target, and nothing has flown yet. There's also a second new element. Ship 42 is set to deploy 26 Starlink V3 satellites into low Earth orbit, three of them carrying cameras to inspect the heat shield. It is still a test flight. But it's no longer a bare one, because a batch of Starlink satellites rides along with the recovery attempt.

Temper that with the record. As of late September, by Wikipedia's count, Starship had flown 14 times, with 9 successes and 5 failures. By our arithmetic, that's roughly a 64 percent success rate. A dual catch stacks two recovery attempts onto a single flight. So a partial outcome is a live scenario, where the booster comes home and the ship doesn't. Markets would read that very differently from a clean sweep. And even a perfect Friday proves the stages can be recovered, not what turnaround or cost per kilogram looks like.

Here's why the payload matters beyond engineering. If Flight 15 deploys its satellites and recovers both stages, Starship starts to look like Starlink deployment capacity, not just a test program that burns cash. That would widen Starlink's lead in deployed capacity while New Glenn, the main non-SpaceX heavy-lift alternative, sits grounded. So the honest read is this. If the relayed plan holds, SpaceX is putting a batch of its own satellites behind a ship catch it has never pulled off. That's a bigger bet than a test flight alone.

Compare that with the challenger that was supposed to break SpaceX's heavy-lift grip. Blue Origin's New Glenn has been grounded since a hot-fire explosion in May. Last week, CEO Dave Limp's internal memo set return to flight for January 2027. Then, in a November 6th interview, Limp reportedly said, "We just said we're going to get back to flight this year." Taken at face value, that contradicts his own memo from two days earlier. But the quote comes from a single aggregator, so it may be garbled, or it may predate the memo. Set it aside, and the dates still don't settle. Limp has said the first analysis after the explosion put the earliest return to flight in mid-2028. That makes January a compressed best case, not a padded estimate. January 2027 and mid-2028 sit about a year and a half apart. So nobody downstream should model a Blue Origin date until the company publishes a launch date. That uncertainty lands hardest on the customer most exposed to it.

That customer is AST SpaceMobile. Just the anchors on its new Blacklift rocket deal. AST plans its own 20-ton rocket on Firefly engines. Each launch carries five BlueBird satellites, with missions targeted to start in late 2028. Meanwhile, New Glenn, one of AST's main launch vehicles, is out of service. And in April, before the grounding, a New Glenn mission had already put an AST satellite into the wrong orbit, one that now has to be de-orbited. That is the merchant-launch exposure Blacklift is meant to hedge.

Now apply a regulatory yardstick. The FCC's April order requires AST to deploy half its approved constellation, 124 of 248 satellites, by August 2nd, 2030. At five satellites per launch, Blacklift alone would need roughly two dozen flights to get there, by our arithmetic. Those flights would have to fit inside about twenty months, from a start date that is only a company target, on engines that haven't flown. Blacklift cannot carry that regulatory clock on its own. That squares with AST's own filing. It says AST will keep using outside launch providers for more than a hundred satellites in 2027 and 2028. So AST's deployment through 2030 still rides on third-party rockets, including a New Glenn that has already misplaced one of its satellites.

There's a softer reading of that clock. The FCC has already waived Amazon's Leo milestone once. If AST's third-party launches slip, it would likely ask for similar relief. In that case the binding constraint isn't the license. It's how soon AST can sell a commercial service. Either way, AST's regulatory problem and its launch problem are the same problem.

On the money side, AST looks more comfortable. Via Satellite reports that AST held more than $3.7 billion in cash at the end of June. Blacklift's upfront cash is roughly $400 million, about a ninth of that pile. So the rocket is affordable on the June balance sheet. Since June, AST has also sold convertible notes at a coupon of just 1.625 percent, per its SEC filing. Part of the proceeds went to capped calls, which offset dilution if the notes convert. Offset, not eliminate, because the notes can still turn into stock. So the question for AST's upcoming third-quarter report isn't solvency. It's cash burn and dilution. Blacklift, in short, is a hedge for 2029 and 2030. It is not a fix for AST's clock between now and then.

If you want to see what operational cadence looks like today, rather than what's promised years out, look at a much smaller niche. Varda Space Industries brought its W-8 capsule home on Thursday, after 35 days in orbit. It landed at Australia's Koonibba Test Range. W-8 had launched alongside a sister capsule, W-9. Varda calls that the first time two commercial reentry vehicles were launched together. A third vehicle, W-7, was also in orbit, and Varda's mission-operations team in El Segundo managed all three missions at once. It was the company's seventh capsule reentry since 2024.

Varda's chief revenue officer, Steve Layne, called this what "high-cadence operations look like in practice." That's the company's own framing, from a press release. Varda is private, and it disclosed no pricing, no revenue, and no backlog. Seven reentries in that span is cadence measured in months, not weeks. So this is proof of fleet operations, not proof of a business. Still, it sets a bar. Other in-space manufacturing and microgravity players will be measured on concurrent missions and repeat reentries, not single demos. And set against Blacklift's unflown engines, Varda is the week's cleanest example of hardware already running in a fleet rhythm.

So where does this leave the orbital economy? What's genuinely inflecting is flown hardware in a fleet rhythm, like Varda's. What's still a demo is the Starship dual catch, which hasn't happened, even if the relayed announcement is right that Starlink satellites will ride along. Blacklift is a demo too, since it hasn't flown an engine. The same goes for any Blue Origin date, until the company publishes one. The open question for the week is whether the challengers' regulatory and commercial clocks can survive their launch clocks. Right now, for AST, the answer runs through rockets that haven't proven they show up on time. For catalysts, watch AST's upcoming third-quarter report. Watch Rocket Lab's too, for any firmer date on Neutron, which has already slipped into 2027. Then comes Starship Flight 15 on Friday the 13th.
