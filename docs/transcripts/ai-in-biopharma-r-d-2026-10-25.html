Cautious Optimism Briefings

AI in Biopharma R&D

Transcript · October 25, 2026

Good morning. It's October 25th, 2026, and this is your briefing on artificial intelligence in biopharma R&D.

Today's thesis has two parts. Protein design just got cheaper and more open. And the money is moving downstream, to the physical work of making and testing what the models design.

Start with the lead. This week Nvidia and the University of Washington's Institute for Protein Design released a new protein design model called RFD4-Proteina. The Institute is David Baker's lab, the group behind the RFdiffusion lineage. The model is open source under a BSD-3 license. Nvidia's own model card says that license covers the trained weights, not just the code, with the Institute named as copyright holder. It's distributed through Amazon Bio Discovery, Benchling, and Anthropic's Claude Science. One disclosure: Anthropic makes the model behind this show, and it is one of those named distribution partners.

Here's why that matters to this show's running argument. On Thursday, we said model IP in this field starts in academia and ends up walled off inside well-funded startups like Xaira, with Isomorphic Labs keeping its design engine fully closed. Yesterday, we said we couldn't tell whether open-weight models are competitive on pharma tasks. RFD4-Proteina is the first fresh data point on both. The lab that started this lineage just gave away a successor, weights included.

Now the headline performance claim needs unpacking. Per coverage in R&D World, the model generated five to ten times as many distinct passing structural clusters per GPU-hour as the evaluated baselines. That is a computational metric. Nvidia's model card is explicit about the limit, stating plainly that computational passers do not establish experimental activity. So treat the five-to-ten-times figure as a measure of how efficiently the model clears an in-silico filter, not of what works in a dish. The baselines also aren't named in anything we could verify. Don't read this as a win over any specific rival engine.

But this time there are wet-lab numbers too, and they come with real denominators. Reporting on the accompanying paper, R&D World says researchers tested 188 lab-synthesized designs against the target TrkA. A hundred and fifty-eight of them bound, at affinities below one micromolar. That is an 84 percent hit rate, pooled across two design campaigns. On enzyme design the numbers are much thinner. Eighty-nine of 288 designs degraded an organophosphate pesticide called paraoxon. And in a synthesis step relevant to the HIV drug lenacapavir, four of 67 designs produced the desired reaction above background. Two sourcing caveats. This is secondary coverage of the paper, not the paper itself. And TrkA is a well-studied target, so the 84 percent is the easy end of the range, not a general expectation.

So the honest summary is that RFD4-Proteina arrives with a computational benchmark claim against unnamed baselines, and separately with some disclosed, assayed hit rates. That is a stronger starting position than this field usually gives you on day one of a release.

What does that mean for the business? If a free, academically pedigreed model can hit an 84 percent binder rate on a real target, then the premium closed platforms charge purely for design capability gets harder to defend. That is my inference, not a reported fact. Xaira and Isomorphic increasingly need to show proprietary data, differentiated biology, or an actual clinical asset, because a strong open baseline now exists for nothing. And Nvidia wins regardless of whose model dominates. It sells the GPU-hours underneath all of them, which continues the picks-and-shovels position we tracked through its BioNeMo work last week.

A computational pass rate isn't a binder in a tube, though. So the next question is what prospective design actually yields in the wet lab on a hard, novel target. Xaira Therapeutics answered part of that this month, and it requires a correction from this show. On Thursday, we said Xaira had disclosed no binding rate and had shown a methods post rather than a result. That was wrong. Xaira's own release does report hit rates with real denominators.

Here they are. Using its generative antibody model, called Vega, Xaira designed 182 de novo sequences against a defined epitope on an undisclosed oncology target, with no prior campaign data to work from. Eighteen were confirmed binders after quality control. Xaira calls that a 10 percent binder rate, in a seven-week cycle from DNA synthesis to lead molecule. On two well-characterized GPCRs the rates were much higher: 32 of 91 designs for CXCR4, and 26 of 91 for APJ. Don't average those easier targets in with the harder 10 percent. The spread is the finding. Target difficulty, not the model, is what moves the number.

The most interesting result is a fourth target, called XA-4. Xaira ran two conventional screening campaigns against it, each with four rounds of selection, using a naive library. It came away concluding that XA-4 is, in its words, not readily druggable by conventional means. Vega then designed a library of 60,000 candidate sequences. From that, six confirmed inhibitors emerged, the best reaching over 96 percent maximum inhibition at 183 nanomolar potency. Read that carefully. It is a designed library beating a naive library on the same target. That is narrower than saying the model designed the answer.

Still, set Xaira's 10 percent on a novel epitope beside the roughly 8 percent we reported from Anthropic's own toxin-binder campaign on Thursday. Those are the first two comparable denominators this show has had for what generative design yields prospectively on a hard target. Call it roughly one in ten. That answers the question we closed with on Friday, which was whether anyone would publish a hit rate with a denominator attached.

It is also not a drug. This is a company blog post, not peer-reviewed or replicated. The XA-4 target's identity is undisclosed, so no outsider can judge how hard it really was. And there are no pharmacokinetic, toxicology, or manufacturability data, and no disclosed IND or clinical timeline for any of it. A confirmed binder is the start of the expensive part.

Which points to the bottleneck. If every design still has to be synthesized and assayed before you know whether it's real, that is where capacity is scarce, and that is where this week's capital went. Warp Sciences emerged from stealth on Thursday with 215 million dollars, led by Lightspeed. It is building U.S.-based scientific infrastructure for drug research, which coverage describes as a chemistry contract research organization combining chemists, AI, and robotics. Its first service, per that coverage, is lead-optimization analog synthesis: physically making the molecule variants that AI tools suggest.

The founder is the signal. Warp's CEO is Kevin Weil, formerly OpenAI's chief product officer and VP of science. Yesterday on this show we covered OpenAI itself buying a workflow company for its GPT-Rosalind effort. Now its former science chief has left to build the physical layer instead. Two frontier-lab alumni paths, and only one of them is a model.

Warp also pitches itself as a domestic alternative to work now done offshore. The supporting statistic is a 2024 BIO-commissioned survey, relayed by Reuters, in which 74 percent of respondents reported contracts with Chinese companies for preclinical and clinical services. Note the scope. That covers those services broadly, not lead-optimization chemistry specifically, so treat it as context rather than a measurement of the gap Warp fills. And separate the two theses here. One is that AI plus robotics changes the economics of synthesis and testing. The other is an onshoring bet riding policy tailwinds. Warp has disclosed no customers, no revenue, and no cycle-time data. So this is capital pricing a thesis, not evidence that the thesis works.

Pull it together. Three open questions. Whether RFD4-Proteina's computational gains show up as hit rates on targets harder than TrkA. Whether Xaira's binders survive the move from confirmed hit to developable candidate. And whether Warp ever discloses a synthesis cycle time or cost that beats an offshore CRO.

On clinical success rates, the realistic read is narrow. Cheaper, more open design compresses the discovery stage in time and cost. It does nothing yet to the attrition that happens in Phase 2, where biology decides. And R&D spend isn't shrinking, it's reallocating, this time away from paying for model capability at all. Design is heading toward free. What you'll pay for instead is the capacity to synthesize and assay what the models propose, and whatever proprietary data a closed platform can still claim once the design layer is a commodity.
