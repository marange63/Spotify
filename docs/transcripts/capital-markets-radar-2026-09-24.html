Capital Markets Cross-Asset Radar

Transcript · September 24, 2026

Good morning, it's Thursday, September 24th. This is Capital Markets Radar.

Yesterday we left you with a split. Risk assets were pricing peace. Rates were pricing persistence. We said whichever side was wrong would face the bigger repricing. Wednesday answered that question, and rates won.

Start with the level and the trigger. The 10-year Treasury yield jumped more than 13 basis points on September 23rd to 5.104 percent, per Yahoo Finance. The day before, it had sat flat near 4.96. Intraday prints varied from source to source, but every account agrees this is the highest level since 2007.

The main trigger was a Fed official. A week earlier, the committee had voted 12 to nothing to lift the funds rate to a range of 3.75 to 4 percent, its first hike in three years. On Wednesday, Governor Michael Barr backed an October hike in prepared remarks. He said policymakers "still have more work to do," and that "further policy adjustments are likely to be needed to ensure inflation comes down to target in a timely fashion." That is a sitting governor naming the next meeting. Chair Warsh, by contrast, submits no dot and has distanced himself from the committee's projections. Boston Fed President Susan Collins added her own voice. She tied her support for this month's hike to persistent inflation and to energy pressure from the Middle East conflict, and she expects another possible hike before year end. So two officials are now describing a path their chair has not endorsed. Until Warsh leans against them, the market will price their version.

The data gave the hawks cover. The flash September manufacturing PMI came in at 57, against a consensus of 53.6. That is more than three points above expectations, and well above August's 53.9. The composite index came in at 58.4. A reading like that undercuts the idea that last week's hike is already cooling activity, and it hands the hawks a growth argument alongside the inflation one.

Now the piece worth sitting with longest. Treasury's five-year note auction tailed on the same day. It priced at 5.033 percent, 3.1 basis points above the average accepted bid. Indirect bidders, the rough proxy for foreign and institutional demand, took just 54.3 percent of the auction, against a twelve-auction average of 65.2. Yesterday we said a flat ten-year on an oil down-day might reflect term premium or supply, and that nothing we had let us separate those from the Fed path. This auction is the first real evidence on that question.

Be careful with it, though. A weak auction in the middle of a violent selloff can simply mean the price concession had not been built in before bidding closed. A strong dollar can also raise hedging costs for foreign buyers without saying anything about their view of U.S. fiscal risk. And the five-year is mostly a Fed-path instrument. The ten- and thirty-year auctions are the cleaner term-premium tests. We could not confirm where the two-year and thirty-year yields closed on Wednesday. So we cannot tell you whether the curve steepened or flattened, and we won't guess. Here is why it still matters. If weak indirect demand shows up again at the longer auctions, higher yields stop being self-limiting. The rise would then reflect less appetite for duration, not just a higher Fed path, and a calmer oil market would not reverse it. Treat the five-year as a signal worth watching, not a verdict.

That rate move carried straight into equities. By midday, per the Motley Fool's tally, the S&P 500 was down 0.75 percent and the Nasdaq Composite down 1.13 percent, while the Dow was off a smaller 0.68 percent. Those are midday marks, not closes. But the ordering is the tell. The tech-heavy index fell roughly half again as much as the Dow, which is the signature of a discount-rate shock rather than a growth scare. That fits a hot PMI day. The economy looked stronger, and stocks fell anyway, because the rate on the other side of the valuation went up.

And the input the Fed keeps pointing to didn't cooperate either. Diesel is doing damage that crude alone isn't. The national average diesel price hit a record $6.5276 a gallon on Tuesday. On a per-barrel basis, that is roughly $207, more than $100 above the price of crude itself. Crude has not been the relief valve either. Brent for November delivery is back near $100 a barrel, after dipping to about $98 on Tuesday's Hormuz headline, with WTI near $90.

Washington's response is where the new risk sits. President Trump has voiced support for a possible ban on diesel exports. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent says the administration is weighing whether a partial or a total ban would work. No ban has been enacted. This is a trial balloon under review, and it deserves skepticism about its stated goal. Analysts quoted by Yahoo Finance warn that a ban would tighten global supply. The likely mechanics run like this. Domestic prices ease briefly. Refiners lose export margin and cut runs. Prices at home drift back up, while buyers in Europe and Latin America face a tighter market than before. So a ban moves the squeeze abroad rather than solving it here, and it is a direct hit to refiners' export economics. Meanwhile freight, agriculture and industrials absorb the diesel cost in their margins, whatever headline CPI shows. The point for the Fed is simple. Any bet that fading crude delivers disinflation now has a leg that no Hormuz headline can fix.

Now the assets that bought Tuesday's relief bid, because they gave it back once the rate move landed. Bitcoin opened Wednesday at about $86,200, near an eight-month high. Nansen senior research analyst Nicolai Søndergaard described the move as "a combination of renewed ETF demand and a large short squeeze." Later in the session the rally reversed. KuCoin reported a drop of 3.85 percent to below $84,000 after a brief touch of $87,000. Other prints that day were higher, so we won't give you a precise close.

Yesterday we said a squeeze-driven rally has a shelf life. We should grade that honestly rather than claim vindication. Søndergaard's own attribution names real ETF demand alongside the squeeze, so part of the bid was genuine flow, not just short-covering. The better read is that a partly flow-backed rally proved rate-sensitive. We have no fresh fund-flow data for Wednesday. So we can't yet tell you whether that institutional demand held through the reversal, or whether it was the marginal buyer now stepping back.

Precious metals told the same story from a different corner. Gold fell 1.23 percent to $4,304 an ounce. Silver fell 3.09 percent, about two and a half times gold's pace, to $65.06. St. Louis Fed President Alberto Musalem and Chicago Fed President Austan Goolsbee added hawkish commentary, and the Dollar Index moved back above 100, per USAGOLD. Silver leading down is the cleaner read on risk appetite, because it carries industrial-demand sensitivity that gold does not. And a hawkish Fed with the dollar back above 100 tightens dollar funding for everyone else, compounding the squeeze on energy importers this desk flagged last Saturday.

Put the board together. Long-duration equities, Bitcoin, gold and silver all sold off on the same catalyst in the same session. Only the dollar and crude went the other way. None of the risk assets acted as a diversifier on Wednesday. That tells you the discount rate, not any single asset's own story, is the variable moving everything else.

A word on what we're still missing. We don't have a reliable read on the curve shape, as we said. We also don't have a single trustworthy probability for an October hike. The same-day snapshots we found conflict too widely to quote one number. Treat October as actively priced, driven by Barr's remarks, not as a settled probability.

What to watch from here. September payrolls land October 2nd, CPI follows on October 14th, and the next Fed decision comes October 28th. Also watch whether the diesel conversation moves from review to an actual export restriction. Until one of those breaks the pattern, treat every asset on this board as trading off the same 10-year yield.
