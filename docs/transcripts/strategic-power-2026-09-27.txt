This week the two chokepoints that decide who gets energy and who gets compute were priced by whoever actually controls them. Summit diplomacy, meanwhile, delivered atmosphere and deadlines instead of access.

Start with Hormuz. Brent crude closed at $103.08 a barrel on September 23rd, up 3.9 percent on the day, according to CNBC. The trigger was Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian vowing that Tehran would not surrender to the United States. That vow lands on a Strait that Iran has, in the reporting's words, kept effectively closed since March. The tools are drones, missiles, sea mines, and small boats. The corridor carries roughly one-fifth of the world's oil and gas. Treat "closed" as a characterization, not a measurement. Nobody in this research window produced a tracked transit figure against the pre-war baseline.

On Friday this beat said the week's chokepoints were being managed by clocks. Hormuz is the one on no clock at all, and this week showed why.

Iran's foreign minister, Araghchi, said Tehran had offered a plan to restore normal passage within seven days. The offer was conditioned on Washington honoring earlier commitments. One of those is a transit route through the Strait that Iran says it has already agreed with Oman. Trump said he turned it down. In his words, Iran wants a deal to open the Strait immediately because they're losing so badly. So a concrete reopening offer surfaced and was rejected in the same news cycle. That is the pattern worth naming plainly. De-escalation talk has been priced as resolution in round after round since spring. None of it has verifiably returned physical flow.

The more dangerous marginal story isn't the Strait itself. It's the bypass. On Thursday, Yemen's Houthis claimed missile and drone strikes on Saudi Aramco facilities at Yanbu, and on what they called a sensitive target in Riyadh. Saudi authorities have not confirmed any damage. Yanbu matters because it sits at the end of the East-West Pipeline. Al Jazeera puts that line's capacity at up to seven million barrels a day. It lets Saudi crude reach export markets without ever touching Hormuz.

My read, and it is analysis: a credible threat to that bypass is a bigger tail risk than another exchange of fire inside the Strait. The pipeline is the Gulf's principal way around the closure. Lose it, and the supply shock stops being partial. So the number to watch isn't the next ceasefire headline. It's whether Yanbu's loadings actually get interrupted.

There's a directional consequence for who benefits. Sustained Hormuz risk moves the strategic premium away from anything that depends on Gulf transit. That favors Atlantic-basin supply, and American producers and exporters above all. And for inflation, triple-digit Brent is a setup, not a result. No October print has landed yet, so treat the pass-through as a risk to the outlook rather than a fact in the data.

Oil's chokepoint is contested with missiles. The other one that matters, compute, got priced by contract this week. And it sits on an island that came up at the summit table in Washington.

Trump and Xi met on September 24th. Bloomberg reported that the bilateral wrapped up roughly an hour after it began, long on atmosphere and short on deliverables. The truce extension to January 10th, which we covered Friday, remains the only concrete outcome. What's new is the texture. Xi asked Trump to oppose Taiwan's independence, and got no commitment. No joint statement was issued. Separately, after a preparatory meeting in New York, the US Trade Representative said export controls on advanced AI chips were not on the agenda for the AI-safety talks.

You can read that silence two ways. Washington may be protecting its lever. Or the chip fight may be drifting onto Beijing's terms. Silence alone doesn't settle it. What it does settle is that diplomacy didn't touch the physical Taiwan question this week.

That brings us to the foundry that actually prices the island's leverage. TSMC has reportedly told customers it will raise wafer prices by up to 10 percent from 2027. That covers both advanced and mature nodes. On top of that comes a reported 10 to 15 percent surcharge on high-performance-computing orders that run past committed volumes.

None of this is confirmed in a filing or on an earnings call. It's Nikkei-sourced reporting relayed through several outlets, and those outlets disagree on the magnitude. But the surcharge detail may matter more than the headline number. It penalizes precisely the customers most eager to scale.

The scarcity underneath it is real. TSMC's 5, 4, and 3 nanometer nodes were fully booked in the most recent quarter. And earlier this month, Bloomberg reported that TSMC had lifted its quarterly tool-purchase estimate to about 1.9 times what it projected in December. That's a chokepoint owner extracting a scarcity premium through a contract instead of a missile. It's the same logic as the Hormuz risk premium, just billed per wafer instead of priced into a barrel.

The binding constraint on TSMC is increasingly physical build-out, not demand. The company faces a shortage of experienced construction workers in Taiwan, even as it accelerates factory development. That is the press-release-versus-poured-concrete distinction in its purest form. Announced capex does not convert one-for-one into operating fabs. Labor, not capital, is rationing how fast TSMC's commitments become working capacity. So the world's most important fab now prices its scarcity, and a shortage of construction crews sets the pace at which that scarcity eases.

Here's the second-order effect worth sitting with, and again this is analysis. Energy and compute costs are rising together into 2027. Triple-digit Brent raises the power and operating costs behind every AI data center under construction. TSMC's reported hikes raise the cost of the silicon inside them. That squeezes inference economics from both ends of the ledger at once. And it arrives just as the industry needs those economics to work.

There's a countervailing force too. A monopolist pricing this aggressively into 2027 hands hyperscalers and governments a stronger case to subsidize second sources outside Taiwan. Think Samsung, Intel, and Rapidus. That's bullish for TSMC and its toolmakers this cycle. But it makes the pricing power partly self-limiting over the next one.

Washington's exposure to energy prices also helps explain its push on the other energy front, in Ukraine. That's my inference, not a stated policy. The durable change isn't the diplomacy. It's money. On Friday the European Union released seven and a half billion dollars from the European Peace Facility for joint military procurement. That's funding released for future production, not contracts signed or munitions delivered. But it's real, and it will still be there whatever this week's talks produce. So Europe keeps building defense-industrial capacity regardless of the ceasefire headlines.
