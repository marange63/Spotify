Good morning, it's Friday, September 4th, and this is your Capital Markets Radar.

This program has spent three straight sessions telling you a September rate hike is live. On Wednesday we quoted you roughly two-thirds odds. Yesterday, for the first time, that number went the other way. Fed Governor Christopher Waller said that if inflation data due next week comes in cool, he "would be inclined" to keep the Fed's benchmark rate unchanged at the September 16th meeting. CME FedWatch-implied odds of a hike fell to 50.4 percent, down from 63.2 percent the day before. That is a thirteen-point move on one conditional sentence from one voting member. Treat those readings as snapshots that reprice by the hour, and treat the direction as the news rather than the decimal.

The rest of the complex moved with him in a single session. The two-year Treasury yield slid to 4.34 percent from 4.39, and the ten-year eased to 4.77 percent from 4.79. Those are the September 3rd closes, and the front end did more of the work than the long end, which is what you would expect if the market is repricing the Fed rather than the term premium.

Equities did not treat it as a marginal adjustment. They treated it as an all-clear. The S&P 500 closed at 7,747.71, the Dow and the Nasdaq both rallied hard, and the Nasdaq's roughly one-and-a-half percent gain made it the best session in a month. Tech led it.

Here is the problem with that reaction. Fifty-fifty is not dovish. Fifty-fifty is undecided. Waller's hedge is conditional on inflation prints that do not exist yet, the August PPI report on the 10th and the August CPI report on the 11th. The market front-ran a condition, not an outcome. And one governor is not the committee. Waller spoke to the level of rates. He did not touch the chair's stated case, which rests on the breadth of price increases across the basket, and nothing this week has tested that argument at all. So a tech-led rally into a still-live hike decision is a duration bet wearing risk appetite's clothes. The longest-duration cash flows in the index rallied hardest on a sentence, and they are the most exposed if next week's prints do not cool.

Here is what makes yesterday's repricing genuinely informative, though. Look at what did not move. Oil sat still. Brent settled essentially flat at $95.52 a barrel, and the ten-year fell anyway.

That resolves a test this program put on the record on September 1st, by the opposite route from the one we specified. We said then that if Brent retraced sharply while the ten-year held its highs, the yield move was about policy rather than energy. What actually happened is the mirror image. Brent held in the mid-nineties, and the ten-year fell on Fed rhetoric alone. Different path, same verdict. Oil in the mid-nineties no longer moves this curve. The curve is trading the Fed's reaction function, not the energy shock sitting underneath it.

And the energy shock is not a headline scare. The genuinely new hard fact this week is a physical one. Vessel crossings through the Strait of Hormuz collapsed from twenty-three the prior Wednesday to ten on Monday, against a backdrop of renewed US-Iran fighting. Treat the military picture itself as developing, because the reporting on the strikes and Iran's response is secondary, with no primary statement we could retrieve. But the transit count is a flow disruption, not a threat premium, and that is the distinction this beat drew a week ago without data behind it. Now there is data.

So run the reverse reading. A curve that shrugs off a near-halving of transit volume is either correctly discounting a supply-side move as transitory, or it is under-pricing the inflation leg entirely. If crossings stay near ten instead of recovering toward twenty-three, that disruption lands in the very inflation prints on which Waller conditioned his hedge. And the same governor's dovish sentence flips hawkish on exactly the same logic that just made it dovish. The market bought a condition it has no ability to forecast.

That leaves this morning's payrolls print, out at 8:30 Eastern, unresolved at the time of this recording. I will not characterize a number that does not exist. What we do have is the setup going in, and it is ambiguous on purpose. ADP's private payrolls rose 38,000 in August, the slowest pace since January, and ADP has historically tracked the official print loosely enough that it settles nothing.

The more useful read is July's JOLTS report, which this beat has not covered. Job openings were little changed at 7.3 million. The number that matters more than that headline is the revision underneath it. June's openings were revised down by 177,000. Downward revisions to openings are how a labor market that looked flat in real time turns out to have been cooling all along. But note that JOLTS is a month staler than ADP. So flat openings paired with a soft private print is the ambiguous middle that lets hawks and doves both claim the data. Which is precisely why one sentence from one governor moved this market more than either release did.

Whoever wins that argument, one bid for short Treasuries is not waiting for the answer. On September 1st, twenty-one banks and financial firms said they will form a new company in the second half of this year to issue a dollar stablecoin. The product itself is targeted for market launch in the first half of 2027. Those are two separate dates, not one. The company does not have a name yet, and the reported roster runs through Citi, Goldman Sachs, Bank of America, Deutsche Bank and UBS, though we have that list from trade press rather than from any bank's own release.

The structural point is not the crypto angle. Under the GENIUS Act, any stablecoin these banks issue has to hold reserves one-to-one in cash and short-term Treasuries, with monthly audited disclosures. That makes regulated stablecoin issuance a price-inelastic buyer of the front end. It buys bills because the rulebook says it must, not because bills look cheap. I cannot give you a dollar figure for how large that pool becomes, because no issuer-disclosed or Treasury-sourced number survived our checks, so take the mechanism as real and leave the magnitude open. The incumbents are choosing to own front-end collateral distribution themselves rather than cede it to Circle or Tether. If that works, the marginal buyer of bills shifts from a sentiment-driven crypto pool to a payments-driven bank pool, and a payments bid is far less likely to disappear in a drawdown.

Now treat the timeline with the skepticism it deserves. An entity that will not be formed until later this year, backing a product that does not launch for another six months after that, is a promise about a promise. Twenty-one competitors agreeing to build shared payments infrastructure is also a fact pattern with a long history of dying in governance. So the threat to Circle's and Tether's reserve economics is a 2027 question at the earliest.

The same institutional money is making a smaller, faster decision in the meantime. On September 1st, spot Bitcoin ETFs saw net outflows while spot Ethereum ETFs recorded net inflows for a twelfth straight day. We told you yesterday that daily flow data is the wrong lens for judging a structural bid. A twelve-session streak is exactly the longer-horizon evidence that lesson demanded, and it says the institutional bid has not left digital assets, it has rotated vehicle. But keep the scale honest. That single day's Bitcoin outflow was more than twenty times the Ethereum inflow. This is a positioning tilt inside the digital-asset sleeve, not a macro verdict, and it means the crypto complex is not currently expressing a view on the Fed at all.

So here is where that leaves you into the weekend. Payrolls this morning, PPI on the 10th, CPI on the 11th, the Fed decision on the 16th. A market that just moved thirteen points in a hike probability on a single sentence still has three real prints standing between here and that decision, and every one of them can push it back the other way.

That's your Radar for September 4th. Trade carefully out there, and I'll see you next time.
