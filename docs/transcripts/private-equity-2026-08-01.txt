Good morning, it's Saturday, August 1st, and this is your private equity briefing.

Start with a number CalPERS just handed to every skeptic of the illiquidity premium. The nation's largest public pension reported its best fiscal year in five: a preliminary 14.8% total return for the year ended June 30th, comfortably above its 6.8% assumed rate. But break that number open and the story flips. Listed equities inside the CalPERS portfolio returned 24.1%. Private equity returned 17%. That's a gap of roughly 700 basis points, in favor of the public book, in a year the fund is calling its strongest since 2021. This is a marked, not fully cash-realized figure, and one fiscal year doesn't settle a premium that's supposed to be earned over a decade. We went looking for CalPERS's five- and ten-year private equity numbers against public equity to test that longer claim, and the fund's own disclosures don't break it out that way. Only the blended, fund-level five- and ten-year returns are public, at 6.83% and 8.57%. So the multi-year verdict stays genuinely open. But the single-year read is hard to spin. Even after CalPERS spent the last three-plus years cutting large-buyout exposure from 46% to 33% of the private book and pushing growth and venture up to 38%, private equity still lagged the daily-marked public comparison it is supposed to outrun.

So what does that do? It puts the core sales pitch of the asset class under a bellwether's own scrutiny, right as that bellwether's peers run their own asset-allocation reviews. Remember what illiquidity is meant to buy you: a return premium in exchange for locking capital up and giving up daily marks. When the largest public plan in the country posts a year where the liquid book beat the locked-up book by 700 basis points, before fees and before you even price the illiquidity itself, the premium stops being an assumption and becomes a question. And CalPERS is not a fringe voice. Its post-2022 overhaul, tilting out of mega-buyout and into growth, venture and the middle market, is exactly the template other large pensions have been studying. If the bellwether's conclusion is that large buyout no longer earns its keep against public equities, the read-through is pressure on mega-buyout re-ups across the largest LP base in the market. That is the thread to watch: not one year of returns, but whether this becomes the number every allocation committee cites in the fee conversation.

And yet the machine that sells that premium keeps compounding regardless. Ares posted record quarterly fundraising of $36.4 billion in the second quarter, with $34.4 billion of net inflows, pushing assets under management up 17% to $671 billion, and fee-related earnings up 20% year over year to $491 million. KKR's trailing twelve-month fundraising hit a record $133 billion through June, and its total operating earnings of $1.53 billion were the highest in the firm's fifty-year history. Blackstone's private equity assets grew 17% to $454 billion, and its distributable earnings of $1.98 billion, up 26%, beat the Street. None of that is a realization. Fundraising and fee-related earnings measure capital coming in the door and management fees on assets already held. They say nothing about whether these firms are actually returning cash to the limited partners who supplied the capital. That is precisely the distinction CalPERS's numbers just made concrete. The marks keep growing, the fees keep growing, and the question of whether the marks survive contact with cash is a separate one entirely.

Two footnotes sharpen that point. KKR recently reclassified a slice of realized performance fees out of the high-compensation carry bucket and into a lower-comp fee-revenue line, which mechanically flatters reported fee-related earnings relative to true carry economics. And Blackstone's earnings beat leaned heavily on AI-related infrastructure and data-center holdings spread across its private equity, credit and real-estate books. The firm doesn't break out how much of the beat is that single factor, but it is worth naming the concentration: a large share of the marks driving this quarter's optics is a bet on one theme, and marks built on one theme move together if sentiment on that theme turns. So what: the industry's own profit-and-loss statement is telling you the fee machine is durable and diversified across a huge asset base. It is not telling you the carry cycle has cleared, and it is quietly telling you that a chunk of the good marks ride on the same AI trade everyone else is long.

So how does the industry actually produce the cash the marks aren't returning on their own? Increasingly, through secondaries, and that valve is now showing its own limits. Global secondary market volume topped $120 billion in the first half of 2026, up 20% from a year earlier, on top of a record $240 billion for full-year 2025. GP-led continuation vehicles, where a sponsor sells a prized asset to a vehicle it also controls, are the engine. Single-asset continuation deals alone came to $34 billion in the first half, more than half of all GP-led volume. But the capital funding those deals isn't keeping pace. Dedicated secondary dry powder actually fell 10% in the first half of the year, even as deal volume surged. Buyers are burning through committed capital faster than fresh capital is being raised to replace it.

Sit with what that structure is. A single-asset continuation vehicle is a GP-led, effectively single-buyer transaction, priced by an advisor the same GP hires. When the sponsor sits on both sides, the distribution it produces is not a market-tested realization. It is a valuation event the sponsor largely authors. And there is a quieter second-order cost. The more the industry leans on these deals, the more it normalizes net-asset-value pricing, rather than a cash-market clearing price, as the reference point for what LPs are told their holdings are worth. So what: secondaries have become the default answer to the distribution drought, but a shrinking pool of dry powder chasing a growing pipeline of continuation vehicles is not an infinite release valve. If buyer-side capital doesn't refill, the arithmetic points toward wider discounts to net asset value later this year. Pricing data for 2026 isn't public yet, so that is a forward risk, not a fact on the tape. But the direction of the capacity squeeze is not ambiguous.

Now the near-term catalysts. Electronic Arts' $55 billion take-private, the largest leveraged buyout ever, clears its last hurdle and is expected to close on or about Monday, August 4th, backed almost entirely by Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund alongside Silver Lake and Affinity Partners. Older reporting had this pushed to late September on a national-security review; that timeline has been superseded, and August 4th is the current expectation. Read it for what it is. That deal is proof the entry side of the machine is wide open at record scale, underwritten by a patient sovereign rather than a fund that owes anyone a distribution. It returns not a single dollar of cash to any existing limited partner. It is the mirror image of the CalPERS and exit story, not a counterweight to it. On the same day, Apollo reports earnings, and Carlyle's results are still pending. Both are the next real test of whether realized, cash performance fees, the money that comes from actual exits, are catching up to the fundraising and fee-related-earnings records the group has been setting.

Pull it together. The private equity cycle right now is two-speed. Dealmaking and fundraising are running hot, while the mechanism that turns paper gains into cash for limited partners is still the binding constraint. CalPERS just showed that even the paper gains are starting to look less special against the public comp. Watch Apollo and Carlyle next week for whether realized carry is finally moving, and watch whether secondary pricing starts to slip as dry powder tightens. Those are the signals that will confirm or break this read.
