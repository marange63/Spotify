Good morning. It's Thursday, August 20th, and this is your briefing on artificial intelligence in biopharma R&D.

All week this beat has argued that AI's value is sliding down the stack, away from the molecule itself and toward workflow and infrastructure. Today the molecule-design layer produced its best checkable evidence of the cycle. So let's test that scorecard against it.

Start with Recursion's second-quarter print from August 5th. Yesterday we read that release for its shrinking licensing revenue and its REC-4881 data. Today, the discovery-layer half of the same disclosure. REC-7735 is an AI-designed inhibitor of PI3K-alpha carrying the H1047R mutation. It has cleared the FDA's IND bar and is set to enter a Phase 1/2 trial for select solid tumors in the second half of this year. Recursion says the compound was developed in about ten months through its AI-native design platform. That is a real, verifiable compression of the traditional candidate-to-IND timeline, which typically runs several years.

But be precise about what it compresses. H1047R is a known, validated oncology mutation. AI here is designing a faster molecule against an already-understood target, not discovering new biology. And the bar it has to clear is not empty space. There is already a class of wild-type-sparing PI3K-alpha inhibitors moving through the clinic. So REC-7735 becomes one of the first real tests of whether a ten-month AI design cycle yields a genuinely differentiated molecule, or just a faster route to a crowded target. IND clearance means permission to dose a human being. It says nothing about whether the drug will actually work. There is zero human efficacy or safety data yet.

The sharper claim in that same release is what Genentech did, not what Recursion built. Genentech, working with Recursion, constructed a whole-genome CRISPR knockout map across a subset of more than one trillion internally manufactured iPSC-derived neurons. It then used that map to option the collaboration's first neuroscience target into a joint discovery program. That is AI-sourced novel biology, not just a faster molecule on a known target. It is a materially stronger claim. And on a closer look, it is not pure marketing. Recursion's own account describes a defined, multi-stage gate. Genentech required successful pathway, functional, and disease validation before it would exercise the option and release the milestone payment. That is a real, jointly-built, financially-backed process, not just press-release adjectives.

Still, weigh it carefully. That validation was disclosed and run entirely by the two contracting parties. No independent academic publication or outside replication confirms the target's novelty. So call this a real proof point on process, short of independent proof on substance. If it holds, though, it matters beyond Recursion. The thing the market has never been willing to pay full price for is AI generating novel validated biology, as opposed to faster hits on targets that humans already picked. A big-pharma partner actually optioning an AI-sourced neuro target is a read-through for the whole platform cohort — the Xairas, the Insitros, the Isomorphics — on whether that willingness is finally starting to shift.

On the money, keep the framing honest. The Roche and Genentech alliance has generated two hundred sixteen million dollars in upfront and milestone payments to date, and could eventually span up to forty small-molecule programs. The separate Sanofi alliance has generated one hundred thirty-four million dollars to date. Both figures are cumulative since each deal's inception. Recursion's disclosure does not break out how much, if any, is new money from this quarter, so don't read either number as a fresh windfall. The through-line: this is still optionality revenue, paid for access to a platform, not revenue from an approved or even efficacious drug.

Now shift from target and molecule design to the biologic-design frontier. This is where the industry's actual benchmarked capability gains are landing. Chai-2, from Chai Discovery, reports a sixteen percent hit rate in de novo antibody design against novel targets. That is more than a hundred times better than prior best methods, which ran under a tenth of a percent. BoltzGen reports success rates of sixty to seventy percent designing nanobody and protein binders against novel targets, using as few as fifteen candidate designs per target. And on structure prediction itself, AlphaFold3 continues to outperform the open-weight alternatives, Boltz-1 and Chai-1, on benchmarks of antibody-antigen complexes.

Here is why that cluster of numbers matters. This is where the workhorse-versus-specialized-architecture question actually resolves. It is not general-purpose language models doing this work. It is geometric and diffusion architectures, purpose-built for molecular structure. The large language models are still the workhorse for the paperwork layer — literature synthesis, protocol drafting, regulatory documents, coding, operations agents. But the design-layer gains are coming from a different, specialized stack. If those hit rates hold up prospectively, in wet-lab and eventually in the clinic, the historical two-to-four-year slog to a lead antibody could compress meaningfully. That pressures display and screening-based CROs, whose business is exactly the throughput the models increasingly substitute for. And it raises compute demand for the generative-design shops themselves.

Two caveats worth holding onto. These are binding hit rates, measured in silico and in early wet-lab screens. They are not developability, immunogenicity, or clinical efficacy rates. And the figures are vendor- and lab-reported, without independent third-party replication at scale. There is also a licensing split emerging. AlphaFold3 carries a restrictive license. The Boltz, Chai-1, and Protenix stack is freely usable. That split is hardening into a two-tier ecosystem: best-in-class accuracy for DeepMind and Isomorphic-adjacent users, open but slightly weaker tools for everyone else.

Which brings us to the definitional example of the gap this whole briefing is describing. Isomorphic Labs, the DeepMind spinout built on AlphaFold, raised two point one billion dollars in a Thrive Capital-led Series B back in May. That brought its total capital raised to roughly two point six billion, still the largest single funding event in AI drug discovery. The company points to commercial validation worth more than one point seven billion dollars from Eli Lilly alone, plus deals with Novartis and Johnson & Johnson. But be clear on what that number is: potential upfront-and-milestone value, most of it years from being earned, not cash in hand. This is standing context, not news. And the figure worth sitting with is what is still missing. No public IND. No clinical trial registry entry. No confirmed dosed patient for an Isomorphic-originated molecule. The company's president said in mid-2025 that dosing was getting very close. That comment is now over a year old.

So pull the threads together. Every fresh molecule-layer win this week — a ten-month candidate-to-IND, a jointly-validated novel target option, hundredfold binder-design hit-rate jumps — lands on developability and design speed. None of it lands on efficacy. There is still no AI-discovered drug carrying full FDA approval. The sector's economics still run on milestone optionality, paid by big pharma for access and speed, not on product sales. That leaves the whole cohort's valuation resting on a promise the clinic has not yet tested, and exposed if pharma R&D budgets ever tighten. The scorecard survived its best week yet. But it survived by staying exactly where it was: strong on speed, and still silent on whether any of it works.

That's the briefing for August 20th. Talk to you tomorrow.
