Good morning. It's Monday, August 3rd, and this is your frontier AI labs briefing.

The thesis for today: the edges that actually decide this race are moving underneath the leaderboard. Google is shedding the scientific talent that used to be its non-LLM moat, while China's labs prove out real cadence and real revenue but keep slamming into a domestic-chip ceiling that capital markets are now pricing directly.

Start with Google DeepMind, because today's news is an escalation, not a repeat. We've spent the past week tracking individual departures out of DeepMind — John Jumper to Anthropic, Noam Shazeer to OpenAI, Denny Zhou to Meta. Today the story got bigger: Google DeepMind has dismantled the entire AlphaFold team, the group behind its Nobel Prize-winning protein-structure model. Nearly a quarter of the original AlphaFold paper's full-time DeepMind authors have now left the company entirely, according to reporting from The Decoder. Most of the rest were reassigned over the past year onto Gemini-centric work, or onto enzyme design, fusion, and genomics — and reporting confirms some went specifically to Isomorphic Labs, Alphabet's own drug-discovery subsidiary, so that slice didn't leave the company at all. DeepMind's VP of Research, Pushmeet Kohli, framed it this way: "Our strategy over the last nine years has been to focus on grand challenges... The strategy has evolved." That's management's account. The other reading, which reporting doesn't cleanly rule out, is that this is talent flight forcing a reorganization rather than a deliberate top-down bet. Both are live.

What's confirmed is that Jumper announced his own move to Anthropic, and reporting says he'll be joined there by fellow AlphaFold co-authors Alexander Pritzel and Jonas Adler — though neither Jumper nor Anthropic has disclosed a title, a team, or a start date for any of them. It's tempting to call this a new competitive front for Anthropic in biology. It isn't quite that. Anthropic had already opened that front before these hires: it announced life-sciences partnerships with the Allen Institute and Howard Hughes Medical Institute back in February, months ahead of Jumper's move, it launched Claude Science, a dedicated biology and drug-discovery product, in June, and it reportedly acquired the biotech startup Coefficient Bio. So the AlphaFold talent is walking into a front Anthropic had already opened, not creating one. The convergence worth watching is whether Anthropic ends up pairing AlphaFold's structural-biology pedigree with a drug-discovery product it already ships. But that's the bet to watch, not a confirmed plan — nobody has said these three will even staff that specific effort. Call it reinforcement, not expansion.

Here's the caveat that matters more than the headcount: this is erosion on specific axes, not collapse. Google still holds unmatched TPU compute and Search, Workspace, and Android distribution. What it's losing is momentum and prestige — the signature non-LLM achievement that gave DeepMind scientific credibility distinct from the chatbot wars. And the flip side is that rivals are the beneficiaries. Anthropic, OpenAI, and Meta are absorbing researchers Google spent years training, so this is a talent transfer, not just a talent loss. Combine that with reporting that Gemini no longer holds top leaderboard positions and that DeepMind's release cadence lags Anthropic and OpenAI, and you get a lab that's strong on infrastructure but visibly bleeding on the talent and momentum dimensions.

While Google sheds scientific talent, the labs on the other side of the Pacific are accelerating. Alibaba previewed Qwen3.8-Max on July 19, and fresh reporting out today puts real numbers on the acceleration story. The model runs 2.4 trillion total parameters with roughly 95 billion active at a time. Alibaba's own account calls it "comparable to leading frontier AI models, second only to Fable 5" — but as of this reporting, no benchmark table or model card has been published to back that claim. Treat it as marketing, not evidence. The verifiable story here isn't the capability claim, it's the cadence: Qwen 3.7 launched just two months before its successor, versus a full year between Moonshot's Kimi K2 and K3. Only the predecessor, Qwen3.7-Max, has verified scores — 92.4 on GPQA Diamond and 80.4 on SWE-bench Verified — so that's the number to anchor to, not the unverified 3.8 claim. The real question this raises is whether that release velocity is starting to outrun the market's ability to independently verify anything. When flagship models ship every two months, benchmark rigor becomes the scarce resource, not model quality. There's also a distribution angle the benchmark noise obscures. Alibaba is bundling Qwen with its own agentic tooling, Qoder and QoderWork, through Model Studio — a mindshare play that runs on cadence and integration, not just raw model quality.

Now the harder question: does that velocity convert into money? Moonshot AI's Kimi K3 says yes, with a catch. Moonshot's annual recurring revenue reached $300 million in June, up from $200 million in April, driven by paid subscriptions and enterprise products including Kimi Work — with daily sales up at least sixfold after K3's debut. That's a rare, concrete revenue data point out of a Chinese open-weight lab, proving open weights and real monetization aren't mutually exclusive. Here's the twist: Moonshot paused new Kimi K3 subscriptions just 48 hours after launch, because user demand pushed its Beijing GPU clusters to capacity. Existing subscribers kept access; new ones got locked out. So the constraint on Chinese AI growth right now isn't demand, and it isn't capability — it's compute supply. And that ceiling has a second-order tell. As long as domestic compute is the bottleneck, Chinese labs stay dependent on gray-market Nvidia silicon, or on domestic chipmakers scaling fast enough to matter. That's the hinge of today's briefing: China's labs are proving they can build and sell frontier-adjacent models, but they're capped by how many chips they can actually get running.

That compute ceiling is exactly what capital markets are pricing at CXMT. The Chinese memory maker's shares surged more than 500% in its Shanghai debut on July 27, and by the following week its market cap had reached 3.54 trillion yuan — 523 billion dollars — making it China's most valuable chipmaker. That's the market making a direct, leveraged bet that the domestic-chip bottleneck constraining labs like Moonshot is going to ease. If that valuation holds, it accelerates exactly the domestic memory and compute investment that would loosen the ceiling over time, which is part of why the market is willing to pay up. But a researcher cited in that reporting cautions the surge reflects a market distorted by AI demand, supply shortages, and state-backed industrial policy — not a broad-based Chinese semiconductor advance. So don't read CXMT's valuation as evidence the constraint is already solved. It's a bet, not a resolution.

Put the two halves together and the pattern is symmetric in an interesting way. The West's binding constraint right now is talent and organizational focus — Google visibly losing its best scientific researchers to rivals even as it keeps its compute and distribution edge. China's binding constraint is silicon — real capability and real revenue growth that can't fully convert into share until the domestic chip supply catches up. Neither race is being decided by this week's benchmark score. It's being decided by who fixes their bottleneck first: Google retaining its researchers, or China scaling its own chips.
