Cautious Optimism Briefings

Strategic Power, Energy, and Institutional Change

Transcript · August 4, 2026

Good morning. This is Cautious Optimism Briefings for Tuesday, August 4th.

Start with the Gulf, because it moved more capital in one day than anything else on the board. Oil has been trading a headline, not a resolution, and that distinction matters more than the price itself. The Strait of Hormuz has been impassable since fighting between the United States and Iran resumed on July 8. On August 3, Trump announced he had canceled a planned strike on Iran and said talks with Tehran would resume Monday. Traders believed him. Brent and WTI both fell nearly six percent that day, with WTI dropping to $79.62 a barrel and Brent to $83.82, unwinding weeks of war-risk premium in a matter of hours.

Here is the problem. Iran's own government says none of that is true. Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmaeil Baghaei stated on the record, quote, "We currently do not have negotiations with America." He said Iran's only active talks are with Oman, over a temporary shipping corridor through the strait, and even that arrangement is described by Tehran as merely nearing completion, not yet operational. So the market rallied on a de-escalation claim that its supposed counterparty explicitly denied at the government level. Trump, for his part, publicly called Iranian leadership duplicitous after that denial. This is not a resolved conflict. It is a single social-media post moving the crude complex more in a day than OPEC-plus's steady output hikes have moved it all summer. And a closed Gulf is not a rounding error. A full cessation of Gulf exports removes close to twenty percent of global oil supply, roughly eighty percent of it bound for Asia. So the war premium can snap back as fast as it unwound, on the next headline, in either direction. Watch for an actual signed statement from either government, not another post, and for whether that Oman corridor ever goes live.

There is a second-order read here worth holding onto. Every week the Gulf stays unreliable strengthens the strategic case for American liquefied natural gas as the alternative supply Asian and European buyers can turn to. Golden Pass, the ninth US LNG export terminal, is the only new terminal expected to begin shipments this year, and Plaquemines is still scaling its modular trains toward full capacity. That is the quiet counterweight to Hormuz risk. It is also a reminder that energy security is increasingly a supply chain the United States can actually build, not just a headline it reacts to.

That energy premium is one of exactly two inflation drivers the Federal Reserve just named. Kevin Warsh chaired his first FOMC meeting on July 29, and the committee held rates at 3.50 to 3.75 percent for a fifth straight meeting. Officials explicitly cited, quote, "both tariffs imposed by President Donald Trump and higher energy costs tied to the Iran conflict" as the recent drivers of inflation that has now run above target for more than five years. Three regional presidents — Cleveland's Beth Hammack, Minneapolis's Neel Kashkari, and Dallas's Lorie Logan — dissented in favor of tighter policy, not looser. Warsh himself rejected the word pause, calling the decision, quote, "a rigorous review of the economic situation."

That is worth sitting with. Warsh was installed by an administration widely expected to want lower rates, and his first meeting produced a hold, not a cut, plus three hawkish dissents. That cuts against the simple story that the Fed has been captured. Now connect it back to the Gulf. If the de-escalation Trump is describing eventually proves real, one of the Fed's two named inflation drivers eases. But only one. Tariffs remain, and five years of above-target inflation does not reverse on a single data point. So a divided, hawkish-leaning committee stays boxed in rather than freed to cut. Rate-sensitive corners of the economy — housing, private credit — stay squeezed. And the contrast with AI capital spending is stark, because that boom is funded on hyperscaler balance sheets that do not need bank credit and barely feel the level of rates at all.

Now pivot from the price of energy to the appetite for it, because that is the deeper structural story underneath both of the above. Global data-center electricity demand is projected to grow 27 percent in 2026, to roughly 132 gigawatts, up from 104 gigawatts in 2025. US data-center demand alone climbs from 31 gigawatts in 2025 to 41 this year, and a projected 66 by 2027. That growth curve is outrunning what the grid can interconnect, which is why hyperscalers are now directly financing baseload power instead of waiting on utilities. Microsoft's $16 billion, twenty-year deal to restart the Three Mile Island sister reactor cleared its last major obstacle when FERC approved a transmission waiver in June. And Holtec's Palisades plant in Michigan, the first full restart of a shuttered US commercial reactor, is calling its current stage a watershed moment, expected to supply about 800 megawatts on return to service.

Treat that optimism with real skepticism, though. Holtec has already missed two earlier restart target dates for Palisades before this one. Announcement is not energized capacity, and that gap is the thing to interrogate every time a gigawatt figure gets quoted. And it runs deeper than any single plant. By one industry estimate, roughly 40 percent of all new data-center capacity through 2030 is expected to connect not to the public grid at all, but to dedicated, largely gas-fired generation, because interconnection queues are simply too long. Treat that particular figure as directional rather than precise, since it comes from a lower-tier source. But the direction matters. The clean, nuclear-powered AI story the industry tells is, underneath, substantially a natural-gas story. And when hyperscalers privatize their own baseload this way, the ratepayers left on the shared grid can end up absorbing the cost of the transmission and capacity everyone else still depends on.

The same announcement-versus-asset gap shows up in the reshoring story. TSMC just posted a record quarter, $40.2 billion in revenue, and raised its Arizona commitment by another $100 billion, bringing its total US pledge to $265 billion. That is a real number, but it is a cumulative, multi-year announced commitment, not deployed capital or operating fab capacity. It matters strategically for two reasons. It disperses TSMC's most advanced capability away from Taiwan, a slow dilution of the island's so-called silicon shield. And Taiwan's own grid leans heavily on imported Middle Eastern LNG, which is exactly why Gulf security and chip supply chains are no longer separate stories. A Hormuz disruption does not just move oil. It raises the landed energy cost of making the most advanced chips on earth.

Close the loop with who controls the raw inputs underneath all of this. Beijing has spent the past three months moving from export-control rule-writing to actual enforcement. Chinese customs detained two Japanese nationals in May over alleged smuggling of export-restricted goods, and detained a Chinese optics executive in June for allegedly mislabeling germanium-containing lenses to dodge licensing. On July 1, a new formal reporting mechanism for strategic-mineral violations took effect. And in June, China added ten US entities to its own export control list, including the two flagship American rare-earth producers, MP Materials and USA Rare Earth. The direct revenue hit may be limited, since those firms already sell mainly into US and allied markets. But the signal is not symbolic. Beijing is telling the market it will use enforcement, not just tariffs, as leverage over the germanium, gallium, and rare-earth-magnet value chain that every reshoring and nuclear-restart plan above ultimately depends on. And so far there is no confirmed allied countermeasure aimed specifically at these summer actions.

Put it together. Energy security is repricing on both sides of the meter at once — the war premium unwinding on an unconfirmed claim, and the appetite for power still outrunning what has actually been built. Across nearly every item today, the announcement is running ahead of the asset. Palisades' restart date has slipped twice. TSMC's $265 billion is a pledge, not a fab. And Iran's de-escalation is a Trump post that Tehran's own foreign ministry flatly denies. The physical build, and the physical confirmation, are still the things to watch.

That's the briefing. Have a good day.
