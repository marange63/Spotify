Here's the thesis. The boldest claim to come out of the frontier labs this week landed in pure mathematics, not on a benchmark leaderboard. That matters because a math claim eventually gets a verdict, yes or no, from proof-checkers and peer review. A leaderboard score never really does. So for today's lead, the competitive question shifts from whose model scores highest to whose output survives outside scrutiny. The commercial models, meanwhile, are still graded by benchmarks that can't even agree on OpenAI's cheap model.

Start with Google. On November 25th, Google Research's multi-agent system, called Cogentic, pushed six math papers into a public GitHub repository in a single commit. According to the reporting, all six were produced by that framework running on Gemini 4. No official Google or DeepMind post confirming any of this has turned up. Everything here comes from secondary outlets covering the repository, not from Google itself. Keep that caveat in mind throughout.

The headline paper claims an unconditional algorithm for factoring integers. Its expected running time is written as L of one-third. The comparison is the best previously proven bound, L of one-half, from Lenstra and Pomerance back in 1992. So on paper, this would improve the strongest result mathematicians have actually proven about factoring. That's a claim about rigor, not a claim to the fastest factoring method ever.

Here's where a deep dive this week filled in the picture. Two secondary analyses put the constant in that formula at 105. An earlier write-up gave a different figure, so treat the exact constant as reported, not settled. The yardstick is the general number field sieve, the fast heuristic method already in use. The sieve runs at the same one-third exponent, with a constant of roughly one-point-nine-two. One analysis put the problem plainly: the new constant sits inside an exponential. So a jump from under two to 105 is not a rounding difference. It's the difference between fast and impractically slow. One estimate went further. It judged the new bound worse than simply trying every divisor up to the key's square root. And reporting says the paper itself never mentions RSA.

So why does it still matter? The sieve only reaches its speed if several unproven assumptions about numbers hold up. A rigorous bound at the same exponent would be a genuine theoretical advance, if the proof survives. It would be a proven rate where before there was only a heuristic one. It is not a faster real-world way to break encryption. Don't let the cryptography angle run ahead of what's actually been claimed.

Which brings us to verification, the real open question. Nobody outside Google has publicly confirmed the proof yet. The promised machine-checked proofs, in the Lean proof language, remain unreleased. Google has given no date for them. No named mathematician or cryptographer has been reported reviewing the papers. That absence of outside confirmation is the central caveat here, not a side note.

There's also a publicity angle worth naming. Six papers in one commit, led by a factoring claim, is a release built for visibility. Factoring reliably grabs headlines. That doesn't make the work wrong. But a single error surfacing in review would hurt the "AI does real science" story more than a slower, independently checked release would have.

Now place this in Google's broader run. This desk has tracked Google's leaderboard position wobbling all month. Its Gemini 4 Carbon model went from claiming first place to no better than third in under a month. And Gemini 4 Neon's lead on LiveBench, which we covered yesterday, rests on a contested score. Cogentic looks like Google moving the competition to a different axis. Rank churn doesn't apply there, because the referee eventually gives a binary verdict. That's our read, and it makes Cogentic a higher-risk, higher-reward bet than another benchmark win.

The second-order effects cut both ways, and this part is our inference. If the proof survives, the bar for AI-for-science claims rises to checkable artifacts. OpenAI and Anthropic would be pressed to publish verifiable outputs, not eval scores. A verified result would also hand Alphabet a differentiation argument that doesn't depend on serving cost or leaderboard rank. Those are the two places Google looked contested this week. If the proof fails, expectations reset across the sector. Either way, six papers landing at once suggests agent output can now outrun human review. Verification capacity, not generation, may become the scarce input for AI-for-science claims at every lab.

So the so-what: Google has staked credibility on a claim that, unlike a benchmark score, can be flatly proven wrong.

The commercial models don't get a referee that clean. Look at OpenAI's own cheap model. GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol launched on November 17th into ChatGPT Work, Codex, and the API, so the launch itself is old news. What this desk hasn't aired is one result from OpenAI's launch materials, as reported by 9to5mac. It comes from Cognition's FrontierCode 2.0 coding benchmark. With both models at maximum reasoning effort, Sol came within half a point of Astra, OpenAI's flagship, at about one-fifth the cost. That's OpenAI's own number, not an independent run, so treat it as OpenAI grading itself.

The figure also sharpens a point from yesterday's briefing. This desk said cost per task hinges on reasoning-effort settings that evaluators rarely disclose. This result is measured at maximum effort, which says little about the settings buyers actually run. And it sits awkwardly against the independent read. On November 23rd, this desk reported that Artificial Analysis's coding index had Sol slipping against its own predecessor. So one yardstick says OpenAI's cheap model nearly matches the flagship, while another says it regressed. Which benchmark a buyer trusts decides whether Sol looks like a bargain or a downgrade. There's a third reading, too. FrontierCode may be saturating at the top. In that case, a half-point gap says more about the benchmark's ceiling than about the two models.

Now layer in that Astra's own list price is already 20 percent below its predecessor's. You get a cheap tier nearly matching the flagship on one major benchmark, while the flagship itself got cheaper. That's margin compression OpenAI is inflicting on itself, and it's our inference. If buyers route coding work to Sol instead of Astra, usage can keep growing while revenue per task falls. It's the margin-dilution dynamic this desk flagged on November 23rd, now running inside OpenAI's own product line. It also supports the read that the coding layer is commoditizing, not consolidating into a winner-take-most market. OpenAI's sharpest price competitor this week may be OpenAI.
