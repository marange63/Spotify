Cautious Optimism Briefings

Robotics & Physical AI

Transcript · November 24, 2026

Good morning. It's Tuesday, November 24th, 2026, and this is your robotics and physical AI briefing.

Since mid-November, this desk has been asking which layer of the humanoid stack is actually scarce: the brain, or the body. In recent weeks, the two most powerful actors in the industry have each moved to standardize one end of it. Nvidia is offering the software and a reference body to the ecosystem outside China. Beijing has set state standards for the hardware inside the body. Neither move is a deployment. Together, they suggest the scarce layer is shifting somewhere else.

Start with Nvidia and its GTC Berlin package, which reached the trade press over the weekend. The headline product is a reference humanoid built with Agile Robots, called the Agile ONE SN. Per Robotics and Automation News, it stands one point four meters tall and weighs forty kilograms. It has thirty-one degrees of freedom. Each of its two five-finger hands carries twenty more, with tactile sensing and force control. Nvidia's own release says it is powered by the company's Jetson Thor edge platform. A second outlet, eWeek, reports that it ships with GR00T 1.7 preconfigured. Availability starts in early 2027.

Last Friday, this desk said physical AI was still a roster of partner names on Nvidia's earnings call, not a line item. Berlin is Nvidia's attempt to turn that roster into a reference platform. Nvidia's chief executive, Jensen Huang, framed it in his own words: "the ChatGPT moment for robotics is fast approaching." Treat that line as what it is. It comes from the company that sells the compute under much of the non-Chinese humanoid field. It is rhetoric, not a capability measurement.

The software underneath matters more than the body. Nvidia's release confirms that Isaac OS, its robot runtime, is open source and built on its Holoscan framework. It also says Isaac Lab 3.0, its training environment, is now generally available. And it adds a dataset called Human 1M, which pairs three-view video with 3D hand tracking. Nvidia gives no size for that dataset, and it mentions no force or tactile data. So this is human video, not robot-collected experience. The so what is simple. Nvidia is giving away more of the software stack, and that only pays if the silicon underneath gets bought.

Now place the Agile ONE SN on the commitment ladder. No price is disclosed. No paying customer is disclosed either. Nvidia's release cites only, in its words, "interest from leading institutions such as Stanford University and ETH Zurich." eWeek adds that the announcement "does not disclose orders from either institution." There is one new number. eWeek reports that Agile aims to produce up to two thousand compact humanoids in 2027. That is a production plan, not a sold unit and not a backlog. It sits on the same rung as the early-2027 ship date.

The most concrete economic number in the whole package isn't about humanoids at all. Toyota Material Handling Europe and SoftServe report that simulation-based development can cut commissioning time by up to fifty percent. That's a partner-reported upper bound, with no stated baseline. But it points somewhere real. This desk's inference is that Nvidia's nearest robotics money may come from commissioning ordinary automation in simulation, not from selling humanoid brains. The competing read is about distribution. Nvidia still shows no robotics revenue line. A wide reference platform is a way to pull Jetson-class silicon into the whole field, whoever wins the humanoid race.

Nvidia also named integration partners, with specifics in its own release. ABB is integrating Omniverse libraries into its RobotStudio HyperReality product. Sereact is using Isaac GR00T with Cosmos Transfer to train its Cortex goods-handling platform. Schaeffler's role is different. It is contributing its actuator technology to a separate Nvidia project called Newton. Kuka, Franka Robotics and Neura Robotics also appear in the release. None of the named partners discloses a deployment count or a customer figure. So Berlin bought Nvidia distribution and a named reference platform. It did not show anyone using a humanoid brain for paid work.

Nvidia is standardizing the software and the reference body. Beijing has just done the same for the hardware inside it. According to the English edition of 36Kr, China's industry ministry issued new standards this month for two of the hardest parts of a humanoid. One covers the integrated electric-drive joint. The other covers the dexterous hand. They are numbered SJ/T 12356 and 12357, and both take effect on issuance. We don't have the technical text behind either one. Nothing reported so far discloses interface dimensions, protocols, or certification requirements. So treat interchangeable parts as the likely intent, and as our inference, not as the standards' confirmed contents.

There are two honest readings. The first is commoditization. A shared joint and hand specification could let China's deep supplier base turn components into interchangeable parts. That would extend the price collapse this desk tracked on November 16th. Unitree's average humanoid selling price fell roughly seventy-two percent in about two years. The second reading is a compliance gate. A state standard can work as a quality floor that favors domestic champions over foreign entrants, without lowering cost at all. What's been reported doesn't let us choose between them.

Set that against the opposite bet this desk covered on Saturday. Hyundai Mobis said it has developed reducers, motors and controllers in-house, for Boston Dynamics' Atlas. That's vertical integration in Korea against state-led standardization in China. They are two answers to the same question about who controls the body. Either way, independent actuator and hand suppliers look squeezed, and that's our inference. Hyundai Mobis already took one flagship Western buyer in-house. Chinese state specs could turn much of the remaining market into a commodity.

The scale behind Beijing's standard-setting deserves a number. Per the International Federation of Robotics, as relayed by Fortune, China installed 354,000 industrial robots in 2025. That was fifty-nine percent of the world total. The United States installed 38,400. By our arithmetic, that's roughly nine Chinese installations for every American one. These are industrial robots broadly, not humanoids, so don't read them as a humanoid count. But they rhyme with the shipment data this desk covered on November 16th. Chinese vendors took about ninety-seven percent of third-quarter humanoid shipments. So these joint and hand standards land on the deepest automation supply base in the world.

Put the two stories together, and a pattern comes into focus. If the brain and the body are both being standardized, neither stays scarce for long. Our inference is that the scarce layer shifts toward integration and proven field reliability. That is uncomfortable for mid-tier Western humanoid makers. If the brain, the simulator and the reference body all come from Nvidia, they're left to compete on integration, data and uptime. Tesla and Figure, with their own in-house stacks, are betting the other way. And nothing in either announcement carries a single uptime, intervention or cycle number. On Sunday, this desk set out a standard: verified performance over announced capability. By that standard, a spec sheet and an up-to-fifty-percent claim are announcements, not evidence.

So where does that leave things? The platform and standards layer is genuinely consolidating, on both sides of the Pacific. Reliable, paid, unsupervised work in the field is still mostly a demo. Watch three catalysts. First, Nvidia's GR00T 2 open weights, reportedly due in December. They'll show whether Nvidia is giving away the brain to sell the compute. Second, publication of the actual MIIT standards text. That would tell us whether this is commoditization or a compliance gate. Third, whether the Agile ONE SN picks up a named, paying customer before its early-2027 ship date.

That's the briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
