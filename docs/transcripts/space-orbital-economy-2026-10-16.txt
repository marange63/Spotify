Good morning. It's Friday, October 16th, 2026, and this is your space and orbital economy briefing. This week the market stopped pricing ambitions and started pricing schedules.

Start with Rocket Lab. Wednesday's 8-K told us the first launch of Neutron, Rocket Lab's medium-lift reusable rocket, is slipping from the fourth quarter of this year into 2027. The slip was a known risk. What's new is that CEO Peter Beck is now on the record about why. Speaking to Bloomberg, Beck says the company found voids forming inside the carbon-composite layers of the propellant dome during manufacturing.

Beck also drew the line on risk in his own words. The voids, he said, "don't necessarily mean that the dome would fail on flight, but our practice is to proceed only when we believe the system has the appropriate margins." And more bluntly: "Possible success isn't our standard." That is a company choosing to eat a schedule hit rather than fly hardware it isn't fully confident in. Beck added one more date that matters. The cryogenic and hydrostatic test campaigns won't finish until the first quarter of 2027. Rocket Lab still hasn't named a launch quarter. But our read is that the test calendar now sets a floor. It is hard to see Neutron flying before those campaigns are done. So there is a floor now, but still no quarter.

The market's reaction tells its own story. Rocket Lab shares fell about eight percent on announcement day, to close at $59.18. Then, in Thursday morning trading, they were down roughly another eleven percent, at $52.85. So the second-day drop was bigger than the first. One reading is new information. Investors are moving past pricing a single slip and starting to reprice the whole 2027 timeline. The other reading is sector contagion. 24/7 Wall Street reported SpaceX and AST SpaceMobile pulling back the same day. Both are probably true, and the tape can't cleanly separate them. KeyBanc's Michael Leshock actually cited both. He cut his price target from $135 to $100 on Thursday, keeping his Overweight rating. Per Investing.com's account of the note, the cut reflects the updated Neutron timeline and "broader multiple compression across high-growth space equities." That same account says he now models Neutron's first launch in the second quarter of 2027, with the first quarter possible only if execution goes well. That's one analyst's estimate, not guidance. But it puts the floor later than the test schedule alone would.

There's a deeper pattern worth naming. This is not Neutron's first tank problem. Back in January, the main-stage tank of the first Neutron ruptured during an overnight pressure test at Wallops, Virginia. Rocket Lab reportedly changed its manufacturing process after that. Now, nine months later, a different defect in the tank hardware is forcing another slip. Two schedule-breaking failures in the same production system raise a harder question than when Neutron flies. Can this tank-manufacturing process be made repeatable at flight-qualification standard? That's our framing, not Rocket Lab's. The economics follow from it. Neutron is the vehicle meant to move Rocket Lab from small-launch pricing into medium-lift pricing. Every quarter it slips, the development capital already spent earns nothing, and the payback clock on it starts later. For Rocket Lab holders, Neutron revenue moves out of 2026 and into an undated stretch of 2027. The near-term equity story now rests on the existing Electron and spacecraft business. Neutron trades as an option, not a dated business line.

The same selloff reached AST SpaceMobile, and AST's problem is the same species: a schedule gap, not a demand gap. First, the backdrop. On October 8th, SpaceX agreed to buy a nationwide block of 800 megahertz spectrum from Grain Management, which had recently acquired it from T-Mobile. The Wall Street Journal, citing people familiar with the matter, put the price at about $8 billion in cash. The deal still needs FCC approval. Why it matters for AST is simple. Owning low-band spectrum outright would let Starlink sell phone coverage without going through carrier partners. AST's whole model runs through those carrier partnerships.

AST shares have slid since that deal, and after Piper Sandler's downgrade to Neutral. This week they traded at $42.14 in one session, down about 10.6 percent. What's genuinely new is an estimate from independent analyst Tim Farrar. He warns AST may not reach sixty satellites in orbit until 2028. That's one analyst's model, not company guidance. And the sell side isn't unanimous. Berenberg and Roth both argued the bullish side after the spectrum deal. But set Farrar's number against AST's own plan. The company's second-quarter update targeted forty-five BlueBirds by early next year. If Farrar is right, the revenue-generating fleet builds far more slowly than that plan implies. And direct-to-cell revenue scales with satellite count, because coverage gaps are what keep a carrier from selling the service. So every year of delay is a year of capital spent on satellites that can't yet sell a continuous product. That reframes the AST debate. The question used to be whether AST could compete on spectrum against SpaceX. The question now is more basic. Can AST build the constellation on the timeline its capital needs? If not, carriers wanting a non-SpaceX satellite partner face a thin alternative for years, not quarters. One more item for the overhang column. A securities class-action lead-plaintiff deadline falls on November 13th. That's a law-firm notice of the kind that routinely follows a big drawdown, not a finding of wrongdoing.

This isn't only a Western-startup problem. Beijing has the same plan-versus-actual gap, with state money behind it instead of venture capital. Late last week, China launched the 27th batch of Guowang satellites, nine more from Wenchang on a Long March 12. Per the trade outlet Mediasat, China planned to launch 310 Guowang satellites this year and has deployed 86 so far. By our arithmetic, that's about 28 percent of this year's plan, with less than a quarter of the year left. Commercial service is still in testing and not open to subscribers. One caution on the numbers. Cumulative on-orbit totals vary widely across trackers, so don't lean on any single running total.

Now contrast Qianfan, China's other megaconstellation. Its documented problem is on the satellites themselves. Independent tracker Jonathan McDowell counted twenty-two Qianfan satellites as failed as of September 10th. That's a tracker estimate, not an operator disclosure. Guowang's gap, by contrast, shows up in launches flown against plan. What we still can't document is why, whether rockets or satellite production. Either way, the simple story of China outbuilding the West doesn't hold up cleanly. Each flagship constellation is stuck somewhere, and for different visible reasons. Here's our read on why the shortfall matters less to Beijing than it would to a startup. State capital can absorb a weak year that public-market investors would punish. So Guowang is not yet a commercial competitor to Starlink, but a slow year is not a strategic failure either.

Pull the threads together. What's genuinely inflecting this week is the market's willingness to discount optimistic schedules, for a public launch company and a public satellite-communications company alike. And the same plan-versus-actual gap is now quantified for a state program too. What's still a demo, not a business, is medium-lift reusability outside SpaceX, AST's direct-to-cell service, and Guowang's commercial offering. In each case the economic cost of delay is the same: capital already committed, with the revenue that pays it back pushed further out. The open question running through all three is whether anyone besides SpaceX can turn a launch plan into a launch cadence in 2027. Watch five things. First, any launch quarter Rocket Lab finally attaches to Neutron. Second, Blue Origin's attempt to fly New Glenn before year-end. Third, a confirmed date for Starship's next flight, which still doesn't exist. Fourth, the FCC's review of SpaceX's 800 megahertz purchase from Grain, which decides whether Starlink can sell phone coverage around the carriers. And fifth, NASA's CLPS 2.0 lunar lander award, now set for November 7th. Those catalysts will tell us whether 2027 becomes the year execution catches up to ambition, or just the year the slips get renamed again.
