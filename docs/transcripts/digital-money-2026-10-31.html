Cautious Optimism Briefings

Digital Money, Crypto, and Market Structure

Transcript · October 31, 2026

Good morning. It's Saturday, October 31st, and this is your Cautious Optimism briefing on digital money.

Yesterday we closed on an open question. When a hack hits the most liquid offshore exchange, do holders of the regulated Bitcoin ETFs de-risk alongside everyone on that exchange? Or does the wrapper insulate them, the way its design promises?

First, a quick reset on the event. The exchange is Binance, and it disclosed the breach on October 29th. Binance now puts the total impact at about $6.3 billion. That makes it the largest crypto heist on record, roughly four times the prior record, Bybit's $1.5 billion in February 2025. Ether was among the assets taken. The first answer to yesterday's question is now in, and it leans against the insulation story.

US spot Bitcoin ETFs posted a net outflow of $643.33 million on October 30th, according to SoSoValue data. That ended the run of inflow days we tracked into the hack. BlackRock's IBIT led the redemptions, with about $233.7 million of that total, more than a third of it.

This desk has watched IBIT lead flows in both directions this month. It fronted the selling on October 22nd. Then it carried an inflow almost single-handedly a few days later. It did the same thing again here. So this wasn't a broad, diffuse retreat. It was concentrated in the fund with the most institutional client base in the complex.

Now size it honestly. Total net assets across spot Bitcoin ETFs stood at about $98 billion. Against that base, $643 million is roughly two-thirds of one percent, by our arithmetic. It's a signal, not a stampede. And it's one session of aggregator data, subject to revision, not issuer filings. The October 22nd outflow was almost the same size, and the complex went back to inflows within days.

The regulated selling wasn't confined to Bitcoin, either. US spot Ether ETFs lost $155.85 million on the same day, with six of eleven funds seeing outflows. Remember, Ether was among the assets the hacker took. So redemption pressure in the regulated wrappers reached the asset class the hack hit, not just Bitcoin. That's a second data point pointing the same way. It came on the same day, under the same macro shock, so it isn't independent confirmation.

What the Bitcoin outflow signals is genuinely ambiguous, so hold two readings. The first is plain institutional de-risking. Allocators cut directional exposure, or de-gross a risk budget, after a volatility shock. The second is mechanical. Some ETF holdings are long spot, hedged with short CME futures. A basis compression after a vol spike can force redemptions with no directional view behind them at all.

The evidence that would separate those stories is CME futures basis behavior. We looked for an October 29th to 30th reading, and found none published. So both explanations stay open. What the pattern does argue against is pure retail panic. A retail-driven selloff would more plausibly show up first in the smaller, retail-skewed funds, not be led by the largest institutional one.

Here's why that matters beyond one day. Much of the ETF-era bull case rests on the idea that institutional ownership dampens volatility. But when ETF holders exit alongside offshore traders, redemptions require authorized participants to sell the underlying Bitcoin. A liquid wrapper can then add to spot selling in stress, rather than absorb it. The wrapper is only as sticky as the holders inside it.

That ETF outflow didn't happen in isolation. The leveraged market was unwinding on the same two days.

Start with the backdrop. The Fed held at 3.75 to 4% on October 28th, with two dissents favoring a hike. Bitcoin fell below $80,000 the next day, and slid into the mid-seventy-thousands on the 30th. So the hack landed on a market that was already leaning risk-off. The reporting links the hack to the cascade, but nobody has measured how much belongs to the hack and how much to the Fed.

Now the forced selling. CoinGlass data puts 24-hour derivatives liquidations at $1.49 billion. A separate tally from Crypto Times puts it at $1.57 billion, with longs absorbing about 84% of the losses. Treat that as a range, because the trackers don't agree. Long-side dominance tells you who was forced out. It doesn't tell you that new shorts are in control.

What both trackers point to is the venue. Binance itself accounted for 49.1% of all liquidations, the largest share of any exchange. So the hacked venue was also the leverage epicenter. Offshore leverage, not the wrapper, remained the main channel for forced selling, even though the wrapper bled too.

That breaks a framing this desk held earlier in the week. We read the leverage book as light, with cool funding and shrinking open interest. We argued that any real stress would show up through ETF redemptions and the dollar float, rather than a liquidation spike. Instead we got both ETF redemptions and a liquidation spike at once. This was the largest forced-selling day of this run, and it arrived alongside a meaningful ETF redemption, not instead of one.

On open interest, the data is a mess. Providers disagree on both the direction and the level. So there's no reliable read on how much leverage is left in the system. It's better to say that plainly than to cite a number that won't hold up.

The dollar float is the other gap, and it matters for Treasury demand. We wanted to know whether USDT and USDC saw net redemptions over the hack window. No issuance data covering those two days is in yet. The latest reading, from DefiLlama, had USDC supply down $4.69 billion over the 30 days to October 25th. That predates the hack, so it can't be read as a reaction to it. But USDC's reserves sit largely in short-dated bills. Sustained redemptions trim issuer demand for bills at the margin. Whether the hack speeds up that drain is a question for next week's data.

Binance was where the forced selling concentrated. It's also where the recovery effort now sits.

The new number is this. Binance says it has identified and frozen about $3 billion in assets that passed through hacker-linked wallets. That comes on top of the issuer freezes we covered yesterday. Tether froze more than $1.567 billion in USDT. Circle, the issuer of USDC, froze funds as well. Don't add those together. Nothing establishes whether they overlap, and Binance describes its own figures as provisional.

What the freeze does is sharpen yesterday's control argument. The recoverable share of that $6.3 billion theft depends on chokepoints run by centralized actors. That means Binance itself, Tether, and Circle. That is close to the opposite of the permissionless pitch this industry sells. In this event, the system's resilience came from its least decentralized parts.

One thing is still missing. We have not seen an updated proof-of-reserves attestation from Binance. Until one lands, the claim that user funds are whole remains a statement, not a demonstrated fact.

Which brings us to the regulated, public company that was supposed to be the clean story in all of this.

Yesterday we flagged that Coinbase's third-quarter GAAP net income of $718.7 million looked flattered by something below the operating line. CNBC now says what. Net income surged thanks to the value of Coinbase's own volatile crypto investment portfolio. In other words, balance-sheet marks, not trading revenue.

The nine-month picture undercuts the headline further. Coinbase posted a net loss of $34.9 million for the first three quarters combined, despite that strong single quarter. Now draw the inference, which is ours and not company guidance. A profit built on marks taken at higher prices is exactly the kind that reverses in a quarter opening with Bitcoin in the mid-seventy-thousands. So Coinbase's GAAP line is effectively levered to the same drawdown now hitting ETF holders. For anyone holding the stock as the regulated winner from an offshore hack, that's the catch. The earnings line carries the asset's price risk on top of the franchise.

Here's the strongest bullish case, stated plainly, because it deserves airtime. The largest crypto heist on record landed in the same 48 hours as the largest liquidation day of this run. The regulated Bitcoin ETF complex still lost well under one percent of its assets in that session. On that narrow measure, institutionalized crypto absorbed a shock of this size better than the headlines suggested. That's not nothing.

So where does this leave us? The first data point leans against the insulation story. The regulated wrapper bled alongside offshore leverage, rather than standing apart from it. But it's one session. It was no larger than the October 22nd outflow, which came with no hack at all. And the Fed is a confound nobody has separated out. So call the insulation thesis wounded, not disproven. Three things to watch from here. First, whether these ETF outflows persist past one session, or reverse the way they did after October 22nd. Second, CME basis behavior, still the only evidence that would tell us whether this was directional selling or a hedged unwind. And third, Binance's updated reserves attestation, which we still don't have.

That's your briefing for Saturday, October 31st. Stay cautiously optimistic.
