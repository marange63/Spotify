Cautious Optimism Briefings

Space & the Orbital Economy

Transcript · September 2, 2026

Good morning. It's September 2nd, and this is your briefing on the space and orbital economy.

Start with the one number that reframes the whole beat. SpaceX listed on June 11th at $135 a share, and the company that now reports to public shareholders consolidates three businesses: launch, Starlink, and the former xAI — a data-center compute operation sitting inside the rocket company. In SpaceX's first quarter of disclosure, per the Motley Fool's read of the earnings release, that AI unit absorbed $15.8 billion of the company's $18.4 billion of total capital spending. It means eighty-six cents of every capex dollar SpaceX spent last quarter went into data-center compute, rather than into rockets or satellites.

The honest framing of that is uncomfortable. The market is no longer looking at a space company with an artificial-intelligence side project. It is looking at an AI infrastructure company wrapped around a space company. That is a repricing of the sector's central player, and it happened without a single engineering event.

Now the segment lines underneath it. Connectivity, which is Starlink, brought in $4.3 billion of revenue, up sixty-six percent year over year. Segment operating profit reached $1.66 billion. That comes straight from SpaceX's own earnings call. Subscribers hit 12 million, double the count a year earlier. The call gave no standalone consumer-only dollar figure, so treat any precise consumer number you see quoted elsewhere as somebody's inference, not a disclosed fact.

The space segment, meaning launch, is where it gets awkward. It is also where you have to be careful about which loss you are citing. Secondary reporting built from the earnings release puts the space segment's operating loss at $542 million on $962 million of revenue. On the call itself, chief financial officer Bret Johnsen gave a different and smaller number. Adjusted EBITDA for the segment was a loss of $205 million. Those are not one figure restated two ways. EBITDA strips out depreciation and stock compensation that the operating-loss line includes. So do not treat them as interchangeable, and do not conclude from either that Falcon 9 itself is bleeding cash.

What actually explains the loss is research and development. Johnsen disclosed space-segment R&D of roughly $1.1 billion, tied explicitly to Starship's push toward full reusability. That single line is larger than everything the segment billed in the quarter. Here is the honest counter-case, then, to the story that SpaceX is starving launch to feed AI. A segment developing a next-generation reusable vehicle has to expense that development somewhere, and this is what it looks like on an income statement. The disclosure does not break out the rest of the cost base, so this stays a question rather than a verdict. And one quarter of segment data is not a capital-allocation policy.

At the company level, revenue was $7.8 billion against a net loss of $541 million. Growth is not the issue here. Where the money goes is.

The compute side, unlike the rocket side, has a trajectory rather than a snapshot. Colossus II, the terrestrial training cluster that AI unit is building, ended the quarter at 1.4 gigawatts of nameplate capacity. A year earlier it was 400 megawatts. Management guided to more than 2 gigawatts by year end. That is not a buildout pausing to let a rocket program catch up. It is the thing the balance sheet is organized around, and it sets the internal competition for every dollar the launch business would like to spend.

Five days ago on this beat we argued that the most durable consequence of the listing would not be the share price. It would be putting the dominant constellation operator's economics onto a fixed public disclosure cadence for the first time. This quarter is that prediction paying off. Every rival's Starlink-dependent business case now gets underwritten against disclosed segment profit instead of modelled guesswork. The disclosure cuts inward as well. Once one segment visibly earns real operating profit while the others consume capital, the long-floated case for separating Starlink acquires published numbers behind it. Treat that as an implication of the disclosure, not a plan. Management has resisted a split. Resisting it gets harder when the arithmetic is public.

Close the item on the mechanical overhang. Shares priced at $135 in June and now sit near $142, essentially back at the offering price. On the Motley Fool's estimate — a forward projection, not a filing I checked — more than 700 million shares unlock for sale in September, with roughly 650 million more unlocking in October. That is real supply arriving into a stock trading level with where the underwriters set it. It is a risk entirely separate from the operating story, and it lands in the same weeks as the flight that operating story is written against.

Which brings us to the flight. We reported the September 15th target and the walked-back tower catch five days ago, so I am not going to re-report either. What is new is the artifact. This is no longer a remark on an earnings call. SpaceX has told the FCC that Flight 14, no earlier than September 15th, is planned as the first genuine orbital insertion in Starship's test history. Every prior flight, including Flight 13's splashdown in July, flew a suborbital arc that reentered within the hour it launched. The filing describes roughly 20 Starlink V3 satellites aboard, bound for operational service rather than another suborbital drop test. Flag the provenance on that: the orbital characterization comes from an enthusiast outlet's reading of the FCC filing, not from a SpaceX statement.

The upper-stage catch back at the launch site now looks unlikely on this flight. Resist the easy read that this is an admission the catch is not ready. SpaceX has not stated a reason, and the more useful interpretation is procedural. Separating the orbital-insertion milestone from the reusability milestone is exactly how you de-risk a genuinely new attempt. But it does mean something concrete for the economics. Without the catch, per-flight cost stays well above the long-run target. So a successful Flight 14 would prove that Starship can reach orbit and deploy operational payload. It would not prove the cost-per-kilogram breakthrough that the segment's entire research bet is aimed at. Capability and cheap access to orbit are different milestones, and only one of them is on the table this month. Note the new asymmetry too. This is the first Starship attempt where a failure gets marked to market on a public equity the same morning.

Now the customer side of that same bottleneck, which is what deferred flight capacity costs somebody who is not SpaceX. AST SpaceMobile has 13 BlueBird satellites in orbit after launching three more in early August. Its second-quarter call disclosed a production rate of six satellites a month, with manufacturing underway through BlueBird 46. Note what those are, though. A production rate and a line position are not a count of funded, manifested, flight-ready hardware.

Then the revenue line. Revenue for the quarter was $31.5 million. Backlog stands at $1.3 billion of contracted revenue from commercial partners and government awards. Full-year guidance was reaffirmed at $150 to $200 million. Do that arithmetic out loud. Backlog of $1.3 billion against a guide that tops out near $200 million means most of that contracted revenue does not recognize until 2027 at the earliest. That is not automatically a red flag. Satellites have multi-quarter lead times, and building ahead of a manifest that may open up next year is a rational bet rather than wasted capital. What decides it is whether the backlog is contingent on service milestones AST has not yet hit, and the record does not settle that either way. Either way, the near-term equity story is execution risk on deployment, not demand risk.

So pull the thread. Money is not scarce at the top of this sector right now. Not at SpaceX, which spent north of $18 billion in a single quarter. Not at AST, sitting on $3.7 billion of liquidity. What is scarce is flights. And the company supplying most of them has now disclosed that it spends roughly six dollars on compute for every dollar on everything else. That leaves it two levers over every operator queued behind it: the price of a ride, and the order of the queue. Watch two things converge inside the same three weeks. Flight 14, no earlier than September 15th, and the share unlocks landing in September and October.

That's the briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
