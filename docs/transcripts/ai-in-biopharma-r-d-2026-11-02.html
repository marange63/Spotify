Cautious Optimism Briefings

AI in Biopharma R&D

Transcript · November 2, 2026

Good morning. It's November 2nd, 2026, and this is Cautious Optimism Briefings. Today's thesis: AI's newest footholds in pharma are bottleneck tools in the unglamorous middle of the chain, while the R&D bill keeps climbing regardless.

Start with the lead. On October 27th, AstraZeneca announced a research collaboration with Tremont AI, a Boston company that only emerged from stealth in September. Tremont was spun out of the Mahmood Lab at Mass General Brigham. It builds foundation models for toxicologic pathology, the slide reads that catch safety signals in IND-enabling studies. Its tools include TRACE, a vision encoder for tissue, and ToxScribe, a vision-language model that drafts written pathology findings. Tremont's case for a specialized architecture is concrete. General-purpose vision models struggle to separate rare pathology findings from background noise. In safety screening, that is exactly the failure that matters, because the signal you most need to catch is the one you see least often. So this is a clean example of the architecture split this show keeps tracking. Language models handle synthesis and drafting. A purpose-built vision engine does the pattern recognition on tissue.

This also moves the pathology land grab one stage earlier in the chain. On October 28th we covered Agilent agreeing to pay up to 270 million dollars for Proscia's slide-data software layer. The same day we covered Agendia's model reading breast-cancer recurrence risk off routine H&E slides. Both were plays on the diagnostic side. Tremont takes the same class of model upstream, into preclinical safety work. And it isn't AstraZeneca's first pathology-AI move this year. Per Modella AI's own release, AstraZeneca acquired Modella, a company built, in its words, "at the intersection of pathology, clinical data, and advanced generative AI." Terms weren't disclosed. My read, and it's an inference, is that AstraZeneca is buying one pathology-AI company and partnering with another, rather than betting on a single in-house model. A research collaboration is a cheap option. An acquisition is a commitment. AstraZeneca now holds both kinds of position.

Now the skeptical turn, because the Tremont deal is thinner than the headline. There are no financial terms and no timeline. None of Tremont's three tools, TRACE, ToxScribe, or its workspace called Tremont Studio, has a disclosed validation metric. Tremont's own CEO says the collaboration lets the company "enhance and evaluate these models against the realities of drug safety assessment." That is a candid admission that the models haven't yet been proven against those realities. And even once they're validated, a board-certified pathologist still signs the GLP study conclusion that regulators rely on. An AI read carries no regulatory standing on its own. So the realistic value is throughput and triage for pathologists, not replacement.

That raises the second-order question for the toxicologic-pathology CROs. The industry literature has long described a shortage of toxicologic pathologists, though there's no current, solid figure for its size. If review capacity really is the bottleneck, tools like this matter more for speed than for better calls. And if sponsors start co-developing their own tox-path models with startups like Tremont, the CRO's read-and-report service is the exposed layer. That's a conditional inference, not something any company has said. Tremont is a throughput tool for IND-enabling work, not a safety verdict.

Tremont is a specialized model doing work language models can't. The next item shows the two being fused. A paper in Nature on October 21st, which this show hasn't yet covered, introduced Pinal. It's a generative model that designs candidate proteins directly from natural-language descriptions of the function you want. It doesn't start from a structural template or a homologous sequence. In effect, it puts a language interface in front of a protein-design engine. The biologist states the goal in words, and the model returns candidate proteins. That's the clearest example yet of the convergence this show has been watching. Language becomes the front end, and the design capability sits underneath.

What's notable is the release choice. Following the precedent of AlphaFold and ESM3, the Pinal authors are releasing inference code, trained weights, and a public web server. The full training release is restricted. That places Pinal in the middle of a spectrum this show has tracked. On October 25th we covered RFD4-Proteina, from Nvidia and David Baker's Institute for Protein Design, shipping trained weights under a BSD-3 license. At the other end, Isomorphic Labs keeps its design engine fully closed. Pinal splits the difference: share the weights, hold back the training recipe. That tells you where its authors think the defensible asset sits. It's not the weights. It's the data and the process used to build them. That choice has a cost, too. Outsiders can run Pinal, but they can't reproduce or audit how it was trained. It's a smaller version of the verification gap we've flagged with closed platforms.

For the AI-discovery names with closed engines, the implication cuts against them, and this is my inference. As academic groups publish strong models with weights attached, a platform's moat has to come from proprietary training data, wet-lab loops, or clinical assets. Model access alone gets harder to charge for.

But don't mistake an elegant interface for a validated capability. Pinal's announcement reports no wet-lab hit rate and no benchmark. Nothing in it shows that natural-language prompts yield proteins that express, fold, and function at a useful rate. A prompt is only as good as the functional spec behind it. And the hard part of protein design has never been generating candidates. It's confirming at the bench that they work. So the idea that Pinal lowers the barrier for non-specialists is plausible, but it's an inference, not a result. Openness now means sharing the weights while keeping the training recipe, and the validation is still owed.

Both of these developments sit before the clinic. One raises safety-review throughput, the other design throughput. So what is pharma actually spending on? Eli Lilly's third-quarter results, reported October 29th, give a hard number. R&D expense rose 23 percent, a bit slower than revenue's 39 percent. Lilly attributes that rise to pipeline investment and molecules added through business development. Nothing in the figure is attributed to AI.

The business-development piece shows up in another line. Lilly's non-GAAP earnings of 7.48 dollars a share included 4.57 dollars of acquired in-process R&D charges. On our own arithmetic, that charge is worth more than half of the per-share figure. Lilly is buying external pipeline, not waiting for internal discovery to get cheaper. Treat all of this carefully. Lilly's growth is an incretin-driven outlier, not an industry proxy. And these numbers say nothing about AI's effect on R&D productivity either way. That fits the call we've been making: R&D spend is reallocating, not shrinking. Lilly's 23 percent is fresh support for it, not a new insight.

Now the lever none of this touches. Flagship Pioneering, whose Etcelera merger we covered on October 31st, put a number on it in the merger coverage. Per Endpoints, Flagship chairman Alec Reynolds argues that about half of drug candidates reaching Phase 2 fail because the industry lacks causal intelligence. He means causal, not merely correlative, biological understanding. Take that for what it is. It's a self-serving claim from the chairman of a company that just launched a causal-biology platform. It is not an independent audit of Phase 2 failures. But it names the lever cleanly. Lilly's rising R&D bill is paying for more pipeline and more throughput tools. It is not yet paying down the Phase 2 attrition that decides the return on that spending.

So pull the threads together. On real-world data, nothing new cleared the bar this cycle. Here's my read on where the money sits. Capital and emphasis today are going into buying pipeline and into design before the clinic. Think of Lilly's acquired in-process R&D, and design engines from open models like Pinal to closed ones like Isomorphic's. The near-term low-hanging fruit is somewhere less glamorous: review-capacity and documentation bottlenecks, like tox-path triage and language-model drafting of findings. The payoff there is time, not better calls. And anything that claims to cut Phase 2 attrition is still unproven.

That leaves three open questions. Does AstraZeneca or Tremont ever disclose a performance or time-saving metric from this collaboration? Does anyone publish wet-lab validation for Pinal-style natural-language design? And will any large pharma attribute a measured R&D saving specifically to AI on an earnings call, rather than just spending more? On clinical success rates, the realistic read is that today's developments don't move them at all. On R&D spend, the pattern holds. Rising budgets are absorbing AI as bottleneck tooling, and they won't shrink because of it until something moves Phase 2.

That's the briefing. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
