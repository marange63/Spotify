Good morning. It's Tuesday, July 28th, and this is your digital money briefing.

All week this show has called Bitcoin a levered proxy for the risk tape, moving with tech stocks and oil headlines like just another momentum asset. Today's data complicates that story, and the complication is the lead.

Bitcoin's daily correlation with the S&P 500 fell to just 0.12 in the second quarter, down sharply from 0.58 in the fourth quarter of last year. That's a near-total collapse in the equity linkage that's defined how institutions have modeled Bitcoin risk for years. In its place, gold. Glassnode data puts Bitcoin's correlation with gold at 0.57 over the same quarter, and at 0.65 over the trailing year. Bitcoin and gold, analysts note, have been selling off in tandem because both now answer to the same real-rate and liquidity forces. So the working thesis needs revising: the driver isn't equity beta, it's the Fed and the real-yield complex, the same variables that move gold.

But here's the wrinkle, and it matters for how you read the next few weeks. Glassnode's own thirty-day reading has flipped negative, to minus 0.53. So the near-term correlation with gold has actually broken down even as the quarterly and annual correlations stay positive. That's not a clean confirmation of the new regime, it's a live complication. Correlations over ninety-day windows are noisy to begin with, and one volatile quarter doesn't make a durable regime. Treat the equity-correlation collapse as the more solid data point, and the gold coupling as directionally real but currently unsettled.

That unsettled state is exactly why tomorrow matters. The Fed's rate decision lands Wednesday at 2 p.m. Eastern, following today's meeting, and it's a non-SEP session with no fresh dot plot. If Bitcoin is now genuinely coupled to real yields rather than tech beta, a hawkish hold pressures it the same way it would pressure gold, not the way a Nasdaq selloff would.

There's a portfolio consequence worth naming here. If this gold-like coupling proves durable, the multi-asset allocators who spent years modeling Bitcoin as a levered Nasdaq position have to re-price it as something closer to a real-rate hedge. That changes who owns it and why. But the same logic cuts against the bulls. A real-rate hedge that sells off alongside gold when the Fed stays hawkish is not the inflation hedge the crypto pitch has leaned on for a decade. Under this coupling, a hawkish hold tomorrow doesn't rotate money out of stocks and into Bitcoin. It pressures Bitcoin and gold together. So the decoupling from tech is not automatically bullish. It just swaps one master for another.

We flagged the marginal-demand setup for this yesterday. The seven-session Bitcoin ETF inflow streak snapped last week, with outflows of two hundred twenty-five million dollars on July 23rd and two hundred forty million on the 24th, roughly four hundred sixty-five million combined, most of it BlackRock's IBIT. Even so, the ETF complex closed a third straight positive week, just under thirty-four million dollars of net inflow. Bitcoin itself ran above sixty-six thousand five hundred mid-week before falling back under sixty-four thousand. The point isn't that the outflow proves anything about the Fed, that link is a coincident setup, not established causation. The point is that this is the cleanest real-time read on the marginal institutional buyer, and that buyer is now hesitating around the same macro event that moves gold.

Oil is the next test of that thesis, and now we have primary numbers to anchor it. The EIA's July forecast cut its third-quarter Brent estimate all the way down to seventy-four dollars a barrel, a twenty-seven dollar downward revision from the prior month's estimate of just over one hundred one dollars, citing the US-Iran ceasefire reopening flows through the Strait of Hormuz. The tape hasn't cooperated. Brent settled near ninety-seven dollars as of July 24th, roughly twenty-three dollars above the government's own forecast. If oil reverts toward that seventy-four dollar forecast, that's disinflationary, real yields ease, and both gold and Bitcoin get room to run under the new coupling. If it holds near ninety-seven or climbs further, inflation risk keeps real yields elevated and pressures both assets together. So watch oil, not just the Fed statement, for where this goes.

Now, away from Bitcoin's macro repricing, to the plumbing underneath it. Circle got a real regulatory win this month: the OCC approved its application for a national trust bank charter, which CEO Jeremy Allaire called a defining step in bringing digital assets into the core of the US financial system. The stock popped on the news. But underneath that legitimacy win, the actual economics are moving the other way. USDC's circulating supply has fallen from seventy-seven billion dollars at the end of March to seventy-three point four billion by July 27th, a three point six billion dollar decline, roughly four point seven percent.

Here's why that number matters more than the charter or the volume headlines. Circle's first-quarter revenue was six hundred fifty-three million dollars out of six hundred ninety-four million total, almost entirely reserve income earned on that float at a three and a half percent yield. Reserve income tracks the size of the float, not transaction volume. So even as USDC posts a two hundred forty-seven percent year-over-year jump in on-chain transaction volume, by Circle's own count, the balance sheet that actually generates its revenue is quietly shrinking. Run the arithmetic on the quoted numbers, a sustained decline of this size implies something like a hundred twenty-five million dollars of annualized revenue headwind, and that's a rough estimate off the disclosed figures, not company guidance. The cause of the float decline, whether it's share loss to Tether, seasonal Treasury demand, or redemption for trading, isn't established, so don't read this as a market-share story yet. Read it as a warning that legitimacy and economics are diverging.

There are two read-throughs worth banking from that divergence. First, on the duopoly. Tether's USDT carries a market cap north of one hundred eighty billion dollars, more than double USDC's float, and Tether runs an offshore, disclosure-light model. Circle's is the opposite: US-regulated, transparent, and now federally chartered. The paradox is that the transparent, regulated issuer may be the more redemption-sensitive of the two, because its float answers to institutions that move money in and out for yield and trading, not to the harder-to-track offshore demand that anchors Tether. That's a hypothesis, not a proven cause, but it's the one to test as the float data comes in. Second, on Treasuries. Circle's reserves sit in short-dated government paper, so a shrinking float is a marginal subtraction from stablecoin demand for T-bills. It's small against total issuance, but it runs the opposite direction of the story bulls have been selling, that stablecoins are a structural new buyer of Treasuries.

So two threads to carry forward. Near-term, watch whether Bitcoin's gold-like coupling holds through tomorrow's Fed decision, or whether the thirty-day breakdown we just described turns out to be the more accurate signal. Structurally, watch whether Circle's regulatory wins ever show up in the number that actually pays its bills.
