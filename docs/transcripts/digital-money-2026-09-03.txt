Good morning. It's Thursday, September 3rd, 2026, and this is your digital money briefing.

Start with the number now driving everything else in this market: the odds of a Fed rate hike. Not a cut, a hike. CME FedWatch puts the chance of a quarter-point increase on September 16th at just above 62 percent. That is down from just above 67 percent a day earlier. It is still nearly double the roughly 37 percent priced a week ago, before Fed Chair Kevin Warsh's Jackson Hole speech. The ten-year Treasury yield backs it up, closing near 4.8 percent this week, its highest close since 2023. Be clear about what those hike odds are, though. They are a market price, not Fed guidance. The September 16th decision has not happened, the various trackers disagree on the exact probability, and August payrolls do not even print until Friday. So the direction of travel is the information here, not the decimal. And the direction is a regime this market has not had to price since the last hiking cycle. Bitcoin is now trading inside it.

Here's the mechanism, and it is not the one the playbook assumes. Renewed US strikes near the Strait of Hormuz pushed crude sharply higher and revived the inflation trade. That is what dragged Treasury yields up, and hike odds with them. So this is a cost-push shock, not the demand-overheating story that drove the 2022 hiking cycle. That distinction is the most important thing to hold onto this week. In 2022, Bitcoin sold off in lockstep with growth stocks because the market was pricing a Fed deliberately breaking demand. A shock that starts in the oil market rather than the labor market does not have to produce the same cross-asset behavior. Anyone reaching for the 2022 correlation map is probably using the wrong map.

Now place the price action inside that. Bitcoin fell below 77,000 dollars on September 2nd as this played out, then recovered back above 77,500 overnight. Gold did not act as the refuge either. December gold futures opened that Wednesday down four tenths of a percent, with the escalation in Iran already on the tape. So the easy bearish story does not survive. Bitcoin did not fail as digital gold by falling while gold caught a safe-haven bid, because gold fell in the same window.

The easy bullish story does not survive either, and here I have to be careful about what I actually know. On Sunday this show flagged that Bitcoin's ninety-day correlation with gold had climbed above fifty percent, and set a test: does that correlation decay once a hawkish path is sustained? There is now an active argument in the market that it has not decayed at all, that it has kept climbing even as Bitcoin's correlation to the Nasdaq falls. I could not confirm a current reading to the standard this show uses, so I am not going to quote you a number for it. What I can tell you is that two readings are live. In the first, Bitcoin is hedging fiscal and currency debasement alongside gold, in which case a hike hurts it less than the consensus assumes. In the second, both assets are simply repricing off higher real yields, which makes them the same trade for a boring reason. One session of co-movement cannot separate those two. The defensible claim today is narrower than either camp wants. Bitcoin's marginal driver this week is the front end of the curve, and one week is not a correlation regime.

If that is the macro, the next question is who actually moved, and the derivatives book answers that more cleanly than price does. Bitcoin open interest sits at 53.5 billion dollars, down 5.25 percent on the week. Funding rates are modest, nowhere near the extremes that flag excess leverage. And long liquidations made up nearly 85 percent of the latest 24-hour liquidation total. Those are aggregator figures with no named venue behind them, so take the precision loosely and the direction seriously. Directionally they say the same thing. This was a leverage flush, not a fundamentals repricing. Traders positioned long got forced out, and the drop was not driven by fresh short conviction. So the book heading into the next two weeks is cleaner and less levered than it was going into Jackson Hole. That cuts both ways. Less forced selling means less downside acceleration if the news turns bad. It also means less mechanical short-covering fuel if it turns good.

There is a second-order effect here the rates conversation keeps skipping. Higher-for-longer real yields raise the opportunity cost of holding a non-yielding asset like Bitcoin. But the same move improves the economics of the collateral layer underneath it, because the dollar tokens and tokenized Treasury funds that crypto trades against earn the front end directly. The rate move that hurts the asset pays the plumbing. So a hike is not uniformly bearish for this sector. It shifts money from the speculative sleeve toward the cash-management sleeve.

Now the cash side of the ledger, which tells a related but distinct story. Spot Bitcoin ETFs took in 3.52 billion dollars of net inflows in August, the strongest month since October of 2025, and a sharp rebound from just 172 million in July. That trend reversed on September 1st, when spot Bitcoin ETFs recorded 236 million dollars of net outflows. The same day, Ethereum, XRP, Solana and HYPE funds all took in fresh money, but none of them more than about fifteen million dollars apiece. It is tempting to call that a rotation, with institutional money leaving Bitcoin for altcoins. Look at the size mismatch, though. A 236 million dollar Bitcoin outflow against inflows in the low tens of millions each is not a transfer. It is an order of magnitude apart. The more defensible reading is that the altcoin wrappers are simply too small to be the destination for Bitcoin redemptions. They happen to be net positive on a day when Bitcoin was net negative. And only that single day of outflow data is confirmed, so this is a data point to watch, not an established trend. The honest read: the market flushed leverage and repositioned within the crypto sleeve rather than actually leaving it.

All of that positioning is now pointed at one specific week. The Senate has scheduled a procedural vote on the Digital Asset Market Clarity Act for September 15th. That is the market-structure bill dividing SEC and CFTC jurisdiction over digital assets, and it needs 60 votes to advance. The date is one day before the FOMC decision. This show has reported that bill as stalled, with no scheduled floor vote, for weeks. It now has a date, and that date sits directly ahead of the rate decision, compressing two major catalysts into consecutive sessions. Three fights remain unresolved: who enforces ethics rules, how far developer liability protections extend, and whether stablecoin issuers can route yield to holders. A scheduled vote is not votes in hand, though. Leadership conceded before the August recess that the 60 were not there, and scheduling this kind of test can be a way to force positions onto the record rather than a genuine attempt to pass something. The vote has not happened, and the outcome is genuinely unknown.

The third of those fights is the one that matters most to capital markets. If stablecoin rewards survive in the final text, a dollar token stops being purely a settlement instrument and becomes a yield product. That puts it in direct competition with savings accounts and money-market funds, which pressures bank deposit funding costs at exactly the moment the front end is repricing higher. That is the transmission channel from a crypto bill to bank net interest margin. Failure, meanwhile, does not just delay clarity. It likely pushes market-structure legislation past the point where it can clear before the midterm calendar takes over the floor.

One closing thread worth naming, because it deflates some of the drama. Whichever way that vote goes, the SEC is not waiting on Congress. On August 18th it formally proposed Regulation Crypto Assets, creating a startup exemption for raises up to 5 million dollars over four years, a fundraising exemption up to 75 million dollars per year, and a conditional safe harbor from investment-contract status for issuers who complete the managerial work they promised. That is a proposal, not a final rule, with a comment period and no set timeline, and a statute could still overwrite it. But it means the offering economics for token issuers are already being settled by rulemaking, in parallel with whatever the Senate does. The one thing rulemaking cannot touch is the stablecoin-yield question. That belongs to Congress alone, and it is the real reason to watch September 15th.
