Cautious Optimism Briefings

Space & the Orbital Economy

Transcript · August 13, 2026

Good morning, it's August 13th, and this is your space and orbital economy briefing.

This week's second-quarter earnings season keeps confirming the same pattern. Growth in this sector is real, but it's coming from everywhere except the core launch vehicle. Today's clearest example is Firefly Aerospace, and it arrives with a backlog number that deserves a lot more scrutiny than the headline gives it.

Firefly posted record second-quarter revenue of $117.7 million, up 659% year over year — the first time it has ever cleared $100 million in a single quarter. The company points to a $1.5 billion backlog as proof of demand still to come. But Firefly's own 10-Q, filed with the SEC, discloses a different and much smaller number: remaining performance obligations of $563.7 million as of June 30th. That's the GAAP-defined measure of contracted future revenue under standard revenue-recognition rules. The marketed $1.5 billion figure is close to three times that. Now, backlog and remaining performance obligations are not the same metric, and companies are allowed to define backlog however they like. But a gap that wide means the number Firefly is selling to investors as its demand signal is not the number sitting in its own financial statements. Treat the $1.5 billion figure as a marketing claim, not a contracted-revenue fact — and treat every backlog number across this earnings cohort with the same caution, because loose backlog definitions are the rule in this sector, not the exception.

Meanwhile the losses are real, and these come straight from the filing. Net loss for the quarter was $92.3 million, widened from $63.8 million a year ago. Free cash flow was an outflow of $106.3 million, wider than the $78.9 million outflow in the first quarter. So the burn is accelerating even as revenue grows. Set that against a cash-and-short-term-investments cushion of roughly $635 million, and you're looking at a runway measured in a handful of quarters, not years, if the trend holds.

And the growth itself is not coming from Firefly's core product. Alpha, its small launch vehicle, is the reason Firefly exists as a launch company — and Alpha keeps slipping. The redesigned Alpha Block 2, forced by an in-flight anomaly on last year's Flight 6, has now been pushed to the fourth quarter of 2026, with the next two launches both targeted for that same window. Instead, this quarter's revenue leaned on the Blue Ghost lunar lander program and on a $93.7 million Space Force contract awarded to Firefly's SciTec subsidiary for ground-based radar digitization. The filings don't break out the exact revenue mix, so don't over-index on precise segment splits. But the direction is unmistakable: the growth is lunar and defense-services revenue, not launch-cadence revenue.

That's worth a reframe. Firefly increasingly looks like a diversified space-services company, not a small-launch pure play — which changes who it competes with. Its comps aren't Electron-class launchers anymore; they're the defense-services contractors and lunar-payload providers, businesses that trade on program execution rather than cost per kilogram. And it changes the strategic risk, too. Every quarter Alpha slips, the U.S. small-launch market outside SpaceX rideshare stays underserved, and non-U.S. customers who need a dedicated small ride keep drifting toward Chinese commercial launchers like LandSpace and Galactic Energy. The takeaway: record revenue and a big backlog look great in a headline, but the vehicle that was supposed to be the moat isn't flying, and the backlog itself is a third of what the marketing implies.

The same order-book-versus-operational gap shows up in direct-to-cell. AST SpaceMobile launched three more BlueBird satellites on August 5th, bringing its constellation to 13 in orbit, with roughly thirty additional satellites in various stages of production. That's genuine deployed hardware. But second-quarter revenue was just $31.5 million — up from $15.8 million in the first quarter, yet still a rounding error against the company's roughly $1.3 billion backlog and more than $3.7 billion in pro forma cash. Do the arithmetic on the guidance. First-half revenue came in around $47 million. Full-year guidance is $150 to $200 million. That implies a second half at least twice the size of the first — a ramp the company has guided to but not yet demonstrated. And the promised beta commercial service is still company guidance, not a proven, repeatable service.

Now, AST does have a real commercial foundation: more than 60 mobile-operator partnerships covering over 3 billion subscribers. And a $3.7 billion cash cushion buys plenty of runway through delays. But that's the subtle trap. A large balance sheet proves the company is well funded, not that its unit economics are near an inflection. Deployed satellites and a fat cash pile are inputs; recognized, recurring service revenue is the output, and the output is still tiny. If beta service slips again, the carriers in those partnerships have optionality — Starlink's own direct-to-cell offering with T-Mobile is waiting in the wings, and a mobile operator can quietly reallocate its commercial priority while AST keeps building.

Now the other side of that trade. The one place hardware keeps reaching orbit on schedule is China. A Long March rocket launched Guowang's eighth satellite batch today — the fourth Guowang launch in under three weeks. And it's worth noting what didn't derail that cadence: the Long March 7A failure we flagged earlier this week, which destroyed a geostationary communications satellite, did nothing to slow the separate low-Earth-orbit constellation stream. Roughly 190 Guowang satellites and 162 Qianfan satellites are now in orbit.

Keep that in proportion, though. The two constellations together are planned to exceed 28,000 satellites, so what's flown so far is still a rounding error against the target — and it isn't established whether any of those satellites are providing commercial service yet, versus still being commissioned. This is a capacity-building phase, not a deployed service comparable to Starlink's operational scale. Two second-order effects are worth banking. First, a compressed cadence — an estimated 70-plus megaconstellation launches this year — strains China's own Long March supply chain, so watch for pad bottlenecks and launch-rate slips on Beijing's side. Second, packing orbital shells with hundreds of satellites raises collision and debris risk in shared low-Earth orbit, which turns any future close approach with a Starlink or Kuiper satellite into a diplomatic incident, not just an engineering one. Still, the discipline of just showing up and launching, week after week, is itself the story — while Western small-launch entrants slip their vehicles, China keeps minting flown hardware.

Pulling the threads together. Across this whole sector right now, value is accruing to whoever can actually fly and recognize revenue, not to whoever has the biggest order book — and even the order-book numbers deserve a second look before you trust them, because a marketed backlog and a GAAP performance obligation can differ by a factor of three. Watch three things from here: whether Firefly's Alpha Block 2 actually debuts in the fourth quarter, whether AST's beta commercial service materializes this year, and whether either company converts its backlog into recognized, repeatable revenue rather than a marketing figure.
