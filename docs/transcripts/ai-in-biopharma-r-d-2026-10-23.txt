Good morning, it's Friday, October 23rd, 2026, and this is your briefing on AI in biopharma R&D.

The deals keep coming, and this week one of them actually paid out. But the amount is undisclosed, and the evidence behind the next round of deals keeps arriving as ratios with no denominator. The cash is real. The scorecard isn't.

Start with the clearest case of money moving. On Monday, this show argued that Recursion's option payment from Sanofi was what a platform deal actually transfers in cash, years into the relationship: a steady drip. On Tuesday, Insilico Medicine delivered the next drip. Insilico announced it had hit the first discovery-stage milestone in its oncology collaboration with the French drugmaker Servier. That milestone triggers a payment from Servier. According to the companies' joint release, the deal carries a total value of up to 888 million dollars. Only 32 million of that is upfront and near-term R&D payments. So the headline is nearly twenty-eight times the committed cash.

Neither company disclosed the size of the milestone payment itself. That silence is worth sitting with. Companies that land a large number tend to say so. The more likely read is that this payment is small relative to the 888-million headline. That's an inference, not a fact the release gives us. Either way, both programs have only completed target nomination, and they remain in discovery. So this milestone tells you the collaboration is executing on schedule. It does not tell you the AI chose better biology than a conventional process would have. Nothing has reached a stage where that's testable yet.

The division of labor matters just as much as the dollars. Insilico leads the AI-driven discovery and early development work. Servier shares R&D expenses, and it takes over clinical validation and commercialization. In other words, the AI company is selling an option on the earliest and cheapest stage of the pipeline. The pharma partner keeps the clinical risk, and with it most of the back-end economics. For anyone holding AI-discovery equities or private marks, the implication is uncomfortable. Platform value now accrues through a slow milestone stream, and much of it is undisclosed. Without amounts, an outside investor cannot tell whether platform revenue is compounding or stalling. That argues for a valuation discount on any platform that won't disclose its milestone cash. Model the cadence and the disclosure, not the headline total.

The same option structure is now reaching a third layer of the stack. On Monday, Eli Lilly signed a deal with METiS TechBio to apply AI to drug delivery rather than to targets or molecules. METiS is listed on the Hong Kong exchange, with research operations in mainland China. Trade coverage reports an upfront payment in the low double-digit millions of dollars. On top of that sit milestones of up to roughly 1.1 billion dollars. METiS leads lipid nanoparticle discovery and optimization, using a generative AI platform. The stated goal is nanoparticles with better tissue specificity, beyond the liver, where most of today's lipid nanoparticles end up by default. No specific tissue, disease, or Lilly program has been named. This is a capability bet, not a disclosed candidate.

A caveat on that number. No primary Lilly or METiS release or filing has surfaced for these terms. Three independent trade outlets converge on the 1.1 billion figure, which raises confidence, but treat it as secondary-sourced. For context, AstraZeneca struck a comparable structure with insitro earlier this month: small upfront, large contingent tail. Put the three together and the pattern is plain. Pharma is buying AI options across more of the stack, from target biology to molecule design and now to delivery. And every one is priced the same way. The committed cash is modest. The big number only shows up if everything downstream works.

So that's what pharma is paying. Now turn to what it's actually been shown.

Two weeks ago, GSK signed a collaboration with Chai Discovery after running its own wet-lab tests of Chai's generative design models. That detail matters. GSK tested the designs itself, rather than taking Chai's word for it. That's a meaningfully higher bar than the self-reported benchmarks this show criticized earlier this week. Chai's models generated designs against GSK's targets zero-shot, without any target-specific training. GSK reported finding binders to all the targets it tested. GSK's R&D technologies executive said the panel was diverse, and the designs showed strong binding affinities.

That's a real result. But look at what's missing. Neither company disclosed how many targets were in that panel, how many designs were screened, or what the hit rate was. "Binders to all tested targets" is a hundred percent ratio with no denominator attached. A panel of three would technically qualify. Yesterday, this show covered a binder campaign that published its hits and its failures together, numerator and denominator. Chai and GSK have not done that. And binding affinity in a test tube is not developability. It says nothing yet about pharmacokinetics or immunogenicity. GSK's own framing is that Chai's models complement its internal platform. That points toward a hybrid build-and-buy approach, not pharma handing discovery to an outside vendor.

There's a conditional second-order effect here worth naming. If pharma-validated, zero-shot binder design becomes routine, early antibody discovery is the part of the stack most exposed to commoditization. That would pressure the discovery-services CROs whose business is generating and screening those early hits. But that only follows if the hit rates hold up at scale. And today nobody has disclosed the hit rates.

The newest entrant makes the denominator problem even plainer. Reflector Bio emerged from stealth yesterday with a model that predicts a compound's effect on cells, rather than its binding affinity. That's a phenotype-first approach, distinct from the structure-first models behind most of this month's deals. The company says it virtually screened 1.9 million compounds and sent just five to lab testing. Four showed cell-line-specific effects at high potency. The company frames that as an eighty percent hit rate.

Say the raw numbers out loud and the framing changes: four out of five. That's an anecdote, not a rate. And the five compounds were chosen by the very model being evaluated. The real claim is the funnel from 1.9 million candidates down to five, and that step can't be checked from the disclosure. This is in-vitro only, with no animal or clinical data. And it arrives in a stealth-launch press release, with no funding disclosed.

So pull the threads together. The field is disclosing more numerators this month: a triggered milestone, a hundred percent binder claim, an eighty percent hit claim. Almost none come with denominators. That's not progress toward verifiability. It's the same problem showing up more often. And it leaves the one-hundred-percent claim and the four-of-five claim impossible to compare, with each other or with conventional discovery.

The second-order read on spend is about where the money sits, not how much there is. Discovery-stage AI capital keeps moving from pharma's internal budgets into external option contracts, one layer of the stack at a time. But none of this week's news touches the stage where R&D productivity actually gets decided. That's efficacy attrition from Phase 2 onward. The biggest open question is whether any of these upstream options ever shows up in Phase 2 success rates. Nothing here tests that yet, because nothing here has reached the clinic.

One honest note: there was no fresh development on AI and real-world data that met the bar for this show this cycle, so we're not padding with something stale.

Three things to watch. First, whether Insilico or Servier ever discloses the size of that triggered milestone. Second, whether the Lilly-METiS terms surface in a primary filing rather than trade coverage alone. And third, whether Chai, or any partner, publishes a hit rate with an actual denominator attached.

That's the briefing. Have a good Friday.
