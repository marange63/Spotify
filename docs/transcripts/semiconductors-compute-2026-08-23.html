Good morning, it's Sunday, August 23rd, 2026, and this is your semiconductor and compute briefing.

Most of this week's airtime went to how the AI buildout gets financed and how memory scarcity gets priced. Today the evidence points somewhere else. It's about where the capacity is physically going, and whether the version of it that politics demanded can actually earn a return.

Start in Arizona. A Bank of America note, reported on August 19th, puts TSMC's Arizona fab at forty-five billion Taiwan dollars of second-quarter revenue. That's about one point four billion US dollars, and it's up a hundred and forty-five percent from a year earlier. Call it four percent of TSMC's total sales. The number that drew attention, though, is the margin. Excluding grants, BofA estimates Arizona earned a thirty-eight percent net margin in the quarter, against deeply negative margins through 2025. Now the caveat, and it matters. TSMC does not break Arizona out in its own disclosures. Every one of those figures is a sell-side estimate relayed through secondary outlets, not a company-confirmed number.

So weigh it against what TSMC itself has said. On its July earnings call, the company put group gross margin at sixty-seven point seven percent, up a hundred and fifty basis points sequentially. Be careful with that comparison. Sixty-seven point seven is a gross margin for the whole group. Thirty-eight is an estimated net margin for one fab. Those are different lines on the income statement, and they are not apples to apples. But the scale of the gap still tells you something, and TSMC gave us a second, cleaner check. Its standing guidance is that overseas fabs, meaning Arizona, Japan and Germany together, dilute group gross margin by two to three percentage points in the early stages, widening to three to four points later. A company that expected its American fab to be earning Taiwan-level economics would not still be guiding to a blended overseas drag. So what the evidence supports is a large, real improvement off a negative base. It does not support the claim that Arizona has closed the gap with home-market profitability.

There are two mechanisms that could make thirty-eight percent more flattering than real, and neither one is quantified anywhere. The first is transfer pricing. Arizona sells wafers into its own parent, so its revenue and its margin are partly an internal accounting choice rather than an arm's-length market outcome, and neither BofA nor anyone covering the note discloses the basis. The second is that phrase, excluding grants. Nobody has published how much CHIPS Act money, investment tax credit, or depreciation treatment was stripped out to get there. BofA's own explanation is, in its words, better yields and a pricing strategy that neutralizes the dilution usually associated with overseas fabs. That is a mechanism described in general terms, not a disclosure. What would settle it is a TSMC segment disclosure for its American operations, and TSMC has never given one.

Here's the so-what, and it is still a real inflection. The board has approved roughly twenty-nine billion dollars of additional US spending, and BofA now models group capex of eighty to eighty-five billion dollars in 2027, a sell-side projection rather than this show's forecast. If a reshored leading-edge fab can earn a positive return without permanent subsidy, the industrial-policy question stops being whether the subsidy was worth it and becomes how fast the footprint can scale. Follow that one step further, because this is where it bites. If US capacity no longer carries an obvious margin penalty, the customer stops paying a resilience premium to diversify away from Taiwan. And the case for diversifying stops depending on subsidy renewal, which matters, because subsidies are politically renewable and margins are not. It also lands squarely on Samsung Foundry and Intel Foundry, who now have to match footprint commitments to hold hyperscaler customers. Just don't round much improved up to parity. That distinction is the whole story.

If a new geography of capacity is being built and it's starting to pay, the companies selling the tools see it before anyone else. We covered Lam Research's and Applied Materials' headline beats on Friday, so here are only the parts we haven't used. Applied Materials' China revenue share is reported at twenty-seven percent, down from thirty-five percent a year earlier. That is eight points of revenue mix gone. And the company still guided its fiscal fourth quarter to about ten point three billion dollars, against consensus near nine point six billion. It raised its 2026 equipment growth outlook above thirty percent. And the stock sold off anyway.

That selloff is a valuation statement, not a fundamentals statement. Expectations were priced for near-perfection. The fundamentals point underneath it is more interesting. Losing eight points of China mix while still beating and raising means AI and advanced-node demand is growing faster than the export-controlled China business is shrinking. That is a stronger claim than the controls being costless, and a more fragile one, because it depends entirely on the AI leg holding. Read it across the group and the same should be true at KLA, Tokyo Electron and ASML. But notice what that decoupling costs them. An equipment sector with no meaningful China mature-node ballast is a pure levered bet on AI capex, with nothing cyclical left underneath to cushion one disappointing hyperscaler capex guide.

Lam's own print carries a correction we owe you. Its June-quarter gross margin came in at fifty-two percent, above the top end of its own guidance, with NAND revenue more than doubling sequentially as the industry converts to 256-layer-and-above devices. On the call, Lam's management described an extraordinary setup for 2027, citing eight, nine, ten new fabs coming online between now and the end of next year. On Friday we told you that a 2026 equipment upgrade relieves a 2028 shortage. Lam's own count says a meaningful slice of that relief lands in 2027, a year earlier than we said. That matters beyond the correction, because it dates something. The foundry and memory pricing power this show has documented all week now has a visible expiration, and Lam just pulled it forward into the 2027-to-2028 window. Capacity arriving against demand that was priced two years earlier is the classic mechanism by which semiconductor up-cycles end. Hold the qualifier, though. Fabs coming online is announced capacity, not qualified capacity, and qualified capacity is still not wafers shipping.

Which is exactly the limit the next data point puts on this whole bullish read. Samsung Foundry's utilization has reportedly climbed to somewhere between seventy and eighty percent, with a path to full capacity by year-end, driven by 2-nanometer accelerator work and high-bandwidth-memory base-die demand. That tells you the order book is real. It does not tell you the wafers are good. Utilization is an input measure, not an output measure, and the yield data you would need to close that loop isn't fresh or reliable enough to put a number on here. So take that as the honest limit on everything else in this briefing. Announced capacity, booked capacity and shipping capacity are three different things, and only the third one relieves a shortage.

One more correction before we close, and again it's on ourselves. Yesterday we told you that closing the remote-access loophole, the one letting Chinese firms rent restricted Nvidia compute through data centers in Thailand and Malaysia, takes an act of Congress, because the House-passed Remote Access Security Act is stuck in the Senate. Reporting since says the Bureau of Industry and Security could implement a rule addressing remote access, in the words of that reporting, in a matter of days, with White House backing. If that's right, we overstated the legislative bottleneck. But bound the correction. A rule that is reportedly being drafted is not a rule that has been proposed, published, or survived a legal challenge, and nobody has yet quantified how much Chinese AI compute actually flows through this channel. Capital redraws the capacity map slowly. Rules can redraw the access map fast, when they actually land.

So here's where that leaves the picture. The reshored, security-driven version of the chip supply chain is, for the first time, starting to look like it can pay for itself, and the tool vendors and foundries downstream are behaving as though that's true. Nvidia's print on Wednesday is the next test of the demand underneath it. But look hard at what is carrying today's story: an analyst's estimate of a segment TSMC doesn't disclose, a secondary-sourced revenue mix at Applied Materials, and an unconfirmed characterization of a rule that hasn't been proposed. The direction is right. The evidence is softer than the conviction it is producing.
