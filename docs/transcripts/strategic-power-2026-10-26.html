Strategic Power, Energy, and Institutional Change

Good morning, and welcome to Strategic Power for Monday, October 26th, 2026.

The thesis this morning is simple. The oil chokepoint is still the master variable. But the weekend gave us a pause in the rhetoric, not a pause in the physical risk. And underneath that noise, the slower contest is the one that matters: who can finance and physically build the power, the fuel, and the non-Chinese supply chains that the AI buildout now depends on.

Start with the Gulf. On Sunday, President Trump ruled out an immediate strike on Iran. His words, as reported, were that "there will not be a strike today." The market took that as a small relief. WTI fell as low as 101.69 dollars on Sunday night, according to Yahoo Finance. It was hovering around 101 dollars in Asian hours Monday, per FXStreet. That follows a gain of more than 4 percent on Friday.

So the accurate description is a modest dip after a sharp rally. It is not a collapse in the risk premium. Brent, the global benchmark, still sat near 116 dollars on Sunday night, according to Trading Economics. That is roughly where it was at last week's high.

The anchor that matters is the pre-war level. WTI settled at 67.02 dollars on February 27th, a day before the Iran war began. So crude is still roughly 50 percent above where it started. One sentence from the president does not close that gap.

Here is why the skepticism is warranted. Trump's statement covers one day. The Houthis have vowed to keep hitting Riyadh's airport, and Saudi Arabia reportedly intercepted more missiles on Sunday. The status of the Strait of Hormuz is contested. The Revolutionary Guard claims closure. CENTCOM says the strait is open. So the open question is whether barrels are moving or whether the premium is simply being priced. We do not have a clean answer.

There is also a calendar risk. Washington and Israel have reportedly drafted Iran strike plans for after the November 3rd midterms. That is reporting, not confirmation. But TD Securities is positioned for it. Their view is that crude "will remain skewed to the upside in the near term" on escalation risk ahead of Israeli elections. So a pause before the midterms could be a delay rather than a de-escalation. And if Brent is already near 116, a re-escalation starts from a high base.

The buffer is thinner too. The United States committed to release 172 million barrels from the Strategic Petroleum Reserve over four months, as part of a 400 million barrel collective action by IEA members. To be clear, that was announced in March. It is not new. But it matters now, because that cushion has already been spent. If the Gulf re-escalates after November 3rd, Washington has less emergency stock to throw at the problem.

That brings us to the sanctions trade. On October 16th, Trump waived the Russia sanctions and secondary-tariff law, two days before it was due to bite. Secretary of State Rubio called it a 60-day window. In his words, the president wants to take that period "to sort of analyse" the impact. Rubio also said the Iran war and the Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia were "starting to crash into each other." Senator Tillis called the waiver "beyond irresponsible."

This is the real signal. Washington is trading coercion of Russia for lower pump prices. The administration cannot sustain maximum pressure on Russia and Iran at the same time when oil is above 100 dollars. The energy constraint is setting the sanctions policy, not the other way around. And that is a credibility cost, both for the sanctions regime and for the Congress that passed the law.

The second-order effect runs to defense. Continued missile fire at Riyadh sustains Gulf demand for air defense. That is a tailwind for the defense primes, and it does not depend on the oil price at all.

Now the handoff to the macro layer. If oil stays near these levels, headline inflation stays high into Wednesday's Fed decision. The market sees the Fed as almost certain to hold rates in the 3.75 to 4 percent range. I can only give you the setup, because the decision has not happened. But the setup is awkward. Oil-driven inflation makes the case for easier policy harder to argue, just as the AI trade is wobbling.

That wobble is the second story. The 10-year Treasury yield sank below 5 percent on October 22nd, for the first time since September 22nd. The 30-year tumbled to below 5.32 percent, the lowest since September 30th. Those moves came during a global tech selloff driven by worries about AI funding.

Here is the number I find most telling. Gold traded near 4,011 dollars on Monday after finding bids below 4,000. It has continued to weaken even as Treasury yields fell sharply. That is a three-month low for the metal.

Think about what that says. Lower yields should have helped gold. They did not. So the market is behaving as if the problem is the cost and availability of capital, not the integrity of the currency. This is a funding squeeze, and in a funding squeeze, hard assets get sold alongside equities. For those holding the hard-money thesis, that is a useful stress test. A day or two of selling is not a regime change. But it shows the haven case fails when the fear is about capital, not about money.

The so-what for AI is this. Financing cost is now a binding constraint alongside power. At a 10-year near 5 percent, and with oil above 100 dollars, funding the scale of the buildout is a test of balance sheets.

That sorts the winners. Hyperscalers with strong balance sheets, and developers who can build power behind the meter, are better placed. Levered neoclouds that depend on capital markets are more exposed. The AI race is increasingly sorted by balance sheet, not just by chip access.

Which brings us to the base layer, and the question of what gets built. Nuclear is the cleanest test of announcement versus capacity. The Nuclear Regulatory Commission finalized a rule on October 22nd that lets advanced reactor developers reuse federal demonstration data. The final rule is scheduled to take effect 30 days after publication in the Federal Register.

That is genuine progress on licensing. But it is a paperwork stage. It is not a reactor, and it is not a megawatt. Licensing milestones tend to move equities. They do not move output.

The real bottleneck in nuclear is fuel, specifically enrichment, given the dependence on Russia. That is where the sanctions waiver bites. If Washington is loosening pressure on Russia, the urgency premium for standing up non-Russian enrichment gets weaker. Political will fades when pump prices dominate. So the fuel chain is a decade-scale project, and it answers nothing about the power and inflation squeeze of 2026 and 2027.

One last item, and it is the quiet one. TSMC is reportedly seeking chipmaking equipment that does not contain Chinese rare-earth magnets. It has asked suppliers for alternatives. I would treat this as a signal rather than a quantified supply fact, because the details are thin.

But the signal is important. The dependence on Chinese rare earths does not stop at the mineral. It sits inside the motors of the fab tools themselves. Rare earths, as one analyst put it, are the most obvious example of an unfinished trade deal. The US-China truce now expires January 10th, 2027, instead of November 10th. That buys time, and it creates another cliff in the first quarter.

So here is the through-line. Oil can ease on a sentence, but the premium and the base are still high. Capital costs are now shaping who can build. And the slower supply chains, from enrichment to magnets, are still years from reducing the dependence.

The watch items this week are Wednesday's Fed decision, the weekly oil flow data, and whether Trump's pause holds past the midterms.
