Good morning. It's October 29th, 2026, and this is your space and orbital economy briefing.

This week the two launch catalysts we've been tracking finally split apart. SpaceX's Starship hardware just got fire-tested for a landmark recovery attempt. ULA's Vulcan comeback flight slipped again, and the reason is something we hadn't reported before. The lesson across both is that certification is not the same as reliability. And reliability, not ambition, is now the binding constraint on who gets to orbit at scale.

Start with United Launch Alliance. On October 28th, ULA said it plans to fly Vulcan again on Friday, November 13th. The rocket would carry 40 satellites for Amazon Leo, the constellation formerly called Kuiper, from Cape Canaveral, pending approval from range authorities. We'd flagged this flight as the near-term catalyst; it has now slipped from October 29th to November 13th. And here's the new piece. Vulcan has been sidelined since February, after an anomaly during a Space Force mission. ULA says the vehicle will fly a redesigned solid rocket booster nozzle, meant to correct the issue behind that failure. So a rocket already trusted with Space Force missions sat out nearly nine months over one hardware fault. That redesigned nozzle is ULA's claim, and it stays unproven until it flies.

That's not damning on its own. Catching a defect, redesigning, and retesting is the system working as intended. The bullish read is that November 13th is where the fix starts being proven. And because the Space Force leans on Vulcan alongside SpaceX for national-security launch, a clean flight is a defense launch-assurance story as much as a commercial one. The real exposure is concentration. Vulcan holds the largest share of Amazon Leo's remaining launch contracts, and no Amazon Leo satellites have flown at all since July 2nd. The consequence landed on Amazon's regulatory desk, not ULA's. Amazon missed the FCC's July 30th deployment deadline, and a conditional waiver is what keeps its license in force. One supplier's nozzle problem became a different company's compliance problem.

Now the scale of the hole. The tracker KeepTrack counts about 392 Amazon Leo satellites in orbit, against a target of about sixteen hundred. A clean 40-satellite flight on November 13th would lift that count by roughly a tenth. But it would close only about three percent of the remaining gap. Every month Vulcan stays down pushes back the point at which Amazon's constellation is serviceable. That, in turn, delays the point at which each satellite starts earning revenue. So the test on the 13th isn't whether Vulcan can fly once. It's whether it can fly repeatedly, because cadence, not a single return, is what moves Amazon Leo's economics.

Amazon isn't the only constellation renting its schedule from someone else's rocket. AST SpaceMobile told Congress it would launch between 45 and 60 second-generation BlueBird satellites during 2026. As of its August launch, it had 13 in orbit. That's the cleanest announced-versus-deployed gap in the sector right now. Meanwhile the partnership column keeps growing. On October 28th, AST secured approval for 30 megahertz of S-band spectrum in Brazil, with no commercial launch date attached. That's the latest in a run of carrier and regulatory wins we've tracked over the past week and a half. Spectrum rights are optionality, not revenue. Each quarter of delay extends AST's cash burn against a subscription base that hasn't arrived yet. For an operator without its own rocket, schedule risk is really supplier-reliability risk. Until AST's satellite count moves, that's the number that matters.

Now turn to SpaceX, the provider on the other side of this divide, and the vehicle meant to give it spare launch cadence. On October 24th we called Starship Flight 15 the first dated test of Starship's reuse economics. Back then it rested on Elon Musk's word alone. This week it became hardware. Booster 22 completed a full-duration, thirty-three-engine static fire on October 25th. Ship 42 finished its own static-fire campaign on October 28th, including a single-engine burn to simulate a deorbit. SpaceX president Gwynne Shotwell now expects the flight's license to cover a return to launch site and a tower catch for both stages. That's her expectation, not an issued FAA license. A catch of both stages would be the clearest evidence yet that Starship's reusability economics can start to approach Falcon 9's.

But be precise about what a catch proves. It demonstrates recoverability, not turnaround cost. Nothing in hand quantifies refurbishment time or achievable reflight cadence. So cost per kilogram on Starship stays unproven, even if the catch succeeds. And there's still no official launch date. November is Musk's target, not a scheduled mission. Our read is that the first beneficiary of a successful catch is SpaceX's own Starlink deployment cost, not any third-party launch customer's price.

Put the two stories side by side, and here's our inference. The one Western provider with spare cadence is about to attempt full reuse, in the same week the alternative ride slipped again. That suggests SpaceX's lead over the alternatives is widening in rate, not just in level. A clean Vulcan flight on November 13th, followed quickly by a second, would partially rebut that read.

That widening lead belongs to a company whose center of gravity is shifting, and this week that shift got a financing structure. According to Apollo's press release, SpaceX has reached a binding commitment to enter a roughly 40 billion dollar master finance lease for GPUs, led by Apollo and Goldman Sachs. Entry still depends on specified closing conditions. Closing is targeted before year-end, with lease payments starting as equipment arrives in the first half of 2027. Here's why it belongs in a space briefing. On its earnings call, SpaceX reported 7.6 billion dollars of quarterly revenue from its AI compute segment. That's now larger than Starlink's 5.2 billion. The dominant launch provider in the space economy is starting to look like a compute company with a launch arm attached.

Our read on what the lease does is this. Because outside lenders front the GPU hardware, SpaceX's own cash stays available for Starship and Starlink rather than being consumed by chips. The price is a fixed payment stream that starts in 2027, whether or not compute demand holds up. For SPCX holders, that sharpens the Starlink spin-out question. If compute keeps outgrowing Starlink, some investors will want the broadband business priced on its own, while others will see less reason to separate it. Either way, anyone using SpaceX as a proxy for the space economy is now buying a large AI-infrastructure bet alongside it.

So here's where this actually inflects, versus where it's still a promise. What's inflecting: Starship's hardware is now fire-tested for a milestone attempt, and SpaceX's compute financing has gone from rumor to a named, structured commitment. What's still a promise: Vulcan's fix is unflown, Amazon Leo's deployment rate is hostage to one supplier, and AST's last reported count is still 13 even as its partnership list grows.

One more thread to hold onto underneath all of it. Every one of these fixes adds hardware to an orbit that's already under strain. The European Space Agency's latest environment report, covering data through the end of 2025, counts roughly ten new payloads launched every day. Against that, just over three intact objects re-enter. That's a structural backdrop, not this week's news, but it compounds with every successful deployment. Fixing launch reliability accelerates traffic in orbit faster than the debris problem is being solved, and that's a cost every low-orbit operator eventually shares.

Watch three catalysts. November 13th, for whether Vulcan actually flies and Amazon Leo's cadence resumes. Sometime in November, for a Starship Flight 15 license and launch date. And AST's Falcon 9 BlueBird launch, reported for November, which will show whether that satellite count finally starts catching up to the partnership column.
