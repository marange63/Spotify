Good morning, this is your semiconductors and compute briefing for September 22nd, 2026.

For two years this industry has kept moving the blame for the AI accelerator shortage. First leading-edge lithography was supposed to be the bottleneck. Then high-bandwidth memory. Then the throughput of the machines that build the machines. This week the constraint everybody has been chasing finally got a number attached to it, and the number is packaging.

On Monday, Taiwan broke ground on a new advanced-packaging industrial park in Kaohsiung's Baipu district. It covers about 88.7 hectares. TSMC is the anchor, and what it plans to build there is a technology-validation lab and a talent-training center. Notice what that is not: a fab. Taiwan's government framed the site around speed. Co-locating equipment and materials suppliers on one campus is meant to shorten the time needed to bring new packaging technology into mass production. That is the tell. If the binding constraint were wafer capacity, you would build wafer capacity. Building a validation and training campus instead says the gate is qualification time — how fast a new packaging process can be proven reliable at volume — rather than how many tools you can bolt to a floor.

The capacity path behind it is getting more specific. One caveat first, and it applies to every capacity figure in this briefing. These come from TrendForce's supply-chain checks, not from TSMC guidance. Those checks have TSMC's CoWoS packaging capacity roughly doubling. That is from about 130,000 wafers a month at the end of this year to 260,000 by the end of 2028. And no capex figure for the Kaohsiung park has been disclosed by anyone. That absence is the pattern this beat keeps hitting: an announcement that is directionally real and numerically thin.

Here is the part that lets you score whether any of this eases the shortage now. A separate TrendForce read from June put the CoWoS supply-and-demand gap at roughly 20 percent at the time, narrowing to about 10 percent by the end of this year as capacity comes online. So the near-term relief was already underway before Monday's groundbreaking. It is riding capacity that is already being installed. The Kaohsiung facilities target the fourth quarter of 2029 for operation. That is after the accelerator generations this park is supposedly meant to unlock. The honest read: packaging finally has a number, but the number that helps this cycle is not the Kaohsiung one.

There is a jurisdiction question underneath all of this. TSMC has put a date on advanced packaging outside Taiwan. Kevin Zhang, the company's deputy co-chief operating officer, said TSMC plans to have CoWoS and 3D-IC capability on the ground in Arizona before 2029. That is a named executive with a date. But no wafer volume is attached to it anywhere. So Taiwan has a number. Arizona has a date and nothing else. Until somebody attaches a monthly wafer figure to Arizona, treat packaging concentration in Taiwan as the working assumption and treat diversification as a promise.

Now, if packaging capacity is the thing being doubled on a public calendar, the next question is where the margin in this cycle lands. Increasingly it lands in memory. SK hynix led high-bandwidth memory revenue share in the second quarter at 62 percent. Micron came second at 21, overtaking Samsung, which fell to 17. The total is not the story. Who moved is the story.

The product race behind those shares is HBM4. SK hynix says it has completed HBM4 development, claiming a 40 percent power-efficiency improvement, and positions itself as the qualified supplier for Nvidia's Vera Rubin platform. Micron has begun shipping its own HBM4 samples. And here is the harder fact: Micron says it has completed price and volume agreements for its entire calendar-2026 high-bandwidth memory supply, HBM4 included. Micron also forecasts that market growing from about 35 billion dollars in 2025 to roughly 100 billion by 2028. Treat that last one as a supplier's forecast of its own market, not an independent estimate.

Two cautions before you extrapolate Micron's share gain into a durable win. First, shipping samples to a customer is not the same as being qualified for volume production. Samsung's HBM4 recovery story still rests on the first gate, not the second. Second, in a sold-out market, share reflects who locked in contracts and when. It does not reflect who out-competed whom on the merits.

There is a real stake attached to that for Samsung specifically. Samsung Foundry has been pitching itself as a credible manufacturing option for AI accelerators partly on the strength of Samsung's memory franchise. If HBM4 qualification keeps slipping behind SK hynix and Micron, that cross-subsidy argument loses its proof point.

So packaging is being built out, and memory is largely spoken for through next year. Everyone trying to build into that stack is also trying to buy around Nvidia. The clearest example this month is Qualcomm and Amazon. They signed a multi-generation custom-chip agreement on September 8th to co-develop custom AI inference silicon and high-bandwidth optical interconnects for Amazon's data centers. That is two weeks old — a standing structure worth re-reading, not breaking news.

The structure is the story. Qualcomm issued Amazon warrants for 25 million shares at 161 dollars and 26 cents apiece. They vest only as Amazon actually places purchase orders. At that strike, the warrants cover roughly four billion dollars of Qualcomm stock. I want to flag one number carefully. Reporting on the deal attaches a 60 billion dollar figure to the arrangement, but 25 million shares at 161 dollars does not get you to 60 billion. Whatever that larger number refers to, it is not the value of the warrants, and I have not seen it pinned down. Ignore it and look at the mechanism instead. The mechanism is the counterparty pricing its own uncertainty into the deal, rather than Qualcomm printing a headline number. Qualcomm has separately raised its fiscal-2029 non-handset revenue target to around 40 billion dollars. What would convert this from announcement to evidence is narrow and checkable: a disclosed vesting tranche, or a named production order. Neither exists today.

Note the second-order effect. This deal fragments the hyperscaler custom-silicon field further, beyond Broadcom and Marvell. That pressures pricing on future ASIC contracts. But every new entrant bids into the same pool of packaging slots and the same contracted memory supply. More suppliers does not mean more supply.

Last, the geopolitical bill. The durable story on the equipment side is China, and it is now visible in disclosed revenue mix rather than in warnings. ASML booked 11.06 billion dollars of China revenue in 2025, roughly 29 percent of its total, and management has flagged a significant expected decline this year. Lam Research's China revenue share fell to 35 percent last quarter, from a peak of 43 percent the quarter before. That is export-control policy landing in the geographic mix, and it runs independently of the AI cycle. Equipment multiples are now being set by two uncorrelated variables at once.

Hold that separate from the share prices. Applied Materials and Lam Research each fell about 6 percent in mid-September, and ASML about 5 percent, on reporting that commentary about a slower pace of AI model development could soften fab-tool order books. No named executive stands behind that characterization. And it sits against Applied Materials posting record third-quarter revenue of 9.12 billion dollars, up 25 percent. That is a sentiment move against record fundamentals, not confirmed evidence of decelerating fab spend.

Put it together. The industry finally has a quantified supply answer to the packaging bottleneck. It is being built inside the one jurisdiction everyone else is trying to diversify away from. And the margin from this cycle keeps accruing to the memory makers rather than to the accelerator vendors footing the bill. Two dated tests from here. Whether TSMC ever attaches a capex figure, or a company-disclosed CoWoS number, to any of this. And Micron's fiscal fourth-quarter earnings call on September 30th, the next real read on memory pricing and supply.

That's the briefing. Talk to you next time.
