Good morning. It's Thursday, November 26th, 2026, and this is your briefing on AI in biopharma R&D.

On Tuesday, this show reported the first consultancy number tying AI to Phase II advancement, built on a thin sample. Today one of the industry's largest sponsors has put its own numbers on AI, and it strikes a more cautious note. Novartis held its Investor Day on November 19th. According to press coverage of the event, Novartis executives said evidence of improved clinical success "will take longer to emerge." The hard numbers Novartis did put on the table sit somewhere else. They sit in trial enrollment and in document writing, not in the probability that a drug works. That's today's spine. The measured value of AI keeps moving downstream, toward operations and paperwork. Upstream, the bottleneck has shifted from designing molecules to validating them.

One caution covers everything Novartis said. An investor day is a selling venue. Every figure here is self-reported, and none comes with a published methodology. We also have the numbers second-hand. A follow-up search found that the Form 6-K Novartis furnished to the SEC for the event carries none of these AI figures. It mentions artificial intelligence only in generic risk-factor language. That fits the pattern we've tracked all week. On the 21st it was pharma interviews. On the 22nd it was a vendor-graded benchmark. On Tuesday it was an unaudited consultancy number. Today it's an investor presentation relayed by the press. Every quantified AI claim this week has come from an interested party.

Start with discovery speed, briefly, because it partly repeats what we told you on the 21st. Novartis now says its discovery-to-candidate cycle time has fallen from three to four years to less than two and a half. Last week, in Reuters coverage, the company's research president gave a different baseline for a similar claim. When a company's own metric drifts within a week, treat it as soft and portfolio-dependent, not as an audited series. Nothing in the coverage separates AI from concurrent portfolio pruning or automation, either.

Now the real heart of the story. Novartis says AI-assisted site selection accelerated enrollment by six months in its V1P program. It also says its enrollment simulations were fifty percent more accurate at predicting timelines than its prior planning process. Keep those two numbers separate. The fifty percent figure measures forecast quality. The six-month figure measures realized speed, in one program. Novartis hasn't said what that six months is measured against. It could be the original plan, a historical norm, or a matched trial. Our follow-up search couldn't find V1P's indication, phase, or site count in any coverage.

Even so, this closes an arc for this show. On the 20th, we called eligibility and enrollment modeling a more defensible near-term lever than discovery-stage claims, though nothing had tested it. On the 21st, we argued the binding constraint was shifting downstream to clinical capacity and enrollment. This is the first sponsor number on that lever.

Here's why it matters more than it looks, and this is our inference. Enrollment time arrives late in development. By then most of the development cost is sunk, and the drug is closer to its patent-protected commercial window. So six months saved there can be worth more than a comparable cut in early discovery. It does nothing to change the odds a molecule succeeds. But it pulls revenue forward on the molecules that do.

The forecast-accuracy number is an economic lever in its own right, and again, that's our read. A sponsor that predicts enrollment well opens fewer surplus sites. It also needs fewer rescue amendments when a trial falls behind. Those are real costs, and better planning cuts them even when the trial runs no faster.

That leads to a second-order effect for contract research organizations, also our inference. Site feasibility and enrollment forecasting have been a traditional CRO value-add. If sponsors run those models themselves, that work faces either insourcing or commoditization. CROs will need to show a genuine data edge, not just headcount.

Third beat: clinical study reports. Novartis says ninety percent of CSR content is now AI-assisted. It says that cut authoring and review time by twenty-five percent. This is the language-model workhorse case made concrete, and it comes from a sponsor rather than a vendor. But be precise about it. Ninety percent assisted is not ninety percent written. The economic number is the twenty-five percent time saving. Novartis didn't name the tool behind it. Our search found no word on whether that saving covers every report or only a pilot. And nothing we found mentions the FDA or EMA weighing in on AI-assisted submission content. This work also happens after a trial has read out. So it saves weeks on a document, not years on a program. It's a cost and time lever, and it touches nothing about whether the drug works.

So pivot on Novartis's own words. Per the coverage, AI-generated findings still need laboratory and clinical validation. And in one executive's words, "A lot of the things that come out just do not translate into even the cell models, let alone into the clinic." That's a buyer, not a vendor, drawing the line. Novartis puts numbers on operations and documents, and it declines to claim a success-rate gain. If design isn't the constraint anymore, who sells the thing that is?

That brings us to GenScript, which convened a London forum yesterday. Its framing is direct. In its words, AI drug discovery "is shifting from a race to design faster to a race to learn faster." Hear that as a vendor's framing. GenScript has a commercial interest in saying it, so it isn't independent confirmation. Still, it lines up with Novartis's warning, and with an arc this show has tracked. On the 20th, Merck's research executive said molecular invention depends on coordinating the wet lab and the dry lab. On the 22nd, Genesis gave away its curated data but kept its model closed. The moat keeps migrating from the model to the evidence.

GenScript puts a number behind its pitch. Its AI drug discovery business doubled year over year, to 37.8 million dollars in revenue in the first half of 2026. That figure is self-reported in a press release, and we couldn't find a segment filing behind it. Doubling off a small base is a growth signal, not proof of scale. Now set it against the multibillion-dollar private rounds still going into design engines. The scale mismatch is the point. The money is still priced on design. The measured value and the stated constraint both sit in evidence generation.

One related signal points the same way. Valo Health and Montai Therapeutics merged on November 11th, with terms undisclosed. Valo pitches the combined company as a discovery platform powered by human evidence. That's the same thesis again, and the deal echoes the Recursion and Exscientia combination. But merging for scale can signal pressure as easily as strength. Without terms, we can't tell which.

The last data point bears on who holds the scarcer asset. At an industry panel yesterday, Insilico Medicine's president said 13 of the company's 35 pipeline candidates are already in human clinical trials. That's self-reported and unaudited. The count also doesn't separate AI-designed molecules from AI-selected targets. Still, it's the volume of clinical exposure that will eventually test whether AI moves success rates. By our read, design-only engines own the least defensible layer. Isomorphic in its Merck deal is the example: it hands designs to pharma, and pharma runs the clinic. Being in the clinic isn't succeeding in it. But it's the precondition for ever finding out.

Pulling the threads together. The biggest open questions are three. Will Novartis, or any sponsor, publish the methodology and baselines behind these numbers? Does a six-month enrollment gain in one program generalize across a portfolio? And does the validation bottleneck start redirecting capital away from pure design plays? On clinical success rates, nothing today moves the needle, by the admission of the company that ran the numbers. The realistic near-term gain is in time and cost per trial and per submission, not in fewer failures. So on R&D spend, our read stays where it's been since last week. Expect reallocation toward clinical operations and evidence generation, not an overall cut. Three things to watch. First, any primary Novartis disclosure behind these figures. Second, GenScript's full-year AI discovery line. Third, whether Isomorphic Labs meets its year-end target for first human dosing, with about five weeks left.

That's the briefing. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
