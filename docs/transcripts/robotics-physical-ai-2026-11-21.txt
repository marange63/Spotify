Good morning. It's Saturday, November 21st, and this is your robotics and physical AI briefing.

All week this desk has asked one question. Who actually carries the risk of building humanoid robots at scale? On Wednesday, the answer was Foxconn-led private money, which carried most of Agility Robotics' public listing. Yesterday, it was the vendor itself. GXO's rental-style letter of intent left the fleet risk with Apptronik. Today we get the most extreme answer yet. An automaker has bought the robot maker outright, and it is now building the robot's parts in-house. The headline is vertical integration. The catch is that integrating the actuator does not integrate the magnet inside it. The China chokepoint doesn't disappear. It moves up one layer.

Start with the ownership event itself. According to Korean business press reporting on Hyundai Glovis's third-quarter filing, the put and call options on Boston Dynamics' remaining shares were exercised and extinguished. That ended SoftBank's 9.65 percent stake. Hyundai Motor Group now owns all of Boston Dynamics. Neither side has disclosed a purchase price, so treat any dollar figure circulating online as unconfirmed. In the same untangling, Hyundai's stake in the Robotics and AI Institute was sold to SoftBank. BusinessKorea frames that as Hyundai clearing out its equity in separate research ventures to focus on commercializing Atlas. So the split is clean. SoftBank keeps a research lab, and Hyundai keeps the robot. Yesterday this desk called Boston Dynamics' chip-fab trial an option on an option. Today the parent has locked down the underlying asset. And this one is a filing-disclosed fact, not a roadmap promise.

Ownership is the easy part. The harder part is the parts. Hyundai Mobis, the group's components arm, says it has completed in-house development of the key actuator components. Those are reducers, motors, and controllers. Korean business press reports that Mobis is slated to start prototype actuator production at its Uiwang research center this month. The next rung is joint verification of performance and reliability with Boston Dynamics. After that comes full-scale supply, which Hyundai targets for 2028. That's four steps: design, prototype, verification, supply. Mobis is only now stepping onto the second. Last Friday, this desk argued from J.P. Morgan's bottleneck list that hands and actuators gate humanoid scale, and that value could accrue to the component layer. Hyundai's answer isn't to buy from an actuator supplier. It's to become one, inside its own group.

That has a second-order effect, and it's our inference. The independent reducer, motor, and actuator vendors that the supplier trade was built around just lost a flagship Western customer. If other integrators follow Hyundai's path, that trade narrows. It narrows toward the one layer Hyundai isn't making, which we'll come to shortly.

First, though, the capacity numbers don't obviously close. Hyundai Motor Group's stated target is annual production capacity for 30,000 Atlas robots by 2028. Note that this is capacity, not output, orders, or deployments. Alongside it, the group plans capacity to make 350,000 actuators a year in the United States. Divide one by the other, and you get roughly eleven or twelve actuators per robot.

Now add the joint count. Korean reporting on Mobis says a single Atlas uses 31 actuators, and that Mobis is expected to supply all of them. That figure comes from industry observers, not a company statement. So treat it as reported rather than confirmed.

On that basis, run the arithmetic. 350,000 actuators at 31 per robot supports only about 11,000 Atlas units a year. That's roughly a third of the robot target. Even if that US capacity were doubled, it would cover about 22,500 robots, still short. No source we found explains where the remaining actuators would be made. Perhaps there's capacity outside the US. Perhaps the robot target is aspirational. Or perhaps the joint count is wrong. Watch whether Hyundai closes that gap, or quietly revises one of the two numbers.

Now the captive-customer question, because it cuts both ways. The bull case is straightforward. Hyundai's own plants give Boston Dynamics guaranteed volume and a real industrial setting to iterate in. There's no sales cycle and no pilot negotiation. The bear case is that internal demand at an internal transfer price is not a market test. Boston Dynamics could hit its deployment numbers without ever proving it can sell Atlas to an outside buyer at a price that covers its costs. Compare that with GXO and Apptronik. There, an outside logistics customer sets the KPIs that gate the rental contract. That's an imperfect signal, but it's a real market one. So when Hyundai's Atlas numbers arrive, they'll be the weaker evidence of the two.

The financials show why that distinction matters. Per the Korea Herald's report on the filing, Boston Dynamics' revenue rose 29 percent over the first nine months of this year, to 136 billion won. Its net loss over the same period was 626.6 billion won. By our arithmetic, that's a loss more than four times revenue. This business is still well short of unit economics. Hyundai is underwriting that burn with an automaker's balance sheet. That's a fundamentally different risk profile from a venture-funded startup burning investor cash on a similar curve.

And that puts pressure on the Western peers, again as our inference. Figure and Apptronik now compete with a rival that has a captive buyer, in-house actuators, and automotive cash flow behind it. The bar they have to clear is no longer just model quality. It's cost of capital and supply security.

But there's one layer of the actuator that Hyundai isn't making. Reducers, motors, and controllers are metal and electronics that Mobis can design and machine itself. The rare-earth magnets inside those motors are a different story. MP Materials' chief executive estimates that each humanoid needs between 8 and 10 pounds of rare-earth magnet content. MP sells magnets, so treat that as an interested party's estimate, an order of magnitude rather than an audited number. Scale it to Hyundai's targeted 30,000 robots a year. By our arithmetic, that's roughly 120 to 150 short tons of magnet a year. None of that appears on Mobis's in-house parts list.

We won't assert where China's broader rare-earth export suspension stands today. Sources this week disagree on whether it lapsed or was extended. But that's not the point that carries the argument. On November 12th, this desk reported that a separate licensing regime was never part of the suspension at all. It covers heavy rare earths like dysprosium and terbium. Those are the dopants that let actuator-grade magnets survive inside a hot, cycling motor. So vertical integration moves Hyundai's China exposure one layer up the stack. It doesn't remove it.

So which reading of today's news holds up? One version is a strategic conviction bet: an automaker staking its balance sheet that embodied AI is the next platform. The other is simpler. SoftBank was exiting a loss-making asset, and as controlling shareholder, Hyundai had to absorb it regardless of appetite. The evidence tilts toward the first reading. Mobis's actuator work is real capital aimed at making Atlas, not just owning it. In-house component design and a prototype line are not what a reluctant owner builds.

Pulling the threads together. What's genuinely inflecting is that a major Western humanoid program has moved past letters of intent and private placements. An automaker is now building owned capacity behind it. What's still a demo is Atlas doing reliable, paid work anywhere at volume. There's still no uptime or cycle data for that. The open questions are where the other two-thirds of the actuators come from, and who supplies the magnets inside them. Watch three things. First, the results of Mobis's joint verification with Boston Dynamics. Second, the first Atlas deployment count Hyundai is willing to stand behind. Third, Boston Dynamics' loss line in future Hyundai group filings.

That's the briefing. Enjoy your weekend, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
