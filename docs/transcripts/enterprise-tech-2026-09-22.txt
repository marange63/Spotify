Good morning. It's Tuesday, September 22nd, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

Sunday's briefing on this beat relayed a claim from Oracle's own chief financial officer, Hilary Maxson, on the fiscal first-quarter earnings call. She said the vast majority of new contracts arrive via prepay or bring-your-own-hardware, and so, in her words, will not require incremental capital from Oracle. Two days later, the checkable fact is that Oracle sold about 141 million shares for 19.9 billion dollars through an at-the-market offering. The company also told investors it plans to raise roughly 40 billion dollars of debt and equity combined this fiscal year. A company whose customers are genuinely prepaying its buildout does not need to sell twenty billion dollars of its own stock in the same year.

To size that raise, remember the capacity it funds. Oracle's quarter carried 28.5 billion dollars of capital spending against negative free cash flow of 5.4 billion, figures this beat has already worked through earlier in the week. Set the 19.9 billion dollar equity raise against a single quarter of capex and it stops looking like opportunism and starts looking like arithmetic.

Fairness requires one nuance. Oracle's chief executive, Clayton Magouyrk, clarified on the same call that the "no incremental capital" language was specifically about not needing to find additional cash, not about capital expenditure disappearing. So this isn't a reversal of what management said. It's an admission that the framing understated how much financing engineering sits underneath the backlog. One more piece of discipline belongs here. Oracle's own quarterly filing states no purpose for the at-the-market issuance at all. Connecting the raise to the data-center program is an inference from the cash-flow statement, not something the company said.

Grade the bull case honestly. Equity raised into a capacity-scarce market is cheap financing against a contracted book, not distress. That's a fair read. But it cuts against, not for, the capital-light story Oracle told investors on Sunday. The bottom line is that the AI buildout is not fully customer-financed, whatever the initial framing implied.

The more informative detail is that this leg came as equity rather than debt. Oracle already carries about 125 billion dollars of notes payable and other borrowings, a number this beat covered earlier in the week, so the debt lever is the one that was already pulled hard. Equity is a different instrument with a different message. Borrowing against a contracted book says management believes the backlog converts. Issuing shares dilutes existing holders to buy assets that depreciate on a fixed schedule whether the backlog converts or not. That is the risk transfer worth watching, and it moved onto shareholders this quarter rather than onto lenders. Note what we cannot say. No rating-agency action dated after the September 11th print could be located, so Oracle's credit trajectory in this window is unresolved, not downgraded.

The other financing lever is headcount. Oracle added 700 million dollars to its restructuring plan, bringing the total to roughly 2.8 billion dollars, and began a fresh round of layoffs on September 14th, with some teams cut by more than 10 percent. Total headcount-reduction numbers circulating in the press, anywhere from 7,000 to 30,000 people, are analyst estimates rather than Oracle disclosure, and shouldn't be treated as fact. Oracle states dollars. It does not state bodies. What the dollars say is that an equity raise and an expanded restructuring charge arrived inside the same few days. None of this proves customer demand is weakening. Nothing in the record shows a contract cancelled. It proves the buildout is being funded from every direction Oracle can reach: debt, equity, and its own payroll.

Two consequences follow. The first is competitive. If the marginal constraint on hyperscale AI capacity is the cost of financing it rather than the ability to build it, then rivals bidding against Oracle Cloud Infrastructure now have a balance-sheet talking point they did not have a month ago. Cost of capital, not capacity, becomes the differentiator in large enterprise AI infrastructure deals. The second consequence is on the services side. Treating legacy software and administrative headcount as a funding source for data-center capex is a template other vendors can copy. If they do, the demand hit lands on enterprise IT services and systems integrators, which is the same conclusion this beat reached from the embedded-AI direction on Monday.

If suppliers are funding this themselves, the more interesting question is whose AI revenue is actually material yet. That's the question this briefing left open on Monday, when Adobe's AI-first revenue worked out to roughly two-and-a-half percent of its total, and Salesforce's Agentforce sat around three percent of its base.

Databricks offers the first real counterexample. The data-platform company reported an annualized run-rate crossing 6.9 billion dollars, growing about 80 percent year over year. That is more than double the growth rate at Snowflake, the natural comparison, which put up 34 percent on its latest reported run-rate of roughly 5.3 billion dollars. The two are converging on scale while diverging sharply on pace.

The number that matters isn't the growth rate, though. It's the mix. Databricks says AI products account for roughly 1.7 billion dollars of that run-rate, or about a quarter of the total. Twenty-five percent of revenue from AI is an order of magnitude more material than anything the seat-priced incumbents have shown. Growth rate was never the right test. Materiality is, because triple-digit growth on a two-percent base does not move a company.

Now apply the same skepticism this beat applied to Adobe on Sunday. Databricks is private. It does not file with the SEC. Every figure here is company-disclosed through press and analyst briefings, not audited. And it defines "AI products" as a category of its own choosing, with no more rigor than Adobe's undefined "AI-first" label. That's the same disclosure problem this beat criticized a day ago, now working in the bull's favor instead of against it, and it deserves the same treatment.

There's also a margin question worth flagging, and I have no figure for it. A data platform earning a quarter of its revenue from AI products may be passing through a lot of raw compute and inference cost. That is a different business from high-margin software. It would also explain why the reporting on Databricks pairs 80 percent growth with shrinking margins. And these figures date to the summer, not to the last few days. They're evidence in an argument, not a new event.

Even so, they're the first candidate answer to where net-new AI budget is actually landing. Not inside the application layer, but one level down, in the data infrastructure underneath it. That sits in tension with the embedded-AI consolidation story this beat has been building all week, and the honest move is to name the tension rather than resolve it. If the data layer keeps capturing net-new spend at this rate, seat-priced suites are defending existing revenue while ceding the growth budget elsewhere.

One more data point on where AI has genuinely displaced software rather than become a feature inside it. SpaceX's all-stock purchase of Anysphere, the maker of the AI coding tool Cursor, closed on August 14th at a 60-billion-dollar valuation. That's stale by five weeks, but it's never run on this beat, and it completes a picture. The two most prominent AI coding tools now sit under two of the largest private balance sheets in technology, Copilot under Microsoft and Cursor under SpaceX. Worth remembering that 60 billion dollars is an all-stock number marked against SpaceX's own valuation, not a cash price discovered by a market. Still, the category where AI most clearly replaced traditional software, rather than merely decorated it, consolidated into two non-independent parents within about a year. The practical effect is that the next repricing of developer AI gets set by two vendors with no independent competitive check, and the capital bar for a challenger from outside just went up.

So here's where the week leaves it. The supplier funding the AI infrastructure buildout is now issuing equity to do it, which undercuts the capital-light story it told investors two days earlier. The clearest evidence of AI revenue actually being material to a business sits inside one private, unaudited company rather than any of the public incumbents. And the AI coding category, the cleanest case of software genuinely being displaced, has already been absorbed into two balance sheets large enough to buy it outright. Two things would settle the open question next quarter. Whether Oracle's financing mix stabilizes without another raise, and whether Databricks' AI-product margins hold up once it discloses more than a topline percentage.
