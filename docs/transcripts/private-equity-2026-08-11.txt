Good morning, it's August 11th, 2026, and this is your private equity briefing.

Start with the deal that closed a week ago Tuesday, because the size was never the interesting part. Electronic Arts completed its sale to the PIF, Silver Lake, and Affinity Partners consortium on August 4th, ending thirty-seven years as a public company in what ABC News and the wire services are calling the largest all-cash take-private ever recorded. Fifty-five billion dollars, two hundred ten dollars a share. That headline you already knew. What we can now confirm, from EA's own closing filing with the SEC, is how it was financed, and that's the story.

The debt side is itemized down to the tranche. There's a first-lien term loan B funded that same day, split across dollars and euros. A three-and-a-quarter-billion-dollar term loan A. A five-hundred-million-dollar revolver. Nearly three billion of secured notes at seven and a quarter percent due 2033, with a matching euro tranche. And two-and-a-half billion of unsecured notes at eight and three-quarters percent due 2034. Add it all up and CreditSights' aggregate figure holds: twenty billion dollars of debt, committed solely by JPMorgan, with about eighteen billion funded at close. On a forecast fiscal 2027 EBITDA of roughly three point one billion, CreditSights puts that near six times leverage. And separately, Yahoo Finance and PitchBook confirm this is the largest buyout debt financing since the financial crisis, eclipsing Medline's 2021 deal.

Here's the part the filing won't hand you outright. EA's 8-K says the transaction was funded by the notes offering, the credit facilities, quote, "the equity contributions from funds affiliated with the Consortium and cash on hand," end quote — but it never itemizes that equity check. No dollar figure for PIF, none for Silver Lake, none for Affinity. Do the arithmetic yourself: fifty-five billion in total consideration, minus eighteen billion of funded debt, and you're left with roughly thirty-seven billion of equity and cash — comfortably north of sixty percent of the deal. That's an inference from disclosed totals, not a number the filing states outright, so hold it loosely. But it's a strikingly conservative structure next to the sixty-to-seventy percent debt that's typical of a traditional LBO.

So what does that tell you. Read it bullishly and it says there is enormous depth in sovereign and permanent-capital equity willing to write a record-size check rather than lean on the loan market. Read it skeptically and it says the leveraged-loan and CLO markets are more cautious about underwriting jumbo take-privates than record public-equity indices would suggest — sponsors chose, or were forced, to lever this well below what a 2021-vintage deal would have carried. Both readings are probably true at once. Either way, this isn't a template most sponsors can copy. A sovereign-wealth-anchored consortium writing a thirty-seven-billion-dollar equity check is not a capital pool most GPs have access to, so don't read EA as proof the broader megadeal market has unstuck itself. It proves the top of the market can still get financed. It says nothing about the next fifteen deals in line.

Now, if the entry side is being underwritten this cautiously, the natural question is what's happening on the way out — and that's where the cycle's real stress still sits. There's genuine movement here: twenty-one US-listed IPOs of private-equity-backed companies have priced through August 5th, according to Dealogic, already ahead of all twenty recorded in the whole of 2025 and the fastest pace since 2021. That's real. But keep the base rate in view: US public listings have accounted for only about one percent of private equity exits since the start of 2022. Twenty-one deals off a channel that thin cannot move the industry's distribution numbers no matter how good the headlines look. And the reason sponsors are leaning on IPOs at all is the M&A side: a shortage of strategic buyers continues to constrain the traditional exit route, pushing firms toward private sales wherever they can find a taker.

Meanwhile the backlog keeps aging underneath the good headlines. Thirty-four percent of portfolio companies had been held more than five years as of this year, per PwC, up from twenty-eight percent last year. That's the number that actually matters, and it's moving the wrong way even during what looks, on the surface, like a recovering exit window. So hold both facts at once: the IPO door is cracking open, and the pile of unsold, aging assets behind it is getting bigger, not smaller. Reported optimism about reopening windows is being outpaced by the sheer stock of what still needs to sell.

That distribution drought isn't just a portfolio-company problem anymore — it's now visibly throttling the fundraising machine itself. Trailing-twelve-month commitments fell to one point two three trillion dollars across two thousand six hundred thirteen funds as of March 31st, down fifteen and a half percent in dollars and a striking forty-five percent in fund count year over year. That's the eighth straight quarterly decline. And the read on why is structural, not seasonal: distributions remain stuck near record lows, so limited partners simply have less cash coming back to recommit to new funds. This is the mechanism, made explicit — the exit logjam we've been tracking all week isn't an abstraction sitting on GP balance sheets, it's now measurably slowing new capital formation.

Dry powder, meanwhile, is being marketed as a sign of strength — near-record levels of committed but undeployed capital. Don't take the bait. Dry powder is rising because deployment is simply outpacing new fund formation, not because the fundraising engine is healthy. It's a mechanical artifact of the same slowdown, not evidence against it. And a fundraising market shrinking this fast tends to concentrate what capital does get raised into the largest, most established multi-strategy platforms — the ones that can point LPs to insurance and credit-adjacent earnings alongside buyout — leaving single-strategy, mid-market shops more exposed.

Pull the threads together and the cycle looks like this. The entry side still works, but only on equity, and only for the handful of players who can write nine and ten-figure checks without leaning on the loan market. The exit side is cracking, but off a base too thin to matter yet, while the stock of aging, unsold assets keeps growing underneath it. And the fundraising machine is now visibly downshifting in direct response. None of that resolves the core problem — it confirms it. The catalyst that would actually refute this read isn't another jumbo take-private or another record fee-related-earnings quarter. It's a wave of genuine third-party exits, priced by real buyers, large enough to move the industry's cash-distribution numbers rather than its paper marks. Watch for that, not for the next headline deal.
