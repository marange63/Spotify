Cautious Optimism Briefings

Frontier AI Lab Competition

Transcript · August 22, 2026

Good morning, it's Saturday, August 22nd, and this is your frontier AI labs briefing.

Twice this week we told you the same thing: stop pricing Anthropic off leaks and wait for an actual S-1. It turns out the filing already happened — quietly, back in June, as a confidential draft. What's reportedly close now is the public conversion: the roadshow, the pricing, an October debut. Forbes, writing on August 13th, puts the ask at more than two trillion dollars, and attributes that number to Anthropic's own shareholders and to investors who spoke to the Financial Times. Not to Anthropic. The company has made no public statement confirming a valuation, a date, or a size. What's firmer is the shape of the transaction. It is being sized against SpaceX's record offering, which raised seventy-five billion dollars, or eighty-six point two billion once the overallotment was exercised, and which reportedly priced SpaceX around one point seven seven trillion. So Anthropic is not just chasing a valuation record. It is chasing a placement-size record — asking public markets to absorb the largest single block of equity ever sold, against a private mark of nine hundred sixty-five billion dollars set only in May. That is more than a doubling in three months on no audited disclosure. And keep one thing about the benchmark in view: SpaceX has since traded back below two trillion. The comparison Anthropic wants did not hold for the company that set it.

Now the number that is real and multiply sourced. Anthropic's second-quarter revenue came in above eleven and a half billion dollars. A year earlier, that same quarter was seven hundred eighty-seven million. That is close to a fifteen-fold increase in twelve months, and multiple outlets also report that Anthropic turned its first positive operating result this quarter. Treat the loss side of the story far more carefully. A widely repeated, eye-catching net-loss figure for Anthropic's 2025 fiscal year does not trace to any source we can stand behind, and it sits suspiciously close to OpenAI's reported 2025 loss — close enough that it looks like a number that migrated from one lab to the other somewhere in the retelling. So the honest framing is enormous growth plus a first swing into operating profit, and no loss figure we will quote. That gap is exactly what an S-1 exists to close. Growth is what a filing discloses first. Margin durability is what it discloses last. On Friday we argued that gross margin, not growth rate, is the number that separates these labs, and a public Anthropic is the first time anyone actually gets to see it. Then a second force takes over: at this size, index funds have to own it, which prices a chunk of the stock on flows rather than on that margin.

So whose demand is this, exactly? OpenAI answered that question with its own data this month, and the answer bears directly on what Anthropic is about to disclose. In an August 12th post, OpenAI reported that as of June, Codex — its coding agent — generated sixty-four percent of the combined output tokens its enterprise customers ran through Codex and ChatGPT. That is a shift from assistance to delegation. Enterprises are not chatting with these models anymore. They are handing them jobs. The spread between the heaviest users and everyone else is widening fast, too. OpenAI's top-decile firms now produce eight point three times more output per user than typical firms, up from a two point six times gap in January. And the fastest growth is no longer in engineering. Legal-function weekly users are up a hundred and eight times since February, against five times growth in engineering itself — off a February base that was almost certainly tiny. Three caveats belong on all of that. This is OpenAI's telemetry, unaudited, on OpenAI's own definition of a frontier firm. It reports no dollars, so token share is not revenue share. And agentic products emit far more tokens per task than chat does, which inflates the sixty-four percent on its own. Directionally, though, the point stands: the fastest-growing revenue line at OpenAI and at Anthropic sits on the same workload. And that workload is the one everyone else is now attacking on price.

Google is attacking it first. Gemini three point seven Flash shipped on August 13th, three weeks after its predecessor, pitched explicitly as a workhorse for coding and agents. The reported benchmarks come from third-party blogs rather than a Google model card, so treat them as claims, not results. Those claims put it at forty-three point six on a composite agent score, against forty-two point seven for Claude Sonnet five. That is a one-point gap, well inside the noise of any eval suite, and nobody should read it as a capability breakthrough. The price is the real signal: seventy-five cents per million input tokens, and Google labels that introductory, which means promotional by definition. But a three-week release cadence on the cheap tier tells you where Google has decided to fight hardest.

Which makes Google's overall position the ambiguous one. Earlier this month Alphabet moved Demis Hassabis out of day-to-day control of Google DeepMind, making him Chair of DeepMind and Chief Scientist of Alphabet. Koray Kavukcuoglu, DeepMind's chief technology officer until now, takes the operating job and reports directly to Sundar Pichai — an internal elevation, not an outside hire. Google frames this as Hassabis choosing to focus on the long horizon. Fortune frames it differently: Gemini three point five Pro reportedly missed three straight release deadlines, and in June alone Google lost Gemini co-lead Noam Shazeer to OpenAI, and AlphaFold's inventor, John Jumper, to Anthropic. That causal link is Fortune's inference. Google's own post makes no such connection, and the two should not be treated as the same claim. But both things can hold at once: a frontier tier that is stalling, and a cheap tier shipping every three weeks. Read together, Google looks less like a lab unravelling and more like one trading frontier prestige for cheap-tier volume. That is a rational trade if the money is in workload share. It is an expensive one if capability leadership still decides enterprise contracts. And note who benefits from the talent flow. A listed Anthropic would hold the most liquid recruiting currency in the industry, which is an actual stock price, and that cuts against Google most of all.

Underneath all of that paid competition sits a floor with no price at all. Alibaba's Tongyi Lab released Qwen three point eight twenty-seven B, a dense open-weight model of roughly twenty-eight billion parameters under an Apache license, which the tech press reports beating Claude Opus four point six Max across several coding and agentic benchmarks. Those comparisons come from secondary coverage, not an Alibaba model card, so the beats-Opus framing deserves real skepticism. A hand-picked benchmark set is not frontier parity. Harder to dismiss is the deployment story. Quantized, the model reportedly runs in about seventeen gigabytes, which fits on a single high-end consumer graphics card. No API fee, no cloud bill at all. That does not threaten an enterprise contract yet, because sustained long-horizon agentic work at enterprise context lengths is precisely where small local models still break down. But it sets a hard floor under what an individual developer or a small team will ever pay for coding assistance.

So here is where the race stands. Anthropic is preparing to ask public markets to absorb the largest equity placement in history, on the strength of a revenue line that Google is undercutting on price and Chinese open weights are undercutting for free. The figure that decides whether a two-trillion-dollar mark survives is not the growth rate on the cover of the S-1. It is the gross margin buried inside it. So watch for the actual filing rather than another investor leak. And watch whether Gemini's introductory price is still there once the introduction is over.

That's the briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
