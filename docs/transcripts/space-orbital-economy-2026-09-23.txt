Good morning, it's Wednesday, September 23rd, and this is your Cautious Optimism briefing on the space and orbital economy.

Yesterday this desk argued that the binding constraint on Golden Dome is the satellite factory. On the commercial side this week, the constraint looks different. The hardware is built, or being built, and it is waiting on rides and licenses instead.

Start with the lead. For five straight editions, September 28th has sat on this show's calendar as the target for Starship Flight 14, with nothing physically behind it. That has now changed. Super Heavy Booster 21 has moved to Pad 2 at Starbase. Spaceflight Now and Starbase observers put its move to the pad on Monday.

Ship 41 is a different story. As of Tuesday, those same observers still described its rollout to the pad as expected, not done. So the full stack is not yet confirmed on the pad. The booster is there. The ship should be close behind, but that is not yet a fact.

What's genuinely new is the regulatory picture. The target remains no earlier than September 28th at 7:15 a.m. Central, and SpaceX's own wording still says "pending regulatory approval." As of Tuesday's reporting, the FAA had not granted the orbital license modification this flight needs. And this is not a routine paperwork step. Spaceflight Now notes it would be the first time the FAA grants an orbital license for an object the size of a Starship upper stage, a hundred and seventy-one feet tall.

That makes the license a precedent, not a formality. The question on the table is no longer only whether the vehicle is ready. It is whether the licensing process can clear a first-of-its-kind vehicle on the operator's timeline. Whatever the FAA writes into this license becomes the template that later heavy-lift entrants, from Blue Origin to Stoke, will inherit. One discipline point. SpaceX has not said why the date moved from September 22nd to the 28th, so I won't pin that slip on the regulator.

Why does the license matter commercially? Because of what the flight carries. The payload, in one clause, is twenty-six Starlink V3 satellites. The useful new piece is a benchmark from SpaceX itself. It says each Falcon 9 launch of Starlink V2 Mini satellites adds about two point seven terabits per second of capacity. Spaceflight Now puts this single Starship flight at twenty-six terabits. That is roughly nine to ten times a Falcon 9 Starlink launch. It's this desk's division of two quoted figures, across two satellite generations, so hold it loosely. But it shows why an operational Starship is the swing factor in the Starlink capacity roadmap, and in the SPCX equity story built on it.

Now be precise about what September 28th can and cannot answer. A clean flight would validate Starship as an orbital deployment platform. That is the biggest open variable in Starlink's buildout. It would not test the cost-per-kilogram case. Both stages are planned to splash down, with no catch. Reuse is what makes Starship's business case close, and this flight leaves it untested. And to be plain, the flight has not happened and the license has not been granted. There is no result to report.

One timing note on the equity. A secondary tracker's compiled lock-up schedule, not an SEC filing, shows up to three hundred twenty-eight million SPCX shares becoming eligible for sale tomorrow, September 24th. The tracker puts that at about seven percent. It lands four days before the earliest launch window. That's a coincidence of the calendar, not a causal claim. But a market watching both will read one through the other, whichever way the flight goes.

So the takeaway on Starship: the pacing item now looks as much regulatory as mechanical. That adds a new layer to a thread this show has tracked all week. Western launch supply is already concentrated in SpaceX. If the constraint on commercial constellations is shifting to rides and licenses, value flows to whoever controls the pad and sets the regulatory template. Today that is still SpaceX. Yet the one vehicle that could relieve heavy-lift scarcity is itself waiting on a first-of-its-kind license.

If Starship is hardware waiting on a license, AST SpaceMobile is hardware waiting on rides.

A correction first. Yesterday's edition said AST's new Verizon agreement came with no revenue figure. That is true of the deal. But it may have left the impression that AST reports no revenue at all. It does. These are second-quarter results from August 10th, so they are not this week's news. This show simply hadn't covered them, and they sharpen yesterday's point.

AST reported second-quarter revenue of thirty-one and a half million dollars. It held full-year guidance at a hundred fifty to two hundred million. And it put revenue backlog at about one point three billion dollars.

Two things fall out of those numbers. First, annualize the second quarter and you get about a hundred twenty-six million dollars. That is below the bottom of the guide. It's desk arithmetic from two disclosed figures, not a company projection. But it means AST needs a real second-half ramp to hit its own number, and that is the figure to hold it to at the next print. Second, the backlog is more than seven times the midpoint of the guide. That is the gap between an order book and recognized revenue, in a single ratio. The disclosure doesn't split revenue between service, government work and hardware. So I won't call that thirty-one and a half million direct-to-cell subscriber revenue.

Now the gap that matters more. AST says production is "advancing through BlueBird satellite 42." Only thirteen BlueBirds are in orbit. Satellites fourteen through sixteen are preparing for the next mission. Read literally, "advancing through" means work is underway on unit forty-two. It does not mean forty-two finished satellites sitting in a warehouse. So treat what follows as this desk's inference, not a confirmed count. The inference points the same way as Starship. AST appears to have more hardware built or building than it has launched.

That has two consequences. Satellites that are built but not flown tie up working capital. And a 2026 guide that depends on putting them into service depends on launch timing AST doesn't control. That is a timing risk to the guide, not a demand risk. Capital is not the constraint. Pro forma liquidity is above three point seven billion dollars. What binds AST is getting satellites off the ground and turning backlog into recognized service revenue.

So the AST guide is, in effect, a bet on launch dates set by someone else.

Defense is the exception that sharpens the split. The Space Development Agency wants to finish its Tranche 1 launch campaign, six more batches of satellites, by the end of spring 2027. It aims to fly two of those batches by the end of this year. Those are agency targets in a program that has already slipped. And as we reported yesterday, the agency's director has blamed hardware, software and supply-chain problems at the vendors for holding Tranche 1 back. So those two year-end launches test the factories, not the pad.

The constraint differs by buyer. Defense constellations are waiting on factories. Commercial constellations are waiting on rides and licenses.

Pulling it together. The orbital economy is inflecting where hardware has actually moved: a Super Heavy booster on the pad, and an AST balance sheet with real revenue and real backlog. It's still paper where the paperwork and the rides haven't caught up. That means an orbital license not yet granted, a second-half revenue ramp not yet delivered, and SDA launch targets that have already slipped.

Watch three things. First, whether Ship 41 joins the booster and the FAA grants that license before the September 28th window. Second, AST's next BlueBird mission, carrying satellites fourteen through sixteen. Third, whether the Space Development Agency gets the first of its two year-end Tranche 1 launches off on schedule.

That's the briefing for September 23rd. I'm your host, and this has been Cautious Optimism.
