Good morning. It's August 18th, 2026, and this is your robotics and physical AI briefing.

This week's headlines on Unitree were about access — the order book, the valuation, who could get an allocation. Today the prospectus finally shows the income statement underneath the mania, and it's the cleanest unit-economics data point humanoid robotics has produced so far.

Unitree priced its Shanghai STAR Market IPO at 150.8 yuan a share, valuing China's most prolific humanoid maker at roughly nine billion dollars. We've covered the pricing and the frenzy around it already this week. What's new is the prospectus P&L. First-quarter revenue surged more than 68 percent year over year, to 422.8 million yuan. Adjusted net profit, excluding one-off items, did the opposite: it plummeted more than 52 percent, to just 40.3 million yuan. Unitree blames the profit squeeze on a significant spike in research, development, and sales spending. So growth accelerated at exactly the moment profitability inverted — the textbook signature of a company subsidizing revenue with margin to hold share.

Now put that against the demand side. The retail tranche of this IPO was oversubscribed more than five thousand times over — an allocation rate near two-hundredths of one percent. That oversubscription measures float scarcity and retail mania. It says nothing about the business. The 52 percent profit collapse is the fundamentals sitting right underneath it, and those two numbers are the same story told from opposite sides.

There's a second crack in the "national champion" story worth recalling. Unitree shipped roughly 5,500 humanoids in 2025, more than any other manufacturer on earth — and we flagged earlier this week that rival AgiBot has already overtaken it on first-half volume share. So Unitree now trails on the unit count and is compressing margin on what it does sell. Volume leadership and economic leadership are diverging inside the same name.

One nuance worth banking, because it cuts against the pessimistic read: Unitree's full-year 2025 gross margin came in at 60.1 percent. That's a full-year figure, not a Q1 print, so it can't be used to say the first quarter's gross margin held up. But it does tell us where the stress is concentrated. A gross margin above 60 percent means the hardware itself isn't being sold at a loss — the damage is happening below the gross-profit line, in R&D and go-to-market spend. That's a more conventional, more recoverable land-grab pattern than a margin-negative hardware business would be.

Which raises the harder question that 60 percent margin doesn't answer: how much of it is actually in Unitree's control. The single biggest cost lever in a humanoid is the actuator, and the actuator's cost is set largely by rare-earth magnets. The NdPr alloy benchmark, the key magnet input, rose more than 21 percent in a single month through the start of July, after Beijing tightened export controls again in late June. That's the same supply base every Western humanoid maker, Figure and Apptronik included, is still buying from. So Unitree's healthy hardware margin sits on top of an input cost that China's own industrial policy sets. That's both why the Chinese cohort can build cheaply today and why the whole category's unit economics stay hostage to a lever no robot company owns. The margin looks fine right up until the magnet price moves.

Unitree's shares are scheduled to begin trading on the Shanghai exchange tomorrow, August 19th — after this broadcast's cutoff, so there's no debut print to report yet. Grey-market brokers have reportedly offered pre-listing allottees 410 yuan a share, a 170 percent premium to the offer price — that's sentiment, not a trade, but it tells you which way the market is leaning into the print.

The read-through for capital markets: this is the first public-market P&L benchmark the humanoid pure-play cohort has ever produced. Every private comparable — Figure AI at its 39 billion dollar valuation among them — now has an actual income statement to be measured against, and it says the category is still buying growth at a loss. That reframes how the humanoid capex cycle should be underwritten. Investors underwriting those valuations need to be prepared to fund several more quarters like this one, not treat the order book as proof the model works. And the private names have every incentive to stay private while the loss-making quarters run, because Unitree's tape is now the comparable their own bankers will be marked against. Tesla's Optimus program, for what it's worth, still hasn't started production at Fremont as of its most recent update — so even the most capital-rich humanoid bet in the West remains pre-revenue while Unitree is already showing real, if ugly, financials.

Now the opposite case — where robotics economics are already proven. Johnson & Johnson just received FDA clearance for its Ottava soft-tissue surgical robot, a table-integrated system aimed directly at the hospitals running Intuitive Surgical's da Vinci platform. This is being described as the first serious US competitive threat to Intuitive's surgical-robotics franchise in roughly two decades. Surgical robotics is the one category in this space with a genuinely proven, cash-generative model — Intuitive's razor-and-blades economics on instruments and service contracts have compounded for years, an annuity that arrives whether or not a new system is sold. An FDA clearance is not installed base, and it isn't share. Intuitive's moat is switching costs and surgeon training built up over two decades, and that doesn't erode overnight. But it means capital finally has a credible reason to contest a market where the unit economics already work, rather than one still being subsidized into existence.

Put the two stories side by side and the pattern for this beat gets sharper: value in robotics is accruing where the economics are already proven, and it's being torched where they aren't. Surgical robotics draws a serious new competitor precisely because the payback is real. Humanoid robotics draws a five-thousand-times-oversubscribed order book precisely because the payback is still a story. The catalysts to watch from here are concrete: Unitree's actual trading debut tomorrow, which will tell you whether the public market prices past the profit collapse or reckons with it, and the first quantified, verified figure — a margin number, a paying-deployment count, anything harder than a demo — that any humanoid maker discloses next.
