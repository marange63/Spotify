Strategic Power, Energy, and Institutional Change

Good morning, this is your Cautious Optimism Briefing on strategic power for Tuesday, November 3rd, 2026.

This week the fight isn't over who can build capacity. It's over who can get access to it, and who can route around the checkpoints meant to control it.

Start with the clearest case of that, out of Georgia. On November 2nd, OpenAI announced a program called Grid Protectors. Under it, data-center campuses OpenAI directly develops will cut their own power use when extreme weather or other emergencies strain the grid. The pilot site is Project Camellia, in Effingham County, a campus expected to need about 3,200 megawatts. Under a 25-year agreement, Georgia Power can curtail up to 1,000 megawatts of that load during high-demand periods. That's roughly a third of the campus. And OpenAI pays the full infrastructure and electric-service costs to serve it.

Now test that 1,000 megawatt number. Georgia Power's own release describes it as "flexible demand response, which enables Georgia Power to reduce energy delivered to the facility during periods of high demand." Notice what that sentence leaves out. It gives no limit on how many hours a year cuts can happen. It gives no notice period, and no compensation or tariff mechanism. Those terms haven't been published anywhere we could find. Without them, you can't tell whether the ceiling is a real constraint on OpenAI or a number that is rarely invoked. So treat 1,000 megawatts as a cap, not a measured burden. Two more limits. Outside Georgia, the program is a stated intention, with no megawatts, sites, utility partners, or dates. And this is demand response, not new supply. As coverage of the announcement puts it, the arrangement "does not create a new power source."

That distinction matters because of the scale it's being measured against. Morgan Stanley's Stephen Byrd, as reported in secondary coverage, puts the U.S. data-center power shortfall through 2028 at 70 gigawatts. That's before counting gas turbines, fuel cells, and converted bitcoin-mining sites. After counting every one of them, the gap is still 43 gigawatts. It's a sell-side estimate, and other trackers using different windows put the gap materially lower. Still, the order of magnitude tells you something. Bringing your own power doesn't close this hole, even though that's where developers are heading. A Bloom Energy report from June found 61 percent of developers plan to bring their own power if the grid can't meet their needs.

And the grid itself is slow. Per the advisory firm Inflect, securing grid power for a new AI facility now takes anywhere from 24 to 72 months. Some utility quotes run 5 to 7 years. On October 29th, this desk said whoever already holds the interconnection holds the scarce asset. My read of the Georgia deal is a hyperscaler paying for that asset in a new currency. Not just capital, but operating flexibility, plus the full cost of service. That link is my inference. No published source says the curtailment bought a faster connection. But set one 1,000 megawatt curtailment right against a 43 gigawatt net gap, and the arithmetic is plain. This doesn't shrink the hole. It changes who gets priority inside it. The second-order effect is that firm, uninterruptible power should carry a bigger premium. That favors owners of existing interconnected generation, and suppliers of gas turbines and nuclear uprates, over developers still waiting in the queue.

That's also the backdrop for today itself. Voters in ten states are deciding 41 local data-center ballot measures. Ohio alone accounts for 21 of them, and some would ban facilities over 25 megawatts. Results aren't in yet. But I'd read the timing as deliberate. OpenAI's pledge to accept curtailment and pay full cost of service landed the day before communities vote on whether to host these campuses at all. The so-what is that hyperscalers are now buying grid access with financial concessions and political cover in the same transaction. Expect utilities to hold up this template when they approve the next large load.

That same gap, between access and a checkpoint that actually bites, shows up in chips. Yesterday we covered Taiwan's economy-ministry plan to add AI servers to its controlled-goods list. Now there's a bill at hearing stage. On November 2nd, the Legislative Yuan's Economics Committee held a public hearing on a Democratic Progressive Party amendment to the Foreign Trade Act. It would ban exports of specified integrated circuits, key wafer materials, components, and chipmaking equipment to mainland China without government approval. Violators would face up to ten years in prison. The sharper point isn't the penalty. It's the legal gap the bill closes. As Asia Times puts it, current law "doesn't make this an export offense." Take the Keelung case, where nine people were charged in an AI-chip smuggling scheme. That case had to be built on breach of trust and document forgery instead.

Keep the stage straight. This is an amendment at hearing stage, not law, with no enactment date, and passage isn't assured. But the direction is telling. The island that fabricates and integrates this hardware is only now writing the law that would let it prosecute the export itself. If it passes, server integrators and their staff in Taiwan face criminal export exposure for the first time. For Nvidia, Supermicro, and the other integrators operating there, my read is that the first hit shows up as compliance and legal cost, not lost sales. And diverted hardware is more likely to hunt for other routes than to stop. So the hole in Taiwan's chip controls has been legal, not just technical, and this bill is the first serious attempt to close it.

Beijing has the mirror-image problem. Its rules are written to reach past its own border, and the pressure shows up in trade data before any formal decision. Beijing suspended the October 2025 expansion of its rare-earth controls as part of its trade truce with Washington. That suspension lapses November 10th, with no confirmed renewal. The wider truce was extended in September to January 10th, 2027, but that extension never named rare earths. What's new is what a lapse would reinstate. Products made anywhere in the world containing even 0.1 percent Chinese-origin rare-earth material by value would need Beijing's sign-off to ship. Five more elements, including erbium, holmium, and europium, would join the licensing regime. That 0.1 percent rule is extraterritorial. It's a claim on factories outside China. A motor plant in Ohio or Germany built around Chinese magnets would need Beijing's permission to ship its own product. That's the opposite of Taiwan's move, which would criminalize exports from its own territory. And the April 2025 licensing on seven medium and heavy elements stays in force either way.

Now the flows, from September's export data. Chinese magnet shipments to the United States fell 28.7 percent from August. Total magnet shipments fell only 6.1 percent over the same month. So the U.S. drop was more than four times the overall decline. That's one month of data, and it could reflect August front-loading, contract timing, or American buyers holding off ahead of the deadline. Whatever the cause, the contraction is concentrated on U.S.-bound flows before Beijing has published anything. The exposure sits with magnet buyers on thin inventories: defense primes, EV makers, and wind-turbine manufacturers. They carry that risk whether Beijing extends the suspension, reinstates it selectively, or reinstates it in full, and all three remain live. The other side of that trade is the non-Chinese supply built over the past year, names like MP Materials and Lynas. A lapse would be the first hard test of that build-out. If Beijing reinstates, that's where the scarcity premium should show up. If it extends, the urgency behind that trade fades.

So watch four things over the next week. Today's ballot results. Whether Beijing publishes anything on rare earths by November 10th. Whether Taiwan's bill advances past committee. And whether any other hyperscaler matches OpenAI's curtailment terms. Each one tests the same question: who actually controls access to the choke point, and who is quietly routing around it.
