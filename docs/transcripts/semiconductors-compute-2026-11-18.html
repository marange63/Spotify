Good morning. It's Wednesday, November 18th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

Last week, this desk named Nvidia's third-quarter print as the test of one question. Friday's AI-chip sell-off had taken Nvidia to a fresh 52-week low, and it hit AMD even harder. Was that a reassessment of demand, or a positioning unwind? The print answers the demand half, and the answer is no. Nvidia reported total revenue of 115.8 billion dollars. That beat the 109.1 billion dollar consensus compiled by LSEG with room to spare.

Data Center did the work. That segment brought in 107.6 billion dollars, up 21 percent from the prior quarter. So more than nine of every ten dollars Nvidia booked came from the data center, and that line is still compounding fast. Whatever sold off last week, it wasn't the order book as it stood through October.

Now bound that claim, because a beat looks backward. Data Center revenue bills hardware committed quarters ago, so it can't rule out orders turning before revenue does. The print rules out a demand collapse already underway. It doesn't rule out one ahead. The forward evidence is the guide and the Vera Rubin ramp, and both are company claims. The takeaway: demand wasn't what sold off, but the proof is backward-looking.

So score the rest of the quarter dimension by dimension, starting with product. Nvidia says its Vera Rubin NVL72 platform has shipped. It calls this "the fastest product ramp in the company's history." That's Nvidia's own characterization. It comes with no disclosed volume, no yield, and no dollar figure. On the ladder from announcement to sustained adoption, Rubin sits at shipped and ramping. It is not yet proven at scale.

Leadership continuity is a separate dimension, and it's still open. This was Nvidia's first earnings call since Jensen Huang resigned last Thursday. Nvidia has framed his exit only as a code-of-conduct matter. Colette Kress, the interim CEO who also remains CFO, ran the call. Her message was continuity: "Our momentum is real." Board chair Harvey Jones then closed a door. The board, he said, "unanimously accepted his resignation and does not intend to reinstate Jensen as CEO." He also refused to take questions on the circumstances, or on what he called "the ongoing investigation." The board has not said what that investigation concerns, who is running it, or whether it touches the business. So the interim now runs straight through what Nvidia calls its fastest-ever ramp.

Here's our inference on what that means for supply. A record quarter under an interim chief suggests Nvidia's capacity from TSMC and the memory makers runs on signed contracts, not on Huang's personal pull. But it says nothing about who runs Nvidia six months from now.

Now the forward look. Nvidia guided fourth-quarter revenue to 130 billion dollars. LSEG's average analyst estimate was 125.1 billion, so the guide cleared the bar comfortably. One report had the shares up about 4 percent in extended trading.

But the guide carries one explicit exclusion. In Nvidia's words: "NVIDIA is not assuming any Data Center compute revenue from China in its outlook." That's not a haircut. It's a zero.

Read the zero two ways. As guidance, it removes China as a downside risk. Anything China contributes from here is upside to the number. As strategy, it concedes the market on paper, not just in practice. That hands Chinese accelerator makers like Huawei's Ascend line and Cambricon a cleaner argument for domestic silicon. Take that as direction only, because we have no volume data to size it. Call it de-risking that doubles as surrender.

That zero sits right next to the Commerce Department's new remote-access rule. The rule requires export licenses for advanced AI chips and servers going to data centers anywhere in the world, if they may provide significant compute to China. Its license trigger counts aggregate compute across one facility or linked data centers. So it aims at cluster-scale sites, and splitting a cluster across linked buildings doesn't escape it.

Here's the tension. Nvidia's zero covers China. The rule reaches further, into the five countries Commerce newly flags as diversion risks, including Singapore and Malaysia. Yesterday we told you to listen for whether management addressed the rule, and what it said about Singapore and Malaysia. The answer is almost nothing. Kress's only on-record comment was that Nvidia is "still going to spend some time studying those" new rules. Per Fortune's account of the call, no analyst pressed on Singapore either. Jones did describe a new board committee on national security and export controls. He said only that it "will enhance our engagement with the U.S. government, including all relevant agencies." That's process, not exposure. So the zero is a floor under China, not under the rule's wider corridor, and nobody on the call priced that gap.

Permission is one gate Nvidia doesn't control. The other is what it costs to build. Gross margin this quarter was 74.8 percent on a GAAP basis. The guide for next quarter steps down to a range of 71 to 72 percent, GAAP and non-GAAP alike. That's roughly three points of margin given back in a single quarter. Nvidia's release names no reason. No management quote ties the step-down to Rubin ramp costs, memory costs, or mix, so what follows is inference. New-platform ramps usually carry extra cost early and recover later. Input-cost inflation is plausible too, given what memory is doing. Nvidia hasn't said which. Treat the step-down as a measured delta with an unexplained cause.

Memory is the input cost everyone is watching, though the cleanest fresh data is for PC DRAM, not HBM. After a month of one-way tightening, TrendForce's latest update is the first datapoint that bends the curve. It projects PC DRAM contract prices rising 13 to 18 percent quarter over quarter in the fourth quarter. That's down from the 18 to 23 percent range it tracked for the third quarter. Prices are still climbing steeply. What's slowing is the rate of increase.

Hold one caveat before reading anything into it. A PC DRAM contract series tells us nothing direct about what Nvidia pays for HBM, so it can't explain the margin guide. And the shift of capacity toward HBM keeps pulling supply away from conventional DRAM, whatever this series does next.

With that in mind, two readings are live. One: deceleration is the classic early signal of a cyclical peak. Two: it's a base effect after an extreme third quarter, while the HBM reallocation keeps conventional supply tight into 2027. One quarter of slowing is not a turn. Here's the second-order point, and it's inference. If increases keep slowing, PC and phone makers buying conventional DRAM feel relief first. HBM buyers, Nvidia among them, may not, because Micron has reportedly locked most of its 2027 HBM at prices above this year's. So watch the second derivative, not the level, and don't read PC relief as cheaper accelerators.

Now widen the lens, because a 130 billion dollar guide is a demand signal for the whole chain. Start with TSMC. It hasn't given a fresh capacity update, so what follows is our inference. Nvidia's racks depend on TSMC's advanced packaging, and a guide that clears consensus says that packaging stays spoken for through at least the next quarter. TSMC gets paid whichever logo wins the socket. Next, the memory makers. SK hynix, Samsung and Micron all say they're shipping HBM4. Who gains most from the Rubin ramp is genuinely unclear. Estimates of HBM revenue share conflict sharply between sources, so we won't crown a leader. Finally, AMD and the hyperscaler custom-ASIC camp. Their opening depends on packaging and HBM capacity freeing up. A bigger Nvidia guide pulls on both, so on our read, that window isn't opening early. The so-what: the guide is good news for the suppliers, and it keeps the challengers waiting.

Here's the scorecard by dimension. Demand: confirmed, on lagging evidence. Product transition: shipped and ramping, in Nvidia's own words, with volume undisclosed. Leadership continuity: unresolved, with an interim CEO through the Rubin ramp. Permission: zeroed for China, still open for the wider corridor. Margins: a guided step-down with no stated driver. None of these should be allowed to answer for the others. As for who moved, on our read: TSMC and the HBM suppliers gained ground as a group, and China's domestic accelerator makers gained on paper. Nvidia wrote its China data-center business to zero, and the custom-ASIC camp is still waiting for an opening.

What to watch next. Whether Nvidia's CEO search produces a name. Whether the rule's Federal Register publication settles its effective date. And whether next quarter's memory contracts keep decelerating or snap back.
