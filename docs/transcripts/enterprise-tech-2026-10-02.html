Good morning, it's Friday, October 2nd, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

Today's thread is where the money actually lands in enterprise AI. It isn't landing at the model layer. It's landing in the infrastructure underneath, and with the integrators who wire it in. And in both places, suppliers are increasingly fronting the risk to win the business.

Start with the clearest example. Anthropic has signed a seven-year, $11.6 billion contractual commitment with Akamai, the content-delivery and edge company, to run workloads on Akamai Cloud. That comes straight from Akamai's 8-K filing with the SEC, so this is a primary disclosure, not a leak. One disclosure of my own: Anthropic makes the model behind this show.

Only the $11.6 billion is contractual. The filing adds an option to expand by up to another $9 billion, for a potential total near $20 billion. That second tranche is Anthropic's choice to make. It is not committed spend.

So what is the capacity for? Akamai's release is specific. It says the deal supports Anthropic's, quote, "CPU workload growth at scale." Later it repeats the point, citing Anthropic's "accelerating CPU workload demands." That's CPU, not GPU training. The filing never uses the words agent or inference. So reading this as capacity for AI agents making tool calls and web lookups is a reasonable interpretation, not something Akamai has said. Either way, a multi-billion-dollar CPU commitment is notable on its own. The next infrastructure bottleneck enterprises should watch may not be GPU supply alone.

Now the structure, because it inverts the usual customer-vendor relationship. Akamai is giving Anthropic a warrant for up to roughly 5 percent of its common stock, at a strike price of $111.33 a share. About 2 percent is expected to vest with the current commitment. The remaining 3 percent vests only as Anthropic expands toward that extra $9 billion. In other words, the vendor is paying the customer in equity, both to win the contract and to grow it.

And that equity was valuable from day one. Akamai's stock rose about 15 percent on the announcement, to around $127 in morning trading, according to 24/7 Wall St. That's comfortably above the strike. So this was not a remote option. It was an immediate price concession, which means Akamai's real deal economics are worse than the headline number.

Akamai is fronting the capital too. It is raising 2026 capital spending by about $1.7 billion to pre-purchase supply-chain components, including memory. It estimates total capex tied to this one deal at around $5.5 billion. Now size the contract against Akamai itself. Its own full-year 2026 revenue guidance, from its second-quarter release, is roughly four and a half billion dollars. So the Anthropic contract is worth about two and a half years of Akamai's entire revenue, concentrated in a single customer.

That's the counterparty logic from yesterday's briefing. We read Anthropic's leaked prospectus, with its reported $518 billion of infrastructure commitments, as a counterparty-credit document for its partners. Akamai wasn't one of the six partners named in that reporting. Now it has signed a seven-year contract of its own, with a lab that reportedly still runs a large operating loss. The difference is the balance sheet. This is the same exposure, carried by a company far smaller than Broadcom or Google.

The memory detail matters beyond Akamai. This week the semiconductor briefings tracked Nvidia, Samsung, and Micron all locking in memory supply. Now even an edge provider is buying ahead of the same squeeze. And one inference worth flagging. A warrant tied to usage expansion is a template that other infrastructure vendors chasing lab demand could copy. If they do, more of the dilution and balance-sheet risk shifts onto suppliers. So the Akamai story lands simply: the vendor bought its way into AI infrastructure relevance with its own equity and its own capex.

Oracle signed a similar single-customer, multi-year capacity deal this week, but with the risk running the other way.

The Financial Times reports that Tencent has signed a five-year lease with Oracle for roughly 100,000 advanced AI chips, hosted across Oracle data centers in Southeast Asia. The deal is reportedly worth about $7 billion, with roughly 30 percent paid upfront. Neither company has confirmed it, so treat it as press reporting sourced to people familiar with the matter. The FT calls it Tencent's largest overseas lease ever. It gives Tencent access to advanced chips that aren't available inside China. Leasing capacity abroad is the workaround.

Compare the risk structure with Akamai's. At Akamai, the supplier handed the customer equity and pre-funded the capex. At Oracle, the customer is prepaying nearly a third of the contract. That asymmetry tells you who holds pricing power. Akamai needed to buy its way in. Oracle is sitting on scarce capacity that a major Chinese buyer will pay for in advance.

The implication for multinational enterprises is that AI compute is becoming a tiered market. Access is priced by jurisdiction and by who is allowed to buy, not by a single global rate for GPU-hours. Expect arrangements like this to draw export-control scrutiny. That's a forward risk, not something that has happened yet. The so-what is simple. When capacity is scarce, the customer prepays. When the supplier is buying relevance, the supplier pays.

Capacity is one side of the ledger. The other side is who gets paid to make it useful inside the enterprise, and that brings us to Accenture.

According to financial press coverage of the results, Accenture's fiscal fourth-quarter revenue came in at $18.7 billion, up 7 percent year over year. New bookings reached $22.2 billion, for a book-to-bill of 1.2. The quarter included a record 141 client bookings worth at least $100 million each. Management also pointed to new agreements signed during the quarter with Google Cloud, Anthropic, and AWS. The shares jumped sharply.

Here's why that matters. The market has spent much of this year pricing systems integrators as structurally threatened by AI. The assumption is that agents replace the billable hours consultancies sell. A record mega-deal quarter cuts against that, at least for now.

But hold two caveats. First, bookings are not revenue. A 1.2 book-to-bill says demand is outrunning delivery today. It doesn't say this work carries classic consulting margins. Second, Accenture's bookings aren't split between AI and non-AI work. Those 141 mega-deals could be mostly traditional transformation and cost-cutting work, relabeled. And the Google, Anthropic, and AWS agreements are partnerships, not disclosed revenue.

There's also a competing read worth taking seriously. Enterprises may be paying integrators this much precisely because agent deployments are still hard to execute. That's a premium on immature tooling, and premiums like that tend to compress as the tooling matures. So the honest version isn't "AI isn't disrupting IT services." It's "not yet." And we can't tell whether this quarter is a new normal or a catch-up cycle.

One quick data point on the delivery side of that same budget, this one from GitHub's own blog. GitHub says Copilot now serves 140,000 organizations, nearly triple a year ago. That's an organization count, not seats or revenue, and no ARR came with it. The timing is what makes it useful. As we covered this week, GitHub's Copilot features flip to default-on on October 22nd. So this is a clean, pre-default baseline. Any jump after the 22nd should be discounted for auto-enrollment, not read as organic growth.

Put it together. Akamai bought its way into an AI contract with its own equity and capital. Oracle is getting paid upfront for compute that's genuinely scarce. Those are opposite risk postures in the same scramble for capacity. One level up, integrators are still capturing real enterprise dollars to make agentic AI work. My inference is that, for this year at least, some of that money isn't reaching standalone agent-platform startups. The marker to watch is Akamai's next 10-Q, and whether that capex line lands as planned.

That's the briefing for October 2nd. Have a good one.
