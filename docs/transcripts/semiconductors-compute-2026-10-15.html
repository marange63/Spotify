Semiconductors & Compute Infrastructure

Transcript · October 15, 2026

Good morning, it's Thursday, October 15th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

Here's the shape of the week. The two companies furthest upstream in the chip supply chain, ASML and TSMC, both just reported record quarters. ASML told the market, in its own words, that demand for its tools will outpace supply through at least 2028. The equity market sold the chip complex anyway. So this isn't a story about physical demand cooling. It's a fight over who keeps the margin, and whether the economics underneath that demand actually hold.

Start with TSMC, because it's the cleanest read on real AI accelerator demand. Its revenue reflects actual wafer starts, not a customer's roadmap slide. Third-quarter revenue came in at $46.7 billion, up 50.9% from a year earlier. That cleared the top of TSMC's own guidance range, which was $45.8 billion. Net income hit a sixth straight quarterly record.

The demand signal sits in the mix. On the call, management said HPC, its AI and high-performance-computing platform, grew 22% quarter over quarter. That platform is now 68% of TSMC's revenue. The forward guide is stronger still. Fourth-quarter revenue is guided to $52.3 to $54.3 billion, a 14% sequential jump at the midpoint. So wafer demand isn't plateauing. It's accelerating into year-end.

Now the part the market fixated on, which is margin. Gross margin was 66.4%. The marginal story is the 2-nanometer ramp. 2-nanometer went from 3% of wafer revenue in the second quarter to 8% in the third. CFO Wendell Huang said the quarter's margin decline was, quote, "primarily due to the margin dilution from the initial ramp-up" of 2-nanometer. He then guided fourth-quarter gross margin to 65 to 67%. The midpoint of that range sits only about 40 basis points below the third-quarter print.

The longer-dated drag is overseas fabs. Huang put that dilution at closer to 2 percentage points today. He expects it to widen to 3 to 4 points in the next few years. So the margin hit is real, but it builds slowly. It's a structural cost, not an immediate shock. Our read is that the market is pricing a multi-year trend early. And 66.4% is now the level investors are asking TSMC to defend, not a peak it has already banked.

On Tuesday we told you to listen for TSMC's tone on 2027 capex and advanced-packaging capacity. Here's what came back. 2026 capex stayed unchanged at $60 to $64 billion. On 2027, management said only that it's "a bit too early to share the CapEx number beyond this year's." On CoWoS, TSMC said it is "still the number one choice for our customer." No capacity figure came with it. Those are two non-answers on exactly the questions that matter. Meanwhile TSMC's ADR closed the session before the release down 3.4%, per Investing.com, and slipped further after hours. So TSMC delivered accelerating demand and modest near-term margin pressure, but no number on 2027. That silence carries information.

Why wouldn't TSMC raise capex in a record quarter? ASML, reporting a day earlier, offers the likeliest answer. ASML posted record third-quarter net sales of €11.7 billion, with a gross margin of 57.5%, above guidance. Net income was €5.0 billion. But about €500 million of that is a mark on ASML's stake in Mistral AI. That's investment-portfolio gain, not equipment earnings, so strip it out before reading the margin trend.

The sentences that matter came on the call. CEO Christophe Fouquet said demand will outpace supply "through at least 2028." He added that customers are "expressing interest in placing orders for 2029 and even 2030." CFO Roger Dassen went further. He said, quote, "our current planned levels of output in 2027 and 2028 will not fully meet the demand of our customers."

Be precise about what that evidence is. It's a seller describing demand for its own product. Interest in a 2029 slot is not a booked order. And on Tuesday we said to listen for ASML's bookings commentary. ASML disclosed no bookings figure this quarter. So the strongest claim in this print rests on management's word, not on a number you can audit.

Even so, it cuts against a narrative this show tracked on Monday and Tuesday. Fab-equipment names, ASML among them, sold off in mid-September on worry that AI spending would decelerate. ASML now says the binding constraint isn't customer appetite. It's how fast ASML can build tools. China held at about 20% of 2026 sales, consistent with prior expectations. So there's no export-control step change in this print, in either direction.

Now put ASML next to TSMC's capex silence. From the outside, capital discipline and a capacity ceiling look identical. TSMC may simply not yet know how much tool supply it will be able to deploy. On Monday we offered an inference: a slowdown in tool orders would tighten advanced-node capacity, not ease it. The evidence now says tools aren't slowing. They're supply-limited. But the conclusion holds either way. Leading-edge scarcity extends, which supports foundry pricing power, even as it cools hopes for a near-term capex jump.

Now the other side of that trade: this week's selloff. The trigger wasn't an earnings miss. It was commentary. Broadcom CEO Hock Tan spoke at a conference in Singapore on October 12th. He said, quote, "A lot of investors have no clue what they're investing in, but set very extreme expectations, only to find out that it's almost like they are in a casino gambling." Keep the speaker in mind. Tan sells AI silicon for a living. That's a remark about investor expectations, not a signal from Broadcom's own order book.

The same day, China's CXMT reported "key progress" on a new DRAM cell architecture. The company says a data-center server product is planned before year-end. Bloomberg described the claim as unsubstantiated. Be precise about what this is. It's a DRAM cell design, not an HBM product. It's a company statement that no independent lab or customer has verified. And scaled production would still run into unresolved export-control limits on equipment access.

The reaction was outsized anyway. By one market account, Samsung fell 13.39% in a single session, its steepest one-day drop since October 2008. SK hynix fell nearly as far. Micron fell about 4% on the CXMT headline. Tan's remark, the CXMT claim and macro worries all landed together, so no single trigger can be cleanly blamed.

Step back and look at the arc. Last week, as we covered Tuesday, the trigger was one AI lab's revenue figure. This week it took a CEO's offhand remark and an unverified Chinese claim to move the same complex. The evidentiary bar for moving these valuations keeps falling. And notice what's actually being priced. On Tuesday we flagged Acer's CEO arguing that new Chinese memory capacity would start easing prices in late 2027. The CXMT headline is the market pulling that commodity-DRAM case forward, years ahead of any confirmed shipment. It is not evidence that anyone is displacing Samsung or SK hynix in HBM.

The memory story connects to a second-order squeeze. Memory makers are reallocating wafer capacity toward high-bandwidth memory for AI data centers. That tightens supply of conventional DRAM. At the same time, TSMC's CEO warned of cost pressure in phones and PCs, despite booming AI orders. Put those together, and consumer electronics is squeezed from two directions. It faces pricier commodity memory, and a foundry whose growth is concentrated in AI. That's the clearest channel so far by which AI capex crowds out price-sensitive silicon.

So where does that leave the question we raised Tuesday, between demand constrained by compute availability and demand constrained by economics? TSMC and ASML answer only the first. Both describe a buildout that is supply-bound, not demand-bound. Neither speaks to whether the economics funding it can hold. That means the revenue hyperscalers and AI labs actually generate against this capex. That's the question this week's selloff is really about. The next tests are whether ASML ever puts a bookings number behind its 2029 language, and whether TSMC commits to a 2027 capex figure. Until then, the supply case looks firm, and the economic case is still open.

That's your briefing for October 15th. Stay cautiously optimistic.
