Good morning, it's Monday, August 3rd, 2026, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

Last week settled one question and opened another. The hyperscaler earnings arc from Alphabet, Microsoft, and Amazon showed that AI infrastructure spending is converting into real, bookable cloud revenue, not just capacity nobody's using. That fight is now largely won at the infrastructure layer. The fight this week has moved up the stack, to the application layer, where software vendors are racing to figure out who actually gets paid when an AI agent does the work.

The clearest evidence of that shift is ServiceNow. At its Knowledge 2026 event, ServiceNow introduced Action Fabric, a new integration layer that any external AI agent has to pass through before it can touch ServiceNow's workflow data or execute a task inside its platform. ServiceNow meters that traffic in units it calls "assists" and bills customers based on how many operations an agent completes, on top of the existing subscription. That's a structural repricing, the same move cloud infrastructure made a decade ago when it shifted from flat licensing to consumption billing. It matters because it hands pricing power to whoever owns the underlying workflow data. If your AI agent, built by Anthropic or OpenAI or anyone else, wants to act on a customer's ServiceNow tickets or Salesforce records, it now has to pass through a toll booth the incumbent controls. That's a real moat, because it taxes third-party agent traffic rather than just competing with it.

Two things to be careful about here, though. First, ServiceNow has not published a per-assist dollar price. Multiple sources confirm that pricing is quote-based and negotiated account by account under NDA, so there's no public rate card to compare across vendors, and you should treat any specific price you hear elsewhere with real skepticism. Second, you may have heard that ServiceNow's AI-specific revenue crossed a billion dollars. That figure doesn't hold up under scrutiny. The billion-dollar milestone ServiceNow actually disclosed at Knowledge 2026 belongs to its Security and Risk business, a separate, non-AI product line reaching a billion dollars in annual contract value. Worth flagging, because it's an easy number to misattribute, and it's a good reminder to distrust any AI monetization figure that isn't tied to a specific, named business line.

What is real and quotable is a production customer story. At Knowledge 2026, ServiceNow put Rolls-Royce on stage. Rolls-Royce has rolled out ServiceNow's Now Assist, an AI agent branded internally as Merlin, to 12,000 of its 45,000 employees. Two numbers stand out. It has deflected or resolved 38,000 IT incidents through predictive intelligence, and it has handed 300,000 hours of shop-floor time back to the engineers actually building jet engines. Mean time to resolve is now down to two days. That's Now Assist, not Action Fabric specifically, a related product rather than the metered layer itself. But it's the strongest evidence available that agentic AI is doing real, sustained production work inside a major industrial company, not just running pilots. Salesforce, for its part, reported Agentforce annual recurring revenue of $1.2 billion in its fiscal first quarter, up 205% year over year. That number is worth citing as trend context, but it's ten weeks old at this point, from the May 27th earnings report, so don't mistake it for a fresh signal this week. Put together, the picture is incumbents with the deepest wells of enterprise data building the metering infrastructure to capture agent value, with at least one named customer proving the usage exists. So what: the application layer is where the next round of software economics gets decided, and data ownership, not model quality, is becoming the moat.

Except the meter needs something to meter, and that's where the second story of the week complicates the first. Cognizant launched a dedicated EMEA AI unit on July 28th, built specifically around what it describes as an IDC-documented 88% failure rate for agentic AI pilots trying to reach production. Cognizant's diagnosis is that most failures trace back to inadequate retrieval-augmented-generation, meaning the agent isn't properly connected to a company's actual contracts, inventory, or records, so it falls back on generic training knowledge and produces confident, wrong answers. Take that 88% figure with real caution. Cognizant is selling the remediation service, so it has an obvious commercial incentive to make the problem sound large, and the underlying IDC report itself wasn't independently locatable. But directionally, it lines up with what ServiceNow's own case studies imply: the vendors with production wins, like Rolls-Royce, are the exception, not the norm. So what: the near-term money in enterprise AI is shifting toward integration and data-grounding services, the plumbing that gets an agent connected to real company data, rather than toward the agent software itself. That's a tailwind for systems integrators and for data platforms like Snowflake and Databricks that own the grounding layer, and a caution sign for platform vendors selling agent seats on the assumption that deployment is straightforward.

Then there's a second wall, and this one's regulatory rather than technical. August 2nd, yesterday, was the enforcement date for the EU AI Act's high-risk system requirements, covering AI used in employment, credit, education, and law enforcement decisions. Companies now face binding obligations around risk management, data governance, human oversight, and conformity assessment for those use cases. As of April, an estimated 78% of organizations had taken no meaningful steps toward compliance, and a proposed delay to 2027 was never actually enacted into law, so the deadline stands as written. Treat that 78% figure as directional rather than precise, and it's genuinely unclear yet whether regulators will enforce aggressively from day one or allow a good-faith remediation period. Either way, this lands directly on the highest-value agentic use cases in HR and lending, exactly where enterprises had the most incentive to deploy. So what: expect a near-term surge in demand for AI governance and compliance tooling, and expect US-headquartered companies with EU operations to start splitting their AI rollout roadmaps by region rather than deploying globally in lockstep.

One piece of plumbing worth a quick mention. Anthropic updated the Model Context Protocol specification on July 28th, moving it from a stateful, bidirectional design to a simpler request-response model that can run on serverless infrastructure, and adding enterprise-managed authentication so IT admins can provision agent connectors organization-wide through Okta or Entra with zero-touch setup. MCP has now passed 400 million monthly SDK downloads, up four times this year. That's a developer-adoption number, not a production-usage number, so don't read too much into it. But statelessness and IT-managed identity are exactly the two things standing between MCP and being infrastructure a CIO would actually approve, and this update closes both gaps.

Put it all together and the pattern for the week is consistent: enterprise software vendors are building the metering, the toll booths, and the interoperability plumbing for an agent economy faster than enterprises can actually get agents into production or past their own regulators. The pipes are being laid ahead of the traffic. Watch whether that gap closes over the next few quarters, or whether it becomes the story that defines this entire product cycle.

That's your briefing for August 3rd. Have a good day.
