Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Credit & the Corporate Credit Cycle

Transcript · November 27, 2026

Good morning, it's Friday, November 27th. This desk has spent the week hunting for real prices on private credit, and the week turned up two new ones. One is a pension that would rather borrow against its fund stakes than sell them. The other is a loan book that sold close to where it was carried. Together they say more about where this cycle stands than another round of spread commentary would.

Start with the pension. Bloomberg reported on November 24th that the New York State Common Retirement Fund is considering raising about four billion dollars through a collateralized fund obligation. The collateral would be its own stakes in around twenty private credit funds, run by Blackstone, Blue Owl, Sixth Street, Pimco and Ares. The pension oversees about three hundred ten billion dollars, so four billion is roughly one point three percent of the fund. That's the desk's arithmetic. It marks this as a portfolio-management choice, not a fire sale. The reporting calls it one of the biggest deals of its kind, and a rare one.

Here's the structure. The vehicle would sell a debt tranche to outside investors, and the pension would keep the equity tranche. That detail is the whole story. Keeping the equity means the pension stays exposed to the first losses if the funds' cash flows weaken, and the reporting says exactly that. So this isn't the pension shedding private credit risk. It's the pension borrowing against risk it already owns. And that new leverage sits on top of whatever leverage those twenty funds already carry. Financing, not selling.

Why borrow instead of selling the stakes in the secondary market? The reporting doesn't say, so treat these as competing possibilities. One is freeing up cash for new commitments elsewhere. Another is bridging a slower pace of distributions from these funds. A third is getting liquidity without taking the discount a straight secondary sale of LP stakes would demand. All three are plausible, and nothing reported lets us pick one.

Two caveats. First, everyone involved declined to comment. The reporting itself says the terms and the fund selection could change, and the deal may still fall through. Second, no coupon, rating or leverage ratio for this structure has been reported anywhere. Those terms simply aren't public yet.

What we do have is a precedent for how skittish buyers can be about this kind of leverage. In August, Bloomberg reported that Blackstone was weighing whether to abandon a roughly three billion dollar CFO of its own. Blackstone went on to shelve it. Per that reporting, buyers balked at the age of the underlying private-equity stakes. They also balked at the leverage stacked on top of them. The junior debt had been marketed at yields of up to twelve percent, and even that didn't clear. Blackstone's collateral was private equity rather than private credit, but the leverage-on-leverage question is the same. So twelve percent is the bar New York will be measured against. If New York's debt has to pay materially more to place, the market is pricing LP-stake collateral more cautiously than it did in August.

Next, who would actually buy this debt. A CFO-market specialist quoted by Alternative Credit Investor says insurers take the top-rated tranche almost every time. In his words, ninety-nine percent of the time, the insurance companies come into the Class A. Asset managers, hedge funds and specialty lenders take the rest of the stack. That changes how to read the outcome. A failed or repriced placement wouldn't just mean buyers got cautious. It would be a direct read on how insurance risk committees price private-credit fund collateral today. And there's a regulatory tripwire underneath. Under the NAIC's bond definition, in force since January 2025, its valuation office can challenge a CFO note's rating if it judges the rating understates the risk. The same specialist says that lever has been quiet so far, with none of the ratings on his firm's deals challenged. Our inference is that a wave of pension-originated CFOs could be what finally tests it.

So what's the takeaway on New York? The risk is being restructured, not removed. And it extends a pattern. Yesterday this desk covered Blue Owl's non-traded BDCs, where redemption requests ran as high as thirty-nine percent of shares against a five percent cap. That was the liquidity search at the retail end. This week it reaches a three hundred ten billion dollar institutional LP, though by choice rather than under duress.

If a pension can borrow against its fund stakes, the next question is what a real buyer would pay for the loans themselves. A Goldman Sachs deal gives one answer. PitchBook reported on November 25th that Goldman is closing a GP-led secondary sale of about one and a half billion dollars of private credit assets. The seller is its non-traded BDC, Goldman Sachs Private Middle Market Credit Two. The price is ninety-four seventy-five. Per the report, that's close to where the fund had been carrying those loans.

Put that against the arc this desk has been tracking. On Saturday it was a BDC buying back its own shares below NAV. Yesterday it was BC Partners' November 11th agreement to buy Investcorp's BDC at ninety cents on NAV. Goldman's book clearing near its marks breaks that run of discounts. It's also the third queue-management option we named yesterday, selling loans, done as a whole portfolio. The reporting doesn't say redemptions drove it, though.

Don't read it as proof the marks are fine, for four reasons. First, the size is stated as of March 31st values, so the valuation basis is eight months old. Second, in a GP-led sale the seller picks the loans, so this may be the strongest slice of the book. Third, it's a single source, and Goldman declined to comment. Fourth, ninety-four seventy-five is still more than five points below par. The fund had already marked that book down before anyone bought it. The honest read is dispersion. A large platform's book held up in a sale, while a subscale manager's book did not. That's a story about manager quality, not a sector-wide verdict.

There's a quieter effect too, and this is the desk's inference, not a reported fact about Goldman's fund. When a manager sells its best or most liquid loans, the book left for investors who stay can end up weaker on average. It's the same dynamic we flagged yesterday when we walked through how managers work a redemption queue.

Both of those prices were one-offs. Apollo is spending to make price discovery routine. On its third-quarter call on November 3rd, Apollo's chief executive, Marc Rowan, said the firm will spend roughly three hundred fifty million dollars over the next twenty-four months to make private credit more tradable. The build covers daily valuations, market-making, and custody and settlement. That's the structural answer to the mark-to-model problem this beat keeps circling. If it works, price discovery stops waiting for a downturn to force it. Our inference is that it would also compress the illiquidity premium the asset class gets paid for. But this is a manager selling its own platform. A CUSIP and a daily model price are not the same thing as a bid when conditions turn.

And the front door is still open even as investors test the exits. Earlier this month, Blackstone closed its fourth European senior direct lending fund, reporting thirteen billion euros of investable capital. That figure spans the fund and other European direct lending vehicles, so it isn't a single fund's commitments. Inflows tell you about appetite. They don't tell you about deployment or performance.

So where does the cycle stand? This week the pressure showed up in liquidity and structure. An LP is engineering leverage against stakes it can't easily sell, and a BDC is selling loans out of its book. Where the marks have actually been tested, they've roughly held, with real dispersion by manager. The biggest unresolved risk is leverage layered on top of model-marked NAVs. That leverage turns a modest markdown into a first-loss hit for whoever holds the equity, and in New York's case that's the pension itself.

Watch three things. First, whether New York's debt tranche places, and at what yield against Blackstone's twelve percent. Second, whether Goldman's sale actually closes at ninety-four seventy-five. Third, fourth-quarter BDC marks and tender results across the sector, read against the two new benchmarks of ninety-four seventy-five and ninety cents on NAV.

That's the briefing. Have a good Friday.
