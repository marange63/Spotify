Good morning. It's Monday, September 28th, 2026.

Here's the thesis for today. This week Washington got prices and paper on the easy inputs. The binding ones stayed exactly where they were, rationed by whoever physically controls them.

Start with the summit. The White House fact sheet from the Trump-Xi summit is out, and this time it has numbers. China will import at least 10 million metric tons of American coal in 2027, and at least 10 million tons again in 2028. Those are two separate annual pledges, not one combined total. Both sides will also recommend more favorable tariff treatment for 30 billion dollars of what the fact sheet calls non-sensitive goods, in each direction.

The fact sheet also tells us what that 30 billion actually is. On the American export side, it's agricultural goods, seafood, wood products, cosmetics and medical devices. On the Chinese side, it's small appliances, toys, holiday decorations and children's car seats. There are no tariff rates and no effective dates. That detail matters more than the headline number. This is a scope, not a schedule. And it's a scope built entirely out of goods neither government considers strategic. A list with no rates and no dates is also easy for either side to slow-walk without ever technically breaching it.

What's missing from the list tells you as much as what's on it. China excluded soybeans, its single largest American farm import, from its agricultural tariff cuts. That's the commodity with the most political weight in US farm states. And on rare earths, the fact sheet says both sides "continue to work on" supply-chain concerns, "with the goal of ensuring shipment levels return to appropriate levels." Sit with that sentence for a second. It is the US government's own document conceding, in writing, that rare-earth shipments are currently below appropriate levels. A senior official made that complaint on September 20th, before the summit. Now it's written into the deliverable itself. It is the one American grievance that no round of diplomacy has moved.

The market consequence follows directly, and this part is analysis. Nothing in this package lets a magnet-exposed buyer stop paying for redundancy. Automakers, defense primes, and anyone else building on rare-earth magnets still has to pay for a second source. So non-China separation and magnet capacity, the kind of exposure MP Materials and Lynas represent, stays economically justified whatever the truce status. The package extends the case for building rather than waiting.

Now the AI piece, and here the evidence cuts in Beijing's favor, so give it its due. China's own account of the summit, relayed through Xinhua, confirms an AI dialogue and a channel for future AI-related incidents. It sets the next round for November. The White House fact sheet says the next exchange will occur by November as well. So this is genuinely bilateral, not a one-sided press release.

That supports the managed-rivalry reading, and it deserves a fair hearing. Small, institutionalized exchanges plus a standing dialogue are exactly how a de-escalation architecture gets built. That lowers tail risk heading into the January expiry of the US-China tariff truce, even if rare earths never get fixed. Here's what would strengthen that reading from here: the November round actually happening, and rare-earth shipments visibly moving before January.

But notice what China's account leaves out. On rare earths, in the words of the reporting, China made no mention of the issue at all. That isn't a vague promise. It's silence. And the US Trade Representative has said chip export controls weren't on the AI-safety agenda. Put those together. Beijing is willing to institutionalize the low-stakes AI track precisely because it doesn't touch chips or minerals. The non-strategic layer of this relationship is normalizing. The coercive layer isn't being negotiated at all.

Rare earths are the input Washington can't negotiate for. Electricity is the one it has to pay for, at home, right now.

Which brings us to PJM, the country's largest grid operator. On Friday we reported that PJM's regular capacity auction cleared at its price cap and still came up 6,831 megawatts short of its reliability requirement. It was the second straight year PJM missed its reserve-margin target. Our read was that when a market pays its ceiling and gets almost nothing new, price isn't the binding constraint.

This Wednesday, September 30th, PJM tests that argument directly. It opens a one-time backstop procurement with a price cap of 555 dollars per megawatt-day. That is nearly 70 percent above the cap on its regular annual auction. PJM has authorized up to 20 billion dollars in spending, with contracts running as long as 15 years. Nothing has been procured yet, and results are due in December.

Why is PJM paying this much? PJM's own analysis attributes 63 percent of the earlier jump in its capacity prices, between the 2024 and 2026 delivery years, to data-center load growth. That's the one number to hold onto. It means the backstop is, in large part, the marginal cost of AI compute showing up as a regulated capacity bill.

Two readings compete here. In the first, the higher price plus the long tenor is finally enough to make new generation bankable. On that view, tenor was the missing piece for financing all along. In the second, the constraint was never money. It's turbines, interconnection queues and permitting. On that view, this just re-prices existing plants and delays retirements without pulling forward a single new megawatt. December decides which one is right. If new build still doesn't show up, the constraint is concrete, not capital.

Either way, the distribution is already clear. Ratepayers pay for the answer. The winners from the wait are owners of existing dispatchable capacity inside PJM's footprint, the independent power producers. And that bill feeds the same data-center siting politics we tracked in Texas on Thursday.

The third rationed input is Russian crude, and here Washington's lever is sanctions, not price. Bloomberg reports that Russia's overseas crude volumes dipped to 3.53 million barrels a day over the four weeks through September 20th. Bloomberg's own word is "nudged," not squeezed. We only have the lede, not the full piece. And nothing in it separates sanctions from returning Saudi competition in Asia, or from ordinary four-week noise. So treat it as a marginal dip, not a verdict.

The more telling evidence is how the trade has adapted. By May, the Kyiv School of Economics tracker showed the US-sanctioned majors, Rosneft and Lukoil, back in control of Russian crude exports. By July, 53 percent of Russia's seaborne oil was moving on sanctioned shadow-fleet tankers, according to the Centre for Research on Energy and Clean Air. And that same month, Urals crude averaged about 60 dollars a barrel. That's well above the 44 dollar price cap the EU and UK still enforce on paper.

So the sanctions architecture shapes the route and the discount Russia takes, far more than it shapes the volume. It leaks by adaptation, and the market adapts within a quarter. With the price cap carrying little current bite, the real leverage has shifted to Washington's own secondary measures. The Russia sanctions law signed on September 18th requires tariffs on goods from the five largest buyers of Russian crude and gas. Those duties are due to rise within 30 days of enactment, which puts the deadline around October 18th. The law itself names no country, so deciding who counts as a top buyer is the administration's call. No top buyer has been designated. The President can still waive. That is the next real test.

That pressure is why Moscow's latest position is worth reading closely. In the current round of shuttle diplomacy, Russia rejected proposals for an energy or aerial ceasefire. It restated its demand for Ukrainian withdrawal from parts of Donbas and recognition of occupied territory. Russia's presidential envoy, Kirill Dmitriev, met US envoys Witkoff and Kushner in the US on September 24th. Both sides have agreed only in principle to resume trilateral talks, potentially in October. Refusing even to pause energy strikes, while your own export revenue is under sanctions pressure, is a signal about how much fiscal room Moscow thinks it still has. One open question: Dmitriev runs an economic and investment channel, not a military one. Whether that meeting was really about sanctions relief, the reporting can't yet tell us.

So, what to watch. Wednesday, whether PJM's backstop window draws real bids for new generation. October 18th, whether Washington names the top buyers of Russian crude for those tariffs, or waives. November, whether the AI dialogue actually convenes. And January, whether rare-earth shipments move before the US-China tariff truce runs out. Every one of those is a physical or administrative fact, not a press release. That's the distinction that decides who's actually gaining leverage.
