Good morning, it's Tuesday, November 17th, and this is your orbital economy briefing.

Yesterday this desk could only report the Vulcan loss through unconfirmed headlines. Today it's confirmed. And the story is bigger than Amazon.

Start with the facts. ULA's Vulcan Centaur failed about ten minutes after launch from Cape Canaveral on Sunday, November 15th, according to Spaceflight Now. All forty Amazon Leo satellites aboard were lost. This was Vulcan's fifth flight, and its first in nine months. SpacePolicyOnline calls it the first total failure in ULA's history. ULA's same-day statement said it had no specific data yet on what occurred. No root cause has been published since.

One caution on the timing. A failure ten minutes into flight does not, by itself, tell you which stage failed. A booster that underperforms can also show up late in the ascent. So any account of the stage at fault is speculation until ULA says otherwise. What isn't speculation is the context. This was the return-to-flight mission. It was, in effect, Vulcan's requalification flight. And it failed.

That matters most to a customer that wasn't even on board: the Space Force. After a second booster incident in February, the Space Force suspended Vulcan from national-security launches, per Space.com. By the same reporting, four missions originally booked on Vulcan have already moved to SpaceX's Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy. KeepTrack names one of them. The GPS III SV-10 navigation satellite was pulled off Vulcan and booked on a Falcon 9 under that earlier suspension. So the drift toward SpaceX predates Sunday. It is not a reaction to it. What Sunday removes is the near-term chance that the drift reverses.

The program most exposed is missile warning. Next-Gen OPIR satellites sit on Vulcan's manifest. In KeepTrack's tracking from before the failure, their status read: waiting, no near-term backup identified. No source has said whether that changed after Sunday. Nor has the Space Force announced any new or extended suspension.

Now a distinction worth keeping straight. Golden Dome is not the program at risk here. KeepTrack reports that all eighteen launches in a one point six billion dollar Golden Dome tranche went to SpaceX back on July 29th. That money never rode on Vulcan. But it rhymes with a point this desk made on Sunday. Golden Dome's first phase rewards hardware already flying at scale. Vulcan's failure is the same logic run in reverse. The rocket that isn't flying loses ground by default.

There is a steelman for ULA. By this desk's read, one failure on a fifth flight is not unusual for a new heavy vehicle. The real question is how long the grounding lasts, not whether Vulcan is finished. Still, the cost lands on ULA's parents, Boeing and Lockheed Martin. Our inference is that a failed requalification flight dents ULA's credibility with commercial constellation customers. And it does so just as its national-security manifest has already been thinned. So the Pentagon's heavy-lift bench just got thinner. In practice, it was already one vendor deep.

The customer that was on board has a different clock running. Amazon Leo, formerly Project Kuiper, stands at 398 satellites across 14 launches, per CNBC. That's about twelve percent of the 3,236 it needs. Counts vary by tracker, so treat CNBC's figure as the reference point. The bigger problem is regulatory. Amazon missed the FCC's interim milestone of 1,616 satellites, which fell due on July 30th. That was months before Sunday. So Vulcan didn't create Amazon's shortfall. It makes the shortfall harder to close.

And the backup isn't available either. Blue Origin's New Glenn, Amazon's other US heavy lifter outside SpaceX, is targeting a January return, with no firm day set. That leaves both of Amazon's US alternatives to SpaceX sidelined at once. The most reliable near-term lift is Falcon 9. And Falcon 9 belongs to SpaceX, the company that runs Starlink, Amazon's direct broadband competitor. No FCC enforcement action or Amazon waiver request has been reported. Don't get ahead of the regulator.

Here's the second-order effect, and it's this desk's inference. When the Space Force and Amazon both route work to one provider, that provider gains scheduling leverage and pricing power. For the Pentagon, that's single-vendor risk. For Amazon, it's worse. Its rival effectively sets the pace of its build-out. So Amazon's license clock and its launch clock are now both running against it.

Widen the lens and the contrast sharpens. China's state-linked constellations are not waiting on anyone. Orbital Radar's count as of November 16th puts about 240 Guowang satellites and 266 Qianfan satellites on orbit. The tracker says deployment is accelerating. Treat those counts as approximate, since trackers differ. Each Chinese constellation on its own is still smaller than Amazon Leo. Together, at roughly five hundred, they now outnumber it. That isn't a breakaway. Both programs are still early in their build-outs. The honest read is that steady cadence from a low base beats a stalled program. Right now China has the cadence, while two of the three US heavy lifters sit idle.

All of this flows to SpaceX. But be precise about where it doesn't flow: Starship. Flight 15 did hit a genuine milestone. CNBC now confirms the tower caught the upper stage returning from orbit on Saturday, a first. On Sunday this desk could only call that well reported. The ship also deployed twenty-six V3 Starlinks, matching the prior flight. So Starship already works as a satellite truck.

The booster is the problem. Super Heavy diverted to the Gulf of Mexico and exploded before hitting the water, instead of returning for its catch. SpaceX still hasn't given an official cause. Per Wikipedia's running list of Starship launches, all four V3 boosters flown, on Flights 12 through 15, have been lost. Be careful with that number. It is not four failed catches, because not every one of those flights attempted a return. The fair statement is narrower. No V3 booster has yet been recovered. And there is still no date for Flight 16.

So the capacity the Pentagon and Amazon now lean on runs on Falcon. Falcon is a mature rocket. It is not the fully reusable architecture that's supposed to crater the cost per kilogram. That step-change hasn't arrived.

For SPCX holders, two readings follow, and both are this desk's inference. First, Falcon's role as the only dependable US heavy lifter strengthens the launch business's near-term cash generation, even before Starship reuse is proven. Second, it deepens the political exposure this desk flagged on Sunday. The more the Pentagon depends on SpaceX, the more SpaceX depends on defense politics. SpaceX's near-term dominance is real. It just isn't yet the cost breakthrough the bull case has priced in.

So where does that leave things? What's genuinely inflecting is SpaceX's practical monopoly on reliable US heavy lift. It didn't come from a strategic masterstroke. It came because the alternatives keep failing or slipping. Still a demo or a setback are Vulcan's requalification, Starship's booster reuse, and Amazon's deployment math against its FCC clock. Rocket Lab's Neutron, the other would-be challenger, has yet to fly at all. The open questions are how long Vulcan stays grounded, and whether the FCC leans on Amazon over its missed milestone.

Watch four catalysts. First, an official ULA or Space Force statement on cause and national-security status. Second, the next Amazon Leo flight, on an Ariane 6 targeted for no earlier than November 23rd. Third, any SpaceX word on the Flight 15 booster abort, and a date for Flight 16. And fourth, New Glenn's targeted return in January.

That's the briefing. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
