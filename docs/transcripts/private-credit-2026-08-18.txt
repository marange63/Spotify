Good morning, it's Tuesday, August 18th, 2026, and this is the private credit briefing.

The turn we flagged Monday at one fund is now confirmed across the whole market. Fitch Ratings said the trailing twelve-month US private credit default rate hit a record 6% in the twelve months ended April 30th. That's the headline print. But the more important number is underneath it. Of 99 default events in the period, 81 were first-time defaulters, the highest count since Fitch started tracking this series in August 2024. That matters because it means loans written in the 2021 and 2022 vintage — underwritten in the cheapest-money, loosest-covenant years of the cycle — are meeting a real downturn for the first time. The "seasoned, resilient book" pitch that direct lenders sell to insurers and wealth-channel investors is being tested, not confirmed.

Now, read the 6% figure skeptically, because it's flattered rather than overstated. More than half of those defaults were driven by interest-payment deferrals and the conversion of cash interest to payment-in-kind, where a borrower pays with more debt instead of cash. That's the classic amend-and-extend mechanism: it keeps a loan technically performing on paper while the underlying credit quality erodes underneath it. So the true stress in the book is probably worse than 6% suggests, not better — deferral and PIK are ways of postponing the recognition of a problem, not resolving it. Sector-level detail backs that up: consumer products companies had the highest default rate at 11.1%, roughly double the 5.9% rate a year earlier — a straightforward read on where consumer spending stress is landing first inside private credit portfolios.

Here's the tension the industry has to explain away: fundraising kept accelerating right through this. Private credit funds raised $119 billion in the second quarter alone, bringing the first-half total to $190 billion, up 53% year-on-year. That's the textbook late-cycle pattern — capital keeps flooding in exactly as loss rates rise, which typically means more dry powder chasing a shrinking pool of good deals, which compresses spreads further just as the market should be demanding more compensation for risk, not less. So the asset-quality story is deteriorating market-wide, even as the marketing story says the opposite.

If that's the asset side breaking down, the liability side is where it's actually showing up first, and Blue Owl is the live case study. Blue Owl had proposed merging its non-traded vehicle, OBDC II, into its listed BDC, OBDC. Investors pushed back, redemption requests exceeded the standard 5% quarterly cap, and in November Blue Owl terminated the merger, with CEO Craig Packer citing what the company's own release called "current market conditions." Rather than force holders into an exchange, Blue Owl permanently halted quarterly redemptions at OBDC II and shifted to ratable return-of-capital distributions instead — funded by selling $1.4 billion of direct-lending loans across three of its funds.

Now, here's where I need to correct the more alarmist read of this story. The obvious question was whether those loans sold below their carrying marks — that would be the first real mark-to-market check on private credit's mark-to-model valuations, the moment retail-forced selling exposes stale NAVs. It didn't happen, at least not here. Blue Owl's own release states the $1.4 billion sold at 99.7% of par value, described as fair value. That's essentially book value. So this transaction is not evidence that private marks were badly stale for this particular pool of loans — if anything it's modest evidence the opposite. The liquidity mismatch in the retail vehicle is real: OBDC II is paying out a return of capital worth up to $2.35 a share, roughly 30% of its net asset value, because it had to manufacture liquidity it didn't otherwise have. But the valuation-opacity thesis — that private marks are quietly wrong — doesn't get proven by this sale. It's a genuine liquidity-management event, not yet a valuation-marking event. Keep watching for the next forced sale; this one just isn't it.

The redemption-pressure framing needs the same correction. Reporting earlier this month suggested redemptions at two other Blue Owl non-traded vehicles, OCIC and OTIC, were expected to peak near 28.5% and 52.9% of assets. Company-level disclosure tells a different story: OCIC's redemption requests actually fell to $3.6 billion in the second quarter, down from $4.2 billion the quarter before, with the standard 5% cap still binding. So at OCIC specifically, the pressure eased quarter-over-quarter rather than accelerating toward a peak. The liquidity strain across Blue Owl's retail vehicles is still real — a merger collapsed, a fund is gating redemptions permanently — but it looks more like a contained, already-being-managed problem than an escalating one.

And the compensation for taking that illiquidity risk has been shrinking the whole time. Direct-lending loans originated in 2017 and 2018 paid BDCs a spread advantage of more than 300 basis points over syndicated leveraged loans. By the first quarter of this year, that premium had collapsed to under 100 basis points. So retail holders locked into these gated vehicles are being asked to give up liquidity for barely a third of the extra yield the illiquidity premium used to pay — reported yield and risk-adjusted return pulling apart at exactly the moment the liquidity risk is being tested for real.

What is unambiguously new and negative is the fundraising divergence. Fitch's aggregate number says private credit inflows are up 53% year-on-year. Blue Owl's own quarter tells the opposite story: overall fundraising fell roughly 16% from the prior quarter and 37% year-on-year, credit fundraising hit a three-year low of $1.8 billion, and inflows from wealthy individuals — the retail channel this whole industry is racing to scale — dropped to $1.7 billion from $4.4 billion a year earlier. So the "capital keeps coming" narrative is now diverging from what's actually happening in the vehicle built to take retail money. That's the piece of this story that should worry allocators more than the loan-sale pricing.

One callback before we close. The Delaware Life and Clear Spring probe we detailed Monday — insurers tied to Mark Walter's business empire that understated related-party private credit exposure, restated from roughly 3% to more than 39% of invested assets — has widened. Fitch placed Delaware Life on negative ratings watch in July, and reporting this week says federal prosecutors are now focused on four Walter-linked businesses as part of the DOJ and SEC probes. No charges have been filed. But it's the same structural thread as everything else today: opacity in how private credit is sourced and marked doesn't surface until a filing, a redemption wave, or a subpoena forces it out.

So where does the cycle actually stand? It's turning market-wide, not at one fund — Fitch's 6% confirms that — but the print understates true stress because PIK and deferral are keeping troubled loans technically current. The binding risk isn't spread levels; it's valuation opacity and liquidity mismatch in the retail vehicles built to absorb this asset class's growth. The Blue Owl episode shows that mismatch is real, but it also shows the marks held up this time, which cuts against the most alarmist version of the story. Watch three things from here: whether the next forced private-credit asset sale prices closer to par or well below it, whether other non-traded BDCs follow Blue Owl into permanent redemption gates, and whether Apollo and Ares disclose their own default and deployment detail when they report — data we didn't get this round.
