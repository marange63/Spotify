Good morning, it's Thursday, August 13th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

All week this show has treated two things in the AI-compute boom as one-way bets: memory's pricing power, and the durability of every marginal unit of accelerator demand. This week, both showed a first crack, even as the physical volume of chips shipping keeps setting records.

Start with memory, because it's the lead. SK hynix and Samsung both reported blowout Q2 numbers at the end of July. SK hynix's revenue and operating profit rose 257 and 557 percent year over year, and its operating margin hit 76 percent, according to the company's own results release. Those are not soft numbers. But when you look at the pricing detail underneath them, a gap opens up. On their own earnings calls, SK hynix quantified its DRAM average selling price as rising approximately 30 percent quarter over quarter, and Samsung put its DRAM ASP increase at mid-40 percent. Sell-side desks, per reporting from the Motley Fool, had been modeling roughly 39 percent for SK hynix and roughly 48 percent for Samsung. Both companies landed below what Wall Street had extrapolated.

So is that a crack in demand, or just a crack in the extrapolation? The evidence points toward the extrapolation. Start with the size of the moves that did land. NAND pricing rose even faster than DRAM — up mid-50 percent at SK hynix and high-60 percent at Samsung, quarter over quarter. That is not the pricing curve of a market losing its bid; it's a market that rose almost as fast as forecast, not one that reversed. On SK hynix's call, management said plainly that major customers are still requesting more memory supply — again, not the language of a market cooling off. The company also disclosed it concluded long-term supply agreement negotiations with roughly ten customers during the quarter, and Micron has separately said about a fifth of its DRAM sales and a third of its NAND sales now sit inside multi-year contracts. More volume is rotating into locked-in agreements rather than trading at spot-like prices, and that mechanically pulls the blended ASP increase below what a pure spot-market model would predict. Samsung's own language on these contracts describes them as minimum price floors that protect the company's downside, not explicitly as caps on the upside — so treat the pricing structure as a hedge against volatility on both sides, not proof that customers are winning a pricing fight.

The so-what here: memory makers are trading peak-cycle upside for demand certainty, and that's a read-through risk for equity narratives priced on unconstrained scarcity, not a signal that AI compute demand itself is turning over. And there's a flip side that cuts the other way. If realized memory costs rise less steeply than the scarcity models feared, that's a modest tailwind for the people buying the memory — the hyperscalers and GPU-system builders whose bill of materials leans heavily on HBM and DRAM. So the same data point that pressures memory-maker multiples slightly relieves the cost side of the AI-infrastructure buildout. One more thing worth banking: SK hynix began mass shipments of next-generation HBM4 in the second quarter, with the fuller production ramp pushed into the second half of the year, meaning the tightest part of the memory story is still ahead, not behind.

That question of demand — how solid is it, and does all of it even run through channels Washington is comfortable with — leads to the other soft spot this week. Bloomberg reported that the Commerce Department's enforcement arm is now systematically examining how Chinese AI firms access advanced Nvidia processors without importing them at all — specifically, by renting compute physically located in other countries. That's a different problem than the smuggling cases regulators have chased for years. Remote access to that compute is not, at present, illegal, which is exactly why this review matters: it's the first sign Washington is looking at closing a legal channel rather than policing an illegal one, and legal-channel enforcement is structurally harder to write rules for. The trigger is notable too — the review reportedly followed Moonshot AI's release of its Kimi K3 model, which scored close to the latest Anthropic and OpenAI systems on benchmarks and prompted questions about how the compute behind it was obtained. Chinese model performance is now itself functioning as an intelligence signal that triggers export-control scrutiny — a new feedback loop worth watching. Treat this as reported, not enacted: it's a review, sourced to unnamed officials, with no primary Commerce Department document and no Nvidia response on the record. If it does harden into a rule, expect it to route pressure toward domestic Chinese accelerators and toward due-diligence obligations for cloud hosts in third countries.

Now the counterweight, because the raw volume underneath all of this is not cracking at all. TSMC's July revenue came in at a record, up 44.7 percent year over year, a number this show already flagged Tuesday. What's marginally new is what came with it: TSMC has raised its full-year 2026 revenue growth guidance to slightly above 40 percent in dollar terms, and lifted its 2026 capital spending range to 60 to 64 billion dollars, according to reporting on last month's earnings call. And the mix tells you what's driving it — high-performance computing, the segment where TSMC books its AI-accelerator revenue, was 66 percent of second-quarter sales. So this is an AI record, not a broad-based one. A capital-disciplined foundry operator does not raise capex that far, against a demand base concentrated that heavily in AI, without order-book conviction behind it.

So here's how the two halves of this briefing fit together: shipped volume at the leading foundry keeps accelerating without hesitation, while one layer above it — realized pricing at the memory makers, and one channel of downstream demand into China — each showed their first real fragility this week. That's not a contradiction. It's a market where allocation, not price, is still the thing customers are fighting over.

Watch two things from here: whether the memory pricing gap widens or closes in the September quarter, and whether the BIS review produces an actual rule rather than remaining a review. That's the briefing for today. Thanks for listening, and I'll be back with you tomorrow.
