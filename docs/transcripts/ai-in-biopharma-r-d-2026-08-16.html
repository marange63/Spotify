AI in Biopharma R&D

Transcript · August 16, 2026

Good morning, it's August 16th, 2026, and this is your briefing on AI in biopharma R&D.

All week this beat has closed on the same line: no AI-discovered drug has reached approval, and capital is running ahead of clinical proof. Today we finally have the numbers behind that line, and they are more precise than anything we've had before.

A tracking analysis compiled this year counted 117 AI-enabled therapeutic assets across 63 companies that have entered human trials. Sixty of those, just over half, have completed Phase 1. Only eight, under seven percent, have completed Phase 2. Two independent trackers, IntuitionLabs and Clinical Trial Vanguard, corroborate that same 117-and-8 count, so this isn't a single outlet's number. It replaces the looser, unsourced pipeline figures that have circulated this year with something you can actually trace to a quote.

Layer in the success-rate data, and the shape of the story sharpens. Phase 1 success for AI-derived molecules runs far above the historical norm. Phase 2, where efficacy in real patients gets tested for the first time, sits at roughly forty percent, the same number the industry has lived with for decades. I want to flag the vintage on that Phase 2 figure directly: it traces back to a 2024 academic study, and neither of today's sources points to a newer primary dataset. They're repeating the same historical benchmark, not remeasuring it. Treat forty percent as the best available estimate, not a fresh 2026 result.

And the headline that hasn't moved despite two more years of pipeline growth: as of mid-2026, no AI-discovered drug has received full FDA marketing approval, and no EMA approval has surfaced either. One tracker put it bluntly — the trial that would prove Phase 3 success for an AI-identified drug simply doesn't exist yet in the public literature.

So here's the clean line this data draws. AI demonstrably improves the steps that determine developability and safety — better molecule design, better target selection, cleaner PK and tox profiles — and that shows up in the Phase 1 numbers. It has not yet been shown to improve the step that predicts whether a drug actually works in humans, and that shows up in Phase 2 sitting exactly where it always has. Zero approvals isn't an anomaly given that split. It's the expected result. Every AI-drug-discovery valuation, every platform partnership, and every "AI-designed" marketing claim needs to be read through that lens: does this claim bear on developability, where AI has receipts, or on efficacy, where it doesn't yet?

If the clinical odds haven't moved, where is AI value actually provable today? Two layers, and both are checkable in a way the efficacy claim isn't.

The first is structural biology. Isomorphic Labs, Google DeepMind's drug-design spinout, has a new model called IsoDDE that goes beyond AlphaFold 3. On the hardest protein-ligand structure cases — those with less than twenty percent similarity to anything in the training data — IsoDDE hit fifty percent accuracy where AlphaFold 3 managed twenty-three point three. That's roughly double, on the cases that matter most because they're the ones where a model can't just be pattern-matching to something it's already seen. The catch: this benchmark comes from Isomorphic's own technical report, with no independent replication yet, and unlike DeepMind's AlphaFold tradition, Isomorphic is keeping IsoDDE's weights fully closed. That's a deliberate IP decision, not an accident.

It's part of a bigger pattern of frontier AI labs internalizing drug discovery rather than just licensing tools into it. Anthropic bought a roughly ten-person stealth biotech startup, Coefficient Bio, for about four hundred million dollars in stock. Its founders came out of Genentech's Prescient Design computational discovery group — in other words, Anthropic didn't just hire biology talent, it bought a team that already knew how to build drug-design models and folded it into its life-sciences unit. Put IsoDDE and Coefficient Bio together and the signal is that compute providers are becoming direct competitors to the pharma companies that are also their customers, not just vendors selling them access.

But that consolidation pressure isn't running unopposed. Chai Discovery, a structure-and-design model company that raised a hundred and thirty million dollars at a one-point-three-billion-dollar valuation in its last round, is now licensed to both Eli Lilly and Pfizer. Terms of the Pfizer deal aren't disclosed, so don't read a dollar figure into it, but the fact pattern matters: pharma is deliberately spreading its bets across multiple structural-model providers rather than locking into one. So you've got two forces pulling at once — a closed, superior model creating a moat for whoever owns it, and a fragmenting vendor layer giving pharma optionality and price leverage. Both are true simultaneously, and which one dominates will shape who captures the economics of this layer over the next few years.

The second provable layer is real-world data, and it answers a question this beat hasn't tackled directly: can AI actually make use of the sea of balkanized health records sitting behind HIPAA and a decade of incompatible digital formats? Regeneron is betting yes. It's investing up to two hundred million dollars alongside TriNetX for access to a de-identified electronic health record network spanning roughly three hundred million patients, a hundred and seventy million of them in the US. The deal explicitly frames the goal as feeding AI training algorithms, and it links Regeneron's own genomic and proteomic data to that phenotypic record set. Regeneron also sits inside the Alliance for Genomic Discovery, which just expanded its core dataset to three hundred twelve thousand whole genomes with Regeneron's addition as the tenth member.

The mechanism matters here: this kind of linkage uses tokenized patient matching and AI-based chart abstraction to pull structure out of messy clinical notes without moving identified patient data around, which is how it sidesteps a HIPAA waiver requirement. What it hasn't yet done is produce a disclosed case study proving it lowers R&D cost or raises the odds of clinical success. That's still an industry assertion, not a demonstrated result — the same caveat that applies to the Phase 2 numbers applies here. The infrastructure is real and it's being built at genuine scale; the payoff is not yet on the record.

Step back, and the through-line is this. The provable value in AI-biopharma today sits in two auditable places — proprietary structural models like IsoDDE, and genomics-linked real-world-data networks like Regeneron's — not in the clinical odds. The Phase 3 cohort now moving through the clinic across the industry is the event to watch: if those trials read out near that forty percent efficacy benchmark, it confirms the ceiling and likely chills the upfront cash pharma is willing to pay for preclinical AI-derived assets. If they diverge meaningfully in either direction, it re-rates the entire AI-native biotech cohort. Until one of those readouts lands, the scorecard we've been citing all week hasn't actually moved — it's just gotten more precise.
