Good morning. It's Thursday, November 5th, and this is your robotics and physical AI briefing.

This week the humanoid story got louder while the evidence behind it got thinner. We'll follow that thread from a Chinese EV factory floor to the dexterous hand inside Nvidia's own reference humanoid. Then we'll end with who is actually carrying the risk when a robotics check gets written.

Start with Xiaomi. On November 3rd, Xiaomi posted video claiming one of its humanoids ran an entire eight-hour shift, unsupervised, at a parts-sorting workstation inside its EV factory. Every number in this story is Xiaomi's own, relayed by Electrek. And the reason the claim deserves attention is that Xiaomi has a quantified track record. In March, Xiaomi reported a humanoid installing self-tapping nuts with a 90.2 percent success rate over three hours, at a 76-second cycle time. By July, the success rate had climbed to 98 percent, and Xiaomi had added two harder stations. One of them was side-cover sorting, the task behind this week's claim.

So here's the tell. For the smaller, earlier trials, Xiaomi published hard numbers. For the biggest claim, a full shift, it published none. No success rate. No cycle time. No output count. No count of human interventions. The company that disclosed 90.2 percent over three hours went quiet exactly when the claim got biggest. The silence, not the eight hours, is the signal.

Take the July number at face value and the gap is still large. A 98 percent success rate means roughly one attempt in fifty fails. A production station runs thousands of cycles, so at that rate someone still has to catch the misses. A shift with no success rate attached is an endurance claim, not a reliability claim. It shows the robot can keep running. It doesn't show the line can trust it.

There is a benign reading. Xiaomi may publish the metrics later, or this may be a single-station showcase on a known fixture. That would be real progress, but narrow. And note the buyer. This is Xiaomi's robot in Xiaomi's own factory. That's the captive-demand pattern this desk flagged yesterday, with Atlas inside Hyundai and Skild's model building Nvidia systems in Nvidia's Houston plant. So the takeaway is genuine pilot progression, not a third party paying a market price.

The most-watched humanoid program has the same gap, just filed in English instead of claimed on video. Tesla's third-quarter 10-Q says only that the company is making preparations and investments for large-scale Optimus production. It gives no volume figures. The 20,000-robots-a-week target comes from Elon Musk's commentary, and as we covered on Monday, he said reaching it will take at least another year because parts of the design are still changing.

To be fair, leaving volumes out of a 10-Q is normal conservatism for a pre-revenue product line. It isn't concealment. The narrower point is that none of the Optimus numbers investors trade on carry filing-level accountability. On both sides of the Pacific, the headline humanoid numbers live in commentary and video. One inference follows. A slower, unmetered Optimus ramp keeps the window open for Chinese manufacturers like Xiaomi to establish factory deployments first. Their edge there is a captive production line, not a better brain.

That makes disclosure itself the scarce asset. As humanoid makers move from pilots to production claims, investors will need standard metrics: success rate, interventions, and output. Firms that publish them earn a credibility that bigger, unmetered claims don't. Agility's first quarterly filings as a public company will be the first US test of whether anyone actually supplies them.

Now, sorting parts or seating nuts on a line is fundamentally a hand problem. And the hand is where even Nvidia leans on China. Nvidia's new reference humanoid, unveiled at GTC Berlin, is the Agile ONE SN, built on Agile Robots' body. It runs on Jetson Thor silicon and ships with Nvidia's open GR00T 1.7 model. Its spec sheet confirms the hands: dual Wuji Hand 2 units, each with 20 degrees of freedom. Per CNBC, Wuji's founder started the company in 2019 and eventually moved much of the team to Shenzhen.

Nvidia's earlier reference design, shown at GTC Taipei, paired a Unitree H2 Plus body with hands from Sharpa. Those hands carry 22 degrees of freedom across the pair, barely more than a single Wuji hand. Sharpa is headquartered in Singapore. But it has said its manufacturing R&D is in Shanghai. And it was co-founded by David Li, chief executive of the Shanghai lidar maker Hesai.

Be precise about what that proves. We can confirm where Wuji's team sits, not where its hands are built. So "China-rooted" is the honest label for both hand suppliers, not "China-made." These are also developer and research platforms, not volume procurement, and a buyer can swap the hands. The benign reading is that Nvidia simply picked the best available hand for a reference kit. But if that's the reason, it tells you where dexterous-hand engineering sits today. We also couldn't confirm whether any non-Chinese hand is qualified in Nvidia's program, so treat that as an open question.

This escalates an arc we've tracked for a week: China moving up the humanoid stack. On October 30th, Hyundai was localizing Atlas suppliers, with reduced reliance on China as a stated criterion. On November 3rd, Meta's Max prototype turned out to be built on a Fourier platform. Now the reference stack meant to seed the Western humanoid ecosystem draws its hands from China-rooted suppliers in both designs. Its first design sat on a Unitree body as well.

Our inference is that the clean story of an Nvidia stack competing with a China stack doesn't hold. Nvidia supplies the brain and the silicon. China's supply chain supplies the hands and, in the Taipei design, the body. The stacks are already interleaved at the component level. If those hands are confirmed as Chinese-sourced, export-control exposure for Western humanoids reaches past magnets into finished subassemblies. That would give Western capital a reason to fund non-Chinese hand makers. That's inference too.

The supply question gets sharper next week because of rare earths. This desk has carried November 10th as the lapse date for China's suspension of its October 2025 export controls. Some reporting now cites January 10th, 2027 instead, so we checked. Per Carra Globe's reading of the legal text, November 10th, 2026 is the legal end of the suspension. The January date traces to a looser Chinese statement about a broader US-China arrangement. No amended, numbered notice has moved the legal date.

The deeper point is a layer that was never paused. China's April 2025 licensing controls on seven heavy rare earths were never suspended. By that same analysis, they reach the terbium- and dysprosium-bearing magnets used in robot actuators and hand motors. So the magnets inside every humanoid joint and hand have been under active licensing all year, whatever happens on the 10th. If the suspension lapses on schedule, the broader October controls return. That includes an extraterritorial rule reaching foreign-made products with Chinese-origin rare-earth content. So the hardest part of a humanoid to build is also the part the West hasn't shown it can source at home, down to the magnet.

When someone does write a real check in physical automation, the contract terms tell you who's carrying the risk. Walmart's roughly 6-billion-dollar, multi-year commitment to Symbotic dates to October 29th, and this desk covered it on October 31st. Two things are new. Reporting now puts the scope at 25 US regional distribution centers, the site count the filing left out. And the concentration is on record: Walmart made up about 85 percent of Symbotic's revenue in the fiscal year to September 2025.

As we noted last week, the commitment can't be reduced without both companies' consent. One report adds that Walmart owes Symbotic money if it buys fewer systems than promised. So better visibility and deeper dependence arrived in the same deal. For Symbotic shareholders, one customer's capex cycle effectively is the investment case. The firmer terms and the concentration have to be priced together.

Compare that with Shield AI. On November 4th, GeekWire reported a 150-million-dollar Navy contract modification for Shield AI, tied to its X-BAT autonomous aircraft. It runs through the Pentagon's Defense Exportability Features program. And Shield AI matches it dollar for dollar with 150 million of its own private funds. The program's name points to export-readiness work, not a production order or a new program of record. The match is the company's own capital, not a customer's vote of confidence. At Walmart, the customer is locked in and carries the build-out risk. At Shield AI, the vendor puts up half the money itself. That's the cleanest contrast this week on who is actually underwriting automation at scale.

Pulling the threads together. What's genuinely inflecting is fixed warehouse automation backed by a binding contract. Factory humanoid pilots, Xiaomi's above all, have real if self-reported trajectories, but they're still short of metered, independently verified reliability. The model layer was quiet this week, so the brain story is mostly whose silicon and open model ship inside the reference kit. What's still a demo is a Western supply chain for the dexterous hand. Watch four things. Whether Xiaomi ever publishes shift-level metrics. What happens to rare-earth licensing on November 10th. Agility's first quarterly disclosures, as the first US test of humanoid metrics. And whether anyone outside China qualifies a dexterous hand at volume. That's where this inflects or stalls.
