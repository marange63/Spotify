Cautious Optimism Briefings

Enterprise Technology & Software

Transcript · July 31, 2026

Good morning. It's Friday, July 31st, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

All week this show has been asking one question: is the AI infrastructure capex boom actually converting into revenue, or is it running ahead of real demand? Microsoft and Meta split the answer Wednesday night. Amazon was the last hyperscaler vote outstanding, and it reported after the close last night. It just landed on the pass side.

AWS grew 36.7% year over year in the second quarter. Andy Jassy, Amazon's CEO, called it "our fastest growth in 18 quarters," and said AWS's AI and chips businesses each eclipsed a $25 billion annualized run rate. That's not a pilot number. Operating income came in at $16.6 billion, up from $10.2 billion a year earlier, at a 39.4% operating margin. So the cloud arm that funds Amazon's entire AI buildout is not just growing faster, it's growing more profitably. That's the clean monetization proof the market wanted from this earnings week: recognized, high-margin revenue actually absorbing the spend.

Now the catch. Amazon raised its 2026 capital expenditure guidance to $220 billion, up from a prior $200 billion, citing higher memory costs. And Jassy told investors Amazon still won't "have enough capacity to meet all the demand we have in 2026," adding, "I believe this dynamic will also be true in 2027 too." Sit with that. The company posting the fastest AWS growth in four and a half years is simultaneously saying it cannot build fast enough to keep up. That reframes the whole debate. This is no longer a question of whether enterprise AI demand is real — AWS just answered that. It's a question of whether the physical buildout, chips, memory, power, data center shells, can be delivered on time. That's a supply constraint, not a demand one.

For enterprise IT buyers, the practical read follows directly. When a hyperscaler tells you it is rationing capacity through 2027, the discount leverage buyers normally enjoy in a maturing cloud market simply isn't there. The move is to lock in multi-year reserved AWS compute commitments now, before capacity tightens further. Expect that logic to pull demand forward into long-dated reserved-capacity deals, which conveniently also gives Amazon better visibility on its own buildout. The buyer trades pricing flexibility for the certainty of having compute at all — and in a rationed market, that's a trade most CIOs will take.

There's a second-order cost that lands on the same buyer. If all four hyperscalers are raising capex into the same constrained supply of high-bandwidth memory, advanced packaging, and power — Amazon from $200 to $220 billion, Meta lifting its floor from $125 to $130 billion, and the others reiterating aggressive plans — then the components underneath cloud AI stay scarce and premium-priced. Amazon naming memory costs specifically as the reason its capex guide went up is the tell: input inflation in the supply chain is real and it flows downstream. So even as cloud growth accelerates, the unit economics of AI compute don't get cheaper for the enterprise on any near-term horizon. The buyer ultimately absorbs that cost, whether in list price or in the scarcity premium baked into reserved capacity.

It's also worth naming, briefly, why one caveat matters. None of the hyperscalers disentangle new enterprise workloads from previously-contracted backlog finally converting to revenue. So some of that 36.7% growth may be capacity coming online that was already sold, not net-new demand. Treat the growth number as real, but not as proof the demand curve is still accelerating from here. And treat the capacity-shortfall language as management characterization — a framing that, conveniently, can also explain away any future growth wobble as supply, not weakness.

AWS now completes the set this week's earnings built. Microsoft's Azure grew 43%, its fastest since early 2022, with Azure's full fiscal year revenue crossing $100 billion for the first time — that's the clear pass. Meta's the cautionary case: revenue grew 28%, but free cash flow collapsed to just $784 million for the quarter, even as it narrowed its 2026 capex floor upward to $130 billion. Google Cloud landed somewhere in the middle, with strong reported growth and a swelling backlog, though those figures come from secondary reporting rather than Alphabet's own release, so hold them loosely. AWS is the clean pass in that set.

One more data point on the monetization-versus-reach question, this time in developer tools. Microsoft disclosed on its earnings call that GitHub Copilot has reached 50 million total users, with revenue growing more than 60% quarter over quarter on usage-based billing. But be careful with that 50 million figure. It folds in free and trial accounts, and it is not a paid-subscriber count. Microsoft did not disclose an updated paid Copilot number with this earnings call, and the last specific paid figure it gave was months ago and far smaller. So the more meaningful signal here isn't the user count, it's that revenue is growing faster than the user base. That's what tells you usage-based pricing is actually working as monetization, even if we can't yet size the paid base precisely. Reach is easy to headline. Paid conversion is the harder number, and it's the one that isn't in the release.

That same monetization discriminator showed up at the application layer, too, in the market's own pricing. On July 27th, Salesforce, ServiceNow, and Workday shares jumped 7%, 8%, and 10% respectively, in what's being called an AI-driven rotation back into enterprise software. Treat this as a market narrative, not a fresh disclosure. The move came off prior declines, so it's a rebound, and the underlying AI revenue figures behind the rally are weeks old at this point. The exact same-day drivers weren't independently confirmed. But the shape of it rhymes with everything else this week: investors are rewarding vendors who can show AI translating into contracted, recurring revenue, and punishing capex without a clear monetization story. The equity market is now sorting software the same way enterprise buyers are sorting cloud.

So here's where the week lands. The dividing line all along was never the size of the capex check — every hyperscaler is spending more than they said they would a quarter ago. The dividing line is monetization visibility: can you show the revenue converting at scale, at margin, in the numbers. AWS answered that question convincingly last night. The question that replaces it, heading into next quarter, is whether the industry can physically build capacity fast enough to keep from leaving that demand on the table through 2027 — and whether the enterprise buyer, now bidding for rationed compute against every other buyer, ends up paying for the shortage either way.

That's the briefing for today. Have a great weekend.
