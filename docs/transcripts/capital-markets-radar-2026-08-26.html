Capital Markets Cross-Asset Radar

Good morning, it's Wednesday, August 26th, and this is the Capital Markets Radar.

Brent has given the entire Iran rally back, and we finally know why. This program called that rally on Sunday — rhetoric-driven, against barrels that were still being pumped, and the most reversible piece of the inflation story on the board — and said on Tuesday that the inflation trigger had gotten materially weaker. As of Tuesday's settle, the give-back is complete and the reason is on the table. That resolution is the story today, because it separates two things the last two weeks bundled together. The inflation leg of this market has broken. The liquidity leg has not. That gap is now doing the work in credit, in equities and in gold at once.

Start with the mechanism. Brent fell 3.9 percent on Tuesday to settle at $88.58 a barrel, its second straight daily loss. That is a give-back of roughly seven percent from the $95.29 Fortune had Brent at on Friday morning, before Washington's latest Iran measures landed. The reason is specific, and it is the piece we were missing on Monday. The new package stopped short of imposing secondary sanctions on Iran's trading partners. That is materially softer than what the market had priced through the prior week. There is also a report, from the New York Times and relayed to us secondhand, that Washington may return diplomats to Middle East embassies. The tape read that as a signal the administration is not bracing for escalation. Take it as reported, not as confirmed policy.

Now the part that did not reverse. Crude is still up roughly 45 percent year to date. The war between the United States, Israel and Iran is in its sixth month. Both sides missed the sixty-day ceasefire window that would have formalised a truce, and traffic through the Strait of Hormuz remains severely constrained. So the premium came out on probability, not on physics. Nothing about the physical disruption that created it has changed. That leaves the market in the least comfortable version of this trade. A weaker sanctions package is a worse outcome than either a tough one or none at all, because it removes the inflation impulse while leaving the supply constraint fully in place. Here is the so-what. This is the cheapest point all year to own the tail risk of an actual Hormuz closure, and it has arrived at the moment nobody is paid to hold it.

That has a direct consequence for rates. Falling crude and falling long-end yields are both disinflationary at the margin, and it is the first time in weeks the two have pulled the same direction. On August 23rd this program said the honest test of the yield move was whether it was real yields or breakevens, and that decomposition still isn't sourceable. But the direction is no longer ambiguous. With the energy impulse gone, there is no live inflation surprise left in the near-term data. That turns the September Fed debate into a growth-and-labour argument rather than an inflation one, and it loads the burden of proof onto Friday, when Kevin Warsh gives his first Jackson Hole speech as Fed chair.

Here is the pivot, and it is the evidence that oil and gold were never one trade. If this were broad geopolitical de-risking, gold would be falling alongside crude. It isn't. Gold pushed to a three-month high into Tuesday, with December futures opening at $4,710.10 an ounce, up 6.7 percent on the week.

And we now have dated evidence that the bid under gold is structural rather than a war hedge. The World Gold Council reports that the People's Bank of China added twenty tonnes in July. That was its twenty-first consecutive month of buying, taking official holdings to 2,366 tonnes. Nothing about a twenty-one-month purchase programme is a reaction to an Iran headline.

The retail leg says the same thing. Chinese domestic gold ETFs took in the equivalent of 6.3 billion dollars from January through July, the second-strongest year-to-date on record, and they have kept adding through August. Those figures are dated to July and early August, so they do not prove what moved gold on Tuesday specifically. What they establish is a persistent official and retail bid that predates the Iran diplomacy entirely. Oil and gold were bundled into one geopolitical gauge. This week they came apart, because they were always two premia. A war premium, which is leaving crude. And a debasement premium, which is not leaving gold.

That distinction matters for how gold behaves on Friday. The lazy version of the call is that gold is the most crowded position on the board and reverses hardest on a hawkish Warsh. Half of that is right. The dollar-and-duration leg of gold's rally is fully exposed. The reserve-diversification leg is not, because a central bank twenty-one months into a buying programme does not sell on a speech. So expect gold to give back less than a pure rates asset would. If it gives back more, the structural story was smaller than the flow data suggested.

The same fiscal anxiety that bids gold shows up directly in the long end. And there, nothing has moved since Monday. Treasury Secretary Bessent has doubled the minimum size of long-end buybacks from two billion dollars to at least four billion. CNBC reports, citing two unnamed Treasury officials, that he may draw on the roughly one-trillion-dollar Treasury General Account to fund a larger push. That is reporting, not policy. Nothing has actually been purchased, and nothing will be before the first operation on September 9th. It stays in this briefing for one reason. It is the mechanism still holding the long end down while the war premium leaves oil. And there is one genuinely new thing in it, which is the market's response function. Three days of escalating buyback headlines have produced progressively smaller yield reactions. Diminishing returns on the same intervention is itself the finding.

So what has that combination of falling crude and falling long yields actually bought? Two things. High-yield spreads sitting near 275 to 280 basis points, in the richest decile of this index's history against a long-run median near 450. And the S&P 500 closing Tuesday at a record 7,677.

Take the credit tightness seriously before you dismiss it. The consensus reflex is to call it complacency. On the facts just described, I don't think that's right. If the energy impulse is dead and long yields are falling, the default backdrop is genuinely better, and the tightness is market-wide rather than one quality tier being mispriced. Credit is trading macro, and on macro it may simply be correct.

What credit is not priced for is concentration. Nvidia fell for a seventh straight session heading into its earnings print, even as the index made a new high above it. And back on August 12th, options desks reported record demand for downside protection in the VanEck semiconductor ETF, alongside a record spread between S&P and Nasdaq-100 volatility. That is positioning established earlier in the month, not a live price today. But it tells you the asymmetry. Equity desks have paid real money to hedge a concentration shock in the one sector driving this entire cycle. Credit desks have not put a single basis point of premium on it.

Trace what happens if that shock arrives. Equity volatility moves first, because that is where the hedges and the systematic triggers already sit. Credit reprices second, and only once the marks arrive. That is the same mark-versus-no-mark asymmetry this program has been flagging in private credit all week. So if you are hunting for the early warning in high yield, there isn't one. In this setup, high yield is the lagging instrument, not the leading one.

Briefly on crypto, and then the calendar. Bitcoin is trading as a rates proxy alongside gold. That was established on Tuesday and nothing has changed. What is new is the clock. Treasury began writing the GENIUS Act's core stablecoin rulebook in mid-August, with an issuance ban on non-compliant issuers taking effect January 18th, 2027. Sitting under that deadline is roughly 184.6 billion dollars of Tether, as of the end of the second quarter, and about 72.7 billion of USDC. That is a dated compliance cliff underneath a quarter-trillion-dollar float, in an asset class currently pricing nothing but rates. The market has assigned it no premium at all. And unlike everything else in this briefing, that deadline does not care what Warsh says on Friday.

Two dated tests are left, and neither had happened as of this recording. Nvidia's print, which is the concentration question resolving. And Kevin Warsh's first Jackson Hole appearance as Fed chair on Friday, which is the liquidity leg resolving. We will tell you what they actually said, not what we expected them to say. Until then, this is a market trading a clean liquidity story with the inflation argument taken off the table.

That's the Radar for August 26th. Trade safe.
