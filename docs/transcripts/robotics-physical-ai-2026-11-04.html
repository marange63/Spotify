Robotics & Physical AI

Good morning. It's Wednesday, November 4th, 2026, and this is your briefing on robotics and physical AI.

The humanoid catalyst this desk has flagged all week has resolved. And the same week's hard order data shows where the dependable money in robotics is actually going.

Start with Agility Robotics. We've flagged this vote since October 28th. On Monday, November 2nd, shareholders of Churchill Capital Corp XI approved its merger with Agility. More than 84 percent of votes cast were in favor. Close is expected today. Agility is expected to begin trading on Nasdaq tomorrow under the ticker AGLT.

Read that vote carefully, though. Eighty-four percent is a share of votes cast, not a share of all shares outstanding. And in a SPAC, holders can vote yes on the deal and still redeem their cash on the way out. Approval is not conviction. The number that measures conviction is how much trust cash stayed in.

That number is small. After Monday's vote, the trust holds approximately $164 million, according to the companies' own capital-raise announcement. The SPAC's December 2025 filing put the trust at $414 million when it was first funded. Nobody has published an exact redemption rate, so treat the gap as directional. But on those two numbers, most of the SPAC's original cash walked out before the deal closed.

You can see the same thing in the headline raise. When the deal was announced, it was expected to bring in more than $620 million in gross proceeds. The figure now is approximately $465 million, before transaction expenses.

What filled the hole was Foxconn, leading a PIPE of about $200 million. A contract manufacturer anchoring the equity reads less like a pure financial bet and more like Foxconn buying a seat at the table with a future customer. That's an inference on my part, not a confirmed motive. But it's a more telling signal than the vote total.

Now to what sits underneath the valuation. Agility's S-4 filing, as reported by The Robot Report, shows 2025 net sales of just $1.8 million. Against that sat a $140 million operating loss. In cash terms, the company burned about $100 million last year. Put plainly, this is a company whose losses run at many times its revenue, walking into public markets.

On the unit side, there is at least a usage record. Agility says Digit has now logged more than 65,000 operating hours across nine customer sites, extending the hours figure this desk cited on October 28th. Under its subscription model, Agility charges about $8,500 a month per robot. That pricing is what matters for the revenue line from here.

So run the scaling math. By The Robot Report's arithmetic, a thousand deployed robots would bring in about $102 million a year in subscriptions. That is still less than last year's operating loss. Agility's own targets call for around 800 Digit units in 2027, scaling toward 25,000 by 2035. Even on plan, subscription revenue needs years of compounding before it covers the cost base, let alone justifies a $2.5 billion pre-money valuation.

The raise buys time, not profitability. On our rough math, $465 million covers something like three to four years at last year's burn rate. And burn is rising, not falling. Spending on operations grew by more than half from 2024 to 2025. A production ramp would push it higher still, so treat that runway as an upper bound.

So the headline isn't that Agility is going public. It's that the market is being asked to fund years more of losses, on a thin public float, with most of the original SPAC cash gone and Foxconn standing in as the real anchor. Don't over-read whatever AGLT does in its first week of trading. A thin float makes for a noisy signal, not a clean verdict on humanoid economics. A yes vote is not a check.

If public investors are hesitant about humanoid bodies, the people actually buying robots this quarter aren't hesitating. They're just buying different robots. The Association for Advancing Automation, or A3, reporting through The Robot Report, says North American companies ordered 10,989 robots in the third quarter, worth $723.5 million. That's up 24.8 percent in units from a year earlier. On October 31st, this desk said the firm money in robotics goes to narrow, provable automation rather than general-purpose bodies. This is that pattern showing up across the entire North American order book, not just in individual deals.

Look at where the growth is concentrated. Semiconductor and electronics orders rose 97.6 percent in units. Automotive-components orders rose 90.4 percent. Automotive OEMs, the traditional anchor buyer for industrial robots, grew just 7.5 percent. So robot demand this quarter is being pulled by the chip and electronics supply chain, not by the carmakers who've historically driven the category.

Two readings are live here. One is that the AI hardware buildout is dragging robot orders along with it. That's inference, but it fits where the growth sits. The other reading is simpler: a weak year-ago base, since a near-doubling can come off a quarter that started low. There's no clean prior-year segment breakdown to settle it, so hold both.

If the first reading is right, there's a catch. Industrial-automation demand would then inherit the AI capex cycle's risk. A pause in chip and electronics spending would reach robot orders, and quickly. That's also inference. But it's the risk any bull case on automation incumbents now has to carry.

And remember what these numbers measure. They're orders booked, not robots installed and not revenue recognized. That makes them a leading indicator, not a confirmed deployment. One more beat. Collaborative robots, the cheaper, flexible arms that sit between fixed automation and humanoids, grew about 56 percent in units. That's more than double the pace of the overall market. The near-term money for general-purpose-ish automation is landing on cobots well before it lands on bipeds.

And the AI hardware line is where the robot brain is showing up too. Recent reporting from TechTarget says Skild AI, Nvidia, and Foxconn began using the Skild Brain model on dual-arm robots. The job is assembling Nvidia's Blackwell GPU systems, at an Nvidia factory in Houston. Note the name. Foxconn is the same company anchoring Agility's PIPE.

Now read the buyer before you read the headline. Skild is Nvidia-backed. So this is an Nvidia-backed model, in an Nvidia factory, building Nvidia's own product. That's the captive-demand pattern this desk has flagged before, with Hyundai's Atlas and with Optimus inside Tesla's own plants. It is not third-party validation that a general-purpose robot brain is ready to sell. We also can't confirm whether the Houston line runs autonomously in production or is still a pilot. So even the best industrial showcase for a robot brain right now is building chips for the company that backs it.

Pulling the threads together. What's genuinely inflecting is conventional and collaborative automation, pulled along by the AI hardware buildout and booked in hard, countable orders. What's still unproven is humanoid unit economics in public markets, and third-party demand for a general-purpose robot brain outside its backer's own family. The biggest open question is whether Agility's first filings as a public company show a credible path to self-funding before the runway runs out.

Three things to watch from here. First, AGLT's Nasdaq debut tomorrow, with no price prediction from this desk. Second, Agility's first quarterly disclosures as a public company, which will show whether the burn is narrowing. Third, China's rare-earth export truce, set to lapse November 10th, six days from now, with the outcome still unknown. It touches every humanoid actuator program at once, Agility's included.

That's the briefing. Talk tomorrow.
