Cautious Optimism Briefings

Digital Money, Crypto, and Market Structure

Transcript · November 21, 2026

Good morning, it's Saturday, November 21st, and this is your digital money briefing.

Three bids were supposed to be permanent: the ETF wrapper, the corporate treasury, and the on-chain dollar. All three have fresh numbers this week. And the biggest surprise is in the one that was never supposed to sell.

Start with bitcoin, because the tape moved. Per The Block, bitcoin was at $61,702 on Friday, down 13.5 percent on the week. That's a four-month low. It followed Thursday's $582 million of spot ETF outflows, led by BlackRock's IBIT, which this desk covered yesterday.

The new piece is the month's scorecard. November's net ETF outflow has reached roughly $3.47 billion. Set that against about $53.5 billion of cumulative net inflows since the funds launched. By our arithmetic, the wrapper has given back about six and a half percent of its lifetime net demand in under three weeks. One caution. Lifetime inflows are not a cost basis, so this says nothing about whether ETF holders are underwater. It's a flow ratio, and a striking one.

Now hold that against what the funds actually own. Per The Block, spot ETFs hold just 6.1 percent of bitcoin's market cap. That's a minority holder. But a minority holder can still set the marginal price when everyone else goes quiet. And the market was thinning going in. Centralized exchange spot volume fell about 40 percent from September to October. That predates this month's selloff, so treat it as the backdrop, not this week's tape.

Coinbase can't fill that gap for equity investors. Its own 10-Q says that since the second quarter, it no longer includes trading volume as a key metric. So the read-through investors most want is one the largest US exchange no longer headlines. Land it this way. The wrapper isn't the whole market, but its flow is hitting a thinner market than a six percent stake suggests. That's a more fragile setup than it sounds. Reports of further weekend price moves fall after this desk's data cutoff, so they get no number here.

If the ETF is the buyer that can walk away, Strategy was supposed to be the one that never sells. That's the correction this briefing has to make, and it's our second in two days. On November 17th, and again on the 19th, this desk told you nothing showed forced Strategy coin sales. That was literally true. But it left the impression that Strategy's coin stack was untouchable. That impression was wrong.

Strategy's own third-quarter 10-Q shows it sold $348.6 million of bitcoin in the third quarter alone. Over the first nine months of the year, sales reached $431.8 million. Those are disclosed sales through September. Nothing in hand shows a sale since then. So don't read this as Strategy dumping coin into this week's drawdown.

The vehicle is what Strategy calls its BTC Monetization Program. Its June 8-K authorizes bitcoin sales for three primary purposes. The first is building a cash reserve, capped at $1.25 billion. The second is funding preferred dividends and interest as they come due. The third is buying back its preferred securities or common stock. Only the reserve leg carries a stated dollar ceiling. The filings don't match each dollar sold to a specific use, so how the proceeds split is our inference.

Here's why the dividend leg matters. Per reporting on the filing, preferred dividends ran $419.6 million last quarter, against $139.9 million a year earlier. That's roughly triple. It also offers a plausible rationale for the buyback pattern this desk has tracked all month. Last week, Strategy spent about six dollars retiring its STRC preferred for every dollar it put into bitcoin. Our inference is simple. Every preferred share retired shrinks a dividend bill that coin sales and common issuance would otherwise have to fund.

Be precise about what this is not. Strategy is not a net seller. One report has it spending $14.33 billion on bitcoin against the $431.8 million sold. By our arithmetic, that's roughly 33 dollars bought for every dollar sold. What changed is the precedent. By the company's own design, bitcoin is now a funding source for fixed obligations, not a buy-and-hold-only asset.

The bull case says that's disciplined. These are disclosed, programmatic sales sized against a known dividend schedule. The 8-K says the program "does not obligate the Company to sell any bitcoin." And selling a sliver of coin avoids diluting the common stock at a depressed price.

The bear case is the funding loop. The dividend bill has tripled just as both sources that pay it, the common stock and the coin, are losing value together. Every leg down means more coin or more shares to cover the same fixed bill. And MSCI drops Strategy from its benchmark indexes at the November 30th close, a mechanical hit to the very stock that funds the structure. So the treasury that never sells has turned coin into a way to pay fixed costs. That's the precedent that matters.

Strategy is selling coin to meet dollar obligations. It turns out the on-chain dollar itself is shrinking too. On November 18th, this desk said it couldn't yet tell whether the stablecoin float had turned back to growth. Now we can close that loop. It hasn't. Total stablecoin supply was about $286.4 billion on November 17th. By our arithmetic, that's roughly $26.5 billion, or 8.5 percent, below May's peak.

The split between the two majors is the new analytical point. Over the trailing 30 days, per DefiLlama, Tether's USDT fell about $8.1 billion and Circle's USDC about $3.2 billion. In dollars, that looks like Tether bleeding faster. Proportionally, by our arithmetic, both shrank by almost exactly the same amount, roughly 4.4 percent. So Tether's bigger dollar drop is an artifact of it being the bigger pool. It's not evidence of an offshore unwind, or of a flight from Tether to Circle.

Both tokens also held their dollar pegs, per CoinGecko. So this reads as orderly redemption, a market-wide retreat from trading collateral, not a run on either issuer. There's a second-order cost, and it's our inference. Fewer on-chain dollars means less collateral for leverage and market-making. So any bitcoin bounce starts from thinner liquidity. One caveat: these figures stop at November 17th, before this week's later slide.

So where did those on-chain dollars go? This desk has floated one answer all week: rotation into yield-bearing tokens. There's real evidence for it, just not enough to carry the explanation. Chainalysis puts the tokenized US Treasury market at $15.42 billion as of mid-October, up 125 percent year-on-year. That's genuine growth while the stablecoin float was shrinking. It's the strongest bullish fact in today's numbers.

But scale it. The entire tokenized Treasury category is smaller than the $26.5 billion the float has lost since May. Even its full year-on-year gain, about $8.6 billion, would cover only about a third of that decline. Those comparisons mix dates, so treat them as rough. Still, the conclusion holds. Rotation is a contributor, not the destination for most of the missing dollars.

The league table adds a twist. BlackRock's BUIDL, long the category leader, sat near $2.3 billion in October and had slipped to third. Circle's USYC led, at $2.41 billion. Now recall what this desk covered this week. BUIDL got a round-the-clock liquidity facility, and Invesco announced a tokenized money fund pitched as stablecoin reserve infrastructure. So BlackRock's fund is getting new plumbing after it already lost the top spot. Infrastructure and market share are diverging, which is capacity, again, not adoption.

There's one more wrinkle for Circle. It owns USYC, the category leader. So some of the yield migrating out of stablecoins lands back in Circle's own economics. That's our inference, and the offset is small. By our arithmetic, USYC is about 3.5 percent of USDC's float. And all of these tokenization figures date to mid-October, so treat them as last month's picture.

Three things to watch. First, whether Strategy's next filings show any fourth-quarter bitcoin sales, and how the stock trades through the November 30th MSCI deletion. Second, whether the stablecoin float stabilizes once this week's data land. Third, whether ETF redemptions persist past the weekend.

The thread is the same across all three stories. The bids that were supposed to be permanent, the ETF, the corporate treasury, and the stablecoin float, are all shrinking in measurable, orderly ways. And the newest evidence is that even the holder that never sells has been selling coin this year to help pay its bills.

That's the briefing. Have a good weekend.
