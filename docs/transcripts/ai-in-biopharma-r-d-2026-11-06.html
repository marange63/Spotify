Cautious Optimism Briefings

AI in Biopharma R&D

Transcript · November 6, 2026

Good morning. It's November 6th, 2026, and this is your briefing on AI in biopharma R&D.

Today's thesis: third-quarter earnings season is showing where pharma's AI is actually landing, and it isn't where the capital is. Deployed AI sits in drug safety, manufacturing and trial operations. Discovery AI is still described in adjectives.

First, the capital side, so the contrast is clear. Investor money is concentrating at the discovery and design layer, and this week brought the extreme case. Bloomberg reported on November 4th that Isomorphic Labs, the Google DeepMind spinout, is in talks to raise about five billion dollars at a valuation near fifty billion. That's more than double the two point one billion it raised in May. Yet its president says its work is still preclinical, and the round hasn't closed. Now set that against what companies say they've actually deployed.

Start with Pfizer's third-quarter call on November 3rd. Chief executive Albert Bourla told investors, in his words, "We have deployed AI in our business with the goal of improving efficiency in manufacturing." He also described a drug-safety system called Genesis. Per Bourla, "Genesis takes on the administrative burden so our safety scientists can spend their time where it matters most." And where it matters most, he said, is "assessing causality, detecting safety signals, and protecting patients."

Notice what that is and isn't. Genesis is not making the safety call. The scientists still own causality and signal detection. The system clears the administrative work around them. That's a throughput gain on scarce expert time, not a smarter decision. And even this side of the ledger is unquantified. Pfizer gave no case volume, cycle time or cost figure for Genesis, or for the manufacturing work. Bank that distinction, because it recurs all morning.

Now put Pfizer's R&D language next to it. Bourla's full line was, quote, "In R&D, we are working to accelerate innovation with faster target validation and better molecule and biomarker selection." Note the tense. Manufacturing is something Pfizer has deployed. R&D is something it is working to do. No metric is attached. Call it the adjective test. AI that's in production gets described as done, and sometimes gets a system name. AI that's still aspirational gets words like faster and better. That asymmetry is the news.

It also answers a question this show asked on Monday. We closed that briefing by asking whether any large pharma would put a measured, AI-attributed R&D saving on an earnings call. Eli Lilly's October 29th print attributed its R&D increase to pipeline and business development, not AI. Pfizer has now given the same answer in its own words. For now, the answer to Monday's question is not yet.

There's a fair counter-reading. Discovery gains take years to surface in pipeline outcomes. No single quarter can measure them. So a missing metric isn't proof that AI is idle in the lab. It only means the lab hasn't produced a number anyone will say out loud.

The other companies checked this season sharpen that pattern. Start with the one that did put numbers on the table. IQVIA's chief executive, Ari Bousbib, said on October 27th, quote, "IQVIA has now deployed 415 agents to date." No other company checked here gave a deployment count at all. It's a count, not an outcome, so read it as a scale signal. Bousbib also cited one client where an AI-enabled site-modeling tool was "shortening the overall program timeline by 9 months." That's a single client example, not a portfolio average. And management credited the quarter's win-rate improvement to "good operational execution," not to AI by name. So the nine-month anecdote is not a claim that AI is winning IQVIA new business.

Here's why IQVIA matters beyond its own stock. In our read, it's the leading indicator for whether contract research organizations can defend their fee base as sponsors build AI in-house. A CRO that deploys agents at scale can argue it's the most efficient place to run trial operations. But those same agents cut into the billable hours that fee base was built on. Which way that nets out isn't visible yet.

Sanofi went the other way: strong language, no numbers. Its R&D head, Paulo Fontoura, said, quote, "AI will be a key lever across the R&D value chain." The company's stated ambition is "to deploy integrated AI agents company-wide and to expand strategic partnerships in this area." No cost, timeline or productivity figure was attached. Ambition is the operative word.

Then Novartis, which gave the most candid version. Chief executive Vas Narasimhan told CNBC, quote, "this is the first quarter I've really seen AI start to improve actually our P&L on a tangible basis." Per CNBC, the clearest gains were in marketing and sales. On R&D, he said only that Novartis wants "to see if it can really increase R&D productivity." That's an executive naming the uncertainty out loud, which is a stronger form of the point than silence.

So across Pfizer, IQVIA, Sanofi and Novartis, the four companies checked here, the pattern holds. Operations and commercial functions get deployments and P&L claims. R&D gets ambition. We found no AI productivity commentary from Roche, Merck or AstraZeneca. That's a gap in the evidence, not evidence of absence.

Here's the second-order read, and it's our inference. If safety-case automation like Pfizer's Genesis scales across large pharma, the exposed layer is labor-intensive, billable pharmacovigilance outsourcing. We made the same argument on Monday about toxicologic-pathology contract labs. There, AI that drafts slide reads puts the lab's read-and-report service at risk. And Thursday's item on DeepMind's chemistry agents was a demo of a language model running standard chemistry tools to replay known drug history. Its value is chemist time saved, not discovery. Today's earnings add fresh support to this week's running conclusion: the payoff is expert time, not better calls.

If operations is where pharma is putting AI to work, it's no accident that's where the frontier labs are shopping. OpenAI announced on October 20th that it had acquired Orchestra Bio. Terms weren't disclosed. Per eMarketer's account, Orchestra "builds AI agents for operations such as clinical trial scheduling and monitoring complex laboratory studies." That's logistics, not molecule design. And logistics is where large language models are already the workhorse. This is also a different kind of money from Isomorphic's. It isn't venture capital betting on a design platform. It's a frontier lab buying into the layer where deployment is already happening. Our inference: OpenAI is buying where its general models are already strong, not a specialized biology architecture.

A disclosure before the next item: Anthropic makes the model behind this show. Endpoints News reported on October 27th that Anthropic acquired Mithril Medicine, a one-person stealth biotech. That follows an April deal for a stealth startup called Coefficient Bio, which is context, not news. The Information and Eric Newcomer reported that one at 400 million dollars in stock. Public reporting gives no rationale for Mithril. Our inference is that a one-person company is a hire, not a platform. For Coefficient, the reporting gives a price but little on strategy. On what's public, neither deal signals a pivot into owning drug programs.

Two consequences follow, and both are open questions. The first is build versus buy. Sponsors may end up choosing between lab-provided agents and the incumbent CRO and eClinical stack, from IQVIA to Veeva and Medidata. Nothing in the record yet shows a frontier lab winning a sponsor contract from those incumbents. The second is custody. When a sponsor feeds proprietary trial or biology data into a lab-owned agent, who controls that data afterward? And who controls the model weights it helps shape? Nobody has resolved those terms publicly. For incumbents, the threat is real but still hypothetical.

So pull the threads together. Capital is still stacking at the design layer, with Isomorphic's talks near fifty billion the extreme case. But the deployments companies will name on a call sit in safety, manufacturing and trial operations. That's today's low-hanging fruit, and it's mostly language-model territory: documents, scheduling and monitoring.

What does that do to pipeline economics? It compresses cycle time and administrative cost, which helps the cost side of R&D productivity. It leaves Phase 2 attrition untouched, and attrition is the term that decides pipeline returns. So the claim that AI raises the probability of success remains unquantified by the very companies that would gain most from it. Nor did this cycle open another route. There was no fresh, verifiable signal on AI over real-world data, or on whether open-weight and specialized models are gaining ground. So any relief on R&D spend comes through operating cost, not fewer failed trials, until someone shows otherwise.

Watch for three things. A sponsor disclosing a real time or cost number for a Genesis-style system. A frontier lab winning an operations contract from an incumbent CRO. And a pharma executive attributing a clinical outcome, not just a workflow, to AI, with a number attached.

That's the briefing for today. Stay cautiously optimistic.
