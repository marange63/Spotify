Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Credit & the Corporate Credit Cycle

Transcript · July 27, 2026

Good morning, it's Monday, July 27th. Let's get into the credit machine.

The lead this week is the first real stress test of the retail private-credit vehicle, and it happened three times at once. Blackstone's BCRED, the largest non-traded BDC in the market, took in Q2 repurchase requests for roughly ten percent of shares and paid out the standard five percent cap, prorating the rest. Cliffwater's Corporate Lending Fund saw requests jump to about seventeen percent of shares, up from fourteen percent in the first quarter, and also capped payouts at five, meaning redeeming investors got back roughly a third of what they asked for. BlackRock and HPS's HLEND fund took requests for about 13.3 percent of shares, up from 9.3 percent in Q1, more than two-and-a-half times its own cap, and it's fulfilling only about 620 million dollars of what was actually asked for. Three of the largest semi-liquid vehicles in the industry, all gating in the same quarter, and for BCRED and HLEND, the second consecutive quarter of elevated requests. That's not one investor with a liquidity need. That's a pattern.

And watch how BCRED's own posture changed between the two quarters. Back in the first quarter, Blackstone and its own employees injected 400 million dollars of fresh capital so the fund could lift its cap to seven percent and fulfill every request. This quarter, there was no such backstop. Requests came in around ten percent, and redeeming investors were simply prorated down to five. When a sponsor stops writing the check to make its flagship whole, that tells you something about how it's now triaging its own liquidity — the rescue was a one-quarter courtesy, not a standing commitment.

Here's why you shouldn't take comfort in the industry's own framing that "the gates worked as designed." Contractually, sure, they did. But that's the tell, not the reassurance. A gate that activates means the liquidity promise sold to wealth-channel and 401k investors was always contingent, and investors are only now discovering the gap between "semi-liquid" and actually liquid. Bloomberg reported managers are quietly dropping the term "semi-liquid" altogether, and this week that's corroborated by named voices, not just press framing. Hightower Advisors' head of alternatives put it plainly: clients hear "half-liquid" and anchor on the liquid half. Goldman Sachs says it's stopped using the term with clients entirely, walking through the mechanics instead. And rivals are needling the industry from outside — JPMorgan Asset Management now calls these products "semi-illiquid," and DoubleLine's Jeffrey Gundlach has attacked the label even more forcefully. When the people who sell the product start avoiding its own marketing term, that tells you something about how the product actually performs under stress.

The general risk here is adverse selection: to meet even a five percent cap, a manager has to sell the most liquid, highest-quality loans first, which quietly degrades what's left for the investors who stay. But that risk isn't uniform across funds, and it's worth being precise. HLEND, specifically, disclosed roughly 7.2 billion dollars of total liquidity as of March 31st — 4.9 billion in available debt capacity, over 700 million in cash, and a dedicated 1.6 billion liquid-asset sleeve — which looks sufficient to fund its roughly 620 million dollar Q2 cap without forcing sales out of its core loan book. So the degradation risk is live, but it's a function of each fund's liquidity buffer, not an automatic consequence of gating itself. Worth also noting Blackstone's Jon Gray told the July 23rd earnings call that BCRED redemptions are, in his words, "down materially" early in the third quarter — that's a positive data point, but it's management's own characterization with no hard number attached, and Gray himself flagged unfulfilled carryover from Q2 still being worked through. Take the improvement as claimed, not confirmed.

Now here's where the bifurcation shows up cleanly. Blackstone reported Q2 earnings the same week BCRED was prorating: distributable earnings up 26 percent year over year to about two billion dollars, record assets under management of 1.35 trillion, and nearly 70 billion dollars of quarterly inflows firmwide. Credit was the single biggest driver — about 33 billion dollars of inflows, nearly half the firm's total — with insurance assets under management climbing to 290 billion. So the same credit franchise that's gating its flagship retail vehicle is simultaneously its fastest-growing fundraising line. That's not one channel offsetting the other — the 33 billion isn't broken out by source, so you can't say institutional money is plugging the retail hole. What you can say is the retail and institutional legs of the private-credit growth story are decoupling. Wealth-channel capital is discovering its liquidity terms are real; institutional and insurance capital keeps piling in at scale.

And the institutional side has a face on it this week. A Marsh survey found fifty-seven percent of insurers plan to add private-credit exposure over the next year or two, rising to eighty-one percent among those managing more than twenty-five billion dollars. So while the wealth channel pulls back, the insurance channel leans in. If that split persists, expect managers to lean harder on insurance-balance-sheet money to fund origination — which deepens exactly the insurance-private-credit interconnection the Financial Stability Board flagged as a systemic channel in May. The liquidity risk doesn't disappear; it shifts toward long-duration, less-redeemable capital, which is more stable but also more entangled with the regulated insurance system.

Which gets us to the question underneath all of this: is the underlying credit actually fine? The headline default data says the cycle is more stressed than the marketing suggests, and the composition is what matters. Fitch put the U.S. private-credit default rate at a record six percent in April. Proskauer's index of nearly 700 loans worth 189 billion dollars showed a 2.73 percent default rate in the first quarter, up from 1.84 percent just two quarters earlier. Different methodologies, but the same direction: rising. The more important number is Moody's estimate that roughly 65 percent of all 2025 private-credit defaults were distressed restructurings — debt exchanges and maturity extensions agreed under duress — rather than clean liquidations. That's amend-and-extend and PIK-toggle activity doing the flattering. Stress is being deferred, not resolved. And that's exactly why a BDC can report a low, stable non-accrual rate while the index-level default data is climbing: the marks haven't been tested by an actual resolution yet, just extended past it. Every extension also pushes the recovery-rate assumption baked into today's marks into a future quarter — so the loss isn't avoided, it's postponed, and the NAV you see today is only as good as an assumption no downturn has forced anyone to prove.

So where does that leave the cycle. The binding risk isn't spread levels — it's the collision of two things: a liquidity mismatch in retail vehicles that's now visibly real, and a credit book where deferred stress hasn't been forced to resolve by an actual downturn. Watch the calendar. FS KKR Capital, downgraded to junk by Moody's back in March on a 5.5 percent non-accrual rate and elevated PIK income, reports August 6th — the clearest precedent for what happens when amend-and-extend stops working. Ares Capital, the largest BDC, reports this Wednesday, July 29th. Apollo follows August 4th. None of those Q2 portfolio-quality numbers exist yet. Until they land, we're reading a two-week-old liquidity event against a five-month-old default read, and the real test — whether retail gating stays contained to three funds or spreads, and whether Q2 non-accruals confirm or refute the amend-and-extend story — is still ahead of us.

That's the briefing for today. Talk to you tomorrow.
