Cautious Optimism Briefings

Space & the Orbital Economy

Transcript · September 18, 2026

Good morning. It's Friday, September 18th, 2026, and this is your space and orbital economy briefing. This week the decisive moves on this beat stopped happening on launch pads and started happening on balance sheets.

Start with Rocket Lab and Iridium. On September 15th, Rocket Lab said it had fully funded the cash portion of its acquisition of Iridium, a deal worth roughly 8 billion dollars of enterprise value. The funding came from a completed at-the-market equity offering of 1.944 billion dollars, issuing 29.3 million new shares. That removes financing risk from a deal signed back on June 29th, structured at 54 dollars a share for Iridium holders — 27 dollars in cash plus Rocket Lab stock. Be precise about what that is and isn't. The deal is signed and financed, not closed. Rocket Lab says it's on track for a mid-2027 close, subject to regulatory approval. Nothing about either company's operating position changed this week.

The logic behind it is straightforward. In satellite communications, the margin sits with vertical integration, not with the constellation business on its own. And the cheapest launch input in the sector, SpaceX's, isn't for sale at any price. Rocket Lab is the first company to respond by trying to buy the other half of the stack outright rather than build it.

So what did it actually buy? Iridium's own second-quarter results give the clearest read. Quarterly revenue was 225.2 million dollars, up 4 percent year over year. Operational EBITDA was 119.1 million, down slightly from 121.3 million a year earlier. That's a margin near 53 percent — genuinely strong, and genuinely recurring. Subscribers reached about 2.63 million, up roughly 6 percent.

Now size the price. Annualize that quarter against the 8 billion dollar headline and the deal prices at roughly seventeen times run-rate EBITDA. That is my own back-of-envelope arithmetic, not a multiple any source reported. Anchor it to the growth it's buying. That is a growth-company multiple paid for a business whose EBITDA actually shrank year over year and whose revenue grew 4 percent. And Iridium has suspended its earnings calls and its forward guidance because of the pending deal, so there's no company-provided number left to check it against going forward.

That arithmetic argues for the duller read over the exciting one. Iridium is a narrowband L-band operator. Its spectrum and its architecture were designed for a different service class than low-Earth-orbit broadband or direct-to-cell. So the consensus framing — that this makes Rocket Lab a Starlink or an AST SpaceMobile competitor — may be importing a conclusion the asset doesn't support. The more defensible read is that Rocket Lab is buying a mature, spectrum-licensed annuity to fund Neutron, its still-unflown medium-lift rocket.

Here's the so-what, and it's about sequencing. A launch company that cannot yet fly its medium-lift vehicle just committed nearly 2 billion dollars of freshly issued equity to M&A instead. That's a statement about where management thinks the near-term returns are. It also transfers execution risk into the share count. Rocket Lab's cost of capital, not its factory floor, is now the variable that decides whether Neutron reaches a pad on schedule.

That's one way to pay for a space business. Here's the only company on this beat currently paying for itself. Planet Labs, the earth-observation operator, reported quarterly revenue of 116.1 million dollars, up 58 percent year over year. It posted adjusted earnings of two cents a share against a consensus estimate of a two-cent loss — its first adjusted profitable quarter. Full-year guidance went up to 430 to 441 million dollars of revenue, with 3 to 10 million dollars of adjusted EBITDA. That's the company's first full-year profitability target. Ninety-eight percent of annual contract value is recurring.

Now say plainly what that is. This is a September 3rd print, about two weeks old, not this week's news. Both the earnings and the EBITDA figures are non-GAAP; Planet has not reported a GAAP profit. A 3-to-10-million-dollar full-year adjusted EBITDA guide is a rounding error, not operating leverage. And the recurring-revenue framing deserves one more push. If the growth is government-led, the renewal risk is a budget cycle, not a churn rate. That is a lumpier risk than ninety-eight percent recurring makes it sound.

Still, take the break seriously. Grade reservations against deliveries across this sector and the delivery column is nearly empty. Amazon's constellation is short of its milestone. China's Qianfan is short of its target. Rocket Lab's Neutron is unflown. Planet is the one name where an order book converted into recognized, profitable revenue. If that holds, it raises an uncomfortable question about where the sector's capital is going. The profitable niche in the orbital economy may be the data layer, not the infrastructure layer everyone is funding.

And here's the third way to pay for a space business. Borrow against one that has never produced free cash flow. Greenlight Capital's David Einhorn published commentary this week arguing that SpaceX's investment-grade credit rating is an anomaly. In his words, he can't find any other examples of investment-grade ratings awarded to a company with a multiyear forecast of negative free cash flow and no history of generating free cash flow. He went further. He wrote that the SpaceX IPO — the company began trading on Nasdaq as SPCX in June — might be something investors look back on as a marker that a major speculative top was near.

Grade that on both sides. This is one fund manager's letter, not a rating action, and no agency has changed anything about SpaceX's actual credit standing. The steelman against him is real. An investment-grade rating can reflect asset value, contracted government backlog and access to capital rather than trailing cash flow. That is how utilities and pipeline operators get rated. But Einhorn's critique lands on numbers disclosed yesterday in SpaceX's own filing, where the launch segment posted a 542 million dollar operating loss on 962 million dollars of revenue. Two independent pieces of evidence point the same way. Launch, priced on its own, does not clear a return.

And the fix for that rests on a capacity step that keeps moving to the right. Starship's Flight 14 is the vehicle's first attempt at orbit rather than a suborbital profile, and its first flight carrying an operational payload. It was supposed to launch September 22nd. On September 17th, SpaceX pushed it to September 28th — a six-day delay, with no explanation given. This briefing has now carried a Starship date in six consecutive editions, and every one of them moved. Treat the slip series itself as the information. The payload is 26 Starlink V3 satellites, each rated at one terabit per second of added network capacity, 26 terabits in total. That is the capacity step the credit story depends on. Every month Starship doesn't fly, that step stays in the future tense.

One correction before we close. On September 15th, this program told you Amazon's Kuiper constellation had passed the FCC's interim buildout milestone. It did not. In fact, at the FCC's June 5th waiver decision Amazon had about 330 satellites against a requirement of roughly 1,600, and projected only about 700 by the deadline. The FCC denied Amazon's request for a two-year extension. It granted a narrower waiver instead, leaving the full constellation deadline unchanged, while stripping spectrum priority from satellites launched after the missed date. That demotion runs until March 2028, or until Amazon reaches half its build-out, whichever comes first. Amazon missed the bar and was granted a waiver. It did not clear it. My apologies for the error.

Two things follow. The priority demotion has not yet been tested by an actual interference dispute, so its cost to Amazon is a priced risk, not a realized one. And Amazon's own stated cause was a shortage of available rockets, despite launch contracts worth billions of dollars. That is the same scarcity Rocket Lab is now capitalizing into an acquisition. When even the best-funded challenger can't buy enough launch, buying an operator that already owns spectrum and customers starts to look rational on its own terms. Western medium-lift scarcity is now being priced into asset values, not just into launch prices.

So where does this stand? The infrastructure layer — Starship, Neutron, Kuiper — is still mostly paper. Signed, financed, or waivered, but not delivered. The data layer, represented today by Planet, is the one place an order book is turning into cash. Watch three things into next week. Whether the September 28th Starship window holds. Whether the Iridium deal draws a second regulatory request, or Rocket Lab finally puts a first-flight date on Neutron, which has had no firm public target since an August 10th earnings-call reference to late 2026. And whether Planet's second half backs up that first full-year profitability guide.

That's the briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
