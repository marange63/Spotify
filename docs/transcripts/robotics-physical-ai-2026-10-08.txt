Good morning. It's Thursday, October 8th, 2026, and this is your robotics and physical AI briefing.

This week, the people setting the terms for embodied AI weren't the labs. They were lenders and a government. Credit funds just put a price on robotaxi risk. And China's magnet export lever comes back into play in about a month. Let's take those in order.

Start with Waymo, which upsized its debut private loan to five billion dollars. That's the next step in physical AI capital moving off venture term sheets and onto balance sheets, a shift we flagged on Tuesday. This time the money is priced by outside lenders. According to Bloomberg's reporting, relayed by several outlets, the loan grew from an initial target above three billion. The lenders are PIMCO, Blackstone, and Sixth Street, with Goldman Sachs arranging. Pricing is five and a quarter percentage points over the benchmark rate. Neither Waymo nor Alphabet has disclosed the deal itself. And it isn't done. Per Bloomberg, the allocated capital is expected to close formally in the near future. The reporting also leaves out the structure. We don't know the maturity, the collateral, or whether Alphabet stands behind any of it.

Five and a quarter points sounds punishing for a company sitting under Alphabet. The market data says otherwise. Valuation Research's second-quarter survey puts spreads on new unitranche issuance this year at roughly four and three-quarters to five and a half points. Waymo sits in the middle of that band, not above it. One caution: nobody has described Waymo's facility as unitranche, so this is a comparison on spread alone. Still, it doesn't read as distress pricing. It reads like ordinary direct-lending terms.

That matters for how you read the deal, because there are three competing explanations. The first is capital intensity. Fleets, compute, and city launches may now cost more than equity alone should fund. The second is a capital-structure choice. An Alphabet subsidiary may simply prefer non-dilutive debt to another equity round. The third is lender appetite. Credit funds want exposure to a scarce, growing, AI-adjacent asset. The upsizing from three billion to five is evidence for that third reading. The ordinary spread argues against the first. Standard terms are what you'd expect if Waymo is choosing debt, not being forced into it.

What debt does change is the economics. Interest is a fixed charge, and it lands on a business that still hasn't disclosed a per-ride margin. That raises the utilization bar every new city has to clear. And the money is tied to a growth target: one million paid rides a week, across twenty cities globally, this year.

Now watch the baseline. The loan coverage says Waymo currently logs roughly four hundred thousand rides a week. But that matches, nearly word for word, what Waymo's own blog said back in February. So the four hundred thousand looks like eight-month-old boilerplate. The fresher figure is the one this desk cited from TechCrunch: about five hundred thousand paid rides a week as of September. On that number, the target is double the current run-rate, with under three months left in the year. Either way, the loan funds an ambition, not a demonstrated run-rate.

Two second-order effects follow, and both are this desk's inference. First, if this facility closes on these terms, expect other capital-heavy autonomy operators to be benchmarked against Waymo's spread. Second, look across at this show's private credit desk. This week it reported gating at Blue Owl's tech-focused fund. It also reported Fed examiners reviewing banks' private-credit exposure, after markdowns on loans to AI-threatened software companies. In the same week, PIMCO, Blackstone, and Sixth Street are lending five billion dollars into a robotaxi fleet. Credit appetite appears to be rotating, away from AI's perceived victims and toward its physical build-out. So what: physical AI's biggest consumer deployment now carries third-party credit, and that credit came on ordinary terms.

Credit markets just priced one input to physical AI. The other input is priced by a government.

China's one-year suspension of its sweeping rare-earth export controls is set to expire November 10th, according to most reporting. We flagged that date last week, so take it as a callback. What's new is the mechanism underneath it. According to TechTimes' analysis, the rule, Announcement No. 61, reaches finished goods made abroad. The trigger is Chinese-origin rare-earth content above zero point one percent of value.

On Saturday we covered German suppliers building robot hands and actuators outside China. We said then that the leverage point just slides one layer down the stack. This rule is that layer. If it snaps back, a humanoid or an actuator assembled in Germany is still in scope. All it takes is enough Chinese rare-earth content inside.

There's no near-term domestic relief, either. TechTimes says US manufacturers won't reach meaningful NdFeB magnet capacity until 2028. Separately, a DFARS defense-procurement ban on Chinese-origin magnets takes full effect January 1st, 2027. For defense autonomy, those two compound. Drone and actuator buyers face one squeeze in 2027, with US magnet capacity a year behind it.

Now the caveats. The zero point one percent scope comes from trade-press analysis, not MOFCOM's own text. The expiry date is disputed too. One source claims an extension to January, though that source contradicts itself. And expiry of a suspension isn't automatic reinstatement. Beijing could extend, or tighten selectively through licensing instead. No robot maker has reported a cost or delay hit. This is inference about exposure, not an observed impact. So what: if the lever is pulled, there is no domestic substitute inside the window.

So who does that lever actually hit? A new cut of IDC's data answers that. We aired IDC's first-half headline on October 1st: almost twenty-five thousand humanoids shipped globally. What's new is the builder's side of the ledger. Per Digitimes, relaying IDC, Chinese vendors built more than ninety-five percent of all humanoids shipped worldwide. That's the builder share. The same IDC data says Chinese customers bought about seventy-eight percent of them. That's the buyer share.

Those two numbers answer two different questions: who builds the robots, and who buys them. And that corrects us. Twice before, this desk treated the seventy-eight percent figure and a higher figure in the nineties as two trackers disagreeing on methodology. They weren't disagreeing. One measures builders, and the other measures buyers, and both come from IDC. What still disagrees is the total. The competing count we cited on Saturday was about nineteen thousand units, well below IDC's twenty-five thousand. And IDC still hasn't published how it defines a shipment.

Inside the Chinese cohort, Agibot led the half, with more than eight thousand six hundred units. That's about thirty-five percent of the global market. Unitree came second, but its figures vary too much between sources to state cleanly. The takeaway: China's lead is in making humanoids, not just in buying them.

Put the magnet story and the shipment story together, and the asymmetry is stark. If Chinese vendors build over ninety-five percent of the world's humanoids, a Chinese magnet control barely touches Chinese builders. It falls on the small slice built elsewhere. By this desk's reading, that slice is mostly the Western cohort carrying the richest valuations. Their cost curve now depends on a counterparty that is also their competitors' home government.

Tesla sits inside that exposed slice. Reporting this week says Tesla placed its first large-scale component order, for roughly five thousand Optimus units. It is also reportedly auditing Chinese suppliers ahead of production. Tesla hasn't confirmed either point, and the report is single-sourced. A component order is not robots built. On October 1st we carried supply-chain claims of parts for fifteen thousand units this year. Don't read the five thousand as a cut. A first large-scale order and a full-year parts figure may simply measure different things. So what: Tesla is the named Western builder most directly downstream of whatever happens on November 10th.

Pulling the threads together. What's inflecting is financing and manufacturing. Paid robotaxi scale is now financeable with outside private debt, at ordinary terms. And China's humanoid manufacturing dominance is now quantified rather than asserted. What's still unproven is Waymo's per-ride margin, the quality of humanoid demand inside China, and whether any robot maker reports a real magnet-driven cost hit. And for the third time this week, this desk found no fresh primary evidence on the model or world-model layer. This has been a week of capital, components, and regulation, not robot brains.

Three catalysts to watch. First, any official Chinese notice on the rare-earth truce before November 10th, and the pace of licensing after it. Second, the formal close and terms of Waymo's loan. Third, Tesla's October earnings call, for a primary Optimus production number instead of a secondhand one.

That's the briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
