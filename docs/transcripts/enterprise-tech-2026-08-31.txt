Good morning. It's Monday, August 31st, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

Here is the fact that has not aired on this show yet. Claude is now the default model inside Slack. Not an option, not a plugin you switch on — the default, powering Slackbot and a new feature called Claude Tag for team decision-making. Alongside it, Salesforce shipped something called Salesforce in Claude. It is a plugin with thirty-seven prebuilt sales skills. A seller can use it to reason over live Salesforce revenue data, automate pipeline updates, and take governed action, all from inside Claude, without ever opening Salesforce.

Quick grounding first, because the print itself is old news here. Salesforce's fiscal second quarter delivered current remaining performance obligation of thirty-three and a half billion dollars, and management raised full-year guidance to a range of forty-six point one to forty-six point four billion. Agentforce's annualized run-rate sits above one and a half billion dollars, a number Salesforce defines itself and does not reconcile against recognized revenue. Those figures, and the mark on the Anthropic stake, ran on this show on the twenty-seventh, the twenty-eighth and the twenty-ninth. The operating business is fine. That is not today's story, and we are not re-litigating it.

Today's story is what Salesforce just conceded. On Friday, this show read the Claudeforce announcement as Salesforce giving up the reasoning layer to Anthropic and retreating to what it actually owns: the CRM data, the workflow, the governance. The Slack default and the thirty-seven-skill plugin say that retreat goes one layer further. Salesforce's own leadership put it plainly. When people stop using Salesforce through the traditional human interface, and start using it through an agentic interface, that dramatically increases the value of Salesforce. Listen to what that sentence concedes. A vendor whose front end is being bypassed has two choices. It can fight the bypass, or it can bless it. Blessing it is cheaper. It is also what you say once you have lost the argument over where the interface lives.

So what does that mean for the business model? Salesforce has historically billed per seat, and a seat implies a person opening the product. If the sales conversation now happens inside Claude, and the coordination happens inside Slack, then Salesforce is billing for access to a system of record while a rival's interface owns the attention sitting on top of it. No pricing has been disclosed. No revenue-sharing terms with Anthropic have been disclosed. No named Claudeforce customer has been disclosed either. That absence matters. Nobody yet knows what a seat is worth once the seat stops opening the product. And for the buyer, the drift toward metering by action rather than by employee starts exactly here, which is the moment a chief information officer loses headcount as a cost lever and inherits a usage bill instead.

There is a concentration risk buried in this too. Making one lab's model the default across Agentforce's reasoning engine, Agentforce Vibes, Agentforce Coworker and now Slack ties Salesforce's roadmap, its latency and its reliability to Anthropic's release cadence. That is precisely the dependency multi-model neutrality was designed to avoid. If Anthropic ships an update that changes agent behavior, or has an outage, Salesforce inherits that across its flagship products and its messaging layer at the same time. Contrast that with GitLab, which on August twenty-first shipped Duo Model Selection as generally available in its 18.4 release. That lets the customer choose the model underneath instead of inheriting a default. It is a feature release with no adoption data attached, so it proves nothing on its own. But it is the opposite architectural bet. Salesforce picked a default; GitLab picked a menu. Both are wagers that the model is not where the retention lives. They just price the risk differently.

If the interface is negotiable, the next question is what is left to defend underneath it. Part of the answer arrived on August seventeenth, when Google's Agent2Agent protocol formally joined the Agentic AI Foundation. That is the Linux Foundation body which already hosts Anthropic's Model Context Protocol. A2A is backed by more than one hundred and fifty organizations, and the foundation's platinum members include AWS, Anthropic, Google, Microsoft and OpenAI. So the hyperscalers and the labs are now jointly governing the neutral plumbing between agents. Google Cloud's own framing is that A2A lets agents discover, delegate and collaborate across frameworks without custom integrations. That is the point. Once the plumbing is standardized and neutral, integration work stops being a moat. Platform teams and systems integrators get a stable target to build against, which is good for enterprise buyers and bad for anyone who was charging for a connector catalogue. One caveat, plainly stated: nobody publishes how many enterprises are running A2A or MCP multi-agent systems in production rather than in pilot. A membership count is a count of logos, not of deployments. The practical takeaway for a buyer is narrower than the headline. Connectivity is no longer something to pay a premium for. Reliability and audit still are.

Which leaves one layer standing: governance. Who authorizes what an agent may touch, and the audit trail behind it. It is the last thing in the stack that a model vendor cannot obviously absorb — except that a model vendor just tried. Last Wednesday this show argued that Anthropic shipping enterprise-managed authorization for Claude's connectors had turned agent permissioning into a platform primitive rather than a product. Under that feature, an administrator authorizes a connector once through the identity provider, and employees inherit access automatically the first time they open Claude, with no individual grant prompts. Now set that against Obsidian Security. At the start of this month, Obsidian closed an eighty-five million dollar Series D, led by Crescent Cove Advisors with participation from every existing investor, valuing the company at one point one billion dollars. More than one hundred of its customers spend over a hundred thousand dollars a year with it, and more than fourteen spend over a million. Obsidian's entire pitch is governing what agents like Copilot Studio, Agentforce and Claude Code are permitted to touch inside enterprise software.

One of those two readings has to be wrong. Either agent governance becomes a checkbox that ships with the platform, and Obsidian gets commoditized from underneath. Or it becomes a genuine, separately budgeted line item, and the labs' version is thinner than it looks. The evidence does not resolve it, and it is worth being precise about why. Anthropic has not published a price for enterprise-managed authorization, and neither has Microsoft nor Salesforce. The strongest honest claim is that the capability shipped with no visible paid add-on attached, which is an inference rather than a disclosure. On the other side, Obsidian's own announcement does not separate agent-governance revenue from the SaaS security posture business the company already sold. Those customer-spend figures are one combined book. So the test is specific. Watch for Obsidian to break out an agent-governance line, or for one of the labs to put a price tag on managed authorization instead of shipping it quietly. Until one of those happens, treat this as an open wager and not a settled trend.

Which brings the week to its point. Today's three load-bearing numbers are a vendor-defined run-rate, a foundation's own membership count, and a private round priced by the people writing the check. None of that is disqualifying. None of it is independent either. And the structural read is this. The layer everyone assumed was safest to hold, the interface, is the one that just got given away. The layer everyone assumed was free is the one venture capital just priced at over a billion dollars.
