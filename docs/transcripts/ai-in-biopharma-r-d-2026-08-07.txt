Good morning. It's Friday, August 7th, and this is your briefing on AI in biopharma R&D.

The freshest hard number this week comes from Recursion Pharmaceuticals, which reported second-quarter results on Wednesday and, for once, put actual numbers on what its agentic AI tools are doing inside the workflow. Recursion says its Target Discovery Agent cut target-mining time from weeks to hours. Its Drug Design Agent cut structural analysis from four hours to thirty minutes. And its Clinical Strategy Agent lifted trial enrollment rates by a factor of 1.3 to 1.6 times. Alongside that, Genentech optioned its first neuroscience target into Recursion's early discovery program, layering onto an existing collaboration that already covers up to forty potential small-molecule programs, with more than three hundred million dollars in potential milestones per program plus tiered royalties running up to high single digits. And Recursion did this while tightening the belt: cash fell from $753.9 million at year-end to $556.8 million at the end of June, but with runway now guided into early 2028 assuming no new financing. Second-quarter R&D spend came in at $89.6 million, down from $128.6 million a year earlier, and full-year cash opex guidance was cut to under $375 million. So the so-what here is that Recursion did something rare on this beat: it paired a quantified productivity claim with visible capital discipline, in the same earnings release, after years of platforms selling story without P&L progress.

It's worth being precise about what kind of AI is actually booking those gains, because the prompt this beat runs on keeps asking us to separate the workhorse from the specialist. All three of Recursion's agents are large-language-model agents pointed at the operational layer. Target mining is literature and database retrieval. The Drug Design Agent's four-hours-to-thirty-minutes win is a structural-analysis workflow, orchestration and code, not the geometric model that generates a molecule's shape. Trial enrollment is patient matching against records. That is the LLM-as-workhorse zone, and it is exactly the part of the stack that is cheapest to measure and cheapest to improve. So the takeaway is that Recursion's disclosed alpha sits squarely in the text-and-operations tier, not in the harder specialist tier where a diffusion model has to be right about biology.

But read carefully where those gains actually sit. Every one of them is pre-clinical, and every one is self-reported. There's no disclosed sample size behind that 1.3-to-1.6-times enrollment figure, no breakdown by indication, and no independent replication of any of the three metrics. And on the Genentech side, the up-to-forty-program headline is milestone-and-royalty-loaded — only $216 million has actually changed hands so far. That's the same gap this beat keeps flagging: headline deal value running well ahead of cash paid, as we saw with Insilico's Takeda deal and again with Pathos.

That distinction matters because of what the industry-wide scorecard shows about where AI's clinical value actually lands. Tracking cited at ASCO this year puts around 173 AI-designed drug programs in human trials, with zero regulatory approvals to date. Phase One completion rates for AI-discovered molecules run 80 to 90 percent, well above a historical baseline of roughly 52 percent. But Phase Two success sits around 40 percent — essentially in line with the traditional 29-to-40-percent range. A separate count presented at ASCO found 117 AI-enabled assets across 63 companies in human trials, of which just over half had completed Phase One and less than 7 percent had completed Phase Two. Neither dataset has a peer-reviewed methodology or a matched historical control, so the elevated Phase One number could just as easily reflect well-funded platforms picking better-precedented targets as it does a causal AI effect. Still, the pattern is now consistent across quarters: AI is derisking the cheap, measurable early step — target and molecule triage, structural analysis, tox prediction — without yet moving the biology-bound efficacy bar that actually decides whether a drug works. Recursion's disclosed gains fit exactly that pattern. Faster target mining and faster structural analysis are real operational value, but they say nothing yet about the odds a molecule succeeds in the clinic.

That gap is also why two very different capital bets are being placed on the same uncertainty right now. Isomorphic Labs, the DeepMind spinout, is the opposite pole from Recursion's capital-light pivot, and it's also the opposite pole on architecture. Where Recursion is booking wins from LLM agents on the operational layer, Isomorphic is betting the specialist tier: IsoDDE is the geometric, structure-prediction lineage descended from AlphaFold, released in February and roughly doubling AlphaFold 3's accuracy on the hardest ligand-binding cases. It's an expensive, de novo, structure-based platform, backed by roughly three billion dollars combined in partnerships with Eli Lilly and Novartis targeting historically undruggable proteins. But its lead candidates are still only in IND-enabling studies, with Phase One entry projected for late this year at the earliest, and there's no preclinical efficacy or tox data disclosed yet. So you have Recursion licensing capability and cutting spend while it waits for biology to prove itself, and Isomorphic making a heavier, structure-first bet with big committed dollars and no data yet either. Neither has clinical proof.

That's a real valuation-methodology problem for anyone trying to underwrite these names: do you price them like pharma pipelines, on projected clinical success, or like tools-and-services businesses, on demonstrated operational throughput? Right now the disclosed evidence points to the second book. Recursion's own quarter is a re-rating happening in real time — spend cuts plus licensing-heavy Genentech economics look a lot more like a services model than a pipeline-NPV story. And there's a second-order tell hiding in that cycle-time compression. If mining a target really collapses from weeks to hours and structural analysis from hours to minutes, the outsourced preclinical work that contract research shops sell by the hour compresses with it. Faster early discovery is good for a licensing biotech's cost structure, but it quietly pressures CRO and CDMO utilization on exactly the low-complexity, high-volume tasks agents are best at. The value doesn't disappear; it moves from billable human hours toward software throughput.

Pulling the threads together: the freshest hard evidence keeps landing on the same side of that question. Value is showing up as disclosed operational efficiency and capital-light licensing economics — Recursion's cycle-time metrics, its Genentech option, its spending discipline — not as movement in clinical success rates. Rentosertib, Insilico's TNIK inhibitor now in Phase Three, remains the standing test of whether AI-native discovery can eventually flip that story; that readout is still ahead of us. Until then, the realistic read on pharma R&D spend is narrow but real: AI is compressing cost and time in early discovery, which changes how the money is allocated and where the CROs get utilized, but it has not yet demonstrably improved the odds that a molecule works once it's in a patient. Watch for whether operational alpha like Recursion's ever converts into a moved Phase Two number — that's the open question this beat still hasn't answered.

That's your briefing. Have a good weekend.
