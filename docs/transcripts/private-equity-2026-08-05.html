Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Equity & the Deal Economy

Transcript · August 5, 2026

Good morning, it's Wednesday, August 5th, 2026, and this is your private equity briefing.

We've spent three straight sessions asking the same question: is realized carry actually thawing across the big alternative managers, or is the industry still just marking paper gains? We now have a partial answer, and it came from KKR. On its July 30th call, KKR reported what it called the highest monetization quarter in company history: $848 million of realized performance income, alongside $220 million of realized investment income. That is a real number, and it is genuinely new. But listen to how KKR described the underlying exits: diversified across strategies, regions, and vintages, with return multiples ranging from 2x up to 20x. Diversified is doing a lot of work in that sentence. Management gave no discrete deal count, and did not say how much of that $848 million came from clean third-party sales or IPOs versus continuation vehicles or dividend recaps. So treat the headline as real cash, but not yet a transparent one.

There's also a wrinkle worth flagging before anyone runs a year-over-year comparison on that figure. KKR this quarter started reporting realized performance fees from its K-Series private equity and infrastructure vehicles separately, in a new fee-related performance revenue line that came in at $255 million. That's a reporting-methodology change, not a market signal, and it means the $848 million headline isn't strictly comparable to prior quarters without adjustment. Keep that in your back pocket.

Now widen the lens, because KKR's number sits inside a broader pattern across the big four. Fee-related earnings — the annuity-like line driven by assets under management, not by exits — is compounding fast everywhere. Blackstone posted fee-related earnings of $1.8 billion for the quarter, up 22% year over year, on total AUM of $1.35 trillion, up 11%. Apollo posted record fee-related earnings of $785 million, up 25%, alongside a record $60 billion of organic inflows. That's the fee machine, and it's working exactly as advertised.

The cleanest tell that this is a two-speed story sits inside a single manager. Ares' private equity group posted fee-related earnings up 55% year over year — but realized performance income in that same segment fell 6%, on lower realized net performance income. Same manager, same segment, fees up sharply, carry down. That is the whole thesis in one earnings table: the base-management-fee annuity keeps compounding regardless of what's happening on the exit side, while the return of actual cash to limited partners stays lumpy, selective, and increasingly concentrated in whichever manager happens to be monetizing that quarter.

So why does KKR look like an outlier rather than the start of a broad thaw? Because the industry-wide exit data hasn't turned. S&P Global counted 1,504 announced private equity and venture exits globally in the first half of 2026, down 6% from 1,601 a year earlier — and exit counts have now fallen for two consecutive quarters. Headline exit value looks fine because a handful of megadeals skew the total upward. But deal count is the truer proxy for broad-based liquidity, and it keeps falling. That's the gap where managers and limited partners talk past each other: a general partner can point to strong exit value on an earnings call while an allocator is still waiting on distributions. KKR had a great quarter. The system as a whole did not.

That gap is exactly why so much of this cycle's liquidity is being engineered rather than realized in the traditional sense. GP-led continuation vehicles hit a record $108 billion in volume in 2025, more than double 2024, with single-asset deals now the most common structure — sponsors moving a prized portfolio company into a new vehicle they still control, booking that as liquidity for the old fund's investors. Layer onto that the growing use of NAV-based credit facilities. ILPA, the limited partner trade body, says its members are increasingly being kept in the dark on the costs and structure of these facilities — and flags a specific concern: NAV loans used to fund early distributions, distributions that are often recallable, can flatter both IRR and distributions-to-paid-in without representing a genuine exit. The Fund Finance Association pegs that NAV loan market at roughly $100 billion today, on a path to $600 billion by 2030 — that's a forecast, not a confirmed figure, but the direction of travel matters. Put those two mechanisms together, continuation vehicles and NAV-funded distributions, and a decent share of what gets reported to limited partners as "distributions" this cycle may be liquidity manufactured within the private equity system itself, not cash that actually exited it. If that engineered share unwinds or gets recalled, some managers' recent DPI improvement looks less durable than the headline suggests.

Now the entry side, which closed the loop this week on the other end of the cycle. Electronic Arts' $55 billion take-private by Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund, Silver Lake, and Affinity Partners closed on or about the close of trading on August 4th — the largest leveraged buyout ever recorded, surpassing the 2007 TXU Energy deal, after nearly a year of shareholder votes, antitrust review, and an unusually long national-security review given PIF's sovereign status. We've covered this deal's mechanics before, so just the marker: the largest deployment of sponsor equity and buyout leverage in history went out the door this week, real capital, real leverage, committed to a single company. And in the same week, every fresh exit data point stayed soft. Entry side wide open. Exit side still jammed.

Pull it together and the private equity cycle is running at two speeds. The fee-related-earnings machine is durable and compounding — 20 to 40% growth across Blackstone, KKR, Apollo, and Ares, and that durability is what's supporting alternative-manager equity valuations even while carry realizations stay selective. But the return of cash to limited partners is still franchise-specific, not broad-based. KKR proved carry can thaw for a platform with the scale and asset selection to engineer its own liquidity. It did not prove the system-wide logjam has broken, and the falling exit count says it hasn't. The binding risk here isn't headline assets under management, which keeps growing everywhere — it's whether that record dry powder, $170 billion at Ares alone, can find genuine third-party exits before fund lives stretch further and fee-earning conversion stalls.

The near-term catalyst that will tell us more: Carlyle is due to report its own second-quarter results this morning, the same day as this briefing, and its realized carried-interest figure — coming right behind KKR's record quarter — is the number to watch for whether the thaw is broadening or staying confined to the biggest names.

That's the state of the private equity machine. Thanks for listening, and I'll see you next time.
