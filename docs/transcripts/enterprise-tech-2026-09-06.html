Cautious Optimism Briefings

Enterprise Technology & Software

Good morning. It's Sunday, September 6th, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

We start with Zscaler, because its September 3rd earnings report forces a correction to an argument this show made twice this week. The quarter itself was strong. Revenue grew 25 percent year over year, to 898.2 million dollars in the fiscal fourth quarter. Then the company guided fiscal 2027 revenue growth to a range of 16.6 to 17.5 percent. That is roughly a third below the pace it had just delivered. On the same call, chief executive Jay Chaudhry made the opposite case about demand. He told investors, as reported, that you cannot win a patching race against AI. So the tension sits inside one company in one week. A guide that says growth slows by a third, next to a chief executive arguing the threat landscape is accelerating.

Here is the correction. On Friday, this briefing built a frame around Palo Alto Networks, and on Saturday it generalized that frame across HPE, CrowdStrike and Zoom. The claim was that fiscal 2027 guide-downs were sorting vendors by how much memory and cloud hosting cost sits inside their own cost of goods sold. Zscaler looked like the case that would break the rule. It is a pure cloud security vendor with no hardware business, and it guided down anyway.

It does not break the rule. Zscaler's chief financial officer, Kevin Rubin, told the call that the company is seeing higher prices and tighter availability for memory, storage and processors. He said capital expenditure will remain elevated during fiscal 2027 because of higher component pricing, especially memory. That is the same input-cost story attributed to Palo Alto and to HPE earlier in the week. The exposure simply lands somewhere else on the income statement. It does not show up in gross margin, where Rubin pointed instead to a one-time government hardware deployment in the year-ago quarter as the real distortion. It shows up in capital spending and in cash flow. So the sorting rule survives, with better evidence than it had before. Three vendors, three different cost lines, one shared component-cost story running under all of this week's fiscal 2027 guide-downs.

One thing Zscaler did not disclose is any dollar figure for its AI business. Chaudhry said Security for AI bookings rose more than 50 percent sequentially in the fourth quarter. Rubin said the pipeline for that same category grew 75 percent. Those are real signals of direction. They are bookings and pipeline, not recognized revenue or annual recurring revenue, so they tell you nothing about magnitude. And on whether the fiscal 2027 guide is genuinely conservative, the honest answer is that we cannot tell. Sizing that would mean comparing this guide against what Zscaler originally guided for fiscal 2026 a year ago, and that original number could not be verified this cycle. Management calling its own guide conservative is framing, not fact. It also comes from a team with an obvious interest in that framing.

Widen the lens across security and the same shape repeats. Palo Alto Networks grew fiscal fourth quarter revenue 34 percent, and its next-generation security annual recurring revenue, the AI and cloud product line, grew 63 percent. It still guided fiscal 2027 revenue growth down to 23 to 24 percent. The stock fell on a beat.

CrowdStrike sits on the other side of that guidance line and tells the same story about mix. It posted a record 332.8 million dollars of net new annual recurring revenue, up 51 percent from a year earlier, and raised its full-year outlook for that metric. Put all three companies together and the pattern is consistent. In every case the AI-linked product line is growing far faster than the company that contains it. In every case the market is pricing the company on the slower number.

That is the finding this week. AI security demand is producing fast sub-lines inside decelerating companies, not faster companies. So the thing to watch next quarter is whether that AI-linked recurring revenue is net-new budget, or whether it is cannibalizing legacy firewall and network security spend inside the same accounts. If it is substitution, the deceleration is honest and the guides are not sandbagged. There is a consolidation read here as well. A vendor guiding growth up carries cheaper currency for platform acquisitions than a vendor guiding growth down. Expect the acquirer set in security to re-sort along the guidance split rather than along size.

The same test applies one level up the stack, at Salesforce. Its second quarter revenue grew 11 percent, to 11.3 billion dollars. Agentforce, its AI agent product, crossed 1.5 billion dollars of annual recurring revenue, up more than 240 percent. That figure has already aired on this beat several times this week. It is the number Salesforce wants covered.

The number that settles the argument is a different one. Current remaining performance obligation is the company's own best measure of forward visibility, and it came in at 33.5 billion dollars, up 14 percent. That is barely above the rate at which total revenue is growing. A product line compounding at 240 percent, sitting inside a forward book growing in the mid teens, is a fast product rather than a shift in the company's economics. Salesforce guides full-year revenue growth of 11 to 12 percent, and nothing in that guide bends.

Be fair to the arithmetic, though. An AI line worth 1.5 billion dollars, inside a company guiding roughly 46 billion of revenue, is too small to move the aggregate growth rate no matter how fast it compounds. This is not evidence that the product is failing. It is evidence that the product is not yet material. There is a duration trap to respect too. That forward-book metric measures contracted duration. If Agentforce is sold on short consumption terms, it would read weakly there even while consuming real budget. Watch whether the gap closes as two pending deals land. Salesforce says its acquisitions of Fin, an AI customer service platform, and Contentful are both expected to close this quarter. Expected, not completed, and two integration workstreams arriving at once.

To close, a counterweight on what deployment actually looks like once you are past guidance language and bookings language. Cisco has been rolling out an internal AI agent called MyAgent to its roughly 90,000 employees, with the rollout starting in August. It is worth naming because it is a named architecture choice rather than an activation count. The system routes simple queries to lightweight models and complex analysis to more capable ones. Most of the infrastructure runs on-premises, which Cisco says gives it direct control over both cost and data security. In the finance function, the company says AI now produces 80 to 90 percent of the first draft of the management discussion section of its public filings, turning work that took analysts days into minutes.

Three caveats belong on that. It is a company claim from a chief financial officer interview, not an audited productivity study. It is not this week's news either, since the deployment was announced back around the start of July. And Cisco sells on-premises infrastructure, so it has an obvious commercial interest in showcasing an architecture that favors exactly what it sells. Even so, tiered model routing paired with on-premises inference is a direct answer to the cost problem running under everything else in this briefing. If it becomes standard enterprise practice, it is a headwind to any frontier lab whose revenue model assumes enterprises will default to the most expensive model for every query.

Put it together and the throughline is this. The AI demand story in enterprise software and security is real at the line-item level, and it has not yet appeared in a single one of these companies' forward growth rates.

That's the briefing. Have a good day.
