Good morning, and welcome to Cautious Optimism Briefings. It's Thursday, September 10th, 2026, and we start with a test this show set on air five days ago that has just resolved.

On September 6th we flagged that Bitcoin's futures book was rebuilding leverage faster than the price it was levered to, with funding running near 10% annualized. We also named the two things that would prove that alarm wrong. Either funding pushes materially higher, or open interest falls back while price holds. Neither looked likely at the time. The second one has now fired.

Funding on perpetual futures now sits at roughly 0.0021% every four hours. Annualize that and you get about 4.7%. That is roughly half the level we flagged five days ago, on exactly the same four-hour convention, which is what makes it the one figure in this story that compares cleanly across the two briefings. And Bitcoin has not paid for the calm with price. It has held the high seventy-thousands all week, with the most recent prints on September 9th running between about seventy-eight and seventy-nine thousand dollars. That is a few percent below the September 4th high of $81,167.

The positioning data says the same thing. Open interest is down 3.8% since August 21st, to 318,600 bitcoin. Treat that as a percentage change inside its own series and nothing more. The source is a secondary aggregator, it does not disclose which venues the figure covers, and this show has already conceded on air that aggregated positioning data told three different stories in five days. Liquidations back up the direction anyway. About $28 million over three days is a quiet tape, not a cascade. Price firm, leverage draining out underneath it.

So the alarm did not fire, and I want to be disciplined about what that does and does not mean. It is not a bullish signal. Falling open interest with a firm price is a deleveraging bid, not fresh accumulation, and that is what this show said on September 7th. The low funding print cuts against us as well as for us. Cheap carry is precisely what lets net-long positioning rebuild without ever tripping the funding alarm traders watch, so a calm reading is weaker evidence than it looks. What changed is not the direction of risk. It is the shape of it.

That shape matters into two binary events next week: the Senate's cloture vote on crypto market structure on the 15th, and the Fed decision on the 16th. A leveraged book gaps. A deleveraged one grinds. Less fuel on the pile means less mechanical downside if the news disappoints, and equally less short-covering fuel if it doesn't. The honest forecast is a smaller move than the positioning chatter implies, with no view at all on direction.

There is a second-order cost to that calm. When funding is this cheap, the carry available to market-neutral funds shrinks. Those are the desks running cash-and-carry: long spot or the ETF, short the futures. Squeeze their spread and they either re-lever to hit their return target or they walk. Either way, an arbitrage bid that has been quietly absorbing ETF creation flow gets thinner right as the calendar gets loud. I can't give you a basis number to size that with, because no dated, quotable figure met this show's sourcing bar this week. Take it as a mechanism, not a measurement.

Which raises the obvious question. If the futures book has gone quiet, who is actually setting the price? The answer is the spot and ETF bid, and that bid has been anything but steady. September opened with a $236.5 million single-day outflow, most of it out of BlackRock's IBIT. Two sessions later it reversed hard, with $730.9 million coming in on September 3rd, the largest single day since mid-January, and IBIT again taking the majority of it.

Then look at the month. Across the five reported trading days, net flow totals $723.5 million. Strip out that one September 3rd session and September is essentially flat. One day made the month. And on September 8th the streak broke again, with $46.6 million coming back out alongside outflows from spot Ethereum funds in the same session.

On four of the last five runs this show has argued that ETF flow is reactive, two-way, and concentrated in one fund's decisions. September 8th is the fourth confirmation, so I'll treat it as settled premise rather than re-prove it. The useful part is the mechanism. Heading into a binary week, the marginal buyer of Bitcoin is a wrapper that can swing by hundreds of millions of dollars on a single headline. Two-sided flow of that kind stresses the creation and redemption machinery and widens the wrapper's premium and discount to net asset value. The ETF transmits volatility to spot rather than absorbing it. That is a thin, jumpy foundation for price discovery into a Fed decision.

So who is that marginal buyer? The correlation data says macro-hedge money. Bitcoin's 90-day correlation with gold hit 0.86 in the first week of September, the highest reading since the middle of 2020. Over the same window its correlation with the S&P 500 fell to 0.18, against readings above 0.65 versus the Nasdaq in the prior two years. On paper, that is Bitcoin trading as a monetary hedge rather than a risk asset.

The statistic has not survived contact with events. The September 4th payrolls print marked Bitcoin down within minutes, which is not how gold behaves. And the Middle East escalation this show flagged on Tuesday produced no safe-haven bid at all. Two live tests in six days, both against the digital-gold read, inside the same window that produced the six-year-high correlation. The more likely explanation is that gold and Treasuries became unusually macro-sensitive this quarter, and a rolling ninety-day window is picking up gold's behavior rather than a change in who owns Bitcoin. Nobody has published the methodology behind that reading either, so attribute the number rather than adopt it.

It matters who is right here, because the two readings imply different holders. A genuine gold-like regime attracts macro funds sizing Bitcoin as a hedge sleeve, and that is patient money with long holding periods. It also repels the trend-followers who had been trading it as a risk-on proxy, and that is the faster money. One last note on the macro backdrop: August producer prices print later today, consumer prices tomorrow, and neither number exists yet. Whatever the correlation argument implies about real yields, it is a pending catalyst, not settled data.

Concentration is the thread running through all three of these stories, and there is exactly one place this quarter where it is measured rather than inferred. Coinbase, in results reported back on July 30th, disclosed that it held an average of $20 billion of USDC inside its own products during the second quarter. That is more than 30% of all USDC in circulation. Same logic as one fund dominating ETF flow and two issuers owning the stablecoin market, except here it is a hard number. A single exchange's balance sheet holds roughly a third of the regulated dollar token everyone else settles trades in.

The rest of that quarter tells you why the float matters so much. Revenue was $1.2 billion, down about 19% year over year, even as spot trading market share hit an all-time high of 10.3%. Record share paired with falling revenue is fee compression winning the argument, not growth. Prediction markets more than doubled quarter over quarter, past a $100 million annualized run rate, which is real but small against that gap. So the earnings story leans harder on float income than on the trading franchise, and that makes the equity partly a levered bet on the front end of the curve. Coinbase's September filing to bring equity perpetuals onshore, meanwhile, still needs CFTC sign-off with no timeline attached. Broader surface area, not yet more revenue.

So here is next week. The cloture vote lands on the 15th, the Fed on the 16th, and the futures book that was supposed to make that window explosive has quietly gone flat. A calm book into a loud week means the news itself will matter more than the positioning around it. That is the opposite of how the last two weeks have traded.
