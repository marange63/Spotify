Good morning, it's September 14th, and this is your private credit briefing.

Start with the number that measures a question this briefing has been circling all week. JPMorgan's tracked pipeline now shows more than 138 billion dollars of private-equity-backed acquisition debt heading for US and European credit markets in the coming months. Roughly 92 billion of that is US financing. The rest is Europe, at about 40 billion euros, its strongest showing since 2021. And the US leveraged buyout calendar is now at its highest level since 2007. Most of the supply is expected to land between late September and early October. All week the argument here has been that the low default rate is really a statement about how open the refinancing window is. This is that window, finally measured in dollars.

Resist the easy read, though. A pipeline record is a volume record. It is not an underwriting record. We went looking specifically for the leverage multiples and sponsor equity contributions on 2026 buyouts, so we could set them against 2007 and against the 2021 vintage. We could not get a figure worth putting on air. Nothing that surfaced this week tells us how levered these deals are, how much equity sponsors are writing, or what the covenant packages look like. So the honest move is to say the comparison isn't available, rather than borrow the 2007 analogy on the strength of a dollar count.

What we do know is that the demand side looks healthy on its face. US leveraged loan funds just posted their strongest weekly inflows since January, in the week ending September 2nd. Banks are underwriting this calendar, but cautiously, with 2022's hung-loan experience still fresh. So the window isn't merely open. It is being actively widened by fund flows. That extends the benign default statistic without telling us anything has improved underneath it.

Now, where does this paper clear, and at what price. European single-B term loans are expected to price around 325 to 350 basis points over Euribor for the stronger credits. Weaker ones are expected to need 375 to 425, with room to tighten toward 300 if demand holds. Those are expected levels on deals that have not priced yet, not achieved prints. They are informative anyway, because they sit inside where direct lenders have needed to price to win business. The reading the evidence supports is this. The borrowers who can clear a syndicated calendar that cheaply are precisely the ones direct lenders cannot hold onto at their required spreads. The counter-case is live too: direct lenders may instead pick up the larger, more complex deals banks won't touch. Only one of those two has a calendar behind it right now.

One thing worth flagging inside the same pipeline. There is up to 80 billion dollars of potential additional US debt tied to data-center transactions. That is explicitly described as potential, not committed. We looked for the named borrowers behind it and found none, so hold the figure loosely. But it is the clearest sign yet that AI capex is starting to route through floating-rate leveraged finance rather than only hyperscaler balance sheets. That matters because it changes the holder base. Loan funds and CLOs take that risk in covenant-light, floating-rate form, which is a very different transmission path from investment-grade bonds sitting with insurers.

So if the borrowers are queuing up to leave, who is supposed to be lending to them. Blue Owl's second-quarter filing is the cleanest primary read available, and it is one of the largest direct-lending platforms in the asset class. Its credit business carries 158.1 billion dollars of assets under management, up 9 percent from a year earlier. But originations in the quarter were 3.6 billion dollars against net deployment of just 0.6 billion. Repayments are very nearly matching new lending.

Read that generously and it's a book turning over on schedule in a healthy market. Read it skeptically and it's adverse selection: the credits strong enough to repay are leaving, and what stays behind is the rest. The numbers alone cannot settle which reading is right. They do, however, point at the incentive. Blue Owl is sitting on 17.5 billion dollars of assets not yet earning fees, worth roughly 229 million dollars of annual fees once deployed. That fee is conditional on deployment, not earned. A record supply calendar arriving just as a lender that size has capital piling up faster than it can place it is exactly the setup where underwriting standards loosen. Not because anyone intends it. Because the fee clock is running.

While new money queues up, old money can't get out. Bridgepoint Credit just moved 1.2 billion euros of senior secured European middle-market loans out of its 2017-vintage direct-lending fund and into a continuation vehicle led by Pantheon. Existing investors can take cash or roll into the new structure. Bridgepoint's Paul Johnson said plainly that some investors expected these loans monetized around year three. They are now seeking an exit nearly a decade in.

Notice what's missing from that deal. No price. No discount to par was disclosed. A continuation vehicle is priced by the parties who arrange it, not by a competitive bid, so treat it as a liquidity mechanism and not as a mark on the underlying credit. The same instinct shows up in a different wrapper. Arcmont's head of NAV financing, Peter Hutton, puts NAV loan adoption at roughly 40 percent among European mid-market sponsors, against about 30 percent in the US. That is his estimate, from a lender with a commercial stake in the narrative, not an industry survey. Either way, neither a continuation vehicle nor a NAV loan is a repayment. Both convert an unrealized position into cash without the underlying loan actually performing.

And a 2017-vintage fund needing a manufactured exit at year nine sets the expectation for what comes next. The 2021 and 2022 direct-lending vintages are far larger, and they are walking toward the same point with much more capital behind them. Pantheon alone has put roughly 4.6 billion dollars into European private-credit secondaries since 2018. That is one buyer's cumulative activity, not a measure of market capacity. The so-what is that the exit valve for this asset class is increasingly a secondary market, and secondary markets eventually set prices the primary marks have to answer to.

One closing data point, because it's the rare case where tail credit got a competed price instead of a model's guess. The Fed's Main Street Lending Program extended 16.6 billion dollars of credit to small and mid-sized businesses back in 2020. On August 20th it sold what was left of that loan book to Marblegate's 1914 Acquisition vehicle for 516 million dollars, roughly 25 cents on the dollar. Say the context in the same breath: more than 75 percent of the original borrowers had already repaid in full. This was the worst of the worst, sold in a real transaction. Treat 25 cents as a floor comparator on untested tail credit, not as a private-credit recovery rate.

So where does the cycle actually stand. Supply is reopening at a scale we have not seen since before the financial crisis. The strongest borrowers have somewhere cheaper to go. And the largest private lenders are accumulating capital faster than they can deploy it, against a book that is not exiting on schedule. The unresolved risk is not the spread level. It is the mismatch between dry powder and discipline. And note that both outcomes cut against the complacent read. If the calendar clears smoothly, the refinancing window widens, defaults stay elective, and the benign statistic extends without the credit behind it improving. If it does not clear, banks are left holding hung paper for the first time since 2022, and that is the channel through which syndicated indigestion reaches private credit.

Watch three things. Whether the late-September calendar actually clears without banks getting stuck. Wednesday's Federal Reserve decision, as a scheduled input into how wide that refinancing window stays, with no outcome assumed here. And third-quarter BDC non-accrual and payment-in-kind disclosures in November.

That's the briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
