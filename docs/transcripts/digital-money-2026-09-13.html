Cautious Optimism Briefings

Digital Money, Crypto, and Market Structure

Transcript · September 13, 2026

Good morning. It's Sunday, September 13th, and this is your Digital Money briefing.

Start with the clearest evidence yet that the private sector isn't waiting for Congress. Coinbase has partnered with the payments infrastructure provider Moov to route stablecoin payments and settlement through more than a thousand US community banks and credit unions. The point of the deal is that those banks can accept and settle USDC under their own charter, rather than through an outside processor. And the timing is not an accident. The Senate holds a procedural cloture vote on the Digital Asset Market Clarity Act this coming Tuesday, September 15th. That is a 60-vote threshold just to open floor debate, not a vote to pass the bill, and passage odds beyond it have been fading. By wiring stablecoin rails into a thousand-plus community banks days before that vote, Coinbase is handing those banks a commercial reason to want the rules settled in their favor. That turns a lobbying fight the bank trade groups have been winning into a harder political problem. Some of their own members now have skin in the stablecoin game.

Be precise about what this is and isn't. It's a signed distribution partnership, nothing more. Nobody has disclosed a live-bank count, a transaction volume, or a go-live date — not Coinbase, not Moov, not any of the roughly ten outlets that covered it. The public language is entirely forward-looking. The infrastructure "will support" consumer payments, merchant acceptance, settlement and payouts. So treat this as rails being laid, not rails carrying traffic. What is concrete is the read-through for Coinbase itself. Stablecoin reserve revenue ran $292 million last quarter, against $1.15 billion in total net revenue, per its 10-Q. Call it roughly a quarter of the company's top line. That is why Coinbase keeps pushing distribution deeper into banking. Every new rail is a hedge against how cyclical its trading revenue is.

The second-order risk worth naming here is for the banks, not the exchange. If stablecoin-adjacent products start pulling core deposits out of these community banks, that is a slow-burn hit to their net interest margin. That deposit-flight mechanism is exactly what the bank lobby has been fighting to prevent. It is also what makes the politics of this deal bite. There is a broader overhang to keep in view too. If cloture fails on Tuesday, the SEC and CFTC jurisdictional split stays unresolved. That split is the single biggest reason larger banks and asset managers stay cautious on tokenization. A failed vote would also reprice the regulatory-clarity premium that has been sitting in Coinbase and Circle shares all year.

That same wiring-into-the-core logic shows up on the asset side. Canada's banking regulator, OSFI, clarified this week that tokenized bank deposits carry no separate legal status from ordinary deposits. That lets federally regulated Canadian banks build blockchain-based deposit products without inventing a new regulatory category. It is a meaningfully different thing from a stablecoin. A stablecoin sits outside a bank as an externally-backed liability, while a tokenized deposit is the bank's own liability. A clean legal answer on deposit tokens removes one of the biggest objections banks raise to on-chain settlement. It is also the kind of template US regulators could lean on once the CLARITY Act's bank-charter questions resurface. Caveat this one carefully. It traces only to a secondary aggregator citing OSFI, not a primary release, and no individual Canadian bank has actually launched a product. It is a permission, not a launch.

On the Treasury side of tokenization, BlackRock's BUIDL has retaken the top spot among tokenized US Treasury funds. It sits at roughly $2.8 billion, about 18.5 percent of a market now sized near $15.9 billion, edging out Circle's USYC. The competition there is settling on fees and on how many chains a fund distributes across, not on yield, which is roughly uniform across issuers. Put the two threads together. A bank-liability tokenization ruling and an asset-tokenization market-share fight landed in the same week. That is the strongest signal so far that this stack is moving from pilot programs into regulator-sanctioned production.

Now the institutional bid itself is rotating. Spot Ethereum ETFs just had their strongest inflow stretch on record. They took in $824 million in the last week of August, their biggest five-day total since launch. Another $218 million followed the week after, and BlackRock's ETHA ran twenty straight sessions without a single outflow day. All of that happened while Bitcoin ETFs were bleeding money. So this is allocation rotating within crypto beta, not just aggregate growth. It is evidence that the institutional category is maturing past a pure-Bitcoin proxy trade. But don't overstate it. That Ethereum streak already snapped, with roughly a 24 million dollar outflow around September 9th. This is a hot run cooling, not an unbroken trend, and ETH-ETF assets remain a fraction of the cumulative Bitcoin-ETF base.

Which brings us back to Bitcoin, where the split between price action and the base layer is the cleanest narrative-versus-evidence case in the market right now. Bitcoin sits near $77,300, down off its recent push toward $80,000. US spot ETFs logged four straight days of outflows into last Friday. Yet the on-chain picture points the other way. Glassnode data show long-term holders — wallets sitting on coins for at least 155 days — now own a larger share of circulating supply than at any point on record. The futures book looks unusually clean too. Funding is running about half a basis point per four-hour period, and open interest is down more than four percent on the week. So read it this way. The marginal, momentum-driven ETF money is stepping back ahead of the Fed meeting. But the coins held longest aren't moving, and leverage isn't stacked high enough to force a cascade in either direction. That is a calmer setup than the drawdowns this market has seen before.

Hold the skeptical line on that, though. A record long-term-holder share is a level, not a flow. It tells you existing holders aren't selling. It does not tell you new buying is happening this week. And light funding is not itself bullish. Cheap carry is exactly what lets net-long positioning quietly rebuild before a big event. The setup to watch is a divergence. If long-term holders keep sitting tight while marginal ETF money keeps exiting, the coiled spring gets tighter. Once Wednesday's decision removes the overhang, that is the kind of book that can move sharply in one direction.

Both live catalysts ahead are still just that — pending. Tuesday's cloture vote and Wednesday's FOMC decision. A fresh August CPI print, up 0.4 percent month over month against July's 0.1 percent, is the input keeping rate expectations unsettled going into that meeting. But the Fed hasn't decided anything, and neither has the Senate, so I won't put odds on either. Notice one wrinkle if the Fed leans hawkish. Higher rates would raise the real yield on the short Treasuries backing stablecoins and tokenized money funds. That is a tailwind for the yield economics behind Circle and BUIDL, even as it is a headwind for the price of Bitcoin. Everything wired up this week — the community-bank stablecoin rails, the tokenized deposits, BUIDL's Treasury lead — is a bet on where those two decisions land. The rails are getting built regardless. The price of what runs on them still answers to Washington.

That's Digital Money for September 13th. Talk to you next time.
