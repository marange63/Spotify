Good morning, it's Tuesday, October 27th. Today's briefing has one frame: enterprise AI is now being paid for by cutting, not adding, and that's true on both sides of the vendor-customer table.

Start with Microsoft. On Monday, Microsoft offered a Voluntary Exit Offer to roughly 60,000 employees, about a quarter of its 223,000-person workforce as of June. Eligibility runs through vice-president level and below, on select teams. Here's the detail that carries the argument. Data centers, sales, and marketing are excluded. Xbox, Windows, Copilot, and Finance are included. So Microsoft is protecting the functions that build and sell AI capacity, and offering exits in the functions that make and administer software. That includes, notably, its own Copilot organization. Even Microsoft's flagship AI product group isn't exempt from the efficiency push. Chief People Officer Amy Coleman called it "new for us, and we'll learn from it." That reads more like an open-ended experiment than a sized cost program.

Now the caveats, because they matter. Sixty thousand is eligibility, not an exit count. The only comparable data point is April's buyout, where just over 30 percent of roughly 8,750 eligible workers took the deal. But that pool was long-tenured, retirement-age staff, not a broad population of working-age VPs and below. Reporting this week says Microsoft itself expects a much smaller take-up share this time, though no percentage has been disclosed. There is no savings figure. There is no company statement linking this program to AI capex. That framing is press and analyst interpretation, not something Microsoft has said on the record. Decisions are due the first week of December, and departures take effect December 31. This also follows a July layoff of 4,800 people, which Coleman said was smaller because the spring program had already thinned the ranks.

Here's the callback. On Saturday, this show covered SAP paying for its AI transformation out of cloud gross margin, a seven-tenths-of-a-point dip that management tied to AI investment. Microsoft is paying for its AI shift out of operating expense instead, by thinning headcount in the functions adjacent to the product. Same pressure, different line on the income statement. And my own inference, not Microsoft's: the company is running its sales pitch on itself. The buyout is a bet that Copilot and agentic tools let fewer people do the same knowledge work, which is exactly what Microsoft sells to its customers.

So Microsoft is doing to itself what its customers are already doing to their software budgets. That's where Futurum Group's October buyer survey comes in. It's the best demand-side evidence yet for this show's running thesis that enterprise AI redistributes IT spending rather than expanding it. Until now, that thesis rested on vendor-side signals, like IBM's consulting mix and SAP's margin. The headline number from buyers: expected 2026 IT budget growth is just 3.84 percent. That means AI builds and existing software renewals draw on the same slow-growing pool of money.

The mechanism is explicit. Among organizations that reallocated budget to cover AI cost overruns, 36.4 percent did it by cutting non-AI software licenses. Keep that denominator in mind. It's not all buyers, just the subset already stretched by AI spend. But it's a real mechanism, not a hypothetical one. One plain caveat: this is a survey of spending intentions, not bookings or recognized revenue. SAP's and IBM's third-quarter prints did not show a license collapse.

The sentiment data points the same way. Futurum's Net Score for enterprise applications fell from 16.4 two years ago to 9.6 now. Machine-learning and AI tooling sits at 47.8, roughly five times higher.

And the erosion is broad. Of 53 tracked enterprise-app products, 45 lost ground. The number running net-negative nearly doubled, from 11 to 20. There are two ways to read that. One is healthy consolidation: buyers trimming tail vendors onto fewer platforms, which incumbents like Salesforce and ServiceNow would claim as a win. The other is displacement: AI builds eating share from the incumbents. Futurum's own title favors displacement. It says spending "lags behind stated platform consolidation goals." I lean the same way, because the license cuts are happening inside a budget that isn't growing.

The second-order effect, and this is my inference: if renewals fund AI, per-seat pricing faces structural pressure, not cyclical softness. Seat counts become the variable buyers cut. That sharpens the pattern this show tracked last week, with Okta and ServiceNow bundling AI free into existing seats. They're defending the very line buyers are raiding.

One illustration from the survey, and it is exactly that, not a trend. A buyer described moving to AI-based apps built on Anthropic's models instead of using Workday, calling Workday's licensing very expensive. It's a single anonymous quote. But it sits in tension with something this show covered on Sunday. Workday and Anthropic announced an MCP integration on October 13th, making Claude a partner inside Workday's own agent layer. So the same lab is, for one buyer, both the integration partner and the replacement. Worth naming, not over-reading. In the interest of disclosure, Anthropic makes the model behind this show.

Which raises the next question. If the substitute is AI built on lab models, how does it actually get switched on inside a company? Anthropic answered part of that last week, and the same disclosure applies. On October 23rd, Anthropic made a unified Claude Chat and Cowork experience generally available to Claude Enterprise customers. That merges ordinary chat with autonomous, agentic task execution. The mechanism that matters is the default. The agentic capability is off by default today. It switches on by default on November 16th, unless an owner keeps it disabled. Each agentic conversation runs on an isolated, Anthropic-managed machine, and existing controls like single sign-on and the compliance API carry over.

Keep the evidence ladder straight. A default-on flag is distribution, not adoption, and no seat or usage figures exist. Recall too that Schwab's Anthropic Enterprise deal, covered here on Sunday, sits on a 20-seat floor. So this flip deepens usage inside accounts that already cleared that bar. It doesn't widen the customer base. What it does is move the decision from opt-in to opt-out, which is how features become standard configuration.

The one productivity data point is self-reported. Anthropic's own S-1 filing, as reported secondhand, describes its marketing-operations team cutting a weekly metrics report from one to two days of work to roughly two hours. That's a single internal case, not an audited customer metric. One more connector. As agentic execution defaults on, inference cost becomes something IT has to govern, inside that same thin budget. Snowflake is marketing its Cortex AI Gateway as delivering up to three times better token efficiency through model routing. No methodology or named customer was disclosed, so treat it as a vendor claim. But it signals where the next budget line is forming.

So the pattern across both halves of today's briefing. On the vendor side, Microsoft is shrinking the functions that make software to protect the ones that build and sell AI capacity. On the buyer side, enterprises are funding AI builds by letting old software licenses lapse, inside a budget barely growing at all. And the next adoption lever isn't a new purchase order, it's a default switch.

Two dates to watch. Tomorrow, Wednesday, October 28th, both Microsoft and ServiceNow report. Microsoft's own guidance from its last call set the bar: Azure growth of approximately 45 percent in constant currency, and capex over 50 billion dollars for the quarter. Watch whether Microsoft quantifies the buyout's cost or ties it to that capex. And watch whether ServiceNow's renewal and net-expansion language shows the license pressure Futurum's buyers describe. Then November 16th, when Claude Enterprise's agentic default flips on. No predictions here, just a flag that those dates will test everything laid out today.
