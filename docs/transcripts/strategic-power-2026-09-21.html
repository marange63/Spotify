Good morning, and welcome to Cautious Optimism Briefings for Monday, September 21st, 2026.

Start with the fact everything else today rests on. The Strait of Hormuz is shut, and has been for months, in the war between the United States and Iran. The market's one physical cushion against that closure was Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline, which carries crude across the kingdom to the Red Sea and loads it at Yanbu, bypassing the strait entirely. Al Jazeera says the line went in during 1981, in the Iran-Iraq war, precisely to reduce reliance on Hormuz. Fortune dates it to the same war and the same fear. That cushion is now offline, and the oil market has priced the outage as something close to a blip.

Who hit it is contested. Fortune says the attack was blamed on drones from Iranian-backed militias in Iraq. Kpler, the analytics firm, says Houthi forces struck pumping stations in two Saudi regions on September 10. Either way, the threat came from outside the geography the line was designed to escape. And headlines from April reported the same line hit and restored earlier in the war, so this is at least the second time.

Now the size of the loss and the time to fix it. The number that carries the argument is Kpler's: before the attack the line was moving about five and a half million barrels a day, with roughly four and a half million of that exported from Yanbu. Al Jazeera puts maximum capacity nearer seven million, and Fortune's lower figure, 2.6 to 4 million, is an average flow rather than a ceiling. The line was running hard when it went down. One trade report has Saudi Arabia signalling half the capacity back within days and the full line in about six weeks. That is guidance, not a restart. Kpler, writing on September 17, said no crude had loaded from Yanbu since September 11, called the half-capacity recovery unconfirmed reports, and put that recovery at about a month rather than days. As of this recording, neither Aramco nor the Saudi energy ministry has confirmed that oil is moving.

Kpler also says Yanbu's terminal storage covers only three to five days of loadings, and that Saudi crude production has fallen below six million barrels a day, a multi-decade low. Al Jazeera warns tanker tracking can miss shuttle ships crossing Hormuz with transponders off, so both the disruption and any recovery may be mismeasured.

Against all of that, Brent traded above 105 dollars on September 14 and sat near 104 by the morning of September 17. That is about 36 dollars above a year earlier. Notice what the price was responding to. It fell on a signal, while the barrels were still not loading.

The market has now been sold three different explanations for contained crude in three days. First it was the Sohar workaround, the ship-to-ship transfers through Oman. Then it was demand destruction. Now it is a Saudi restart signal. Each story arrived after the move it was used to explain. The rotation itself is the finding. There is no stable read here, and the tape is being fitted with whichever narrative is nearest to hand.

The two big agencies split the same way. The International Energy Agency's September report sees world oil supply falling 5.7 million barrels a day this year, about six percent, with demand down 2.5 million. That forecast is built on a delayed return of normal Gulf flows. In plain terms, the IEA assumes the strait stays shut. The Energy Information Administration, by contrast, forecasts Brent averaging around 90 dollars in the second half, on an explicit assumption of gradually increasing flows through Hormuz. Tehran's stated condition for opening the strait is that Washington stop obstructing and come back to the table, which has not happened. So the tape is validating the IEA's assumption, not the EIA's. Price is being capped by consumers leaving, not by supply returning, and that leaves no cushion for the next disruption.

The Gulf is only half of why refined product is tight. Russia is the other. On Sunday the thirteenth, Russia struck a Warsaw-bound train near the Polish border. The strike came a short time after another train carrying foreign dignitaries passed along the same track. Russia said it had hit railway infrastructure used to transport military cargo from European countries. The European Union's foreign policy chief, Kaja Kallas, said it was clearly meant to scare Western countries out of supporting Ukraine.

What did not happen matters as much. Poland's foreign minister reportedly called it an escalation but said there was no border violation, and I found no report of an Article 4 consultation at NATO. Meanwhile the IEA's characterisation is that Ukrainian strikes have pushed Russian product exports toward a near halt. So two product sources are impaired at once, and Moscow has just tested a logistics corridor at a price the alliance has so far set at a statement.

Two supply shocks are hard for any central bank. This Fed has decided not to look through one. On September 16 it raised the target range a quarter point, to 3.75 to 4 percent, saying the action will support a timelier return to its 2 percent goal. The ten-year Treasury yield topped 5 percent on September 15, the first close at that level since 2007, and it has hovered just under since. The monetary buffer is being spent, not held, and the long end is where borrowers feel it.

That also bears on the hard-money argument. Bitcoin's 90-day correlation with gold reached plus 0.50 in early September, the highest since 2020, and the digital-gold camp is celebrating. I read it the other way. Bitcoin fell 54 percent peak to trough from its 126,080 dollar record in October 2025, and gold fell 22 percent from its January high over the same stretch. Two assets falling together against rising real yields is a shared liquidity factor, not a vindication of debasement trades.

The bill for five percent money arrives first wherever something has to be poured in concrete. Texas shows how. This is August material, not news this week, but it is now the standing posture. Governor Abbott directed regulators to pause pending data-center interconnections until each project passes an audit. ERCOT's queue holds about 474 gigawatts of large-load requests, and roughly 90 percent of that is data centers. Reported comparisons put the queue at several times anything the Texas grid has ever served.

Read that plainly. A grid operator is conceding it cannot yet tell real load from optioned load. ERCOT is targeting a December 10 filing on the audit, and says it will not have its Batch Zero study done by April 9, 2027. So it will take more than a year to find out. It is the same test as the pipeline: announced capacity against delivered capacity. It also follows the fight in PJM, the mid-Atlantic grid, over who pays for capacity that data centers may or may not end up using. The pause is not a full stop, though. Seventeen large loads, mostly data centers, have already passed a stability assessment and are scheduled to come online later this year.

Now the cost of building what is real. NRG has aligned on principal commercial terms with an unnamed hyperscaler for a gas plant in Texas. The initial phase is 1.2 gigawatts for 3.2 billion dollars, with delivery targeted for late 2029. The deal is not final. That money is priced against a ten-year near five percent, and it does not produce a watt for three years. So grid access is turning into industrial policy administered project by project. Hyperscalers with balance sheets self-supply, speculative developers get squeezed, and incumbents with existing sites win.

Everything so far is an input to one meeting. Xi Jinping arrives in Washington on Wednesday, and the White House meeting is Thursday, September 24. This is the setup. Nothing has happened yet.

Start with the bargain already on the table. Under the Busan truce, Washington suspended its Affiliates Rule in exchange for continued Chinese exports of rare earths and critical minerals. China's suspension of its own measures is reported to expire on November 10, though I have seen no Chinese notice confirming that date. One analyst there expects the extension to land at about a year.

Both capitals will call an extension a win, which tells you it settles little. A year of truce locks in Chinese processing leverage for another cycle. It also drains the political urgency that funds Western refining and magnet capacity. Detente and dependency can deepen together.
