Good morning, this is Capital Markets Radar for Wednesday, September 23rd.

On Tuesday, oil and the AI trade bought a peace signal, and the bond market didn't. That split is the story. Whichever side of it is wrong has the bigger repricing ahead.

Start with crude, because it's the freshest catalyst. Brent fell 2.1 percent to about 98 dollars on Tuesday, and WTI fell 2.6 percent to about 89, per Yahoo Finance. The trigger was an Iranian signal that it would reopen the Strait of Hormuz within seven days if Washington took steps toward ending the blockade of Iranian oil exports.

Read the condition carefully. A senior Iranian official, in a Reuters account relayed by CNBC, said the US needs to announce officially that it wants a diplomatic resolution, and then agree a timeline. That is a political step, not a military stand-down. The proposal itself went to Washington through mediators on September 16, nearly a week before Tuesday's price move. We found no on-the-record US response.

On Monday this desk named the dated test for oil: a confirmed Saudi statement on pipeline capacity. That is not what arrived. What arrived is a conditional signal, from a different party, about a different chokepoint. And the Saudi leg still isn't confirmed. The reported restart of the East-West pipeline rests on unnamed sources cited by Arab News. Saudi Aramco did not respond to a request for comment. Even that account has the line pumping at a low rate.

So both pillars of Tuesday's relief are conditional, unconfirmed, or both. The International Energy Agency has called this the largest supply disruption in the history of the global oil market. Against that backdrop, a two-dollar retreat is a lower war premium, not an ended war. Part of Tuesday's drop may also be an overshoot unwinding. Without transit data, which we don't have, the two can't be separated.

So who actually bought the relief? Start with crypto. Bitcoin opened Tuesday near 86,600 dollars, back to levels last seen in January. That's up almost 11 percent on both the week and the month. Ethereum moved with it, also back to January levels. The fuel was a liquidation cascade. Over twenty-four hours, more than 115,000 traders were liquidated for a combined 770 million dollars, mostly on the short side. All of that comes from a single Yahoo Finance aggregation, so hold it accordingly.

Now the escalation. On Saturday this desk covered a squeeze of about 470 million dollars in shorts that took Bitcoin to roughly 81,000. This leg is bigger, and we still have no ETF-flow or on-chain data showing new money behind it. A squeeze-driven rally has a shelf life. Once the shorts are flushed, the forced buyer is gone. And if the Hormuz offer lapses, the asset that ran hardest on the relief has nothing mechanical underneath it. This is positioning unwinding, not capital arriving.

The equity version of that same bid showed up in one stock. Meta's new AI agent, Muse, reached number one on Apple's US free iPhone chart ten days after launch. It passed ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude and Instagram to get there. Meta shares rose 11 percent Monday and another 1.5 percent Tuesday. Yahoo Finance calculates that added roughly 192 billion dollars of market value in two sessions. That's a derived figure, not a company disclosure. JPMorgan upgraded the stock from Neutral to Overweight and lifted its price target from 640 dollars to 820.

Here's the skeptical read. There is no revenue number behind any of this. The market is capitalizing a download rank, not financial results. The competing read is that an agent that completes purchases is the first consumer AI product with a direct transaction take-rate. On that view, pricing it early is rational rather than euphoric. Maybe. But an adoption curve is not a monetization curve, and nobody has shown the second one yet.

The bigger point is concentration. On Sunday this desk observed that the rate move hit everything except the thing it should have hit hardest, cap-weighted tech. Meta just extended that pattern. A ten-year near 5 percent should weigh on long-duration equity. Instead, one mega-cap with a product catalyst overrode it. So the cap-weighted index is being carried by a narrative, not by a lower discount rate. That masks a tighter backdrop for everything without an AI catalyst.

Now the side of the tape that refused the relief. The Fed hiked to 3.75 to 4 percent on September 16, its first increase since 2023. What's new is the forward pricing. CME's FedWatch tool, as relayed by FXStreet, now puts the odds of a December hike near 89 percent. That's a different meeting from the one this desk tracked on Saturday, when October hike odds stood near 58 percent. So don't read the two numbers as a trend. Read the December figure on its own terms. The market treats one more hike before year-end as close to settled, even on a day when oil fell.

And on the same Tuesday that oil fell 2 percent, the rate complex gave nothing back. The ten-year Treasury sat at 4.959 percent, per CNBC, less than a basis point lower on the day. The dollar index held at 100.6, near its highest since late July, per FXStreet.

The committee's own voting says the same thing. September's vote was unanimous. InvestmentNews reports that July's meeting had three dissents, all in favor of a hike. So the committee didn't moderate its hawks. It converged on them, and that raises the bar for any dovish turn.

Chair Kevin Warsh tied the hike to inflation that has been, in his words, too high for too long. He also cited tension in the Middle East. But his stated test is whether underlying inflation is moving to target clearly and at sufficient speed. That's language about persistence, not about the price of a barrel. That gives you two readings. In the first, rates are right, and cheaper crude doesn't buy a pause. In the second, rates are simply slower. If Hormuz genuinely reopens, the input Warsh named goes away. Then 89 percent December odds become the most exposed price on the board. One honest limit: a flat ten-year on an oil down-day could also reflect term premium or supply, and nothing here lets us decompose it.

If the rate path holds, it bites floating-rate borrowers first. That's the leg this desk has called untested all week, and it now has a hard number. Fitch's trailing twelve-month private credit default rate hit a record 6.3 percent at the end of August, per Bloomberg's reporting on the Fitch report. That covers about 1,300 borrowers, and it tops the prior record of 6.1 set a month earlier. It's one methodology, and default estimates vary widely depending on who's counting. But the direction is the point.

Pricing moved too. Lord Abbett's data has new-issue private credit spreads averaging 502 basis points over the three months to August 31st, up from 475 in the first quarter. The share of sponsor-backed direct-lending deals priced between 500 and 549 basis points jumped from 25 percent to 52 percent. That makes LBO financing more expensive, which is a headwind for deal activity even if defaults plateau.

The twist is where the stress sits. Software, the sector everyone feared AI would gut, saw its default rate fall to 0.6 percent. Healthcare, industrials and manufacturing each ran at 9.9 percent. The feared casualty is the healthiest book. The stress is in old-economy borrowers squeezed by financing costs and input costs at once. Those are the same input costs that Brent feeds.

Two caveats. First, this data runs through August, so it predates the September 16 hike entirely. With December odds near 89 percent, coupons keep resetting higher into a book that was already at record defaults before the Fed moved. Second, the spread widening cuts both ways. Lord Abbett frames it as a lender-friendly reset, with tighter covenants and less leverage alongside the wider spreads. On that reading, lenders simply regained pricing power, which is arguably better underwriting rather than distress. So the credit test has started, in the channel this desk named, but its post-hike mark is still ahead.

That leaves three dated tests. First, whether Washington takes the diplomatic step Iran's offer requires, inside that seven-day window. Second, the next Fitch monthly print, the first to capture post-hike defaults. Third, the calendar: payrolls on October 2nd, CPI on October 14th, and the FOMC on October 28th. None of those has printed, so treat any certainty about the hiking path as provisional until they do.

That's the radar for September 23rd. Trade carefully, and I'll see you tomorrow.
