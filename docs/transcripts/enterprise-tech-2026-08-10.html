Good morning, it's Monday, August 10th, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

This show has spent the last week watching the market punish good earnings prints on revenue-quality fears. Beats weren't enough; buyers wanted proof the growth was durable, not a one-quarter sugar high. This week, the demand side answered back, and it answered in backlog, not just growth rates. That distinction is the whole briefing today, so hold onto it.

Start with Amazon Web Services. On its late-July earnings call, Andy Jassy told analysts that AWS revenue hit 42.2 billion dollars in the quarter, up 37 percent year over year, the division's fastest growth rate in 18 quarters. That's notable on its own, but the more important number is what's sitting behind it. Jassy said, quote, "our backlog stands at 496 billion dollars, growing triple digits year-over-year." Back in April, on the Q1 call, he'd put that backlog at 364 billion dollars, and explicitly noted that figure excluded the just-announced Anthropic deal, worth over 100 billion dollars on its own. So contracted, committed AWS revenue jumped by more than 130 billion dollars in a single quarter. Worth flagging: the transcripts don't clarify whether that Anthropic commitment landed inside the new 496 billion number, so the exact apples-to-apples jump is a little fuzzy. What isn't fuzzy is the direction. Jassy also gave two more verbatim data points. AWS's custom-silicon chips business now runs at over 25 billion dollars annualized, growing triple-digit percentages year over year. And AWS's AI revenue run rate, separately, has also climbed past 25 billion dollars, same triple-digit growth. Two years ago those were rounding errors. Now they're two separate 25-billion-dollar businesses tucked inside one cloud division, and the chips line in particular is Amazon quietly building its own margin cushion against buying every accelerator from a third party.

Google Cloud told a similar story the same week. Revenue surged 82 percent year over year to 24.8 billion dollars, blowing past the roughly 64 percent consensus estimate. Its backlog reached 514 billion dollars, even larger than Amazon's. And Alphabet didn't just report the beat. It raised 2026 capital expenditure guidance to a range of 195 to 205 billion dollars, up from 180 to 190 billion previously, with the CFO signaling 2027 spend goes materially higher still. Sit with the ratio for a second. Alphabet is committing north of 200 billion dollars of capex against a cloud business still recognizing under 25 billion a quarter. The company is spending years ahead of the revenue it has already contracted, which tells you management reads the backlog as real demand it can't yet physically serve, not as a number to manage down.

Put those two together and you get the real signal of the week. Backlog, meaning contracted, committed future revenue, is now growing faster than recognized revenue at both hyperscalers. That's the strongest cross-vendor evidence yet that enterprise AI workloads are shifting from discretionary pilots into committed, multi-year production spend. But be precise about what backlog does and doesn't prove. It proves commitment. It doesn't prove those workloads are live in production today rather than reserved capacity waiting to be consumed. A signed contract and a running workload are not the same thing, and the gap between them is exactly where a slowdown would first show up. Rising capex against that backlog is the real risk now sitting on the table. The open question has moved from "is enterprise AI demand real" to "can the hyperscalers build capacity fast enough to serve what they've already sold" — and every dollar of that answer is a dollar of capex spent before the revenue lands.

That same backlog-ahead-of-revenue pattern shows up a layer up the stack, in enterprise applications. ServiceNow reported subscription revenue of 3.877 billion dollars for the quarter, up 24.5 percent year over year. Its AI product line, Now Assist, crossed 1 billion dollars in annual contract value for the first time. Current remaining performance obligations, the near-term committed-but-unrecognized bucket, reached 13.2 billion dollars, up 21 percent. Again, growth in commitments outrunning growth in booked revenue. This matters because it's the cleanest live rebuttal to the bear case that AI agents cannibalize per-seat SaaS licensing. The fear all spring was that autonomous agents would collapse seat counts and gut the recurring-revenue model. ServiceNow isn't being disintermediated by AI. It's attaching a fast-growing new revenue line on top of its existing platform, and pricing it as a distinct product rather than folding it into the base subscription for free. So what: watch whether Salesforce's Agentforce and Workday follow the same playbook, reporting AI as a separately priced line rather than a bundled feature. If the incumbents can monetize AI as an add-on, the "agents kill SaaS" thesis is looking more like an add-on story than a disruption story.

The pattern isn't confined to the US. SAP's current cloud backlog reached 22.9 billion euros, up 27 percent year over year, actually outpacing cloud revenue growth of 22 percent. It's the same backlog-ahead-of-revenue shape, and it was reported as reversing two prior quarters when backlog growth had lagged. That's a European data point saying the same enterprise AI and cloud-migration wave that's showing up at the hyperscalers is broadening into legacy ERP customers moving off on-premise systems, not just American hyperscaler-adjacent names. When the ERP base that runs the back office of the industrial economy starts committing multi-year to cloud migration, that's the least speculative corner of the whole AI-demand story lighting up.

Now, where does all this backlog actually land inside a company day to day? Cisco is rolling out personalized AI agents to its entire 90,000-person workforce starting this month, each employee's requests routed to whichever underlying model fits the task. That's one of the largest enterprise-wide agent deployments disclosed to date, as opposed to a departmental pilot. Cisco's CFO has tied this to the company's own AI-infrastructure order book, which hit roughly 2 billion dollars in fiscal 2025 and is guided to 9 billion dollars in fiscal 2026, driven largely by hyperscaler networking demand. So Cisco sits on both sides of this trade at once: selling the networking gear the hyperscalers need to serve their backlog, and consuming agents internally. Two caveats worth holding onto. There's no disclosed productivity or engagement data yet, so this is an announced rollout plan rather than proven ROI. And it follows roughly 4,000 layoffs earlier in the year, which keeps a labor-substitution reading alive even as Cisco frames the agents as augmentation.

Step back and the through-line across all four is the same. Backlog and contracted commitments are outrunning recognized revenue at the infrastructure layer and the application layer alike, in the US and in Europe. That reframes the real risk. It's no longer whether enterprise AI demand is durable — the contracts say it is. It's whether capital spending, power, and networking capacity can be built out fast enough to actually deliver what's already been sold. And here's the second-order sting for software buyers. If capacity stays tight while backlog keeps climbing, the hyperscalers gain pricing leverage over exactly the application vendors — the ServiceNows and SAPs — trying to pass their own AI costs through to customers. The demand question is settled. The one that decides who keeps the margin is capacity, and that fight is only starting.
