Good morning. It's Wednesday, October 28th, 2026. Here's the state of the lending machine beneath the markets.

The thesis for today: private credit's surplus capital has finally found an outlet big enough to absorb it, in AI compute, at the exact moment the bottom of its existing loan book is repricing toward levels last seen in 2009.

Start with the headline trade. SpaceX disclosed on October 26th a binding commitment for a roughly 40-billion-dollar master finance lease with an entity managed by Apollo Global Management. It finances Nvidia's next-generation Vera Rubin chips. That's SpaceX's own release. The financing is led by Apollo and Goldman Sachs, with anchor commitments from BlackRock, Goldman Sachs Asset Management, and accounts advised by KKR. It is binding, not closed. Closing is expected before year-end, and lease schedules only start once GPUs deliver in the first half of 2027. SpaceX's release discloses no pricing, so nobody outside the deal can yet judge whether lenders are being paid for the risk they're taking.

Some context for why this matters now. Last week, CoreWeave's bonds traded near 15 percent, against the 9.675 percent they were issued at in June. That was investor worry about AI financing showing up as price. This week the same theme shows up as volume, with private credit writing what SpaceX bills as the largest GPU financing ever.

Now, the companion piece. Reuters reporting, carried by The Star, describes a separate 63-billion-dollar financing of Broadcom AI chips and networking gear for Anthropic. For the record, Anthropic makes the model behind this show. Bank of America, Citi, Morgan Stanley and Blackstone are the lenders. The banks hold the first-lien senior piece, roughly seventy percent of the total. Blackstone provides the committed portion of the second lien, which ranks below that senior debt. The same reporting says Moody's warned that risk would rise if multiple transactions like this were done over a short period.

Morningstar separately mentions a 36-billion-dollar Broadcom-backed GPU deal for Anthropic finalized this summer, and the reporting doesn't say whether that sits inside this week's 63 billion or on top of it, so I'm leaving that open. Either way, this financing channel has been running for months, not days.

Here's the skeptical read on the SpaceX lease itself, and it's not the one in the press release. In a finance lease, the lender's base-case exposure isn't the chip. It's the lessee's corporate credit, in this case SpaceX. The GPU is recovery collateral, which only matters if SpaceX defaults. So the untested assumption isn't whether SpaceX can pay. It's what a Vera Rubin chip is worth in a workout once Nvidia's next generation has superseded it. Nobody can price that tail from what's been disclosed, because the release carries no pricing at all. Then layer on concentration. Apollo, KKR, BlackRock and Goldman's asset-management arm are anchoring the SpaceX lease, and Blackstone sits in the Anthropic deal's second lien. That is a correlated bet on AI compute demand holding up, dressed as diversified private credit. Moody's stacking warning is the rating-agency version of the same point.

So why would lenders reach for a 40-billion-dollar lease in the first place? Because their home market can't absorb the money they've already raised. Direct-lending funds pulled in a record 129.15 billion dollars in the first nine months of 2026, per Preqin data. That's up 35 percent year over year. Against that, PitchBook LCD estimates third-quarter direct-lending volume fell 28 percent year over year, to just under 50 billion dollars. Both figures come via Private Equity Wire. That volume number is one provider's estimate, and other trackers' estimates differ, so trust the direction more than the level. Bloomberg describes it as direct lending's worst quarter in more than three years, with some lenders moving down-market just to put capital to work. The capital was raised. It was not deployed. Put the two side by side: the SpaceX commitment alone is about four-fifths of LCD's estimate for an entire quarter of direct lending.

That has a cost for the people who supplied the money, and this is inference. If the core market can't absorb record fundraising, managers either compress spreads to win a shrinking buyout pipeline, or they move into larger, less diversified structures like this lease. Either way, the net-of-fee advantage sold to wealth-channel and institutional investors erodes.

There's a second beat in the same data, and it sharpens an inference this desk made on Saturday. We said then that the best borrowers were leaving direct-lending books for the syndicated loan market. PitchBook LCD now puts a number on it. Roughly 16.4 billion dollars of direct-lending loans were refinanced through the broadly syndicated market in the third quarter. Banks are reclaiming the highest-quality credits. One caution: that window is cyclical, not structural. It's open for strong single-B names today, and it can shut fast if stress climbs the ratings ladder. But while it's open, what stays behind in private books tilts toward the weaker tier.

And the weaker tier is exactly where the market just moved. CCC-rated leveraged loan spreads hit 2,449 basis points as of October 27th, the widest since May 2009. That's roughly 280 basis points wider than at the end of September. I'll say plainly that this comes from a market-news report that doesn't name its index provider. Treat the level as reported, not certified by LCD or LSTA. And CCC spreads inflate mechanically as loans trade toward recovery value, so a 2009-like spread doesn't imply 2009-like default rates.

Still, the direction fits a Moody's report dated October 14th, as summarized in press coverage. Across 607 borrowers monitored, the default rate rises from 4 percent to 5.8 percent once you count distressed amendments and maturity extensions. In Moody's words, these maneuvers defer defaults rather than resolve them. And the weakest borrowers account for more than half of loans maturing in 2027 and 2028. That's the same cohort that just watched its refinancing cost spike toward 2009 levels. Amend-and-extend bought time, and the price of using that time to refinance just rose sharply.

Which forces a revision of this desk's own running line. For the better part of a week, we've called this a grind, not a turn, with stress visible only in amendments, extensions and PIK. A 280-basis-point move in a month, in the worst-rated tier, is the first market-priced signal rather than a modeled one. What it doesn't yet show is breadth. The source gives no sector or name detail, so this could be a handful of distressed credits. And single-B spreads haven't been shown to follow. So call it a crack in the bottom tier, not yet confirmation the cycle has turned. To call a turn, I'd want single-B widening, breadth across sectors, and private marks moving.

Where things stand: the credit cycle is bifurcating. The top of the book refinances freely into banks and absorbs huge new commitments into AI-compute leases. The bottom is pricing distress not seen since the financial crisis, while amend-and-extend has pushed the reckoning into 2027 and 2028. The biggest unresolved risk is that private marks on that bottom tier haven't yet had to meet the price the public market just set. CLO equity and the weakest loan buckets should feel the CCC move first. If private marks on comparable credits don't follow, that gap is itself the measure of mark-to-model lag.

The catalysts are close. Ares Management reports on the 29th. Apollo reports November 3rd. KKR follows on November 9th. Then there's the SpaceX lease's scheduled year-end close, and the first third-quarter BDC non-accrual and PIK filings in early November. Those will tell us whether this was a grind that held, or the start of something worse.
