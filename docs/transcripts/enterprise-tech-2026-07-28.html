Good morning, it's Tuesday, July 28th, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

Today's theme is a single AI budget wave pulling in two directions at once. On one side, agentic software is converting into funded federal contracts. On the other, that same wave is starving adjacent line items inside ordinary enterprise IT. We'll take both faces in turn, starting with where the money is landing.

The Department of Veterans Affairs has awarded Salesforce a $1.6 billion Agentic Enterprise License Agreement, built around Agentforce, Slack, MuleSoft and Data 360, to serve a veteran population Salesforce's own announcement puts at more than 17 million people. Notice what's in that bundle beyond Agentforce. MuleSoft handles the integration, and Data 360 handles the data layer. That's the tell that the durable lock-in here is the plumbing underneath the agents, not the agent SKU by itself. Now read the contract structure carefully. Salesforce's release describes it as a one-year base with two optional one-year renewals, and $1.6 billion is the ceiling if both options get exercised, not money that's been committed or spent. Still, the signal matters more than the number. Federal agencies are not speculative buyers. A funded, multi-year agentic vehicle out of the VA is a stronger adoption tell than any private-sector pilot announcement, because institutions this risk-averse don't sign multi-year software commitments on a whim.

It didn't land in isolation. Days earlier, ServiceNow reported that AI-related annual contract value has surpassed one billion dollars, on track for management's one-point-five-billion target by year end, with subscription revenue growth of 23% in constant currency, a point and a half above the top of its own guidance. ServiceNow also said deals including five or more of its AI products grew five and a half times year over year. That second figure is the more revealing one. It says customers aren't buying a single AI feature and stopping. They're standardizing on the whole agentic suite, which is exactly the land-and-expand motion incumbents need to defend renewal pricing. But treat the ACV headline for what it is: a company-defined, non-GAAP metric, not directly comparable to Salesforce's own Agentforce annual recurring revenue, because each vendor decides for itself what counts as an "AI" booking. And ServiceNow's number is company-wide, not federal, so the federal-procurement story rests on the VA and, before it, the Pentagon's roughly seven-billion-dollar Oracle software consolidation from late last week.

Put those three together and a pattern is building across a single week: Oracle at the Pentagon, Salesforce at the VA, ServiceNow across the broader public sector, each carving out its own government beachhead for agentic AI. But they're staking different ground. Oracle's win is a legacy on-premise license consolidation, durable, but not really an AI-native workload. Salesforce and ServiceNow are selling agentic workflow itself. Worth watching which model becomes the default for government IT modernization, and whether these federal reference deals become the case study private-sector risk, compliance and healthcare buyers cite to justify their own budgets. And apply the same discipline to all three. Oracle's $6.99 billion Pentagon headline is a ten-year ceiling sitting on a $3.31 billion five-year base, the same ceiling-versus-committed gap as the VA number.

There's a tension worth holding here rather than resolving too quickly. Late last week, this show carried the skeptical case on Agentforce specifically: KeyBanc's downgrade, built on a CIO survey finding Agentforce "isn't there yet," with more IT buyers planning to deprioritize Salesforce spend than raise it. That matters because, as of Salesforce's most recent quarter, Agentforce annual recurring revenue had reached $1.2 billion, up more than 200% year over year. Real momentum, but momentum the CIO survey suggested was already hitting private-sector pricing resistance. A $1.6 billion federal win is the first real data point cutting the other way. But one large public-sector reference deal doesn't refute broad private-sector pricing resistance on its own, and no usage or deployment data accompanied the VA award. What we have is a committed budget signal, not proof that agents are actually running in production. So the arc from Friday is complicated, not broken. File it, and watch whether the two optional renewal years actually get exercised. If they don't, that headline number will look inflated in hindsight.

Now the other side of that trade. If agentic platforms are pulling federal dollars in, IBM's second quarter shows what the same AI wave is pulling out of ordinary enterprise budgets. IBM reported revenue of $17.16 billion, up just 1.1% year over year, missing consensus, with operating earnings per share of $2.93 against an expected $2.97. Infrastructure revenue fell 7%, and within that, mainframe revenue fell 42%. Management's explanation, on the earnings call, was that customers redirected capital spending toward AI-related servers, storage and memory ahead of anticipated supply shortages and price increases. That's a specific, dated claim from management, not independently verified against actual procurement data, and it's one quarter at one vendor, so resist generalizing it into a sector-wide law just yet.

It's also worth noting the tension in IBM's own story. Management raised full-year guidance to a 4-to-5% constant-currency range in the same release that missed the quarter. That suggests the cannibalization framing is partly doing the work of explaining away a miss, and it puts a question mark over the AI-driven productivity savings IBM is leaning on to expand margins. Worth watching in later quarters whether those are genuine internal efficiencies or partly an accounting reclassification.

But the underlying mechanism is plausible, and it connects to something this show has tracked all week on the semiconductor side: memory prices climbing sharply, with the DRAM market widely described as heading into a genuine shortage. If enterprises really are pre-buying servers, storage and memory now, ahead of expected price spikes, that's budget being crowded out of adjacent software renewals in the near term, not demand for that software disappearing. IBM's own software segment still grew 5% to $7.76 billion even as infrastructure fell, which is consistent with a reallocation story rather than a collapse. The thing to watch across third-quarter reports is whether Dell, HPE and other vendors selling on-premises infrastructure and transaction software see the same crowding-out. That would turn this from an IBM-specific wrinkle into a genuine, if temporary, deflationary drag on non-AI IT spending.

So where does that leave the picture. Every marquee figure from this week, the $1.6 billion VA ceiling, ServiceNow's billion-dollar-plus AI ACV, Salesforce's $1.2 billion Agentforce run-rate, is still a bookings number, a ceiling, or a contract value, not recognized revenue and not measured production usage. And the next real test is close. Microsoft reports its fiscal fourth quarter tomorrow, and its Azure and Copilot figures will tell us whether this redirect-the-dollar dynamic is feeding the hyperscalers or squeezing them too. The question for enterprise software isn't AI demand versus no demand anymore. It's which vendors capture the redirected dollar, and which ones watch it drain toward hardware instead.

That's the briefing for July 28th. Have a good one.
