Cautious Optimism Briefings

AI in Biopharma R&D

Transcript · October 7, 2026

Good morning. This is Cautious Optimism Briefings for Wednesday, October 7, 2026. Today's argument is about structure: pharma is renting its AI, on terms built to be cut. Over the past three weeks, the build-versus-rent question this show has been tracking got tested from both sides, and the deal terms tell you more than the headlines do.

Start with the builder. On Thursday, October 1, this show led with Roche's investor day. There, Genentech's research chief, Aviv Regev, framed the company's direction as what she called AI independence. In her words, you have to own the learnings and the improvements that AI can develop. Roche, in other words, wants to own its models, its infrastructure, and what they learn.

This week brought a test of that plan. Endpoints News reports, in an exclusive, that Richard Bonneau, Genentech's global head of AI for drug discovery, has left. We found no statement from Genentech or Roche, and the reasons have not been confirmed, so we will not guess at them. The point for this show is narrower. An in-house AI program concentrates its risk in a few people. If a vendor loses a scientist, you can switch vendors. If you own the function, the knowledge of what the models are for walks out with the team. That is our inference, not reporting, and one departure is not a failed strategy. The thing to watch is who leads Genentech's AI work permanently.

Now the other side of the question. If building concentrates risk, the alternative is to rent, and AbbVie just did. On September 21, AbbVie and Iambic announced a multi-year collaboration to discover and develop small molecules. The scope covers immunology, neuroscience, and oncology, with what the announcement calls "first-in-class and best-in-class potential." The platform is Iambic's Enchant v3, which the company says "is trained on over 6,000 molecular properties." Note what that number measures. It tells you how wide the training inputs are. It does not tell you whether the predictions are accurate, and no prospective validation has been cited.

The money is the more informative part. Iambic will receive "an upfront payment," plus success-based milestones and tiered royalties on net sales. No amounts were disclosed. That is an option structure. AbbVie keeps most of its money until programs prove out. This is also not Iambic's first large-pharma deal. Earlier this year, Takeda and Iambic partnered on small molecules for cancers and for digestive or immune conditions. BioPharma Dive reported that deal "could be worth more than $1.7 billion." That headline includes milestones and royalties, and the upfront was not disclosed. So two large pharmas are now renting the same platform, and neither has said what it paid up front.

To see what these structures look like when the numbers are public, take Insilico's alliance with SK Biopharmaceuticals. It was announced at BIO 2026 in San Diego on June 29. Insilico's own release puts the total deal value above $2.5 billion in potential milestones. The same release says Insilico is eligible for up to $18 million in upfront and near-term payments. Do the arithmetic. The near-term money is under one percent of the headline.

Think about what that ratio means for each side. For the buyer, eighteen million dollars is a rounding error inside a large pharma R&D budget. It buys the right to keep going if the science works, and the right to walk away cheaply if it does not. For the platform company, the two and a half billion is a sequence of gates, each tied to a development, regulatory, or commercial event that is years out. Every one of those gates is a point where the buyer can stop paying. So the headline number is best read as the price of success, not the value of the deal. If AbbVie's undisclosed terms look anything like this, its real commitment to Iambic is small today, and it grows only as programs survive. That is the back-loaded pattern in its clearest form. Headline AI deal values are mostly option value.

There is a counterweight here, and it matters. Last Saturday, this show said that Chai's four large-pharma deployments, with no values disclosed, were four logos, not necessarily four large commitments. The same caveat applies to AbbVie. We also raised a less flattering reading then: pharma may be spreading bets across design platforms because no single model has proven decisive. AbbVie fits that reading. A large pharma with its own discovery organization is still buying outside access. And renting has its own cost, which is the one Regev's framing was built to avoid. Whoever rents a platform does not fully own what the model learns from the work. So the buyer keeps the option, the seller carries the timeline risk, and the question of who owns the learnings stays open.

Renting does not only mean specialist models. On September 29, CSL announced a collaboration with Amazon Web Services "to apply artificial intelligence and cloud technologies to support the company's research and clinical development." No financial terms were stated, and no productivity metrics have been published. So this is a statement of intent, not a measured result. What it does show is where a large company is willing to buy AI. CSL is renting the general-purpose layer from a hyperscaler, as infrastructure. The specialist platforms then compete for the narrower design layer. The so-what is simple. The general AI layer is being bought the way pharma buys compute, as a variable cost.

Put the postures side by side. On one side are the builders. Roche wants to own its models, and so does Isomorphic Labs, which, as this show noted on Saturday, keeps all its models locked in-house. On the other side are the renters. Chai rents its design models to four large pharmas. AbbVie now rents a specialist layer from Iambic, on terms that defer most of the money. CSL rents the general stack from AWS, with no terms disclosed. Count the new commitments of the past three weeks, and renting looks like the default posture. If R&D budgets stay roughly flat, as this show has read them, that is exactly what you would expect. We are not saying the AI effort is shrinking. We are saying the commitments are being structured so they can be cut.

What does that mean on the other side of these deals? For Iambic and Insilico, near-term revenue is small, and valuations rest on whether programs progress, not on the size of the press release. For cloud providers, CSL's deal is a sign that pharma buys the general AI layer as infrastructure, which is steadier revenue than milestone-dependent design work. For in-house builders, talent churn may push even committed organizations toward partnerships. That last point is inference, and it depends on who leads Genentech's AI work over the long run.

The open questions are concrete. Who permanently leads Genentech's AI drug-discovery work? Does AbbVie disclose the size of its Iambic upfront in its next quarterly filing? Does CSL publish any cycle-time metric for its clinical work? Each answer would tell us more than another press release.

Finally, the number that would justify bigger commitments: clinical success. Nothing in this week's reporting changes the picture. On Saturday, this show cited a reported industry synthesis that puts Phase 2 success for AI-discovered drugs at roughly 40 percent, which is about the industry's ordinary rate. That figure comes from a single secondary compilation. And by one count, only eight AI-enabled programs had completed Phase 2 by mid-2026, far too few to show an edge in either direction. That is the real reason the deals are back-loaded. Until AI-derived molecules beat the ordinary Phase 2 rate on a meaningful count, a buyer has no evidence to justify paying more up front. A company renting its AI is buying optionality on the next program, not a shorter path through Phase 2. A company building its AI has to keep the people who know what the models are for. Both are sensible ways to run a portfolio. Neither is yet a sign that AI has changed how often a drug works.

That's the briefing for Wednesday, October 7. Thanks for listening.
