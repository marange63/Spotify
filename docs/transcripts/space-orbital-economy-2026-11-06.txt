Good morning. It's Friday, November 6th, and this is your space and orbital economy briefing.

Here's the thread for today. Blue Origin's New Glenn, the challenger rocket that was supposed to carry a big share of Amazon's constellation, just slipped again. The capacity standing in for it gets its first real test one week from now, on November 13th. And the bill for that delay lands on Amazon, while the benefit flows to SpaceX.

Start with New Glenn. Blue Origin's CEO, Dave Limp, told staff in an internal memo that the return to flight is moving to January 2027. The Financial Times reported the memo first, and CNBC confirmed it. This is a staff memo, not a public launch announcement, and no specific day or week in January has been given. CNBC says the earlier internal target was the end of this year, and that January is "about 10 days later." By that yardstick, the slip is days, not months. The bigger signal is the pattern. Until this week, the only November date came from trade press, while Blue Origin's public line was "before the end of 2026." Jeff Bezos told Fox News just last month that the company was "still expecting New Glenn to fly again in December." Now the operator has put its own name on a date, and it lands after every one floated before it.

Limp's framing is careful. He called it a "small adjustment" that "doesn't diminish what we've accomplished." The work in question is the rebuild after May's hot-fire explosion, which damaged New Glenn's only pad, Launch Complex 36 at Cape Canaveral. Limp credited the team: "In just six months, Team Blue has accomplished what many thought would take years." Take that seriously. Rebuilding a pad after an explosion is hard, and Limp concedes that "rebuilding at this pace hasn't been easy." But a target that has moved repeatedly in about a month is not a schedule.

That makes the binding constraint the ground, not the rocket. Every New Glenn flight leaves from that one pad, and CNBC ties the latest delay to bad weather slowing the rebuild. There is a more generous reading. If the delay really is pad-limited, the vehicle risk may be lower than the calendar suggests. But we have no closed FAA mishap investigation to confirm that, so it stays a possibility, not a finding. Either way, a high-cadence provider doesn't carry that kind of single-point dependency.

That dependency has a cost you can count. CNBC reports that New Glenn had been "slated to deploy 48 satellites for Amazon's Leo internet-from-space service." It's a single-source figure, but a concrete one. CNBC adds the consequence: "Amazon has turned to other providers, including United Launch Alliance and Arianespace, in the interim." So the gap is already being patched with other companies' rockets.

Blue Origin also chose an odd day for this news. On the same day the slip surfaced, it unveiled new hardware. One piece is GS3, a hydrogen-fueled third stage for missions direct to geostationary orbit and beyond. The other is Northstar, a new booster engine that, per Spaceflight Now, "produces 715,000 pounds of thrust." That's real engineering, and Northstar is reportedly already test firing. But GS3 isn't targeted to debut until 2028, a year or more past even the slipped January date. Neither piece adds a single launch to 2027 supply. Spectacle and operations run on two different clocks, and Blue Origin just ran them side by side.

So who carries the load New Glenn was supposed to share? That brings us to Amazon Leo, the constellation formerly known as Kuiper. Its near-term buildout now rests on ULA's Vulcan and Arianespace's Ariane 6. Leo hasn't launched since early July, and Amazon CEO Andy Jassy has promised initial service this year. With only a fraction of the constellation up, that would be a coverage-limited beta, not a Starlink competitor. The next check is a Vulcan flight targeted for next Friday, the 13th, reportedly still pending FAA range approval. Another batch is expected on Ariane 6 later in the month. Neither date has an Amazon-sourced confirmation, and two scheduled flights are not a cadence.

Remember the regulatory backdrop, because it frames the cost. Amazon's license carried an earlier buildout milestone. In June, the FCC's Space Bureau waived it. Per the tracker KeepTrack's summary of the June 5th order, the new bar is that "all 3,232 satellites must be in orbit by July 30, 2029." On our reading, that waiver is the regulator pricing in launch scarcity. It removes near-term regulatory jeopardy, which is good for Amazon. But a regulator that waived once can be asked again, and the run to 2029 is now a launch-supply problem.

Now put a number on that problem. Trackers put Leo at a little under four hundred satellites on orbit. Against the 3,232-satellite bar, with under three years left, our own arithmetic says Amazon needs more than eighty satellites a month, every month, starting now. One New Glenn flight was slated to carry 48. So Amazon needs nearly two New Glenn-sized batches a month, for now on rented capacity, and it hasn't flown a batch since July. That's where the capital-markets cost sits. Amazon carries the constellation's capital spend while revenue waits on coverage, so each month of delay stretches Leo's payback period. The waiver lets that clock run longer. It doesn't stop it. Today's reporting carries no launch prices, so the direction is firm but the dollar size is not.

The other side of that ledger is SpaceX, and the value shows up in a less obvious place. Rival scarcity hasn't turned into launch profit. In SpaceX's latest quarter, its launch segment was roughly breakeven, while Starlink earned about two point four billion dollars of segment operating income. So on our reading, SpaceX's launch lead pays off through Starlink, not through launch pricing. Every month Leo stays thin is another month Starlink compounds without a scaled Western broadband rival. For SpaceX shareholders, New Glenn's slip is a Starlink story, not a rocket-pricing story.

One scale check on that competitive field. Orbital Radar, updated November 4th, "tracks 231 Guowang and 248 Qianfan satellites on orbit, with deployment accelerating." Those are tracker estimates, not official disclosures. On those counts, each Chinese network is individually smaller than Amazon Leo. Together, though, the two are above it. And they keep adding batches while Leo waits on launch windows it doesn't control. On either side, satellites on orbit still aren't subscribers.

And on that same Friday, the 13th, a second test lands: Starship Flight 15. SpaceX's VP of launch, Kiko Dontchev, said at the UP.Summit conference that the company is targeting Friday, November 13th for the flight. After weeks of looser targets, that's a specific day from a named executive. It's still a conference-stage target, though. SpaceX hasn't published a mission page or a flight profile.

One correction: earlier this week we described Flight 15 as the first attempt to catch both the booster and the ship. Today's sourcing doesn't confirm the ship catch. So treat the double catch as reported intent, not the confirmed plan.

What would a clean flight prove? It would show the vehicle is recoverable and edging toward an operational cadence rather than a test program. It would not prove turnaround cost, or cost per kilogram. And it would not create recognized commercial revenue, because Starship still books none.

Put the two together, and November 13th is a split screen, not a head-to-head contest. SpaceX tests the vehicle meant to widen its own cost lead. Amazon tries to restart deployment on rented capacity, because the rocket it was counting on just slipped again. Starship still has to show turnaround cost. Amazon Leo still has to show sustained cadence.

So where does that leave the sector? The genuine inflection is SpaceX's widening lead in US launch supply. New Glenn's slip extends it through at least January, and on our reading the FCC's waiver has already priced that scarcity into Amazon's deadline. Still a demo: Starship's real cost per kilogram, Amazon Leo's sustained cadence, and New Glenn's January date, which has no day or week attached. The biggest open question is whether Amazon can line up more than eighty satellites a month of launch capacity it doesn't own.

The catalysts start Monday, November 9th, with AST SpaceMobile's third-quarter call. AST has tied its 2027 satellite target to New Glenn's availability. So the metric to watch is whether that target moves now that New Glenn is in January. Rocket Lab's report is expected the same day, per analyst previews, though that date isn't confirmed. There, watch whether management finally names a quarter for Neutron's debut, which has already slipped into 2027. Then Friday the 13th brings the Vulcan Leo flight and Starship Flight 15. After that come the Ariane 6 Leo flight later in November, and whatever specific January date Blue Origin eventually names.

That's the briefing. Have a good one.
