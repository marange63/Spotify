Good morning, it's Tuesday, November 3rd, midterm Election Day, and this is your Capital Markets Radar.

Two forces are pulling cross-asset pricing in opposite directions this week. Oil just softened the inflation case for a December rate hike. At the same time, the AI buildout's own cost of capital is rising on terms that have nothing to do with the Fed. We'll take them in that order, because the second one is the reason the first won't rescue the capex trade on its own.

Start with oil, because it's the move this desk flagged last Thursday. On October 29th we argued that much of Brent's price looked like risk premium rather than scarcity, and that one headline could erase it. On Monday, a headline did a lot of that work. Brent settled just under one hundred five dollars a barrel, down more than seven and a half percent on the day, per wire settlement figures. WTI fell almost as much, to about ninety-six dollars. The trigger, per Yahoo Finance's market coverage, was hopes of diplomatic talks to end the conflict in the Middle East, with reports centering on an Iran-Qatar channel.

Now treat that carefully. The talks are market narrative, not a confirmed diplomatic breakthrough. Only the price reaction is hard fact. And the other side of the ledger hasn't gone away. Iran's Revolutionary Guard has claimed that tankers hit mines near Oman, though only one attack has been independently confirmed. The Wall Street Journal has also reported that the administration may resume military action once today's vote is behind it. So this is a premium draining on a headline, not evidence that supply risk has resolved. The same mechanism runs in reverse. If the talks stall, a seven percent move can refill as fast as it unwound, and today's vote is the next scheduled headline. The so-what: the market may be over-reading one session as disinflation, when it's really a repricing of event risk around the election calendar.

The cleanest read of what oil just did shows up in gold. Gold jumped about one and three-quarters percent to near three thousand nine hundred fifty dollars an ounce in early Asian trading, per FXStreet. That came just a couple of sessions after gold hit a one-year low. FXStreet's explanation is that falling oil prices have tempered inflation worries. Notice what that implies. Gold didn't catch a haven bid while the war premium was live. It's rallying now on lower oil, not on geopolitical stress. That says gold is currently trading as an inflation and real-rate instrument, not a conflict hedge. One caution: a single rebound day after a slide to a one-year low doesn't establish a new regime. The so-what: don't read gold's bounce as fear coming back. Read it as the market pricing a softer inflation path.

Which brings us to the Fed, and a plain question. The hawks' case was never only about energy, so does cheaper oil actually change their argument? Start with what the FOMC did. On October 28th it held at three and three-quarters to four percent, with two dissents in favor of a quarter-point hike. Then Chair Kevin Warsh described financial conditions this way. Quote, "while overall financial conditions are tighter, I'm unwilling and unable to call them tight." Here's why that line matters for the oil story. Warsh framed his case around financial conditions broadly, not around energy prices. If the chair already won't call conditions tight, a drop in oil loosens them further. On our read, looser conditions are no argument for the hawks to stop. So Monday's oil slide weakens one input to the hike case without removing its core.

What about the pricing? Polymarket, as of 11 p.m. Eastern on Sunday, gave a December hike sixty-nine and a half percent, with no change at twenty-seven and a half. That snapshot predates Monday's oil move. It's also a prediction market, not CME futures, so don't splice it with other venues and call it a trend. We don't yet have a verified read on Treasury yields, breakevens, or hike odds after the oil move. So whether rates followed oil lower is still an open question. What will actually decide December is the October payrolls report this Friday, November 6th, and the October CPI print on November 10th. Neither has been released.

The dollar did report in, and it adds a wrinkle. Per Babypips, the dollar index slipped about a fifth of a percent on Monday, to near one hundred one point eight. Along the way it touched its lowest level since September 30th. Treat the precise level as indicative. A softer dollar, with hike odds near seventy percent going into the week, looks odd. One candidate explanation, and it's our inference: cheaper oil removes some of the energy-shock bid the dollar had been carrying. Either way, a dollar easing rather than catching a haven bid argues against a dollar-funding squeeze right now. That's a modest tailwind for dollar-sensitive risk assets. The so-what: oil has softened one leg of the hawks' case, but it hasn't settled the argument. Friday's payrolls and next week's CPI will do that.

But even if the policy rate stops climbing, the AI buildout's cost of money is rising on its own terms. Per Yahoo Finance, Oracle plans to sell ten billion dollars of investment-grade bonds and a similar amount of stock, to fund AI capital spending and dividends. The stock fell five percent, as investors view the company as more leveraged. This is the next rung on the financing ladder this show has been climbing since late October. We went from neocloud bonds, to private lenders, to hyperscaler free cash flow turning negative, to Microsoft's big investment-grade bond sale last week. Oracle is the first rung on that ladder where a major AI spender is selling stock to fund the gap. For scale, Yahoo Finance calculates that the biggest AI and chip stocks have shed about five trillion dollars in market value since October 6th.

Here's the competing read, though. Funding half the raise with equity is arguably the credit-protective choice. It defends Oracle's investment-grade rating instead of piling on more leverage. So that five percent drop may be as much about dilution mechanics as a verdict on AI capex. The detail that deserves the emphasis is that some of this money is earmarked for dividends, not just data centers. Raising equity partly to sustain a payout is not what funding growth from strength looks like.

Now the second-order effects, and these are our inference rather than reported fact. First, decoupling. The buildout's marginal cost of capital can rise even while the policy outlook softens, because AI-linked issuers now face their own equity and credit premia. Second, supply. Oracle's bonds land right behind Microsoft's deal, and both compete for the same investment-grade tech-credit buyers. Microsoft's deal came from a cash-rich issuer the market had just rewarded. Oracle's comes from an issuer the market sees as levered. So watch whether the next AI-linked deal has to pay a concession to clear. The so-what: the market used to sort AI spenders by whether earnings justified the spend. It's now sorting them by how they fund the gap.

The highest-beta expression of that same trade is crypto. Spot bitcoin ETFs saw six hundred forty-three million dollars of net outflows on Friday, October 30th, per SoSoValue data cited by CoinDesk. That was the same session the Nasdaq fell nearly three percent, per the Rio Times. What's new is the year-to-date total. Per CoinDesk, before that session the funds' net inflow for all of 2026 was just over one billion dollars. So one bad day wiped out most of the year's net institutional bid. Total ETF assets also fell below one hundred billion dollars, per Bitfire Research. Two cautions on reading that. Assets slipping under a round number mixes price decline with redemptions, so it isn't a pure flow signal. And net flows near zero don't mean the holder base is small, because gross holdings remain large. The so-what: same-day timing isn't proof of cause, but bitcoin is trading with the AI tape as its high-beta tail, not as the diversifier the institutional pitch promised.

One structural note sits underneath that price action. The OCC gave final approval to Ripple's national trust bank charter. That lets Ripple manage reserves for its RLUSD stablecoin and offer digital-asset custody. The so-what: stablecoin reserves are starting to move onto federal bank charters. If that becomes the template, the regulatory gap between stablecoins and bank money keeps narrowing.

So, pulling it together. Oil's slide softened the inflation case for a December hike, though there's no market read yet on whether rates or hike odds moved with it. The AI buildout's own cost of capital moved the other way, with Oracle selling stock to fund capex and dividends. So an easier Fed path, even if it comes, won't rescue the capex trade on its own. Watch three things from here. First, whether the oil diplomacy holds once today's vote is in the rearview mirror. Second, Friday's payrolls and next week's CPI, neither of which has printed. Third, how Oracle's raise actually prices, and whether the next AI-linked bond has to pay up to clear.

That's the radar for November 3rd. Trade carefully, and I'll see you tomorrow.
