Digital Money, Crypto, and Market Structure

Transcript · August 23, 2026

Good morning. It's Sunday, August 23rd, and this is your Cautious Optimism briefing on digital money.

Let's start with the reversal, not the rally. On Friday, bitcoin traded up to about seventy-nine thousand five hundred dollars, its highest level since May. By Saturday it had slipped out of the seventy-eight thousand range and back into the seventy-seven thousand range. That modest move triggered five hundred twenty-nine million dollars of perpetual futures liquidations in a single hour. Roughly four hundred seventy-eight million of that came from long positions, against just over fifty million from shorts. For the first time all week, the longs were on the wrong side.

Put that next to Wednesday. On August nineteenth, a Treasury announcement doubling the size of long-end bond buybacks and a White House summit with crypto executives landed on a market that was badly positioned in the other direction. Leveraged funds on the CME were net short about seven thousand contracts going into that week. That's Commitment of Traders data as of August eleventh, so it describes the setup, not where the book sits now. The squeeze that followed liquidated two point nine nine billion dollars in a single session, ninety-one point six percent of it shorts. Eighth-largest liquidation event on record.

Here's the asymmetry, and it's worth naming precisely, because this is not a clean round trip. Saturday's down-leg was roughly a fifth the size of Wednesday's up-leg. That tells you the book has flipped sides, not that it has unwound. What the two events share is the lesson. A market that liquidates violently in both directions inside three days is a leverage market. And neither event, on its own, tells you anything about underlying spot demand.

We should own our own record here, because we have been arguing this mechanism all week and getting it partly wrong each day. Yesterday we told you the honest statement was that we knew Friday's move had gotten wider across the complex, but not that it had gotten deeper. Saturday is the first piece of evidence that arrives from the other side of the book, and it points the same way.

On-chain data adds a second, independent signal in the same direction. On August twentieth, short-term bitcoin holders sent a record forty-three thousand three hundred coins to exchanges in profit — the largest profit-taking move of 2026, according to CryptoQuant. That cohort's spent-output profit ratio hit its highest reading since April, against a realized cost basis of sixty-eight thousand seven hundred dollars. Hold the coin count loosely; a second aggregator reported roughly forty-four thousand for what looks like the same event. The direction is what carries the argument. Real coins were moving toward exchanges into strength, at record scale for the year, during the same session the squeeze was running. That argues against a pure short-covering vacuum, and toward a market where leverage and distribution were both live at once.

Now the number we could not get, which happens to be the one that would settle this. If Wednesday was a short squeeze, the short book should by now have been replaced by a crowded long one, and you would see it in current open interest and in perpetual funding. We could not obtain a dated, quotable reading of either as of Saturday, and the most recent Commitment of Traders release still predates both liquidation events. So current positioning is unknown. We are not going to infer a crowded long book from the single fact that longs got hit once. That is the falsifiable test, and it is still open.

On the macro leg, it is tempting to read bitcoin as lagging Treasuries by one session, but the data only partly supports that. The thirty-year yield closed Friday at five point two eight percent, up two basis points on the day, and still five basis points below the five point three three percent nineteen-year high it set on August eighteenth — the session before the buyback was announced. That is a partial retracement of the buyback relief, not a full one. So the honest framing is that bonds had begun handing back some of the announcement effect by Friday, and crypto started handing back its own gains a session later. Neither market has round-tripped. And keep the nature of that buyback in view. What happened on the nineteenth was an announcement that operation sizes would at least double, with Treasury Secretary Bessent saying afterward that it could run beyond four billion dollars per issue. The operations themselves don't begin until September ninth, a date we put on the calendar yesterday. So this is an announcement escalating faster than any execution, and crypto is borrowing a liquidity story that has not been delivered yet.

So who actually bought, on the flow side rather than the leverage side? U.S. spot bitcoin and ether ETFs pulled in two point six billion dollars combined for the week — one point nine billion into bitcoin funds, six hundred ninety-seven million into ether funds. That is the strongest week since October of last year. It is genuinely bullish, and it deserves to be said plainly before it gets tested.

Here is the test, three ways. First, two point six billion dollars of net creations across an entire week is smaller than what got liquidated in the single session of August nineteenth. Second, combined ETF trading volume more than tripled to twenty-nine billion dollars, with bitcoin volume up two hundred nineteen percent and ether up two hundred fifty-nine percent. That is roughly eleven dollars of secondary turnover for every dollar of net creation, which is churn. The wrapper is being traded, not accumulated. Third, bitcoin ETF net assets rose twenty-five percent on the week to about ninety-six billion dollars, but only one point nine billion of that nineteen-and-a-half-billion-dollar increase came from creations. The rest is mark-to-market on units already outstanding, and the ninety-six billion headline will get reported as adoption anyway. The flow channel is real and it is improving. It is still not the thing setting the marginal price.

The second-order effect there is uncomfortable. ETF flow is becoming a worse proxy for institutional demand at exactly the moment it becomes the most widely quoted one. Two caveats on the data itself. These are aggregator figures, not issuer disclosures. And the week they measure ends at the price peak, before Saturday's reversal, so the flow number and the price move it is used to explain are not independent observations.

If volume tripled, who got paid for it? For that we have to reach back to Coinbase's second-quarter results, reported earlier this month. Not news from this week, but the only clean public read on exchange economics we can set against this week's volume. Revenue came in down nineteen percent year over year, at roughly one point two billion dollars, below estimates, with a net loss of three hundred fifty-nine million. That is the third straight quarterly miss. And in the same quarter, Coinbase posted an all-time-high ten point three percent share of crypto trading volume. Net revenue excluding bitcoin spot trading is now eighty-eight percent of the total — bitcoin spot trading is twelve percent of the mix — with subscription and services revenue at five hundred fifty-five million, or forty-eight percent of net revenue. Record volume no longer converts into exchange revenue the way the equity market still assumes. Winning share of a shrinking fee pool is not the same as winning.

One number in that print belongs to this show's running concentration thread. Average USDC balances held in Coinbase products hit an all-time high of twenty billion dollars, more than thirty percent of everything in circulation, sitting at a single venue. That is a redemption chokepoint in the on-chain dollar system, not just an exchange metric.

Which is the bridge to the last stop, the collateral layer. BlackRock's tokenized Treasury share class, BSTBL, has grown to roughly two and a half billion dollars, and it is increasingly used as collateral for on-chain borrowing and leveraged trading. JPMorgan's equivalent, JLTXX, launched on public Ethereum in May with a hundred million. Both figures come from secondary summaries rather than fund fact sheets, so hold them loosely. And don't oversell the scale. Two and a half billion is marginal against the roughly forty-eight billion dollars of bitcoin futures open interest in the mid-August snapshot. This is not systemic. But the channel now exists, and it runs both ways. The same leverage that fired in both directions this week is increasingly margined against tokenized money-market fund shares. That means a money-market stress event now has a path into a crypto liquidation cascade, and a crypto cascade has a path back.

Two dates. September ninth, when the buyback stops being an announcement. September fifteenth, the Senate's procedural cloture vote on the CLARITY Act. But the real test for everything in this briefing isn't a date. It's whether ETF creations survive the pullback week, because that is the one number that would separate durable flow from a squeeze that happened to coincide with it.
