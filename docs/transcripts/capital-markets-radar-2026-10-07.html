Good morning. It's Wednesday, October 7th, 2026, and this is Capital Markets Radar.

On Monday, the ten-year Treasury touched its highest level since April 2002. By Tuesday's close, the S&P 500 had set its 28th record close of the year, and the Nasdaq Composite had notched another record of its own. Today's question is how both things are true at once. The short answer is that they are the same bet, priced from opposite sides.

First, a correction. Yesterday's episode said Monday had no major data release we could verify. That was wrong, and the release we missed is the catalyst for this whole move. The price index inside the ISM services survey jumped to 74, per CNBC. That pushed its trailing-year average to the highest since early 2023. It also sent the ten-year as high as 5.349 percent intraday, which is CNBC's basis for the multi-decade-high headline. Hold that number in its proper place. It's an intraday screen print. On Treasury's own closing series, Monday's ten-year finished at 5.31 percent, the figure we gave you yesterday.

That distinction is the first over-read worth flagging. A multi-decade-high headline sounds like a regime signal. But it was an intraday print, and it reversed the next session. What drove it matters more than the level, so let's go there.

Here's the puzzle. Yesterday this desk showed that inflation compensation has been flat through this selloff. Subtract Treasury's real ten-year yield from the nominal, and you get a breakeven of roughly 2.36 percent. That held on Monday, the ISM day, and it held again on Tuesday. So how does a hot services-price survey leave breakevens untouched? Our inference is that this is a policy-path shock, not an inflation shock. The Fed raised rates last month. Sticky services prices raise the odds that it stays restrictive, or tightens again. The market prices that through real yields, while it keeps trusting the Fed on inflation itself. One caveat belongs here. A survey price component is not CPI, and a flat breakeven over a handful of sessions is a thin sample.

The rate pricing fits that reading. CNBC relayed market odds of roughly 80 percent that the Fed holds at its next meeting. Read that correctly. In a hiking cycle, the alternative to holding is another hike, not a cut. So this is hold-versus-hike pricing, not a delay to cuts that were never on the table. The so-what is that the long end is being repriced off the policy path. That puts the Fed, and not just bond supply, at the center of this move.

Then Tuesday, the long end finally fell. On Treasury's closing series, the ten-year dropped four basis points to 5.27 percent. That's its first down close in the sessions this desk has tracked since October 1st. The real ten-year fell by the same amount, so breakevens didn't budge. That's consistent with a real-rate unwind and nothing more. We're deliberately not making a curve-shape call on Tuesday. The two-year readings we have for the session disagree across sources, so the slope stays unresolved.

Now score it honestly against the test this desk has set all month. Can the long end fall on calming news? Tuesday's drop came with no soft data at all. It came ahead of the September FOMC minutes, which makes it pre-event positioning. Call it a weak pass at best. Those minutes are due at 2 p.m. Eastern today, and they had not been released as this was recorded. So we assume nothing about their tone.

That down-day matters more than its size, because of who is positioned against it. According to a TokenPost report, the SG CTA Index of trend-following funds returned 15.7 percent through the third quarter. The S&P 500 returned 11.7 percent over the same stretch. Part of that edge came from shorting Treasurys into September's selloff. It sat on top of long-dollar positions and long-oil bets placed before the Iran war. Those are the same trends this desk tracked when the dollar index hit its year-to-date high last week.

Here's the mechanism to watch, and it is mechanism, not observation. We have no current CTA positioning data. But if the long end rallies for real, trend models shift from shorting to covering. That buying would add to the rally. It could also spill into equity volatility with no new fundamental news behind it. One soft session doesn't flip a trend model. The takeaway is that the bond market's next big move may be mechanical rather than fundamental.

So why didn't equities blink at a multi-decade-high yield? Because the earnings leg showed up. On Monday, and again on Tuesday, this desk said valuation support would have to come from earnings, not from the discount rate. This week it did. Marvell's investor day on Tuesday is the clearest example.

Marvell guided fiscal 2031 revenue to a range with a midpoint of 80 billion dollars. The Street's estimate, as reported by KSL, was 46.85 billion. That's not a beat at the margin. It's a different business plan. And even the near-dated guide, for fiscal 2028, came in above what analysts had modeled.

Treat the long-dated number as what it is. It's a management bet several years out, not a booked result. Its value sits almost entirely in the cash flows furthest from today. Those are exactly the cash flows most sensitive to the discount rate the bond market just repriced. And it extends a pattern. Last Friday, this desk noted that more than 75 percent of Micron's fiscal 2027 shipments were already covered by supply agreements. We read that as AI demand being contracted, not just forecast. Marvell now pushes the claimed horizon out to the end of the decade. Each data point lengthens the duration of the AI revenue thesis.

There's a composition point buried in the guidance too. Marvell raised its custom-chip revenue target for fiscal 2029 to 12 billion dollars. That's hyperscalers designing their own silicon. Part of it is a share shift away from merchant GPU vendors, not purely new demand. So the so-what for chips is dispersion. The AI pie keeps growing, but the leader's slice and margin are now a live question.

The same lengthening shows up outside chips entirely, in physical power contracts. On Tuesday, Alphabet and Constellation Energy announced a 20-year power-purchase agreement. Per Google's release, it drives more than 4.3 billion dollars of investment to upgrade systems at Constellation reactors across Illinois, Pennsylvania and New Jersey. Alphabet also signed a separate 15-year, 2,700-megawatt agreement with Constellation for additional power from the PJM grid.

Read the first deal carefully. It funds upgrades at an existing fleet, not new plants. Our Strategic Power briefing applied the same discount last Friday to Amazon's Calvert Cliffs deal, where only a small slice was genuinely new capacity. Still, the deals are escalating. Alphabet's upgrade commitment is larger than Amazon's, and it comes with a second long-dated contract stacked on top. Australian uranium miners moved in sympathy, with Boss Energy and Deep Yellow each up 4.6 percent. But we have no fresh uranium price, so don't read that as the fuel market confirming anything.

The bigger point is what these contracts do to utility equity. Multi-decade hyperscaler offtake changes the risk in these stocks. Exposure to merchant power prices shrinks. Exposure to one concentrated counterparty, and to long-term rates, grows. Constellation's 12.25 percent jump on Tuesday prices the first effect and largely ignores the second. There's a political risk alongside it. On Tuesday, our Strategic Power briefing reported, via Forkast, that Oracle's nuclear subscription in Wisconsin is a primary driver of a proposed electric rate hike there. That's the ratepayer backlash these deals invite. The so-what is that AI is now writing twenty-year paper, and utilities are being re-rated as long-duration claims on Big Tech.

Put it together, and the lead and the two follow-ons are one trade. The bond market pushed the ten-year to a multi-decade intraday high on a real-rate story. Equities answered not by discounting that rate, but by buying further out the curve, into end-of-decade chip guidance and multi-decade power contracts. That's the under-read in this tape. The S&P's records rest on cash flows dated further out each week. So the index's sensitivity to the ten-year is rising, even as the index ignores it. And AI chips and nuclear utilities are no longer diversifiers to each other. If the long end resumes climbing, they fall together.

The tests are dated, and no outcomes are assumed. The FOMC minutes land this afternoon. September CPI follows on October 14th, and the FOMC decides on October 28th. Watch whether a hot print finally lifts breakevens. And watch whether the ten-year can fall on soft data, rather than on positioning.
