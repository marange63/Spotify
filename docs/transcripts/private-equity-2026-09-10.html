Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Equity & the Deal Economy

Transcript · September 10, 2026

Good morning, it's September 10th, 2026, and this is Cautious Optimism Briefings.

Two sponsor-held businesses went to corporate buyers in the last days of August. KKR agreed to sell the insurance broker USI to Aon on August 30th. The next day, Apollo and Triton agreed to sell the industrial cooling company Kelvion to the oilfield-services giant SLB. Two cash take-outs of sponsor assets by strategic acquirers, one day apart, is the kind of sequence that gets read as the exit market reopening. It is not that. Start with the Kelvion terms, because they are more precise than the headline.

Apollo's own investor-relations release states that SLB will pay approximately $3.4 billion in cash, and will also assume roughly seven hundred million dollars of Kelvion's debt. Total consideration is therefore about $4.1 billion, not the $3.4 billion cash figure in the headlines. That distinction carries information. It tells you Kelvion took real leverage into this sale, and SLB is taking that leverage onto its own balance sheet along with the business. The deal is also signed, not closed. Apollo's release is explicit that this is a definitive agreement subject to regulatory approval, with closing expected in the first half of 2027. So a cash cheque has been agreed. It has not been written yet, and it may not be for the better part of a year.

Ownership here is worth getting right. Triton built Kelvion over roughly twelve years. Apollo bought majority control from Triton in a deal that closed in January of this year, so Apollo has held this asset about eight months, not a full cycle. At the time of the SLB agreement, Kelvion was majority owned by Apollo-managed funds, with Triton retaining only a minority interest. And no primary source from either firm discloses what Apollo paid in January. There is no way to compute what either sponsor actually made here. Treat the $4.1 billion as a sale price, not as a scorecard.

Now the turn, because the headline — strategic buyer pays cash for sponsor asset — undersells what is actually happening. Look at what each buyer is buying. SLB is buying data-center cooling infrastructure; Apollo's own release describes Kelvion as a leader in cooling solutions for data centers. Aon is buying insurance-brokerage scale. These are acquirers solving specific strategic problems in consolidating verticals. That is not a reopened, general-purpose market for sponsor-held businesses. For this to become a channel rather than two data points, you would need a strategic buyer showing up for an asset outside those two verticals. Watch for that, not for more insurance or data-center deals.

The same fortnight produced the opposite flow. Permira and CPP Investments completed the two-point-seven billion pound take-private of JTC on September 1st, delisting it from the London Stock Exchange. CPP put in roughly 350 million pounds for about a 20% stake, with Permira holding the majority. That structure is worth noting on its own terms. A large pension fund is acting as a direct co-investor in the transaction itself. It is buying the asset rather than writing a check into Permira's fund, which changes both the fee it pays and the exposure the manager keeps. So the same fortnight that sent two companies to strategic buyers also pulled one company off the public market and onto sponsor books. Ownership is churning in both directions at once. It is not net draining the industry's unsold inventory.

The sharper version of that story is Thoma Bravo's agreement, announced in mid-August, to take the insurance-marketplace platform Accelerant private in a deal worth more than $4 billion. Accelerant listed at $21 a share roughly a year ago. By August 12th it was trading at $13.61. Thoma Bravo is paying $20.25, a 49% premium to that depressed price, and still below the original IPO price. That is a complete round trip in about twelve months. A public listing, a stock that never recovered, and a sponsor buying it back before the public market gets another chance to reprice it. The bullish read is that Thoma Bravo sees value the market missed. The less flattering read is that the IPO stopped being a durable exit and became a temporary parking spot, undone once the verdict came in unwelcome. And this was not an auction. Altamont Capital controls roughly 82% of the voting rights and has already committed to vote in favor, so do not read that premium as a competitive market clearing price. This deal, too, is not expected to close until the first half of 2027.

Put the two take-privates together and the effect compounds. Every company that leaves the public register removes one more comparable against which a private mark can be checked. Accelerant is the clean case, because the public market actually delivered a verdict — 35% below issue — right before the company was withdrawn from public pricing altogether. There is a supply consequence as well. Every recently listed, sponsor-backed company now trading below its issue price is a take-private candidate, which is a fresh source of deal volume that does nothing to drain the exit backlog.

All of that is ownership changing hands. The question a limited partner actually cares about is whether cash reaches them, and on that question no new industry-wide distribution data landed this fortnight. There is no fresh, quotable figure for distributions-to-paid-in across the asset class. So the hard evidence comes from one manager's print: Carlyle's second quarter, reported in late July. Use it as the most recent hard data point, explicitly not as news. Carlyle distributed nearly $7 billion to limited partners in the quarter, and $37 billion over the trailing twelve months, against $485 billion of total assets under management. That is real cash, and it landed alongside record fee-related earnings and Carlyle's highest distributable earnings in nearly four years.

But separate those two earnings numbers before calling it a recovery. Fee-related earnings are driven by the asset base itself. They are durable, and they are why the market now prices these managers like annuity businesses rather than cyclical carry vehicles. Distributable earnings are the fragile half. That number jumped on a realization wave, and realizations depend entirely on being able to sell or refinance assets — the same narrow, vertical-specific channel the Kelvion deal just illustrated, or the GP-led continuation-vehicle route this show has covered all week. Carlyle's own in-carry ratio, meanwhile, slipped to 79% from 84% a year earlier, which the firm attributed to recent realizations working through the system. That shrinks the pool of assets still eligible to generate carry. A firm can post a record quarter and thin its own future earning base in the same print. And one manager's distributions are not an industry-wide recovery in cash returned to limited partners. There is no credible industry figure to make that claim with.

So where does the cycle actually stand? The corporate bid for sponsor-held assets is real, but it is concentrated in verticals where a specific buyer needs a specific asset, not a general reopening. The listed universe keeps shrinking from both directions — take-privates removing comparables, and now Accelerant showing that even a recent IPO is not safe from being withdrawn. And one large manager can show real distributed cash without that translating into an industry-wide return of capital.

Three things to watch. Whether the Kelvion sale actually closes on the terms agreed, given a regulatory clock that runs into the first half of 2027. Whether a third strategic take-out shows up outside insurance or data-center infrastructure, which is what would turn two data points into a trend. And whether third-quarter realizations, when the managers report in late October, broaden beyond a handful of trophy exits or stay concentrated the way Carlyle's did.

That's the state of the machine. Thanks for listening, and I'll see you tomorrow.
