Good morning. It's Monday, November 16th, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

The per-seat license is being squeezed from both ends at once. ServiceNow is pointing AI at the cost of leaving a rival's installed base. At the same time, it is moving its own revenue away from seats. So far, the evidence for both is a limited launch and a ratio, not a single displaced customer.

Start with the attack. ServiceNow announced its Autonomous CRM Engine on November 12th. The pitch is to map a customer's existing CRM usage, redesign the processes around it, and move that customer onto ServiceNow's own CRM. In the company's release, an executive put it this way: "We redefined what an enterprise CRM can be. Now we're redefining how fast enterprises can deploy and scale it." The product is in what ServiceNow calls Controlled Availability today, with expanded capabilities coming in 2027. That is a limited-customer stage, not general availability. ServiceNow has named no customer that has completed a migration, and it has disclosed no pricing.

Two framing points matter here. First, ServiceNow's own materials never name the target. They say legacy platforms. The trade site diginomica reads the launch as a shot at Salesforce. That's a fair reading, but it's the journalist's inference, not the company's claim. Second, the timeline. Some coverage describes migrations measured in weeks. But when ServiceNow executive Amit Zavery spoke to diginomica, he was more careful. He said the work "can be done in months versus years or weeks instead of months." That is a compression claim, not a promise of weeks.

Here's why this matters beyond one launch. Yesterday, this desk argued that revenue recurring inside an installed base is more credible AI evidence than a backlog. ServiceNow is betting that AI can pry installed bases loose. An installed base is only as sticky as the cost of leaving it. If AI can compress the implementation and process-redesign work that makes a CRM switch painful, that evidence looks less durable than we assumed.

But compress isn't eliminate. Data migration, custom integrations, user retraining, and contract timing are not mainly software problems. Neither is the ecosystem of add-ons built around an incumbent platform. AI-led process redesign touches only part of that stack. So the realistic first effect isn't wholesale migration. It's leverage. A credible cheap-exit option shows up in Salesforce's renewal negotiations long before it shows up in Salesforce's customer count.

There's a services echo too. If AI really compresses CRM migrations, the multi-quarter implementation projects that systems integrators bill by the hour get shorter. That's the same substitution this desk found at EPAM Systems on November 11th, where AI-native delivery was replacing headcount-priced work. The point to bank is this. Today, ServiceNow's CRM engine is a renewal-leverage story, not a displacement story. It stays that way until ServiceNow names customers who have actually finished a migration.

Now the twist. The company attacking a seat-priced installed base is also walking away from seats itself. ServiceNow's third-quarter earnings release quotes its chief financial officer, Gina Mastantuono, on agentic AI. She said year-to-date consumption in production was up more than thirteen times year over year. Note what kind of number that is. It's a year-to-date multiple, not a single quarter. And ServiceNow gives no dollar figure underneath it. A small starting base can produce a large multiple on its own.

Then the second figure. At a BMO investor conference on November 12th, the same day as the CRM launch, a different ServiceNow executive said: "Over half of our revenue in Q3 was non-seat based." That sounds like a fresh milestone. Treat it with care, for two reasons.

The first reason is that ServiceNow has made a similar claim before. In late August, coverage of ServiceNow's commentary reported non-seat deals at about half of new business, referring to the second quarter. That was a share of net new business. The November claim is a share of total revenue. Those are different measures, so you can't draw a trend line from one to the other. It's better read as a recurring talking point applied to a new quarter than as a clean acceleration.

The second reason is that non-seat is not the same as agentic. My inference is that the figure likely includes older pricing tied to capacity or transactions, not just agent consumption. ServiceNow hasn't said how it splits. So place both figures carefully on the evidence ladder. They sit above a product announcement, because they describe actual revenue and usage. They sit well below what would settle the question, which is disclosed agentic revenue in dollars.

And consumption cuts both ways. Revenue that scales up with agent usage can also contract without a single customer churning. A majority non-seat ServiceNow is trading the visibility of a seat contract for usage upside that nobody outside the company can yet size.

Visibility is already the market's sore point. In that same third-quarter release, ServiceNow guided fourth-quarter growth in current remaining performance obligations to eighteen and a half percent in constant currency. Per the Motley Fool, the stock fell 27 percent on October 29th, its worst day ever, after that guide landed alongside a third-quarter beat. One reading is worth naming. A very public fight with a seat-priced rival also hands a pressured stock a growth story. That's a reading, not a confirmed motive. So what? ServiceNow is genuinely moving off seats. But it hasn't yet put a consistent, dollar-based number on how far.

If ServiceNow is the attacker on seats, Cisco shows what bundling AI into your own installed base buys you instead. Cisco's fiscal first-quarter release had product revenue up 32 percent. Security, in the same release, grew just 7 percent. That's the segment where Cisco is layering in Splunk's agentic security-operations tooling, which it is reinforcing with the newly closed WideField Security acquisition. One quarter can't separate a slow-to-monetize agentic product from ordinary integration drag or a tough comparison. So this is this desk's reading, not Cisco's framing. On this evidence, AI layered onto an incumbent's security base looks like retention defense, not a new growth engine. For Cisco, agentic security is a feature, not yet a category.

When the seat stops being the unit of spend, the buyer needs a new way to control it. That's the piece arriving with Anthropic's enterprise change today. Standing disclosure: Anthropic makes the model behind this show. As of today, Claude Chat and Cowork run as one Enterprise experience by default for existing organizations. Yesterday, this desk called that default flip distribution, not adoption. The more durable news is the control set shipping alongside it.

That control set is reported by secondary coverage, including PYMNTS, not verified against Anthropic's own documentation. Work that executes code or files moves into an isolated cloud session, with network access off by default. Connector credentials are kept outside that sandbox. A beta endpoint-detection sensor runs inside the session. There's a centralized security console. And there's beta cost-code tagging for usage. None of it comes with adoption figures, and several pieces are still in beta. Whether sandbox defaults actually shorten security approval cycles is unproven.

But the logic is clear. Cost-code tagging is chargeback plumbing for metered AI. As vendors like ServiceNow shift revenue from seats to consumption, procurement loses the seat count as its budget lever. AI spend moves from a fixed license line to a variable one. Governance moves with it, away from seat negotiation and toward finance-style chargeback and security review.

Pull the three layers together. ServiceNow is attacking a rival's installed base while loosening its own seat model. Cisco is defending its installed base, with AI as a feature rather than a growth line. And Anthropic is building the controls buyers will need once seats stop being the budget lever.

Three things to watch. First, whether ServiceNow names a Controlled Availability customer that has finished a production migration. Second, whether ServiceNow ever puts a dollar figure under that thirteen-times consumption number. Third, Palo Alto Networks reports later this week. That's the next read on whether security buyers are rewarding platforms or bundles.
