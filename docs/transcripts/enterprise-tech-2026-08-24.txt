Good morning, it's August 24th, 2026, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

Start with a number GitHub published about itself. In its own postmortem on this month's outage, GitHub says monthly commits on the platform grew from 1.4 billion in April to 2.9 billion by August. GitHub's own sentence is that the growth explains the pressure on its systems. Note what it does not say. The post never names AI agents, never names Copilot, and gives no agent-authored share of that growth. The link from commit volume to agentic coding tools is this show's inference from the timing, not GitHub's claim, and I'll hold that caveat in place for the rest of this story. Because on August 17th, whatever was driving the volume, GitHub could not keep up with it. A critical infrastructure component in its Central U.S. data center failed to scale with a traffic peak, and the site was down for seven hours and forty-seven minutes.

Set that against where this briefing was a week ago. On August 17th we used Microsoft's own disclosure that one in three GitHub pull requests already involves an agent, and we argued that governance — who gets to authorize what an agent touches — was the layer that would matter most. GitHub's postmortem points somewhere else. The thing that broke first wasn't permissions. It was throughput.

The outage landed on an unusually bad day to be GitHub. Three days earlier, on August 14th, SpaceX closed its all-stock acquisition of the AI coding startup Cursor. That deal was first announced in June and values Cursor at 60 billion dollars, so what's new here is the close, not the price. Cursor said that joining SpaceX gives it, in its own words, access to the largest fleet of GPUs in the world. And on August 17th, the same day GitHub went dark, Cursor shipped Origin — a code-hosting platform built directly into the Cursor editor, with repositories, pull requests, reviews and merges, aimed at code written by agents rather than by people.

Now hold two things in view before this becomes a tidier story than it is. First, Origin has no adoption behind it. No repositories migrated, no named customers, no usage figure at all, a week after a beta launch. And the reporting describes a product that interoperates with GitHub rather than displacing it. The one concrete line available is that your GitHub repositories can sit alongside the ones Cursor hosts. That is a launch, not a migration. Second, the mundane read of the timing is fully available. One data-center component failing under a traffic spike is an ordinary capacity incident, and shipping a rival product into a competitor's worst day costs nothing if it flops. That's opportunistic marketing, not proof of a structural shift.

What tips this past the mundane explanation is GitHub's own response. Its postmortem says the company has already added more than three million CPU cores, 120 petabytes of high-speed storage and significant network capacity. It also says GitHub is now applying consistent retry limits, retry budgets and variable timeouts across its internal service calls, to stop retry storms from cascading. That is not the language of a company that had one bad day. That is a company re-architecting for a load level it did not plan for.

So the honest version isn't that GitHub is losing its franchise. It's that the franchise is contestable for the first time, and by a challenger that is no longer scrappy. Cursor itself — the coding business, not its new parent — had passed roughly 4 billion dollars in annualized revenue by June, with about 2.6 billion of that coming from enterprise customers. That's three-quarters of the business sold to companies rather than to hobbyists. A vendor at that scale integrating downward into source control, at the moment agents are becoming primary code authors, is a different kind of threat than a better-looking git host. And the so-what runs wider than these two companies. If commit volume can double in four months, capacity becomes the competitive line in developer platforms rather than features. Capacity is a cost line, not a moat. GitLab, Atlassian and every continuous-integration vendor face the same arithmetic, and GitHub now has to defend it in public.

If agent-authored work is roughly doubling code volume, it is very likely doing something similar to token volume underneath it — the inference calls that produce all that code. And somebody has to decide which model provider gets paid for each of those tokens. That is exactly the layer Stripe just bought.

On August 16th, Stripe said it has agreed to acquire OpenRouter, a platform that routes business requests across more than 400 models from over 80 providers, choosing the model on price, speed or reliability for the task at hand. Bloomberg reported the price at more than 7 billion dollars. Stripe has not confirmed terms. What makes that number remarkable is the timing. OpenRouter raised a 113 million dollar Series B in May, three months earlier, at a reported 1.3 billion dollar valuation. Divide one by the other and the markup is more than five times in a single quarter — and that division is our arithmetic on two reported numbers, not a figure either company has put its name to. There is also no public revenue figure for OpenRouter anywhere. So nobody outside the deal room can test whether seven billion dollars is a strategic price or simply a scarcity price for a category with almost no other scaled independent player left to buy.

This fits a read this show made on August 22nd, off Ramp's corporate spending data. Businesses run more than one lab and route work by cost, which makes the frontier model a substitutable input rather than a platform anyone commits to. If that's right, the layer that decides the routing is the asset. And notice what Stripe gets beyond whatever thin margin routing itself earns: visibility into which provider gets paid for enterprise inference, across a very large number of buyers. That data position is the durable part of the purchase. It also leaves the remaining standalone gateway vendors hunting for a strategic buyer rather than a growth round. The so-what: when the model becomes an input, the router becomes the platform, and an incumbent rather than a challenger now owns it.

Here's the tension worth sitting with. Two buyers, SpaceX and Stripe, paid up hard this month for direct exposure to rising agent volume. Public equity markets spent that same month pricing that same volume as a reason to discount software.

On August 18th, a rotation lifted beaten-down enterprise software names: Monday.com up 6.9 percent, HubSpot up 6.6, Intuit up 5.2, with semiconductors falling in the same session. None of that came from software news. It came from AI capex anxiety. Anthropic told investors it projects 190 to 200 billion dollars of revenue in 2028, and that figure landed below the whisper numbers circulating in Silicon Valley. Separately, the Wall Street Journal reported that nine major tech companies now carry roughly 3 trillion dollars of off-balance-sheet commitments, mostly tied to AI infrastructure. Workday, still the subject of the takeover talks this show covered last week, sits inside that same anxious complex. The pattern is plain enough: when AI capex looks shakier, software goes up.

Which sets up a loaded week. Intuit reports Tuesday, Salesforce Wednesday, Workday and Autodesk Thursday. None of them have reported as I record this. There are no results, no guidance and no beats to discuss yet — only the setup.

Put the two halves together and the disagreement is stark. Strategic and private buyers are paying escalating prices for exposure to exactly the agent-driven volume that public markets are treating as a discount factor on software. Both cannot stay right for long. And here is the uncomfortable part. If this week's prints come in strong, that does not simply validate these stocks. It removes the AI capex anxiety that has been lifting them. For a rotation built on fear rather than on fundamentals, a good number is genuinely ambiguous news.
