Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Credit & the Corporate Credit Cycle

Transcript · August 5, 2026

Good morning, it's August 5th, 2026. The last of the major direct lenders finally reported, and it answers the question this show has been sitting on since Sunday.

Apollo posted record second-quarter results yesterday, and the headline numbers are real: fee-related earnings of 785 million dollars, up 25 percent year over year, spread-related earnings of 877 million, a record, and adjusted net income of 1.3 billion dollars, or two dollars eleven cents a share. But the number worth dwelling on isn't the bottom line, it's what drove it. Capital solutions fees, the upfront revenue Apollo earns arranging direct loans and asset-backed financing, hit a quarterly record of 277 million dollars, up 28 percent year over year. That's the credit platform, not the private equity business, doing the heavy lifting. Apollo originated 74 billion dollars in the quarter, and that figure excludes the 35 billion dollar Broadcom financing tied to Anthropic's AI buildout, which will be recognized over future quarters instead. Organic inflows across the platform hit 60 billion dollars, the most the firm has ever taken in a single quarter. Total assets under management crossed 1.05 trillion dollars, up 208 billion, or 25 percent, year over year. So the largest annuity-funded lender in the market just confirmed, with its own numbers, that the institutional pole of private credit is compounding faster than ever, and its growth engine now sits squarely in credit and AI capex financing rather than buyouts.

That raises an earnings-quality question worth separating out before we move on. Capital solutions fees are deal-dependent and upfront, paid when a transaction closes. That's a different animal from the recurring, AUM-linked management fees that have powered the compounding story at KKR and Blue Owl. If Apollo's fee growth increasingly leans on deal flow rather than a growing base of assets simply sitting and earning, that fee stream is more cyclical than it looks in a quarter this strong. Worth watching whether it holds up if large-cap deal activity slows. And on the origination number itself, remember the 35 billion dollar Broadcom deal is sitting on the sidelines of this quarter's 74 billion dollar figure. When it's recognized, a future quarter's origination headline will look artificially inflated against this one. Don't read 74 billion as the clean run rate.

On credit quality, Apollo's call gave a more precise picture than the initial wires suggested, and it's worth getting right. Rather than a single blended spread, management disclosed a bifurcated structure. Investment-grade-equivalent originations, roughly 75 percent of the 68 billion dollars in debt originated this quarter, priced at 280 basis points over Treasuries, while the remaining 25 percent, the sub-investment-grade slice, priced at 440. The average investment-grade piece carries a BBB-plus rating, the sub-investment-grade piece averages B. On the face of it, then, Apollo is still getting paid meaningfully more, in absolute spread, to go down in quality. But look one layer deeper. Measured against comparably rated public corporates instead of Treasuries, the investment-grade paper earned about 200 basis points of extra spread, while the sub-investment-grade paper earned only about 150. So the genuine illiquidity-and-origination premium, the part you can't get in the public market, is actually richer at the top of the quality stack than at the bottom. That's a tell that competition further down the curve is quietly squeezing the compensation for taking real credit risk. When you hear that Apollo is writing mostly investment-grade paper, that's true by volume, but the pricing shows the firm hasn't simply chased the safest, lowest-margin corner of the market, and where it's being paid best is not where the default risk is highest.

Now the other leg of this print, because it's arguably the more interesting one for the retail-versus-institutional debate this show has been running all week. Apollo's own retail credit vehicle, Apollo Debt Solutions, reported redemptions in the current quarter running at half the pace they were in the second quarter. That's a decline, not an acceleration, and it cuts directly against the idea that the wealth channel is uniformly stalling or gating the way Blue Owl's and Blackstone's retail vehicles have. Management also pointed to performance as the reason advisors are sticking with the product: a dollar invested in Apollo Debt Solutions grew to about a dollar forty, against a dollar twenty for high yield over the same stretch. So the caveat here is important: don't generalize from Blue Owl and Blackstone to "the retail channel." Apollo's insurance-anchored retail product looks like it may be capturing share from peers rather than facing the same investor flight. That's a genuine divergence within the bifurcation, not a confirmation of it, and it's the kind of nuance that gets lost if you round every retail vehicle into one basket.

Underneath all of this sits Athene, Apollo's annuity and insurance arm, which took in 22 billion dollars in the quarter, including 12 billion from retail annuities and 4 billion from flow reinsurance, bringing first-half inflows to 42 billion, on track for an 85 billion dollar full-year target. Gross invested assets reached 414 billion, up 14 percent year over year. That's the funding base underneath everything Apollo originates, and it's growing steadily, which is exactly the "stable institutional pole" this show flagged on Sunday.

Ares Management's print from earlier this week corroborates the pattern from a different angle. Fee-related earnings rose 20 percent year over year to 491 million dollars, realized income rose more than 30 percent to 522 million, and dry powder hit a record 170 billion dollars. We already covered the record 36 billion dollar quarterly fundraise and roughly 671 billion in total AUM on Sunday, so those aren't new, but the dry powder figure is, and it's the number that matters most here. Demand is clearly not the constraint: Ares's alternative-credit fund Pathfinder Three closed the quarter at its 8.5 billion dollar hard cap, well above a 6.5 billion dollar target. Put Ares's 170 billion of dry powder alongside Apollo's origination surge and you get a picture of capital piling up faster than it can be deployed into quality borrowers. That's the mechanism behind the spread compression showing up at the investment-grade, large-cap end of the market: too much money chasing a finite pool of the best credits.

So here's the synthesis. This is still the story from Sunday's bifurcation, institutional and annuity-funded capital accelerating while pockets of the retail channel struggle, but two things sharpen this week. First, record dry powder plus an investment-grade tilt at the top of the market means the mega-managers are competing spreads away exactly where they now concentrate their lending, even as the wider 440 basis point spread on sub-investment-grade paper shows real compensation still exists further down the quality curve. Second, Apollo's own retail vehicle bucking the redemption trend is a reminder that the credit-quality test hasn't actually happened yet at the mega-manager level. Fee growth at the top is not the same thing as tested credit performance in the loan book, and none of what we heard this week from Apollo or Ares resolves that.

The near-term catalysts to watch: Blue Owl Capital Corporation, the BDC arm, reports after the close tonight, and Blackstone Secured Lending reports tomorrow morning. Blackstone's non-accruals had already jumped to 3.1 percent of fair value in the first quarter, from just 0.1 percent a year earlier. Those are the numbers that will actually test whether the deterioration flagged all week in the lower-middle market is spreading, or staying contained down-market and away from where the biggest lenders now write their paper.
