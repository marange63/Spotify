Frontier AI Lab Competition

Transcript · August 25, 2026

Good morning. It's Tuesday, August 25th, and this is your frontier AI labs briefing.

This show has spent a week arguing that the price floor under frontier AI isn't set in San Francisco or Mountain View. It's set in Hangzhou and Beijing, at zero, because free Chinese open weights undercut whatever the American labs charge. In the last ten days, the people setting that floor started charging for it. So today the floor is rising from underneath. The ceiling — meaning the buildout at the top end — is being financed in a way that raises its own questions. And for the first time, an outsider has tried to grade the labs' safety claims instead of taking their word for it.

Start with the capability side, because it's what justified the price move. On August 21st, DeepSeek shipped an experimental multimodal model called V4-Flash-Vision. On DeepSeek's own benchmark table, that model beats Anthropic's Opus 4.8 on three of eleven evaluations. Two of them are worth naming. It scores 27.3 against 25.7 on a test called Agents Last Exam, and 35.0 against 34.0 on ZeroBench. Take that with real skepticism. DeepSeek ran the comparisons itself, on its own internal harness, with no independent replication. That's narrowing perception, not proven parity. But perception is exactly what a company needs before it can raise a price.

And DeepSeek did raise its price. Effective August 16th, it restructured V4-Flash billing into peak and off-peak tiers, with off-peak set at half of peak. On its face that sounds like a discount. It isn't one.

Before the change, V4-Flash billed a flat 14 cents per million input tokens. After the change, even the cheaper off-peak tier costs 22 cents. That's 57 percent more expensive than the old flat rate, and off-peak is the good tier. At peak hours the input rate is 44 cents, more than three times the old flat rate. One caveat on the before-price. The current rates come off DeepSeek's own pricing page. The old flat rate traces only to independent pricing trackers, because DeepSeek's changelog describes the new structure without any historical dollar figures. So this is a genuine price increase dressed as a billing restructure, landing in the same week the model claimed to be closing on Anthropic's flagship.

Alibaba is moving the same direction from the other end. Reuters reports that Alibaba will require large commercial users of its next flagship, Qwen3.8-Max, to share the revenue they generate from it. That's Reuters citing two people familiar with the plan, not an Alibaba announcement. The weights themselves stay free to download. Open weights and free to deploy have just stopped being the same claim. This answers a question this show left open on Sunday, when we said the license on Alibaba's largest Qwen model wasn't confirmed open. It's confirmed now, and it isn't free at scale. Alibaba hasn't attached a percentage to the revenue share, or defined how large a large commercial user has to be. Both are still under negotiation. The template already exists. This show flagged it on Friday as a toll booth on commercial resale. Moonshot's Kimi K3 requires a commercial agreement above 20 million dollars of annual revenue, with a revenue share of up to 30 percent. Alibaba appears to be copying that.

Not every open-weight lab is moving that way, and the split matters. Meta released a multimodal model called Muse Glimmer on August 10th, roughly 30 billion parameters and licensed under Apache 2.0. Genuinely free, with no revenue string attached. That one comes from aggregated reporting rather than Meta's own announcement page, and no verified benchmark exists for it, so treat it as a licensing fact only. Alibaba and Moonshot are monetizing their largest models. Meta is giving away a mid-size one it was never going to charge for anyway. The price floor under frontier AI is now a range, not a zero.

Two consequences follow, and they run on different clocks. The near-term one is that the strongest external threat to the US labs' top-tier pricing power just got weaker. Gross margin, not growth rate, is the number separating these labs, because free open weights capped what they could charge at the top. If both major Chinese labs are converging on the Americans' economics, that cap loosens. It changes the setup for Anthropic's reported plan to go public this autumn — better pricing power on the same revenue forecast — without changing a single number in it. The slower consequence is the one nobody books this quarter. A revenue-share threshold set high enough to exempt startups still leaves those startups facing a licensing cliff at scale. That is the moment a US lab gets a second chance at a developer it had already lost.

If the cheap end just got more expensive, price the expensive end too. Reporting on August 17th describes a securities filing under which Nvidia provides more than 105 billion dollars of financing for a new OpenAI data center campus in Ohio. That is secondary reporting, not a direct read of the filing. Now set that 105 billion against what Nvidia expects to collect there. The campus runs exclusively on Nvidia chips, roughly 1.5 million GPUs, and on Nvidia's own accounting that comes to 150 to 200 billion dollars of potential revenue per chip generation. So the money going out is smaller than the money the vendor expects back. The initial build is 4.25 gigawatts of capacity. That is grid-scale power, not campus-scale, and the option to expand beyond it is an option, not a commitment. A SoftBank-backed developer called SB Energy will build and own the site, then lease it back to OpenAI on a long-dated lease.

Nvidia is also putting 1.5 billion dollars directly into SB Energy itself. So Nvidia is lending the money that buys Nvidia's own chips, and taking an equity stake in the landlord collecting the rent. That is ordinary vendor financing in a capital-goods business, a positive-spread bet rather than a giveaway.

The real exposure is that the spread only holds if OpenAI monetizes inference at a scale nobody has demonstrated yet. Nvidia has just converted a sales relationship with its biggest customer into a credit relationship with it. And notice where the asset sits. SB Energy owns the physical site and carries the construction and lease risk, not Nvidia and not OpenAI. That is the same pattern Anthropic used when it moved compute purchases into separately financed vehicles, off its own balance sheet. The risk keeps moving toward the party with the least visibility into whether the demand shows up. Nvidia reports earnings tomorrow, August 26th. Nothing here predicts that print.

Last dimension, and it is neither price nor capital. TechCrunch reported on August 22nd that a standards group called Guidelight published the first cross-lab assessment of how prepared each lab is to contain a rogue model. It's a single-source item, built entirely from what the labs have published. OpenAI scored highest, 3 out of 5, specifically because it has actually paused workloads and training runs after past safety incidents. Anthropic and Meta scored lowest. Guidelight found that Anthropic's published risk report doesn't list limiting a model's deployment as a possible outcome of its process. It found no evidence that Meta has a containment plan at all. xAI didn't respond, so it is unscored rather than unprepared.

Treat that ranking carefully. It grades documents, not behavior. A lab with a real but unpublished containment plan scores the same as a lab with nothing. And Guidelight is a new standards body whose methodology hasn't been independently vetted. An index that ranks the safety-branded lab last is exactly the result that gets a new entrant noticed. The mechanism still matters, though. Third-party grading turns safety branding into an auditable claim instead of a marketing line. It also flips an incentive that had been pointing the wrong way. When the only graders are the labs themselves, disclosing a near-critical finding costs more than staying quiet. Once an outsider is scoring the disclosures, silence scores as a zero. Anthropic's cheapest response is to publish a deployment-limiting protocol it probably already runs internally, which would make this index self-correct within one cycle. That tells you how little the first ranking actually measured.

Put it together. This week's picture isn't about who has the better model. Capability at the top has looked frozen for over a week. It's about who controls the terms. The Chinese labs are raising the floor they were supposed to be flattening. Nvidia is financing the buildout at the top that it also sells into. And an outsider has started grading the safety claims the labs used to grade for themselves. Two things to watch. Whether DeepSeek's other tiers follow Flash's price climb. And whether Alibaba ever attaches a hard percentage to that Qwen revenue share before the model ships.

That's the briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
