Good morning, it's August 10th, and this is your private equity briefing.

All week we've been telling you the same thing: cash isn't coming back to limited partners the way it used to. Today we can finally put a hard number behind that claim, and it comes from a primary source rather than a stray estimate. Bain and Company's Global Private Equity Report 2026 finds that distributions as a share of net asset value have stayed below 15% for four straight years. That's Bain's own language, as reported by CFO.com, and it's the worst distribution run since the 2008 financial crisis. The same report puts the industry's unsold-portfolio backlog at roughly 32,000 companies worth about $3.8 trillion, with average holding periods now stretched to about seven years. So the drought is no longer a qualitative worry we've been asserting for five days. It's an industry-body measurement.

Sit with what sub-15% for four years actually means. In a healthy cycle, a mature fund hands back a meaningful slice of its value every year as companies get sold. Four consecutive years below that threshold means an entire cohort of funds is aging in place — marked up on paper, but not converting those marks into wired cash. That seven-year average hold is the mechanical footprint of the same problem: assets that would normally have turned over are simply being carried longer, waiting for an exit window that hasn't opened.

One caveat worth keeping precise, because it's the number people will want to repeat. Separate reporting has claimed that 2018-to-2021 vintage buyout funds are still stuck below a DPI of 1.0, meaning many of them haven't yet returned limited partners' original capital. That specific vintage-level claim traces only to a single commentary piece. It is not in Bain's report, and we couldn't confirm it against Preqin or PitchBook vintage data. Treat it as plausible, unconfirmed, and worth watching, not as established fact. And to be clear on the concept itself: a DPI under 1.0 is a return-of-capital statement, not a total-return one. It says LPs haven't gotten their cash back yet. It does not say the funds are losing money. Those marks may well be real. They just aren't liquid.

Now, why does this drought persist rather than clearing itself? Because it costs the managers almost nothing. Fee-related earnings compound off assets under management regardless of whether any individual fund is distributing cash. We've walked through the record fee-related earnings prints from the big multi-strategy managers all week, and the mechanism they reveal is the same one at work here. The incentive to force an exit at a discount, just to get LPs paid, is structurally weak when the fee engine keeps running either way. And here's the part the market tends to get backwards. A rising paper mark doesn't relieve the drought — it deepens it. The higher a manager marks an asset, the less willing it is to sell that asset for anything less, so the marks climb while the cash spigot stays narrow. Those two things can coexist indefinitely from the manager's side of the table.

Limited partners have noticed. Twenty-one percent of them now cite distributions-to-paid-in as their single most important performance metric, up from just 8% three years ago. That shift is quietly reshaping how general partners raise their next fund. A pitch built on paper IRR and total-value multiples doesn't land the way it did in 2021. Managers now have to lead with a demonstrated cash-return track record, and that structurally disadvantages any shop whose recent vintages haven't proven they can actually get money out the door. Fundraising is turning into a referendum on realized liquidity, not modeled performance.

So how are managers manufacturing liquidity while the exit window stays shut? Continuation vehicles. Their use has roughly tripled, from about 6% of mature-fund distributions in the 2016-to-2020 window to around 20% from 2021 through 2025. That is no longer a niche workaround — it's approaching a fifth of how distributions happen industry-wide. And that scale is exactly why a live court case now matters. In Delaware's Court of Chancery, the Abu Dhabi Investment Council is suing Energy and Minerals Group over a continuation vehicle built around an asset called Ascent Resources. The allegation is that the sponsor was a "net buyer" in its own vehicle — putting more of its own money into the new structure while the transaction reset its carried interest in a way that made the sponsor more likely to profit on the eventual sale. That is the conflict this structure has always had baked in: the general partner sitting on both sides of the trade, setting the price at which it buys assets from its own limited partners. For the first time, that conflict is being tested in front of a judge rather than debated at a conference. The sponsor agreed to defer closing toward an arbitration process around March of this year, and the case's status beyond that point isn't confirmed in what we have. So treat this as live litigation with the outcome still pending, not a verdict on the structure.

Think about the second-order effects on both fronts, because they point the same direction. On fundraising: if the 2018-to-2021 vintages keep missing that 1.0x threshold, successor raises for those same managers get structurally harder. That likely accelerates consolidation toward the largest multi-strategy platforms — the ones that can point to insurance, credit, and infrastructure realizations to paper over weak traditional buyout distributions. A mid-sized buyout shop with one sleepy vintage and no other business lines has nowhere to hide; a diversified giant does. So the distribution drought is quietly a consolidation engine.

On the liquidity plumbing: if the Delaware case produces an adverse ruling on how general partners conduct themselves inside continuation vehicles, expect pressure toward independent fairness opinions or LP-led pricing processes across the entire continuation-vehicle market. That would add friction and cost to precisely the mechanism currently doing the most work draining the distribution backlog. You'd be slowing down the one valve that's actually releasing pressure, right as it became the dominant route.

Put it together and the cycle isn't resolving its distribution problem. It's financializing around it — layering continuation vehicles and NAV-based lending on top of a backlog that keeps growing, rather than clearing it through genuine third-party sales. The reservoir is filling faster than it drains, and the industry is building ever more elaborate plumbing to move water around inside it. The one catalyst that would actually refute this read isn't another continuation vehicle or another record fee-related earnings quarter. It's a wave of real, independent exits — strategic buyers or public markets absorbing portfolio companies at prices GPs are willing to accept, and DPI climbing back toward historical norms. Until that shows up in the data, the drought is the story, and now it's the story with Bain's numbers behind it and a courtroom testing the workaround.
