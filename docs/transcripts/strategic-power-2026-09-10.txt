Good morning. It's Thursday, September 10th, and this is your Cautious Optimism briefing on strategic power.

Twice in the past week this beat has described China's rare-earth leverage as an option nobody had actually exercised. A suspended package. An intact licensing architecture. A trigger sitting untouched until November. That framing was too generous to Beijing's restraint, and correcting it is today's lead.

Beijing has already run the rare-earth play to completion against a US treaty ally. Chinese and Japanese customs data, cited in reporting published this week, shows Japan received zero shipments of dysprosium, terbium and yttrium from China in June, the most recent month with data. That extends a cutoff that began last November. So that is at least seven straight months with none of the heavy rare-earth oxides that go into electric-vehicle traction motors and precision-guided munitions magnets, aimed squarely at Tokyo, in apparent retaliation for Prime Minister Takaichi's remarks on Taiwan. The same reporting shows the squeeze is wider than the magnet metals. Japan received no gallium and no germanium from China in January or February, a single gallium shipment in May, and then nothing again in June. Those are semiconductor inputs, not motor inputs. The campaign spans two different supply chains.

Be precise about what that evidence does and does not establish. The zero-shipment record runs through June. No July or August trade data was available in this research pass, and there is no dated Chinese notice confirming the restriction's legal status today. So the honest statement is that the lever was demonstrably pulled and held for most of a year, not that a shipment is being blocked this morning. What is current is the architecture around it. Beijing's 2026 import-export licensing catalogue now adds samarium, gadolinium and lutetium compounds to the controlled list, and China's commerce ministry has formalized a public reporting mechanism for export-control violations. That last piece does not expand what is controlled. It raises the probability that a violation gets caught. Put it together and you have an enforcement posture hardening while the headline package sits suspended.

Here is why that matters for the date this beat has been circling. The broader October 2025 rare-earth control package is suspended under the Xi-Trump trade truce, but only until November 10th. We flagged that expiry on Sunday without being able to pin the date, and pinned it yesterday. It is no longer just a calendar entry. It is a date with a demonstrated precedent behind it. And it sits roughly seven weeks behind the leader-level meeting we flagged yesterday, with Xi due at the White House on September 24th. Now the fair reading on the other side. The most current reporting describes Beijing and Washington as set to discuss whether to extend the truce before it lapses, with a breakdown in those talks as the trigger for further tightening. That framing matters. Extension is the status quo outcome being negotiated toward. A suspension expiring is still not the same thing as controls being reimposed.

But there is a symmetry worth naming, and it cuts against Beijing. A lever used this loudly is a lever the rest of the world starts designing around. Seven months of zero shipments to Japan is close to the most effective advertisement Australian, Vietnamese and American magnet capacity could ever have bought. This is the mirror image of the argument we ran yesterday about Washington's compute controls, where tightening the rule hands Chinese foundries a captive domestic market and shortens the chokehold's own useful life. The same mechanism runs in reverse here. Every month Beijing holds this line, it is teaching its best customers to build a supply chain that does not need China. So the so-what is a pricing question. If the option premium on November 10th is not already visible in magnet-exposed defense primes and EV makers, then the market is quietly assuming the suspension renews by default, and it is doing so without much scrutiny.

That is the input the West has to negotiate for. Now the input it is trying to build its way out of: electricity for artificial intelligence, and specifically the nuclear bet.

Big Tech has signed contracts for more than ten gigawatts of prospective new nuclear capacity over the last year, across more than a dozen agreements. Microsoft, Google, Amazon and Meta each have at least one on the books. The largest single block is Microsoft's twenty-year, sixteen-billion-dollar power purchase agreement for the restarted Three Mile Island plant in Pennsylvania: eight hundred thirty-seven megawatts, and it does not come online until 2028. The rest of the stack is thinner still in engineering terms. It is small-modular reactor orders, a site conversion, and a request for proposals — technologies that have not yet been built at commercial scale in the United States, on timelines that begin with a groundbreaking rather than a grid connection. So the pattern holds across the whole set: contracted, not operating. A signature, not an asset.

The honest bear case is not that the deals are fake. A twenty-year purchase agreement is a real balance-sheet commitment, and Three Mile Island is a restart of a unit that already exists, which is a far shorter path than new construction. The bear case is about timing. None of this ten-gigawatt commitment touches the power crunch happening between now and 2028. The megawatts that actually serve AI load in that window are gas turbines and uprates on existing plants. So the trade splits the same way the enrichment point split it yesterday, when we argued that contracted separative work, not reactor announcements, is the honest gate on any small-modular timeline sold to a data center. Near-term, this is a combustion-and-uprate story owned by incumbents. The nuclear story being marketed to investors is a 2030s asset being valued today.

That makes four straight briefings on this beat where an announced commitment failed to convert into delivered capacity: interconnection queues, oil quotas, capacity auctions, enrichment task orders, and now nuclear power purchase agreements. Today there is one place the test actually passes, and it is worth asking why.

NATO's own July data shows European allies and Canada raised core defense expenditure by nearly twenty percent in 2025 against the prior year. Five allies are already expected to meet the three-and-a-half percent of GDP core-defense guideline this year, and seventeen to meet the broader one-and-a-half percent security-investment guideline — years ahead of the 2035 deadline agreed at The Hague. Those are July figures, so treat them as the standing scoreboard rather than this week's news. But this is budget execution, not pledges. And the reason it converts where a megawatt does not is structural. A defense budget is appropriated annually by a sovereign with taxing power. A megawatt needs a permit, a turbine and an interconnection queue slot that no appropriation can conjure.

Now the skeptical turn, because a spending guideline is an input measure, not an order book. A twenty percent surge into a supply-constrained industrial base — propellant lines, interceptor production, shipyard slots — can just as easily show up as higher prices for the same output as more units on the line. That is the identical conversion failure that turns a capacity price into a transfer to incumbent generators rather than into new generation. So the so-what is a discipline: watch deliveries and order backlogs at the primes, not national spending ratios.

And the war underwriting those budgets has no scheduled end. US envoys Witkoff and Kushner shuttled between Moscow and Kyiv in early September. A narrow, capitals-only strike pause held for about three days and then lapsed on schedule. Russia resumed strikes on Kyiv on the 7th and again on September 9th, injuring fourteen people including two children — a figure from a single contributor report, not a wire service, so hold the count loosely. Meanwhile the joint US, UK and EU shadow-fleet sanctions list stood at 178 vessels as of July, up from zero two years earlier. Absence of a public breakthrough is not proof of no private progress, and belligerents do escalate to improve terms at the table. But the practical effect is the same either way: a geopolitical floor under European gas prices, rising compliance and insurance costs across tanker markets, and no near-term catalyst for a European energy disinflation surprise.

Put the ledger together. The instruments that have actually been used or funded this cycle are Beijing's rare-earth chokehold and Europe's defense budgets. They are already spent, or already withheld. The instruments the West is counting on to answer them — ten gigawatts of nuclear contracts and a negotiated end to the war in Ukraine — are still paper. Three dates tell you which side of that ledger moves next: September 24th at the White House, November 10th, and whether Japan's rare-earth shipments resume before either one.

That's the briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
