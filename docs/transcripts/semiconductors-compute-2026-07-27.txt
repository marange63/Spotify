Good morning. It's Monday, July 27th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

Memory has been this week's pacing signal, and today the equity market rendered a verdict the shortage narrative did not want. The memory complex fell into a bear market even as the underlying businesses printed records. Micron is down roughly 30 percent from its 52-week high, erasing more than 400 billion dollars in market value. Samsung and SK hynix are each off more than 20 percent from their own highs. This is happening against a backdrop of genuinely strong fundamentals. Micron's fiscal third quarter revenue came in at 41.5 billion dollars with an 84.9 percent gross margin, and the company has guided to 50 billion dollars in revenue at 86 percent gross margin for the current quarter. So the tape is not reacting to weak results. It's pricing something forward-looking, and what it's pricing is a cyclical peak, not the structural, multi-year shortage that suppliers keep insisting is underway.

That skepticism has real evidence behind it, not just sentiment. Korea Investment and Securities has cut its estimate of SK hynix's second-quarter operating profit to about 60.4 trillion won, roughly 8 percent below the 65 trillion won the market had been expecting. Two things are driving that cut. First, full-scale mass production of HBM4, the next-generation high-bandwidth memory that underpins Nvidia's upcoming Vera Rubin platform, has slipped to the third quarter, removing an upside analysts had already priced into the second quarter. Second, KIS revised its estimate of SK hynix's blended DRAM average selling price growth down from 50 percent quarter over quarter to 28.9 percent. That's not a rounding error. It's a meaningful step down in the pricing momentum that has been the whole bull case for memory this year. So this isn't just a valuation scare. There's a real, quoted fundamental crack underneath it, even if it's a single analyst's estimate rather than a confirmed multi-quarter trend.

It's worth being clear about what is not new here, because it changes how you read the move. The idea that 2026 HBM pricing could weaken is not a fresh discovery. It traces back over a year, to Goldman Sachs research flagging oversupply risk as competitors began catching up to SK hynix's technical lead. That's old context, not this week's trigger. What's new is not the thesis. It's that the equity market has finally started pricing a bear case that has been sitting on the table, ignored, for more than a year while the shortage story ran. Sometimes a market doesn't need new information to reprice. It just needs to change its mind about which of the facts it already had actually matter.

Now here's the divergence that makes this week interesting rather than just another selloff. Even as public markets are selling memory on fear of a cycle peak, the strategic buyers of that memory are still committing serious capital to lock down supply for years out. Late last week, Nvidia and SK Group announced an expanded partnership worth more than 500 billion dollars, spanning AI-factory buildouts in Korea and joint development of next-generation HBM. Days earlier, AMD committed up to 5 billion dollars in equity to Anthropic, alongside a deal to deploy up to 2 gigawatts of AMD's Instinct accelerators. Neither of those is new news today. But they're the context for the fresh development this week, which is that Amkor Technology disclosed a multi-year partnership with Nvidia in which Nvidia will prepay 1.5 billion dollars to expand Amkor's advanced packaging and test capacity in Arizona. That capacity stands up a second, US-based source for the kind of high-density chip packaging that TSMC's CoWoS process has effectively monopolized, and CoWoS has been sold out through this year. So this is Nvidia using its own balance sheet to hedge the binding constraint on accelerator supply, not just placing purchase orders and hoping capacity shows up.

There's a second reason that Amkor move matters, beyond securing Nvidia's own supply. If the Arizona buildout scales, it starts to erode the pricing power TSMC has enjoyed precisely because advanced packaging was scarce and concentrated in one place. A credible second source doesn't just add capacity. It changes the negotiating leverage on every packaging contract behind it. So even a single prepayment is a marker of where the constraint, and the margin, may migrate next.

Put those two threads together and you get a real tension. Operators like Nvidia and AMD are betting on durable scarcity, backing that bet with billions of dollars of prepayments and equity stakes. The public equity market is betting the opposite, that memory pricing and packaging economics are approaching a classic cyclical top. Both sides can't be fully right. And it's worth being honest that the capital-commitment side is not disinterested evidence. When AMD takes equity in Anthropic, or Nvidia prepays its own suppliers, those structures blur vendor demand-signaling with genuine capital allocation. A vendor financing its own customers and suppliers is, at some level, buying its own demand forecast. So the prepayments tell you these companies have deep visibility into their multi-year roadmaps, but they also have every incentive to make the scarcity look durable.

Which brings me to the part of this that's genuinely counterintuitive. If the bears are right and the tape keeps punishing memory, the selloff may partly defeat itself. SK hynix only listed on Nasdaq on July 10th, and the Korean makers lean on public markets to fund aggressive HBM4 and HBM4E capacity expansion. Sustained equity pressure raises the cost of exactly the capital they'd need to add supply. And if new supply gets more expensive to finance, it comes online more slowly, which reinforces the very tightness the bears are fading. So there's a circularity here that investors distinguishing valuation risk from demand risk should sit with. A memory-sector derating is not the same thing as a memory glut. It can, at the margin, delay one.

The other side of that same coin runs through Nvidia. Nvidia is not a neutral observer of memory pricing. On the one hand, softer HBM pricing in 2027 would cut its input costs, and HBM is one of the largest bills of material in an accelerator. On the other hand, the same softening would signal that the compute-scarcity thesis underpinning Nvidia's own demand is starting to crack. That's an awkward position. Nvidia is both a beneficiary of cheaper memory and a hostage to what cheaper memory would imply about the durability of the buildout it's financing. It is quietly rooting for scarcity in its suppliers even as it complains about the cost of it.

Which side has the better information is genuinely unresolved right now, and that's not a hedge, it's the honest state of play. The prepayments and equity stakes are forward commitments from companies with the deepest visibility into their own multi-year accelerator roadmaps. But they're also financing structures that carry an agenda, so they're not proof either. The first hard test comes fast. SK hynix reports earnings on July 29th, and Samsung follows on July 30th. Those prints will either validate the KIS cut and the HBM4 slip, confirming the market's cyclical read, or they'll show the shortage narrative was right and the sell-off was a buying opportunity. Watch two lines in particular: the actual blended DRAM pricing trajectory, and management's guidance on HBM4 ramp timing. Those are where the cyclical-versus-structural argument gets settled, not in the headline profit number. Either way, this week's divergence between the tape and the capital commitments gets tested within 72 hours.
