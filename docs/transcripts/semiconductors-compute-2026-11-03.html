Semiconductors & Compute Infrastructure

Transcript · November 3, 2026

Good morning, it's Tuesday, November 3rd, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

On Saturday this desk said the accelerator makers look like the next stop for the memory transfer. Now we have a first look at the invoice. Samsung has reportedly opened negotiations on HBM4 for 2027 delivery. Local media reports, relayed by the Korea Herald, put its ask in the mid-to-high four-dollar range per gigabit. That's more than triple the roughly one dollar fifty per gigabit of its current HBM3E. The Korea Herald is explicit on two points. These are reported asking prices, not final contract terms. And Samsung has not publicly confirmed them. So treat this as an opening bid, not a clearing price.

There's a second caveat. The comparison runs across generations. HBM4 carries more bandwidth per stack than HBM3E, so "triple" is not a like-for-like price increase on the same product. In late October, this desk carried expectations that HBM prices would more than double next year. The new figure sounds like an escalation from there, and partly it is. But some of that jump is simply the price of a new generation.

Now consider who is asking. Samsung is the number two in HBM. Counterpoint data, cited by Silicon Analysts, gave it a third of second-quarter HBM revenue, against half for SK hynix. That's a backward-looking share, not a 2027 allocation. Benzinga reported in September that SK hynix is at volume on 16-layer HBM4 for Nvidia's Rubin, while Samsung skipped that part. A laggard asking for triple points one of two ways. Either the shortage is severe enough that even second-source supply commands a premium. Or this is a headline number that gets negotiated down before anyone signs. This desk leans toward the first reading. A supplier without the leading part can only push this hard if buyers have few other places to go. But only a signed number settles it.

The contract-structure story deserves equal weight, with one timing flag. Back in July, TrendForce relayed a Korean press report that SK hynix is dropping the industry-standard price cap from its long-term agreements. Under that structure, spot-price gains pass fully into contract pricing. The same report says suppliers are stretching those agreements from one year to three to five years. None of that is new this week. But it reads very differently next to Samsung's ask.

Recall the test this desk set on October 29th. We said the deciding evidence for a structural boom would be fixed contract prices for 2028 through 2030. This structure runs the opposite way. Volume is committed for years, but price is left to float. There are two readings. One is seller confidence that the shortage lasts. The other is the cyclical tell: buyers will lock in supply, but refuse to lock in price at what may be the top.

Scope matters too. TrendForce frames this as a change to memory agreements generally. It does not describe an HBM-specific clause. Applied broadly, uncapped pricing reaches commodity DRAM and NAND, and with them PC and smartphone makers, not just accelerator buyers. And no SK hynix filing or call quote confirms any of it. So carry it as reported, not settled.

To size what's at stake, start with Nvidia's own developer blog. It says a Rubin GPU carries up to 288 gigabytes of HBM4. What follows is desk arithmetic, not a sourced figure. That's roughly 2,300 gigabits per chip. At four dollars fifty per gigabit, the low end of Samsung's reported range, that's on the order of ten thousand dollars of memory per GPU. At the HBM3E baseline of a dollar fifty, it's roughly thirty-five hundred. The gap is six to seven thousand dollars per chip. That would come out of accelerator gross margin, or get passed to the buyer, if the ask held across the market. A compute-constrained hyperscaler racing to deploy can swallow that. A buyer whose returns depend on revenue per token cannot swallow it so easily. So the cost lands hardest on the economics-constrained end of the market.

If memory is trying to set the terms, the buyers' answer is to diversify the silicon. Tech Times, citing unnamed sources, reports that OpenAI is in talks to buy Google's TPU hardware outright, rather than rent cloud capacity. That's a single-outlet report with no dollar figures attached. Google has not confirmed selling TPU hardware to an outside party. Even if it's true, it's a customer-relationship and supply-resilience move. It isn't evidence of TPU architecture leadership. And it says nothing yet about software portability away from CUDA.

The broader custom-silicon pull is better documented. A Seeking Alpha analysis of Broadcom's most recent quarter puts its custom-accelerator shipments up about three and a half times year over year. Those chips now make up nearly three-quarters of Broadcom's AI revenue. Marvell's custom-silicon agreement with Google carries a ceiling of up to 120 billion dollars through fiscal 2033. That's a ceiling on a purchase period, not booked revenue.

Here's the catch. Every one of those chips still buys its HBM from the same three suppliers. That holds for a Google TPU, a Broadcom accelerator, and an Nvidia GPU alike. So the three-vendor strategy shifts bargaining power away from Nvidia, toward the logic vendors competing for AI labs. It does nothing to discipline the memory makers. And if it grows total accelerator volume, it may raise aggregate HBM demand. Diversifying around Nvidia is not diversifying around memory.

The other buyer-side hope is new supply, and the memory windfall is funding it. China's ChangXin Memory, CXMT, reported third-quarter net profit attributable to shareholders of about 54 billion yuan, roughly eight billion dollars, per Gizmochina. That's a net figure, not operating profit. So don't set it against the Korean makers' operating margins. CXMT says it plans limited HBM3E production within about two months. But TrendForce, citing Reuters, puts its HBM3E yields at just 20 to 30 percent. SK hynix runs at roughly 70. TrendForce also reports that CXMT's part performs closer to SK hynix's older HBM3. On the ladder this desk uses, that's samples shipped and production planned. It is nowhere near customer qualification at scale. In our judgment, a yield that low on stacked memory is a cost structure only a state-backed balance sheet tolerates for long. So CXMT threatens commodity DRAM pricing and China-accessible demand. It doesn't threaten the Rubin-class HBM4 negotiation now under way.

The same refusal to cede control shows up one layer down, in logic manufacturing. A single DigiTimes report, relayed by other outlets, says Elon Musk may soften his insistence on running the proposed Texas Terafab for Tesla and SpaceX. That would open the door to a bigger TSMC role. TSMC chairman C.C. Wei has been blunt: "the fab has got to be run by TSMC." Nothing is finalized, and that quote states TSMC's position, not an agreement. Last week this desk tracked the Intel and Tesla 14A claim, which Intel's own filing never confirmed. A Musk tilt toward TSMC would thin Intel Foundry's external-customer case even further. Manufacturing control, like HBM supply, is the asset incumbents won't hand over cheaply.

A brief market check, because the equity tape disagrees with the contract table. Memory names were the hardest hit in the recent chip selloff. SK Hynix fell 10 percent and Samsung Electronics 7.4 percent. Tokyo-listed Kioxia fell as much as 13.3 percent. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index stood more than 7 percent below its October high. So at the negotiating table, memory makers are asking for triple and floating their prices. In the stock market, they're the most-sold names in the sector. One of those two signals about how long the shortage lasts will prove wrong. Two things will settle it. Watch where 2027 contract prices actually land. And watch whether any buyer signs longer-dated fixed pricing.

The first read on that could come tonight. AMD reports third-quarter results after today's close. It's the first accelerator vendor to report since the HBM4 pricing signal surfaced, and its results aren't known as this goes out. The number to listen for isn't gross margin against AMD's guide of about 56 percent. It's what management says about 2027 memory cost and supply for the MI400-series ramp. That's where today's lead story would show up first.

The through-line: buyers are diversifying around the GPU vendor, but not yet around the memory makers. That's it for today. Stay cautiously optimistic.
