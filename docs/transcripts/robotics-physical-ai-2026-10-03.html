Robotics & Physical AI

Transcript · October 3, 2026

Good morning. It's October 3rd, and this is your briefing on robotics and physical AI.

All week this desk has kept running into the same bottleneck in humanoid robotics: the hands and the joints. This week a group of German suppliers stepped forward to say they could supply them. And the US Army picked a humanoid built around exactly that problem. Let's take those in turn.

Start with volume, because it's the backdrop, not the news. Euronews, citing the research firm Smart Analytics Global, puts global humanoid deliveries at about 19,100 in the first half of 2026, with 97 percent of them from China. Hold that share loosely. On October 1st this desk reported IDC's count, which put China at about 78 percent of a larger global total. That's two research firms, two undisclosed definitions of a shipment, and a twenty-point gap between them. Either way, China leads assembly by a wide margin, and that isn't in dispute.

The live question, which Euronews raised this week, is who supplies the parts inside those bodies. That's where the economics live. By Euronews's account, a humanoid needs more than 40 joints, against about seven for a conventional industrial arm. And actuators make up roughly half of a humanoid's total cost. Put those together and the point is plain. Whoever controls the joint and the hand controls most of the bill of materials, no matter who bolts the robot together. Semiconductors have the same split. Assembly scale is one business. Owning the critical component is a different, often more defensible one.

This layer isn't new to this beat. On September 30th we reported that Beijing's securities regulator is reportedly screening humanoid listing candidates on whether they own core technology, in CNBC's words "robotic brain or hands." Tesla has reportedly struggled to build Optimus hands reliably at scale. And on October 2nd we flagged the rare-earth magnets that actuators depend on. That's three angles on one layer. What's new this week is that a supplier side has stepped forward.

Germany's industrial suppliers are positioning for exactly that layer. Bosch and Schunk have been working together since June on an integrated robot hand. It's designed to drop into different humanoid platforms. Euronews describes it as moving beyond individual parts to a complete key component. That's the strategic tell. A supplier that sells a subsystem keeps more of the margin than one that sells commodity parts. But today this is a development program. There's no disclosed customer, no volume, and no price. Don't mistake the ambition for an order book.

The durability argument behind the German pitch is also thinner than it sounds. Euronews leans on an academic at the University of Erlangen-Nuremberg, Sebastian Reitelshöfer. His point is an analogy, not a measurement. Chinese humanoids ran a half marathon in Beijing, he argues, because "they wouldn't withstand a full marathon." That's a fair jab at demo culture. It is not failure-rate data, and nobody in this story has published any.

There is a real German order on the books, though. It's just not new, and it's not the one Euronews described. In May, Schaeffler agreed to become preferred actuator supplier to the UK humanoid maker Humanoid. The deal covers more than half of that company's joint-actuator demand for its wheeled platforms, running through 2031. In return, Schaeffler plans to deploy a four-digit number of Humanoid's wheeled robots across its own factories by 2032. That's a named, dated commitment, which is more than the Bosch-Schunk story can show. And notice the structure. Schaeffler sits on both sides of the trade, as a component seller and as a buyer of robots. So the fair framing isn't that Germany just entered the race. One German supplier already has a seat at the table, five months ahead of this week's headline. The rest of the German supply base is chasing that template.

Now the limit on all of this. A German actuator doesn't get you out of China's grip. These actuators run on rare-earth permanent magnets. According to The Next Web, China holds about 90 percent of the world's processing capacity for those magnets. As that outlet puts it, an actuator assembled in Germany still contains a magnet refined in China, and no amount of supplier qualification changes that. No German supplier has disclosed a magnet-light design or non-Chinese magnet sourcing. So even if Germany wins the joint and the hand, the leverage point just slides one layer down the stack. That's the same magnet exposure this desk flagged on October 2nd, ahead of the November 10th expiry of the US-China trade truce.

So where does that leave the value-capture thesis? Sharpened, not settled. If component suppliers do capture the margin, listed European industrials become a way to own humanoid exposure without picking a robot maker. That's a picks-and-shovels trade layered on top of China's assembly win. But it's conditional. China installs more industrial automation than anyone, so its own component makers ride the same learning curve at greater scale. A German durability premium may simply lose to a lower price, the way it has in other hardware categories. That last part is this desk's inference. The so what: the joint is now the battleground, and the only disclosed German order predates this week's news.

Now the other half of the week. If the body is where the hard engineering lives, the US Army just voted the same way. The Army's xTechHumanoid competition picked Alex, an 85-kilogram humanoid from the Florida Institute for Human and Machine Cognition, known as IHMC. According to The Defense Post, Alex has impact-resilient actuators and integrated ballistic protection. It runs a hybrid autonomy system that, in the outlet's words, "blends AI with human control." Notice what the Army didn't pick. It didn't pick a fully autonomous general-purpose brain. It picked a hardened body with a human still in the loop. That's the honest state of autonomy wherever failure is expensive. Human control is still doing real work.

Keep the caveats sharp, because this is a competition win, not a contract. No dollar figure, production timeline, or unit cost has been disclosed. IHMC is a research institute, not a manufacturer with a production line. It's also worth placing Alex on the ladder this desk has been building. On September 30th we called the Army's counter-drone contract ceilings the one unambiguous paying customer in physical AI. On October 1st we corrected that in favor of Waymo's paid rides, because a ceiling is only a maximum. A competition prize sits one rung below even a ceiling. The fair bull reading is that defense often funds a capability years before it becomes a commercial product, as it did with drones. So this could seed a demand channel for ruggedized actuators and batteries, separate from the industrial capex cycle. That's our inference. It only becomes real the day a funded prototype contract follows, and that hasn't happened.

Pull the threads together. What's genuinely inflecting is Chinese assembly volume, plus a contest over the component layer that's now visible from several directions at once. Chinese regulators want listing candidates to own the hands. German suppliers want to sell them. The Army wants them armored. What's still a demo is everything without a disclosed order: Western hand programs with no customer, and defense humanoids with no contract. The biggest open question is whether the joint layer's margin goes to durability or to price. And beneath both sits a magnet supply that China still controls.

Three catalysts to watch. First, Agility Robotics holds its analyst and investor day on Tuesday, October 6th, ahead of its SPAC merger. It's the first time a Western humanoid maker will present a financial outlook in public, and its merger filing already showed how much it has to prove. Treat nothing from that day as known until it happens. Second, Tesla's October earnings call, the next chance for a company-sourced number on Optimus and its hand bottleneck. Third, the November 10th truce expiry, which matters to anyone building an actuator, in Germany or anywhere else.

That's the briefing from Cautious Optimism. Talk to you next time.
