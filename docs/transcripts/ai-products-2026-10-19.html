Good morning. It's Monday, October 19th, and this is your Cautious Optimism briefing on AI products.

This week, agentic software picked up two pieces of infrastructure it needs to act instead of just advise. One is a cart that works across stores. The other is a sandbox safe enough to run inside. Both shipped as open infrastructure rather than proprietary features. That's a specific bet: whoever sets the standard ends up owning the layer that gets built on top of it.

Start with the cart. According to TechCrunch's reporting, Google's Universal Cart is now available to U.S. users, as of October 15th. It lets you collect and monitor products you find across Google Search and the Gemini app in one persistent cart. You no longer lose track of what you found the moment you close the tab. Checkout runs on the Universal Commerce Protocol, an open standard Google introduced earlier this year.

Here's why that status change matters. Last Thursday, this beat covered Constructor shipping checkout inside a single retailer's own site. We called that the easy trust problem. The hard one was buying across different stores, and we flagged a company called Crossmint only as a name to watch there. Universal Cart is that harder, cross-retailer version. And Google shipped it as a general release to U.S. users, months after previewing it, not as a pilot. That's the difference between a feature demo and a product with consumer-scale distribution on day one. Google Search and Gemini are where a great deal of product discovery already starts.

Now the skeptical turn, because "open standard" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. The checkout protocol is open. The cart, the discovery surface, and the monitoring all still sit on Google's own properties. My read is that Google is opening the rails while keeping the station. This beat traced the same pattern on Friday with Google's Gemini Enterprise model selector. Leverage sits with whoever controls the shelf and its defaults, even when the plumbing underneath is nominally open to anyone.

Follow that through to the retailers, and label this as inference. If the cart becomes the persistent starting point for purchases, the customer relationship moves from the retailer's site to Google's surface. The retailer still fulfills the order. But Google holds the list, the reminders, and the moment of decision. That's the tenant-versus-landlord trade this beat has described for software incumbents, now applied to merchants.

It's also worth being precise about what this is not. Universal Cart is not an autonomous agent buying things on your behalf. You still assemble the cart yourself. The product adds persistence and monitoring, not independent purchasing authority. Keep that line between assistant and agent clear, because the marketing language around "agentic commerce" tends to blur it.

Two things are also missing from the public record. The reporting discloses no count of how many merchants actually support checkout through the Universal Commerce Protocol. And no fee structure has been disclosed for either merchants or consumers. So here's the honest summary. The hard version of agentic commerce now exists at consumer scale. Whether it becomes open rails or a walled garden with a friendly door depends on how many retailers sign on. A protocol with few merchants is a press release, not rails, and we don't yet have that number.

That leads to the other half of today's story. Once software can act on your behalf across stores, the next question is where that software runs, and what it's allowed to touch while it's running. That's the second piece of infrastructure that shipped this past week.

Perplexity open-sourced SPACE, the sandbox that, per coverage of the release, has contained its autonomous Computer agent since July. It's released under the Apache 2.0 license. In Perplexity's own words, "SPACE gives each agent task its own Firecracker microVM and controls credentials and outbound traffic from outside the VM." In plain terms, every task gets its own isolated virtual machine. The keys and the network connection live outside that machine, not inside it where a compromised agent could grab them.

Perplexity backed that design with its own red-team report. Frontier models attempted sandbox escapes across 108 separate runs. In the company's words, "No VM-to-host escape was observed in 108 runs." None of them got out of the box. That said, four models did bypass the network restrictions, through DNS spoofing or shared-IP routing. Perplexity then patched the gateway, adding source-address validation among other fixes. It reran the experiments to confirm the bypasses no longer worked.

Here's why this matters beyond one company's agent. For the past week, this beat has repeatedly found agent governance arriving after the fact. GitHub's agent sandbox shipped off by default. Anthropic's tracing tools amounted to observability rather than control. And the compliance API we covered yesterday gave administrators eyes on an agent's actions, not hands. SPACE breaks that pattern. It's containment built into the architecture before the agent ever acts, not a log reviewed afterward.

The skeptical caveats still apply, though. Isolating each task in its own microVM is not a novel technique. The news is that Perplexity packaged it as a usable runtime and gave it away. A vendor testing its own sandbox is not the same as an independent security audit, however clean the result looks. The network bypass is a reminder of that, since it was found and fixed in-house. And there are reports that cloud providers CoreWeave, Akamai, and Crusoe are partners in the push. That traces to a Semafor reporter's own account of his reporting. It is not a confirmed statement from Perplexity or those companies, and nothing yet says what form that hosting takes. Treat it as reported rather than settled.

There's a strategic contrast worth naming here too. Yesterday, our enterprise technology briefing covered Cohere's letter of intent to acquire Aisle, alongside reported talks between OpenAI and Trail of Bits. We read both as labs bringing agent security in-house. Perplexity is doing the opposite with the same layer of the stack. Instead of building or buying proprietary containment, it's giving its containment away. Those are two different bets on how the agent-security layer gets won: one through ownership, one through adoption.

That also puts pressure on anyone selling sandboxes as a product. On Saturday, that same enterprise desk covered Modal, which reports its agent sandboxes as a standalone business line. My inference is that a free, permissively licensed runtime pushes basic isolation toward table stakes. That lowers the cost of building a safe agent for startups. It also forces paid sandbox vendors to win on operations, scale, and support rather than on the isolation itself.

So where does that leave things? For the first time this week, a control showed up as a wall before an agent acts, rather than a record checked after it already did something. Whether SPACE becomes the default agent runtime, the way containers became commodity infrastructure for cloud computing, depends on adoption nobody has confirmed yet.

Put the two stories together, and the shape of the week is the same in both. Google opened a checkout protocol while keeping the cart, the search surface, and the monitoring on its own turf. Perplexity opened its entire sandbox, code included, betting that becoming the default earns more than keeping it proprietary. Both companies are giving away a layer of infrastructure in the hope of owning what gets built on top of it.

Three things to watch from here. First, whether a real merchant count emerges for the Universal Commerce Protocol. Second, whether any outside company confirms deploying SPACE, rather than just being named as a partner. And third, already on the calendar, Google's Gemini 4 Argon switching on by default inside Gemini Enterprise on October 27th.

That's the briefing. Have a good Monday.
