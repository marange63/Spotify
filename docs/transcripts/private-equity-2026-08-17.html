Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Equity & the Deal Economy

Transcript · August 17, 2026

Good morning, it's Monday, August 17th, and this is your private equity briefing.

The deal economy is visibly reopening at the top of the market, but on terms that shift risk off leverage and away from genuine third-party cash realizations. Today's evidence: the biggest LBO ever closed on sovereign equity rather than debt, a recent IPO is already being bought back below its offer price, and a major bank is trying to shed the very leverage sponsors have been using to engineer liquidity.

Start with the record. Electronic Arts' take-private closed August 4th, and at $55 billion in enterprise value it is now the largest leveraged buyout in history, surpassing the 2007 TXU Energy deal at $32 billion. The buyer is a consortium led by Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund, alongside Silver Lake and Jared Kushner's Affinity Partners. Final ownership landed at PIF holding 93.4 percent, Silver Lake 5.5 percent, and Affinity just over 1 percent. EA is off the Nasdaq.

Here's the counterintuitive read. This is called the largest leveraged buyout ever, but it's barely leveraged by the standards of the category. Financing ran roughly $20 billion of new debt, carrying about $1.8 billion of annual interest, against roughly $36 billion of sponsor equity. That's close to two-thirds equity, one-third debt — call it a debt-to-purchase-price ratio around a third, well below where prior-cycle megadeals sat. So the size record is really a story about scarce jumbo-debt capacity and patient sovereign capital substituting for leverage, not a signal that underwriters are back to levering the biggest paper the way they did before 2022. And if this structure becomes the template, the checks for future record deals get written increasingly by sovereign and strategic capital, not by leveraged-loan desks. That reshapes who can even compete for the biggest assets: the marginal buyer of a trophy company at the top of the market is no longer a debt-levered sponsor but a patient balance sheet that doesn't need the loan market to say yes.

Now the other end of the take-private spectrum, and it cuts directly against the idea that the IPO window is reopening. Thoma Bravo agreed on August 13th to buy specialty-insurance platform Accelerant for more than $4 billion in enterprise value, all cash, at $20.25 a share. That's a 49 percent premium to where the stock closed the day before the announcement. Sounds like a rich number until you place it against the company's own history: Accelerant IPO'd at $21 a share, fell as low as $9.36 in February, and is now being bought back at $20.25 — still below its original listing price. The deal is expected to close in the first half of 2027, so it's signed, not done.

So what that tells you: the 49 percent premium is a premium to a busted stock, not to fair value. A company that only recently went public is already being taken back private near or below where it listed. If that pattern repeats with other recently-listed names, it undercuts the marketing line that public markets are reopening as a clean exit valve for private equity portfolios, and it keeps the pressure on sponsors to manufacture liquidity through channels other than a straightforward sale to public shareholders.

Which is exactly where the exit-and-distribution problem keeps showing up. This show has already covered the record run in GP-led secondaries and continuation vehicles, and the mechanics of NAV lending — so no need to rebuild that from scratch. But there's a fresh escalation worth flagging. JPMorgan has reportedly been seeking to offload risk on more than $4 billion of loans made against private equity fund assets — so-called NAV loans. The structure being discussed keeps the loans on JPMorgan's own balance sheet while outside investors would absorb part of the potential loss, a synthetic risk transfer rather than an outright sale. The NAV-loan market itself is estimated at roughly $100 billion today, with projections putting it near $350 billion by 2030. This is dated to reporting from around May, so treat it as an ongoing process rather than a completed trade, and there's no confirmed SEC enforcement action here — just reported scrutiny. But the signal matters: a sophisticated bank counterparty actively trying to reduce its exposure to fund-level leverage is a judgment call on risk, not just commentary. So what: the same NAV-loan capacity sponsors lean on to bridge the exit drought and fund LP distributions may get repriced or harder to access right when they need it most — the cost of engineered liquidity rising at exactly the wrong moment.

Last thread — who's actually getting capital in this environment, because the fundraising machine is not healing broadly, it's concentrating. The clearest fresh data point comes from KKR: the firm has now raised $305 billion against a three-year, $300 billion fundraising target, with two and a half years still left on the clock. That's a scaled platform clearing a multi-year goal early, which is a concentration signal more than a health signal. The same exit drought starving mid-market and first-time-fund managers of commitments is redirecting capital toward the handful of franchises that can actually prove distributions to their limited partners. And that concentration is structural, not a passing quarter: it thins the commitment base for the middle of the market and for first-time funds that most need it, and it is precisely the fuel behind the GP-stakes and manager-consolidation trade, as capital pools around the few platforms that can still return real cash.

The other side of that redistribution shows up at CalPERS, one of the largest and most closely watched allocators in the world. Its private equity program now sits at 19.3 percent of a $637 billion total portfolio, and it returned 17 percent for the fiscal year ended June 30th. But the more important number is the mix shift: CalPERS has cut its large-buyout exposure from 46 percent in September 2022 down to 33 percent by March of this year, while rotating toward growth and venture, partly executed through secondary-market purchases. So a bellwether pension is deliberately reducing conviction in the mega-buyout model at precisely the moment the mega-funds are raising most aggressively. That's a real divergence in view between the biggest managers and at least one of their biggest historical backers — and if other large pensions follow CalPERS' lead, the largest flagship buyout vehicles face a structurally smaller pool of their traditional LP capital even as they set fundraising records.

Pull it together and here's where the cycle stands. The deal economy is genuinely reopening at the top — record deal sizes, premium take-privates, fee-related earnings running hot — but on terms that shift risk off traditional leverage and away from clean, third-party cash realizations. Sovereign equity is substituting for debt in the biggest deals. Recently-listed companies are getting bought back near or below their offer prices instead of trading up. And the leverage that sponsors use to manufacture distributions is starting to make sophisticated bank counterparties nervous enough to try to shed it. The binding risk is still the exit-and-distribution logjam, not headline assets under management. What would actually confirm this cycle is turning is not another record fee print or another fundraising close — it's a run of clean, third-party exits that put real cash back into limited partners' hands.

That's the briefing for today. Have a good Monday.
