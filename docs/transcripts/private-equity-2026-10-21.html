Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Equity & the Deal Economy

Transcript · October 21, 2026

Good morning. It's Wednesday, October 21st, and this is your private equity briefing.

For the better part of a week, this show has been counting deals in one direction only. Capital has gone into new take-privates, and nothing signed has come out the other side to a genuine third-party buyer. That pattern just got its first real test, and the test failed. Blackstone-backed AirTrunk has reportedly postponed its Singapore data-center REIT listing to 2027.

Start with the asset. AirTrunk is the Asia-Pacific data-center platform Blackstone bought with Canada Pension Plan Investments, with Blackstone as majority owner. CPP's own announcement valued that deal at an implied enterprise value of over twenty-four billion Australian dollars. At the time, the business ran eight hundred megawatts across data centers in Australia, Hong Kong, Japan, Malaysia, and Singapore. It also had land banked for more than a gigawatt of future growth.

Now the deal that didn't happen. The REIT was a partial listing of that platform, not a sale of Blackstone's whole position. The Wall Street Journal reported it could have raised about one and a half billion US dollars, and other reporting put the REIT's expected valuation near fifteen billion. To get it done, the offered yield was raised from five and a half percent to around six percent. Demand didn't move. So the listing was pulled, and the relaunch now sits somewhere in 2027, with outlets disagreeing on the quarter.

Two cautions before the interpretation. First, there's no official Blackstone or AirTrunk statement on any of this, and AirTrunk didn't respond to requests for comment. Treat it as reported, not confirmed. Second, resist the temptation to set that fifteen billion against the twenty-four billion Australian dollar entry price. Nobody has disclosed which facilities the REIT would have held, or what stake Blackstone would have kept. So the two numbers can't be compared, and this is not evidence of a markdown on the whole position.

So what does the postponement mean? There are two honest readings, and I won't collapse them into one. The bearish reading is a price gap. Public investors wanted more yield than Blackstone would give, even after the concession. That means they valued these AI-linked data-center cash flows below what the sponsor was willing to accept. The benign reading is timing. The Journal's report ties the delay to Asian equities sliding to more than two-month lows. That's a bad week to price a REIT, and Blackstone isn't a forced seller, so it can wait for a better tape. The reporting doesn't settle which reading dominates, and neither can I.

What I can tell you is the mechanism, because it works the same way under either reading. A postponed listing means no transaction. No transaction means no markdown. The mark stays exactly where it was. That's how private marks stay sticky through a rough quarter. It isn't because they've been proven right. It's because nothing forced a repricing.

For limited partners, the cost shows up in cash, not in value. Distributions tied to this monetization now slide into 2027 at the earliest. And this is one of the marquee AI-infrastructure positions the industry has leaned on to justify the asset class. Paper value holds, and the cash defers. The plain takeaway: a pulled IPO protects net asset value and costs distributions.

One footnote, named and then set aside. Firmus Technologies, a separate Australian AI-infrastructure company, formally withdrew its listing on October 9th after investors balked at the valuation. It's tempting to pair Firmus with AirTrunk as a second sponsor-exit failure. But reporting describes Firmus as Nvidia- and venture-backed, not a private equity exit. So it tells you how skeptical public markets are of AI-infrastructure equity in general. It's not a second data point on sponsors specifically.

So is the exit window actually shut? Not by the headline count. US issuers have priced a hundred and fourteen IPOs in 2026 through mid-October, raising a hundred and forty-seven point six billion dollars. That's already well above the total for all of 2025. Note that it's every US issuer, not just sponsor-backed deals. Morgan Stanley's research says sponsor-backed listings have run at roughly a third of US IPOs in recent years. That's a multi-year average, though, not a 2026 figure. Still, by volume, the window is plainly open.

What AirTrunk shows is that the window is selective on price. It's most selective where the asset is capital-intensive and rate-sensitive. That describes a lot of where private equity's AI-infrastructure exposure sits. The gap between "open by count" and "open for the specific asset you're trying to sell" is the real story. It means the headline tally overstates how open the route is for the long tail of aged sponsor holdings.

The same repricing has been showing up all week on the debt side, where the cost of capital for AI infrastructure keeps rising. AirTrunk is the equity-exit face of that same move. Buyers on both sides of the capital structure want more compensation to own these cash flows. That's the shared signal, and it points the same way.

Now, if the listed route won't clear on a sponsor's terms, the industry's other engineered exit is the retail wrapper. These are the semi-liquid, evergreen vehicles built to bring wealth and 401(k) money into private equity. In theory, they give sponsors another buyer for illiquid assets. That channel is showing its own strain. Partners Group disclosed in June that its Global Value SICAV evergreen private equity fund saw redemption requests of about nine point eight percent of net asset value for the second-quarter redemption period.

That's June data, not new, and no third-quarter figure has surfaced. It's also a request figure, not a payout figure. We don't know whether Partners Group met those requests in full, pro-rated them, or gated them. And one fund's quarterly request level is not a run. But the signal still counts. When investors ask for close to a tenth of a fund back in a single quarter, the wrapper is carrying the same liquidity mismatch as every other vehicle built around illiquid assets. It's another channel with its own liquidity limits, not a free exit.

So where does that leave the cycle? Deployment is visible and real, and sponsors are still signing large take-privates. Distributions keep flowing through the engineered channels: continuation vehicles, take-privates that need insider consent, and dividend recaps. But the one arm's-length, public-market exit attempt we've watched in real time did not clear on the sponsor's terms. And there is still no signed sale of a sponsor-held company to a genuine third-party buyer in view. The biggest unresolved risk isn't headline assets under management, or dry powder. It's that marks can keep holding simply because sponsors can afford to wait rather than transact.

The catalysts that would confirm or refute this read are close. Blackstone is expected to report this week, as this show has flagged, though the exact date is still unconfirmed. Ares follows on October 29th, then Apollo on November 3rd and KKR on November 9th. These are catalysts, not results. None of these managers has posted third-quarter numbers yet. When those prints land, the question isn't whether fee-related earnings grew. It's whether the realizations came from genuine third-party or public-market sales, or from the engineered channels that never test the mark.

And watch AirTrunk's relaunch terms in 2027. Whatever yield finally clears will be the cleanest read we get on whether AI infrastructure's private marks and public clearing prices have actually converged.

That's the briefing for today. Stay cautiously optimistic.
