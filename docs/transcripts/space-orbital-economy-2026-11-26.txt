Good morning. It's Thursday, November 26th, 2026, and this is your space and orbital economy briefing.

This week SpaceX's value moved off the launch pad and onto paper. The company won a federal license for a constellation nobody has built. The same day, its stock fell on a possible merger with Tesla that has nothing to do with rockets. Only one of those two things actually got priced. Let's start with the license.

On Wednesday, November 25th, the FCC gave SpaceX conditional approval for an orbital AI data-center constellation called Starmind. TechCrunch reports it covers up to one million satellites at altitudes between 500 and 1,002 kilometers. A legal analysis from Diplomacy and Law identifies the grant as FCC Order DA 26-1273. That analysis is explicit that the order sets a regulatory ceiling, not an operational constellation of a million spacecraft.

On Sunday, this desk called orbital compute a research line, not a business. Three days later, it has a license. It is still not a business. The clocks attached to the grant tell you why. SpaceX has seven years to get its first operational satellite into orbit, so deployment must begin by November 2033. It then has until November 2035 to reach 100,000 satellites. The full constellation is due by November 2040, fourteen years out, according to Ars Technica. A regulator that allows seven years to fly a single satellite is not expecting capacity any time soon.

Those milestones also imply a cadence nobody has demonstrated. Here's our arithmetic. If SpaceX uses the full window and starts in late 2033, it needs that hundred thousand in roughly two years. That's on the order of 50,000 satellites a year. For comparison, the Space Atlas tracker counted about 11,200 Starlink satellites in orbit as of November 22nd. So the 2035 milestone alone asks for more than four times Starlink's entire current fleet, every year. Launch cadence, not just licensing, sits underneath every one of those dates.

Now, what's still open matters more than the headline number. TechCrunch reports the FCC has not yet approved SpaceX's orbital debris plans for satellites operating above 600 kilometers. SpaceX's requested waiver of the five-year reentry rule is also unresolved. The legal analysis adds that the FCC reportedly deferred the requests on alternative disposal methods above 600 kilometers. Deferred, not denied. By our arithmetic, that upper band covers about four-fifths of the licensed altitude range. So the agency has effectively split this filing into a granted low band and an open high band. That later ruling, not Wednesday's headline, decides whether Starmind is buildable at the scale the FCC just authorized.

The engineering record is even thinner. The reporting contains no disclosed figure for Starmind's per-satellite power, compute, cooling, capital cost, or customers. None. Some outlets have circulated numbers, but they conflict with each other, and none traces to a SpaceX filing. Treat any you've seen as unverified. What the order does confirm is the basic architecture. The primary links are optical, laser links between satellites, with radio-frequency links as backup. That is a communications design, not a business plan.

Now look at who objected: Amazon, Viasat, and the American Astronomical Society. Amazon called the application "a lofty ambition rather than a real plan" and said it "risks real harm." Weigh that as a competitor's line in a licensing fight, not as neutral analysis. The astronomers' objection was more technical. They flagged interference at infrared wavelengths from heat radiated by the satellites' computing equipment. That's a pointed objection, because waste heat is exactly the thermal problem nobody has disclosed a solution for. The FCC approved it anyway. Per CNBC, it dismissed environmental concerns as outside its authority, along with the concerns about optical astronomy. We'd expect the infrared question to resurface in future orbital-compute filings.

Set that beside what we reported on Tuesday. The FCC's June waiver for Amazon Leo kept Amazon's license alive. But any satellite Amazon launches after its July deadline loses its seniority in the FCC's priority order. Now the same regulator has licensed SpaceX's million-satellite filing over Amazon's formal objection. That's our inference about competitive positioning, not a finding that the FCC favors SpaceX. Regulatory standing keeps accruing to whoever is already flying.

The bull case for Wednesday's grant is that the real value lies in locking in orbital shells and spectrum position, years before any rival's filing catches up. There's a precedent effect too, and that's also our inference. Other megaconstellation applicants now have a template to cite: a conditional grant for enormous scale, ahead of demonstrated feasibility. So bank the grant as a fourteen-year option on orbital compute, not as capacity.

The market didn't pay for that option on Wednesday. It was busy repricing SpaceX's share count. According to Twenty-Four Seven Wall Street, Tesla's board has formed special committees to review a possible combination with SpaceX. Elon Musk has stepped back from those talks because of a conflict of interest. That describes a process, not a deal. No merger agreement exists. Still, SpaceX shares fell about 7.5 percent on November 25th, to 116 dollars and 32 cents.

The selling came alongside dilution arithmetic from analyst Gary Black. In his scenario, SpaceX is the buyer, and it pays Tesla holders in new SpaceX shares. He estimated that a 25 percent premium for Tesla holders would force SpaceX to issue 52 percent more shares. That's one analyst's scenario, not deal terms. But if a combination ever proceeds on terms like those, SpaceX holders face heavy issuance.

The selling didn't stop at SpaceX, and that breaks a pattern we documented on Sunday. Last week SpaceX was the listed space name that barely moved while the pure plays sold off. This week, per Twenty-Four Seven Wall Street, Rocket Lab and AST SpaceMobile sold off alongside SpaceX, on SpaceX news. Reported single-day moves for the two vary by source, so we won't put a number on them. The direction is the point. Neither company's business has anything to do with a Tesla deal.

There are three ways to read the sell-off. The first is dilution math, but that only touches SpaceX holders. The second is a governance discount. A related-party deal between two companies with the same controlling founder invites one. But that discount also attaches to SpaceX alone. The third is correlation, meaning the public space trade is still priced as a single factor. The co-movement suggests the third, because neither of the first two reaches Rocket Lab or AST SpaceMobile. One caveat. This is a few sessions of price action, not a settled regime. But if it holds, the listed space complex is trading on a company none of the pure plays control.

That has a cost, by our read. On Sunday we argued that this kind of trading raises the cost of capital for cash-hungry space names. Now that cost tracks SpaceX's headlines, not their own execution. The Tesla process also leaves the long-rumored Starlink spin-out an open question. A combination with Tesla points toward a conglomerate structure. A clean Starlink listing points the other way. Nothing on the record today tells you which wins. For holders, the takeaway is that SpaceX is starting to behave like a factor in this sector, not just a constituent.

If paper value is this volatile, government money is supposed to be the floor under the sector. According to SpacePolicyOnline, on November 23rd the White House asked Congress for a 5 billion dollar Golden Dome anomaly in the next continuing resolution. That's a request, not enacted funding. It sits beside a plan to route almost all of the 17.5 billion dollar fiscal 2027 request through a third reconciliation bill, rather than appropriations. On Sunday we called the gap to that full request a bet on a funding vehicle. The White House has now placed a side bet on the stopgap. So the floor is real. But it now runs on a stopgap's timetable and a reconciliation bet, not on a program's milestones.

So where does that leave things? What's genuinely inflecting is regulatory standing and orbital-planning position, and both are accruing to SpaceX. Government demand for missile defense is real too, even if its timing is hostage to Congress. Everything else is still paper. There's no Starmind hardware, no disclosed power or cost figures, and no signed Tesla terms. The biggest open question is whether the debris ruling lets Starmind use the upper four-fifths of its licensed band.

Four catalysts are worth watching. First, the FCC's ruling on the debris plan above 600 kilometers and on the deorbit waiver. Second, any signed terms or a committee recommendation from Tesla's board. Third, whether Congress grants the 5 billion dollar Golden Dome anomaly. And fourth, December 14th, when final proposals are due in NASA's commercial space-station competition, as we flagged on Tuesday.

That's the briefing. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
