Good morning, it's Tuesday, August 25th.

For four briefings running, this program has asked whether Treasury's bond buyback was real duration removal or just a signal. For three, it called oil the one asset still rising in a straight line. Monday answered both questions. Both answers point at plumbing rather than fundamentals. That's the thesis today. Gold, the dollar, the front end and crypto are no longer separate trades. They are one dollar-liquidity trade. And Friday is the only scheduled event that can reprice it.

Start with the lead. Treasury has escalated its buyback program in stages. First it said it would at least double the maximum size of its operations in long-dated debt, from two billion dollars to at least four billion. That was CNBC's reporting last Wednesday. Then Secretary Bessent declined to cap it there, telling reporters the size "could be more than the four billion per issue." Now CNBC reports Treasury may fund the expanded buybacks not by issuing new bills, but by drawing on its General Account, the cash balance it keeps at the Fed. That balance stood at 935 billion dollars last Thursday. Treat this as reported and under consideration, not as a decision.

The funding choice matters more than the headline size. New bill supply drains cash out of money markets. It shuffles duration along the curve without adding anything. Drawing down a cash balance that already exists does the opposite. It puts cash into the banking system. That's the difference between rearranging the furniture and adding a piece of it. And the easing impulse would land in bank reserves and in repo rather than in the bill market. That's a liquidity channel sitting entirely outside the Fed's balance sheet, arriving into the September and October window where reserve scarcity is already being argued about. But be precise about what we don't know. Officials would not say how much of that balance they would actually draw. There's no way to size the reserve impulse from anything reported so far. Treat the mechanism as real and the magnitude as undisclosed.

The market's own verdict tells you to be skeptical about how much this is working. The first relief rally reversed inside a day. The 30-year yield jumped intraday to 5.25 percent last Thursday. Then Monday's General Account report pushed the 10-year yield down four basis points, with the 30-year falling by the same amount. That's a smaller move than the reversal it's answering. Bessent's own line on the whipsaw was that "everything that happens within 24 hours is just noise." That's a Treasury Secretary conceding his intervention is not yet moving the level.

And nothing has actually been bought under the expanded plan. The first larger operation doesn't begin until September 9th, so every basis point so far is a reaction to an announcement rather than to a bid. One more caution, on the risk framing. The case that spending down the cash balance is low-risk rests on the next debt-limit constraint being far away. The sourced basis for that is not Treasury. It's an independent projection from the Bipartisan Policy Center, which puts the next binding constraint somewhere between late winter and mid-summer of 2027. Spending a buffer front-loads liquidity now and thins the cushion later. So what: the long end is now trading as much on Treasury's cash-management choices as on the Fed, and the market is pricing those choices as noise-prone rather than as a structural fix.

The same Treasury Department did something else on Monday, and it landed on a market that had been waiting on it for a week. For three sessions this program called the Iran sanctions rhetoric rather than a documented event. On Monday the event arrived. Roughly 60 Iran-linked entities, individuals and vessels were designated, alongside five sectoral sanctions determinations. That detail comes from TRM Labs' analysis of the action rather than from the Treasury release itself. CNBC reports the package carries no exemption for China. Crude fell anyway. Brent lost 2.5 percent on the day, per CNBC's market reporting.

Two readings compete here, and one session doesn't settle them. Either the announcement was fully priced during the prior week's run-up, and Monday was profit-taking. Or the market has judged that enforcement won't actually keep Iranian barrels off the water. The second reading is the latent risk nobody is positioned for, precisely because the news that should have caused a spike didn't. So what: the inflation trigger this program has been watching just got materially weaker. That puts more weight on the liquidity story, not less.

Buried in that same sanctions package is the first thing of its kind, and the asset it targets rallied anyway. Treasury's determination named digital assets a sanctionable sector for the first time. That's a structural expansion of compliance exposure for exchanges and stablecoin issuers, well beyond Iran-linked flows, and the precedent is reusable against other sanctioned jurisdictions. It's a permanent margin item sitting inside an asset class currently being priced purely off rates. The market assigned it no price at all. Bitcoin was up roughly 24 percent on the week, and ether up 31. To be clear, the designation and the rally happened in the same session. I'm not claiming either one moved the other.

CoinShares' James Butterfill supplied outside corroboration for a read this program advanced on its own. Short-dated yields have fallen, he said, which he called a clear signal that bond investors no longer expect further Federal Reserve rate hikes. His summary was that this is "a macro story, not a crypto one." The flows fit that reading. Spot bitcoin ETFs took in roughly 1.6 billion dollars over four days last week. That figure comes from flow aggregators rather than a single issuer's own dataset, so hold the total loosely.

But state the correction plainly, because this program reported bitcoin's strongest week in nearly two years without it. Bitcoin is still down 32.6 percent from a year ago. Ether is down 48.4 percent. A bounce inside a drawdown is a different object than a bull market resuming, and it implies different buyers. Money arriving after a decline that steep, on a rates repricing, is allocator re-entry on a macro view. That's the most reversible capital on the board. It leaves on the same view it arrived on.

Which makes the front end the only thing any of this is actually trading. So look at what the front end is looking at. The most recent labor read is July's jobs report, and it was ugly. Payrolls fell 23,000 against a Wall Street consensus for a gain of 83,000. May and June were revised down by a combined 103,000. The unemployment rate improved, but only because labor-force participation fell to its lowest level in more than five years. An unemployment rate falling for that reason is not a strong labor market.

That sits directly against the strongest business-activity survey in four years, which this program covered on Sunday. Now layer on the constraint inside the Fed itself. The July meeting produced the most policymakers dissenting with a unified view since September 2016, and that bloc wanted tighter policy, not easier. It raises the bar for how dovish anyone can credibly sound. The dollar has been sliding through all of it. The index sat near 98.7 on Monday, its weakest since mid-May. Gold's rally this month has moved as something close to the dollar's mirror image.

All of that resolves, or doesn't, on one scheduled event. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh delivers his first Jackson Hole keynote on Friday, August 28th. It has not happened yet. Nothing about its tone or content should be assumed, and this program won't guess at it. But name what is riding on it. Bonds first, because a dovish surprise accelerates the dollar's slide and steepens the curve further. Then gold and the debasement trade, which is the most crowded position on the list and the one likely to reverse hardest on a hawkish surprise. Then crypto, which is currently pricing as a rates proxy rather than on its own news. And finally the labor-versus-survey growth question this program has been running all week. This is the last briefing before that speech. We'll tell you what it actually said, not what we expected it to say.

That's the radar for August 25th. Trade carefully into Friday.
