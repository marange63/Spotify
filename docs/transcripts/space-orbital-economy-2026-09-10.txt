Good morning. It's Thursday, September 10th, and this is your space and orbital economy briefing.

All week this show has described launch scarcity as somebody's commercial problem — a scrubbed rocket, a slot in someone else's queue, a rival's manifest. Today it comes with a national-security number attached.

Start with the number, and with where it comes from. The Space Force's National Security Space Launch program is described in trade reporting this week as a projected seventy-six billion dollar effort covering 337 missions. That framing reaches us through a trade outlet summarizing the program, not through a Space Force release we could read directly, so treat the scale as reported rather than as official. What isn't in dispute is why the program exists. It was built over three decades and multiple rounds of competition for one explicit reason: to make sure the United States never depends on a single rocket to reach orbit. As of this week, two of its three certified providers cannot fly.

Take them in turn. ULA's Vulcan Centaur has been grounded from national-security missions since a February booster anomaly. The only on-the-record government language is conditional. Space Force Colonel Eric Zarybnisky said, in his words, "until this anomaly is solved we will not be launching Vulcan missions." That is a statement about an unfinished investigation, not a date. ULA has since said more, after a successful solid-rocket-booster retest, telling reporters it expects Vulcan back in flight by the end of the year. But that is a general company target rather than a scheduled return. Its likely first flight is a commercial Amazon mission, not an NSSL one. And nobody on the government side has put a date on Vulcan's return to national-security launches specifically.

Blue Origin is worse off. Its only New Glenn pad, Launch Complex 36, was heavily damaged in a static-fire explosion in May. CEO Dave Limp has given a specific damage inventory. The support tower needs repair but not replacement, and the propellant and cryogenic tanks came through fine, but the lightning tower and the transporter-erector were destroyed. Blue Origin's own target is flying again before year-end. NASA administrator Jared Isaacman separately floated 2028 as plausible for the pad, then clarified on social media that he was talking about Artemis moon-base mission timing, not the pad repair. So that number shouldn't be read as an independent estimate of when New Glenn flies again. Net effect either way: no binding date exists for either grounded vehicle.

That leaves Falcon 9 carrying essentially the entire national-security manifest. And it's worth being precise about a related fact. SpaceX swept all eighteen Space Based Space Test missions awarded this summer, worth roughly one and a six-tenths billion dollars according to trade reporting. It would be easy to call that a default outcome given the circumstances. The more accurate, and more uncomfortable, read is that those launches ran through NSSL's Phase 3 Lane 1 — a lane structured for open competition among commercial providers. SpaceX didn't inherit them by attrition. It won them as the successful bidder, with the field open. The market chose Falcon 9 anyway. Note the sourcing there too: the award figures come from several trade outlets reporting the same contract, not from a government notice we could read.

So resist the obvious framing. This is not America losing assured access to orbit. By any measure of demonstrated cadence, Falcon 9 alone is flying more missions, more reliably, than the redundant fleet NSSL was designed around ever managed. The risk isn't capability, it's correlation. One vehicle means one grounding order, one open anomaly investigation, one company's manifest priorities setting the pace for the Pentagon's missile-warning and Golden Dome tracking-layer buildout. That concentration also hands SpaceX pricing and scheduling leverage in a market where, right now, the buyer with the most purchasing power on Earth has essentially none. Which sets up the procurement consequence. The rational response to a redundancy failure is to buy redundancy at almost any price, because the thing being purchased is independence rather than cost per kilogram. That changes what a second medium-lift vehicle is worth, independent of what it charges.

So if the Pentagon wants a second rocket, what's actually on offer, and what does it cost. Rocket Lab is the clearest near-term answer. The company has now published its own price for Neutron, its medium-lift vehicle: fifty to fifty-five million dollars average selling price, with an explicit commitment not to discount early launches. That's worth flagging against where this show stood a few days ago. On Sunday we used roughly fifty million as an analyst's working estimate, and said so, because Rocket Lab had published no price list at all. Now it has. A company that refuses to discount a rocket that hasn't flown yet is a company that has read the same redundancy math the Pentagon just discovered it needs. Meanwhile the underlying business keeps compounding regardless of Neutron's timeline. Rocket Lab flew its 94th Electron mission on September 2nd — evidence the company can fund the wait.

Be careful with the schedule, though. Management's guidance is that Neutron hardware reaches the pad in the fourth quarter, which starts three weeks from now. That is a hardware milestone, not a launch date, and Rocket Lab has not given a maiden-flight date. And the certification trap doesn't go away just because a price list exists. National-security launch certification runs on demonstrated flight heritage, which cannot be bought forward. A vehicle that hasn't left the pad supplies none of it. So even a clean debut opens a multi-year road to actual redundancy, not an immediate one. The Space Force's problem is real, and the nearest fix to it is still years away no matter how well this goes.

This shortage isn't a purely American condition either. The other side of the orbital race is drawing on a constrained launch market too. China's Qianfan constellation, also known as SpaceSail, sits at roughly 238 satellites on orbit as of early September, per independent tracking. On Sunday this show put it near 218 as of its July deployment group. That comparison is our own arithmetic across two different sources, not a like-for-like series, so hold it loosely. Against a state-approved total of 15,000 satellites, though, that pace is nowhere close to what the buildout implies. It's the same pattern this show named yesterday: deadlines set as a multiple of a demonstrated rate rather than a plausible one. The gap between announced capacity and deployed reality is the story on both sides of the Pacific.

New demand keeps piling onto that same narrowed supply. Starcloud, a startup building orbital data centers around Nvidia silicon, just closed a 250 million dollar raise at a 2.3 billion dollar valuation, with Nvidia named among the new investors in the announcement. That's real capital. But treat the valuation as a price paid for an option, not evidence of a working business. There's no disclosed revenue and no named paying customer, and the binding constraints on space-based compute are thermal rejection and power generation at scale, not chip density. An Nvidia customer buying Nvidia chips isn't independent validation. What it does confirm is the pattern: the marginal claim on launch capacity keeps rising while the number of credible ways to satisfy it keeps shrinking.

Pulling it together. The genuine inflection this week is structural, not cyclical. The redundancy the Pentagon paid three decades to build has narrowed to one company, by accident and by competitive outcome simultaneously. What's still a demo is everything downstream that assumes cheap, abundant launch keeps arriving on schedule — orbital data centers, megaconstellation buildouts on both sides of the Pacific. The testable catalysts are three. A dated Vulcan return-to-flight commitment specifically for national-security missions. A dated New Glenn return from Blue Origin. And Neutron hardware physically reaching the pad before the fourth quarter closes. One more thing worth naming precisely: SpaceX has filed to target September 15th for a Starship flight, but that date is unconfirmed, and this show isn't characterizing what that flight will or won't achieve until it happens.

That's the briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
