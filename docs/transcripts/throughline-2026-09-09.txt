Good morning, this is The Throughline for Wednesday, September 9th, 2026. Today's headline: the Treasury tried to talk long-end yields down with a bond buyback, and yields went the other way. That landed on the same day oil broke back above a hundred dollars, with the strategic petroleum reserve at its lowest level since 1982.

Start with the macro backdrop, where the export-control regime just broke open in public. Strategic Power has New York Times reporting on Aivres, a US server maker one-third owned by the blacklisted Chinese firm Inspur. The Times says Aivres shipped at least five point six billion dollars of advanced technology into Southeast Asia, including Nvidia Blackwell servers whose compute ultimately served Chinese customers. The mechanism is a drafting choice, not an enforcement failure: the Entity List ownership trigger sits at fifty percent, and Aivres sits at thirty-three. That surfaces fifteen days before Xi Jinping is due at the White House.

Capital Markets Radar has the price action behind that headline. A six-billion-dollar Treasury buyback of ten- to twenty-year debt sent the ten-year yield as high as 4.85 percent, instead of down. Six billion is less than a thousandth of the debt rolling over by year-end, so the desk reads that as the market pricing the signal rather than the purchase. Brent's break above a hundred dollars extends a twenty-five percent run since early August, and the reserve has no cushion left to absorb it. Both tighteners land on a Fed already in blackout, with only tomorrow's producer prices and Friday's consumer prices left to move the odds before the 16th.

The same rate story runs straight into crypto. Digital Money has the spot Bitcoin ETF complex still about a billion dollars short of breaking even for the year. That is after its best month of 2026 and a three-week inflow streak, which makes ETF flow reactive rather than a floor. Bitcoin itself fell toward seventy-nine thousand on last week's payrolls beat and stayed capped below eighty thousand, failing a second live test as a hedge.

Private Credit and the Corporate Credit Cycle has the leverage side of that same rate picture. Banks refinanced deals out of private credit and back into the syndicated market by roughly three to one last quarter. That is an inference, not something the data shows. The read is that the borrowers who can leave are the strongest ones. So the book left behind is being selected for weakness rather than simply deteriorating.

Private Equity and the Deal Economy has the year's first real cash exit, and a catch. KKR agreed to sell USI Insurance to Aon for seventeen billion dollars, a three-point-four-times return that is signed rather than settled. The reporting places USI on KKR's own balance sheet, and nothing available describes any of it reaching a fund's limited partners. So the marquee realization may not touch the liquidity problem it appears to solve.

Now the AI and compute stack, where today's currency is equity rather than purchase orders. Semiconductors and Compute Infrastructure has the deal itself. Qualcomm handed Amazon roughly four billion dollars of warrants to win a multi-generation AWS inference-silicon deal. Those warrants vest against up to sixty billion dollars of future purchases. Semiconductors calls that sixty billion a ceiling, not a backlog, and reads the warrant as what a challenger pays to enter an incumbent's account.

Frontier AI Lab Competition is on the Washington side of the same stack. The NSA, CISA and the FBI have jointly named six Chinese labs, including DeepSeek and Alibaba, as running industrial-scale distillation against US models. That is the agencies' assessment, published without evidence anyone outside them can check.

Still with Frontier AI Labs, Anthropic's stock-market listing has slipped from early September to mid-October. The Pentagon says its supply-chain-risk designation against Anthropic remains in force, contradicting Commerce's earlier suggestion that the relationship had thawed. So whenever that prospectus lands, a live federal fight will be one of the first things public investors can read about a frontier lab.

The same access fight shows up in prices. AI Products and Applications has Google putting AI song generation into its consumer app worldwide at no cost, though whether the full-length output is free is still undisclosed. At the other end, OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra priced at two and a half times the prior generation's rate, while Anthropic quietly let its own promotional price expire without raising it. Enterprise Technology has the scale version: Broadcom's AI semiconductor revenue hit sixteen point seven billion dollars, up two hundred twenty-one percent, and is now more than half the company. That is the threshold where an AI line stops being an attach number and becomes the business.

Into the applied frontier. Robotics and Physical AI has the People's Liberation Army newspaper calling for humanoid robots on military training grounds. Reuters reviewed more than a hundred Chinese procurement documents and found no named program, no unit count and no budget line, so read it as signaling. The fielded, paying capability in this sector is still software, with L3Harris licensing Shield AI's counter-drone perception stack onto a platform already proven in combat. Space and the Orbital Economy finally has Amazon's own count to work from: Kuiper has deployed 396 satellites, a realized pace of about 28 a month. Its FCC deadline requires roughly 94 a month from here, and the rocket its manifest leans on, Blue Origin's New Glenn, is still grounded with no dated return to flight.

One thread to carry into tomorrow. Yesterday this show flagged Mistral taking Samsung on as an equity investor, and called it a genuinely new funding template. Today the same trade ran the other way, with Qualcomm paying Amazon in warrants to win a design slot. Two days running, the largest deals in AI infrastructure were settled on cap tables rather than in purchase orders. Access to compute is being priced in equity now, and that is worth watching for a third day.
