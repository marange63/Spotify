Good morning, it's Friday, November 6th, and this is your Capital Markets Radar.

Here's the thesis up front. Thursday turned a contained AI derating into a market-wide degrossing. It also moved the one thing that had refused to budge all week, the pricing of a December Fed hike. But the Fed put is still far out of the money. So whether Thursday was positioning or a real repricing of the AI buildout will be settled in credit and private lending, not in the equity tape.

Start with the tape, because the scale matters. The S&P 500 fell 4.36 percent on Thursday, its worst session since April of 2025, per CNBC. The Nasdaq Composite closed more than ten percent below last month's record, which puts it in a correction. The iShares Semiconductor ETF fell more than 12 percent, its worst day since March of 2020. And the VIX briefly touched 28, its highest reading since the end of March.

That breaks this desk's own framing. On Wednesday we called the drawdown concentrated, not systemic, with the S&P only about three point six percent off its record. That read did not survive Thursday. We also had the direction of the positioning risk wrong. On Monday, and again yesterday, this desk argued that crowded hedges loaded the upside with squeeze risk. The unwind came through crowded longs instead. So what: this is no longer a sector story, and the positioning risk we flagged broke the other way.

Now the mechanism, because two competing stories fit the facts. The first is positioning. Nomura's Charlie McElligott called it an institutional shakeout. In his words, "They're shooting the generals." His read is that hedge funds are locking in 2026 gains by selling their most crowded longs, not reacting to a fundamental break.

The second story is build-out delay. Coverage of the selloff tied it to the Democrats' sweeping midterm victory and the divided government that follows. The market's reading is that this raises the risk that AI data-center construction slows. UBS strategists, via Reuters, put a number on it. They estimate that around 30 percent of expected 2028 US data-center capacity additions could be at risk from potential construction pauses. UBS itself names only those pauses, not a specific law or ruling. So treat delay as a real but still vague overhang, not the confirmed cause of Thursday's drop.

The dispersion inside the selloff is the best arbiter between those two stories. Micron, AMD and Marvell each fell between 16 and 17.5 percent. Nvidia fell only about half as much. AppLovin, an application-layer name, jumped 23 percent in Thursday's premarket after a third-quarter beat. And the same crowded memory trade unwound abroad, with SK hynix down 9 percent in Seoul. That pattern looks like funds selling what they own most of, wherever it trades.

Timing points the same way. The UBS estimate concerns capacity two years out. On our read, Thursday's selling hit chip names on their near-term order books. The UBS estimate doesn't touch those. So positioning best explains the size of Thursday's move. Build-out delay may still set the medium-term direction.

One more structural change sits apart from the hedge-fund story. Vanda Research says retail investors sold a net 358 million dollars of US stocks, with, in its words, "no signs of buying the dip." If the retail dip-buyer has stepped back, the next leg down has fewer natural buyers underneath it.

A shock that size finally reached rate expectations. CME FedWatch, via CNBC, now puts the odds of a December hike at 70.1 percent, down from 82.7 the day before. The ten-year Treasury yield fell six basis points to 4.829 percent, per FXStreet. Gold traded above 4,100 dollars an ounce while the dollar index sat essentially flat. Once again, gold is trading on rates rather than as a haven.

But don't read this as the Fed riding to the rescue. Start with a correction to our own inference. Yesterday this desk suggested that easier financial conditions might reinforce the hawks. Thursday did the opposite. An equity crash and a volatility spike tighten financial conditions. Only the drop in long yields loosens them. On the chair's own financial-conditions lens, that weakens the case for a hike.

And yet the market still prices roughly a seven-in-ten chance of one. It does so with core PCE inflation running at 3.1 percent year over year. That gap is the measure of how far out of the money the Fed put sits. The worst equity day in a year and a half barely dented it.

There's a portfolio point underneath that. A growth scare that pulls hike odds and long yields lower, while core inflation is still well above target, is stagflation-adjacent. It is not a disinflationary easing setup. That makes duration a less reliable hedge for an AI-heavy equity book than it would be in a cutting cycle. October payrolls print at 8:30 Eastern this morning, and October CPI follows on November 10th. Neither has been released, so read that 70 percent as pricing going into the data, not a verdict. So what: the market now needs the data to justify a hike it had been pricing on financial conditions.

If the Fed isn't the backstop, the next question is who is actually holding the risk. Morgan Stanley projects that private credit will supply an additional 800 billion dollars of data-center financing over the next two years. That projection comes relayed through a Quinn Emanuel client alert, so it's a forecast, not a disclosed pipeline. The same alert notes that these lenders face no bank-style capital requirements, stress testing, or ongoing supervision.

The calmer view comes from Moody's. Speaking to CNBC on November 2nd, before Thursday's rout, Moody's Marc Pinto said he sees no near-term systemic risk. His argument is that private credit is still small next to the 80 trillion dollars of debt Moody's rates. But that's a size argument, not a risk argument. Concentration risk is about who holds the exposure, not its share of all rated debt. And notice what CNBC's own summary of the interview concedes. Pinto was explaining why what CNBC calls record credit default swaps are not a red flag for AI data-center debt. So hedging demand on this debt is running at records even as the rating agency waves it off.

Here's our inference on how that plays out. If the build-out delay risk to 2028 capacity is real, it lands in this less liquid, less transparent private book, not in public equity. And it won't reprice smoothly. Private marks lag public prices and tend to reprice in lumps. Public BDC shares can gap on equity contagion even while the underlying loans keep performing. We have no November BDC disclosures showing that marks have moved yet. The confirming signal would be public BDC shares sliding further below their reported net asset values while loan performance holds. So what: private credit is where the equity shock is most likely to land next, and it would show up there late and all at once.

The fastest-moving version of that same risk-budget story is crypto. Yesterday this desk said bitcoin's one-session break from the AI trade was "not a regime." That caution looks right. Bitcoin fell about 5.8 percent over the past day to roughly 75,800 dollars, giving back its climb from earlier in the week. The ETF inflows that would have signaled a turn lasted exactly one day. Spot bitcoin ETFs took in 74.6 million dollars on Wednesday. Then, by one count citing SoSoValue, they lost about 430 million on Thursday, led by Fidelity's and BlackRock's funds.

Here's the cleaner test. The dollar index was flat on Thursday, so bitcoin's fall can't be a dollar story. It traded with the same risk budget as semiconductors. There is one counterpoint. By one outlet's 24-hour count, crypto liquidations reached 1.25 billion dollars, with longs making up nearly 89 percent of the total. So part of this move is forced deleveraging, not fresh selling. That's the same mechanical-versus-fundamental split running through the equity tape. So what: the pitch that bitcoin diversifies an AI-heavy book failed its first real risk-off day.

So heading into the weekend, three things to watch. First, this morning's payrolls against that roughly 70 percent hike pricing, with CPI following next Tuesday. Second, whether credit confirms or fades what equities just did. We don't yet have a dated read on investment-grade or high-yield spreads for Thursday's session. The nearest marker predates the rout. Meta's bond priced on November 3rd, and its 2056 tranche came about 90 basis points wider than a comparable Meta note from April. That says the price of AI duration was rising before Thursday hit. Third, whether bitcoin holds the mid-70,000s with ETF flows still negative.

Positioning explained the size of Thursday. The data and the credit tape will tell us its direction. Our working assumption is that private credit, not the Fed, is now the pressure point.
