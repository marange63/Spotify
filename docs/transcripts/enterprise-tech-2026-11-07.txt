Good morning, and welcome to Cautious Optimism Briefings. Today is Saturday, November 7th, 2026.

Yesterday this show argued that the doubt running through enterprise software sits with seat-priced models, not consumption-priced ones. Atlassian's decelerating guide was the evidence. Two earnings prints that traded in the same Thursday session now put that claim to a harder test. The sharpest new signal is a pricing choice at Figma. The company is giving customers more AI usage for the same price, and its finance chief says that will dent retention before it pays off. That is the cost of bundling AI into an existing plan, and it lands in the vendor's own metrics first.

First, the print itself. Figma reported third-quarter revenue of three hundred ninety-one point four million dollars, up forty-three percent year over year, per its earnings release. It also raised its full-year revenue guide to about one and a half billion dollars, or forty-two percent growth at the midpoint. By any normal software yardstick, that is a clean beat and raise. The market did not treat it that way. Figma shares fell seven percent in extended trading on Wednesday, the night of the report. Thursday's regular session took the stock lower still, a steeper drop than the roughly ten percent, selloff-driven declines in Palantir, CrowdStrike, and Okta.

Why the harsher reaction? Start with timing. The after-hours drop came before Thursday's broad selloff, and the coverage tied it to Figma projecting slower growth in the current quarter. Figma guided the fourth quarter to four hundred two to four hundred four million dollars. Per its own release, that implies thirty-three percent growth at the midpoint. That's a ten-point deceleration from the forty-three percent Figma just posted. This is a company-specific signal, and it comes straight from the filing.

The pricing choice sits on top of the guide. On the earnings call, Figma's finance chief, Praveer Melwani, said the company repriced its AI credits in August. Each add-on now includes more credits at the same price. He acknowledged that could pressure net dollar retention in the near term, with the payoff meant to come through broader long-term usage. Retention is still strong today. Net dollar retention was one hundred thirty-one percent at the end of September. And more than eighty percent of customers above ten thousand dollars in annual recurring revenue were consuming AI credits by the end of October. So the business is healthy on absolute measures. But the forward guide is slower, and management told investors that a deliberate pricing choice would dent retention before it pays off. In plain terms, Figma is trading near-term retention for broader AI adoption.

Now back to yesterday's claim. So is seat pricing itself the problem? Figma's forty-three percent looked like a no. But Figma's own guide drops to thirty-three percent next quarter, so the slowdown is real, not just investors marking down a category. A ten-point slowdown in the guide and a flagged retention drag are enough to explain the drop on their own. That is one company, and Figma has its own reasons, chiefly the credit repricing. So investors priced Figma's trajectory, not its pricing model.

Same session, opposite reaction, and a different revenue model. Cloudflare's stock rose on a day when software stocks sold off. Its thirty-nine percent revenue growth, the fastest pace in nearly four years, was already known. What's new is the forward look. Cloudflare guided the fourth quarter to eight hundred forty-five to eight hundred forty-six million dollars. That compares with the seven hundred eighty-two point five million it just reported. By my arithmetic, that works out to roughly eight percent sequential growth. It's the first forward number from a usage-priced vendor since Snowflake let its customers set hard AI spending caps on Tuesday. The guide shows no visible sign of a cap effect. That is weak evidence, not a refutation, because the caps are only days old. So what: for now, usage-priced growth at Cloudflare is still compounding, not bumping into ceilings.

The more interesting claim came on Cloudflare's earnings call, not in the release. Per third-party call transcripts, chief executive Matthew Prince said the company recorded its first revenue from products aimed at monetizing AI-agent and bot traffic. He also pitched Cloudflare's isolate-based architecture as better suited to agent workloads than hyperscalers' container-based systems. That second point is a vendor's own architectural claim, with no customer evidence behind it yet. Now put the revenue claim on the evidence ladder. It's more than a product announcement, because management says money is changing hands. It's well short of meaningful revenue contribution, because the release puts no dollar figure on these products. And Prince's agentic-cloud label is exactly the kind of framing to distrust by default. Some of what's sold as agent-traffic monetization may be existing bot-management revenue under a new name.

Keep these agents separate from the enterprise agents in Okta's recent survey. That survey found enterprises missing their own agent rollout schedules eighty percent of the time. The traffic Cloudflare is charging for appears to be mostly automated crawlers and bots. Those are not the governed, identity-managed agents enterprises are struggling to deploy. So what: Cloudflare has a story for revenue that scales with machines, but not yet a number.

Put the two side by side. Both companies beat. Figma sold off on a forward guide that slows by ten points, plus a pricing choice that leans on retention. Cloudflare rallied against the tape on a guide that keeps compounding, plus a claim on revenue from machine traffic. So the split between them follows the forward guides, not seat versus usage pricing. That is the same answer the Figma numbers gave on their own. Atlassian's decelerating guide fits the same pattern. The market is grading the forward trajectory, not the quarter delivered. I can't tell you how much of Cloudflare's rally the agent claim earned, versus the guide itself. But the market is giving no visible credit to AI stories that come without their own disclosed number. Figma reports what share of its larger customers use AI credits, but its release puts no revenue figure on them. Atlassian disclosed no AI monetization figure at all. Cloudflare at least claims a line that grows with agents rather than headcount, even if it hasn't sized it.

Two things follow, both inference rather than disclosed fact. First, expect more seat-priced vendors to break out AI or usage revenue as its own line. They'll do it to avoid being read purely through slowing seat growth. Figma shows the risk of the alternative. Bundle AI into the existing plan, and the cost lands in retention before the revenue shows up. Second, suppose agent and crawler traffic keeps becoming billable at the network edge. Then some AI value capture shifts toward infrastructure layers like Cloudflare, and away from the model and application layers. Whoever runs an agent would face a new access cost just to reach the content it needs.

Three things to watch into December. First, whether Figma's next quarter puts a revenue number on AI credits, and whether net dollar retention dips the way its finance chief warned. Second, whether Cloudflare ever puts a dollar figure on its agent-traffic products, rather than just claiming they exist. Third, two security prints. Okta reports on December first, and CrowdStrike is estimated for early December. Both stocks were repriced this week ahead of any new earnings data. So both prints will test whether identity and endpoint security spending is holding up.
