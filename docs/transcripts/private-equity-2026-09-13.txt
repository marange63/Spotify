Good morning. It's Sunday, September 13th, and this is your private equity briefing.

Start with a correction to ourselves. On Tuesday this show argued that pressure on GP-led continuation vehicles would not remove the industry's need for liquidity. It would simply relocate that need into NAV lending, the corner of fund finance that discloses the least. Reporting published that same day says the money did move into NAV lending. It also says the money is doing something other than what we assumed. That turns out to be the worse outcome, not the better one.

The shift is in use of proceeds. A NAV facility is a loan secured against the net asset value of a fund's entire portfolio, rather than against any single company. According to a September 8th report in Alternative Credit Investor, these loans are no longer primarily funding distributions to limited partners. Distribution-funding was the practice that drew SEC and ILPA scrutiny over the past two years. General partners are now using NAV facilities mainly to extend investment capacity, finance add-on acquisitions, and pay down portfolio debt. Arcmont's Peter Hutton calls NAV loans, quote, definitely much more of a standard part of the fund management toolkit. KBRA's Tom Speller says the market has, quote, moved decisively away from funding distributions.

Take the adoption numbers next, and take them narrowly. Roughly 40 percent of mid-market European private equity managers have executed a NAV loan. In the US the figure is about 30 percent, a market that trails Europe by a few years. That is a count of managers who have used the product at least once. It tells you nothing about how much has been borrowed, or at what loan-to-value.

One sourcing note before we build on any of this. There is no dollar size for the NAV lending market that we can stand behind. On Tuesday this show cited an outstanding-market estimate for this channel, and it does not survive scrutiny, so we are retiring it rather than repeating it. We commissioned a second research pass specifically to find a credible total, and it came back empty. No advance-rate or pricing figure cleared the bar either. The absence is itself informative. A market this significant by every qualitative account still has no trusted denominator.

Now why the new use case is worse for limited partners, even though it sounds more responsible. A NAV loan that funds a distribution is visible. It shows up as cash in an LP's account, and it is scandal-shaped precisely because anyone can see it and ask where the cash came from. A NAV loan that funds an add-on acquisition is invisible. It places fund-level debt ahead of LP equity in the capital structure. It never appears in a distribution statistic. It surfaces only when the portfolio is finally sold, and the LP discovers what was borrowed against its claim in the meantime. Reported net asset value can hold up while the residual claim underneath it quietly thins. Distribution-funding was the practice that got policed. Deployment-funding is the practice that is growing.

The mechanism behind that shift is the deployment clock. Bain's start-of-year report — standing backdrop, not a fresh release — finds the average private equity fund now takes five and a half years to deploy 90 percent of committed capital. At the 2021 peak it took four and a half. So the investment period runs out before the capital is spent. A NAV facility is how a general partner keeps buying without going back to LPs for a raise it may not be able to complete on schedule. Fund-level leverage is becoming a substitute for a fundraise, not just a substitute for a sale. It is also, quietly, a substitute for a continuation vehicle. If borrowing against the portfolio is cheaper and less painful than a GP-led deal, the same economics get routed into the one channel where limited partners have built no disclosure norms at all.

Now the closest thing to good news here, and it deserves grading rather than applause. KBRA's own July research — the agency's release, not a magazine's paraphrase of it — puts cumulative rated NAV loan issuance since 2018 at more than 82 billion dollars across 157 transactions through the first half of this year. Across 279 surveillance reviews conducted between 2020 and the middle of this year, 95 percent of rating actions were affirmations, 4 percent were upgrades, and 1 percent were negative. That reads like a market performing well.

Check the vintage math before you accept it. A record 23 billion dollars of that cumulative total was rated in 2025 alone. That is more than a quarter of everything issued since 2018, arriving in a single year. This is a young book that has barely had time to season. It has not lived through a distressed-NAV cycle, where portfolio marks get tested against a genuinely hostile exit environment. A 95 percent affirmation rate on a book that young tells you the structure has not been tried, not that it works. Notice who is speaking, too. Arcmont lends against NAV, and KBRA is paid by the issuers whose facilities it rates. Nobody publishes a use-of-proceeds breakdown, so the claim that the market moved away from distributions is a characterization from inside the trade, not a disclosed statistic.

Underneath all of it sits a circularity worth naming plainly. These facilities are advanced against net asset values that the borrowing manager itself reports, on assets it has not been able to sell. That is the mark-to-model question this industry keeps deferring, arriving now as a credit exposure rather than a valuation debate. There is a regulatory mismatch on top of it. The SEC and ILPA built their disclosure pressure around the distribution use case. If the industry's own account is right, that is the shrinking part of the market, and the fastest-growing part is the one nobody is examining.

So why borrow instead of raising a new fund? Apollo, at the very top of the industry, is the instructive case. Apollo launched Fund XI, its flagship buyout vehicle, in February, with a target of 22 to 25 billion dollars. That would be its largest buyout fund ever. As of early August, Apollo had drawn about 12 billion dollars, roughly half the target, some six months into marketing. That figure is now about six weeks stale, and we could not confirm any fresh close since. President Jim Zelter told analysts the firm was, quote, seeing strong support across geographies from both new and existing investors. That is what a president says mid-raise, and it carries no information on its own.

Be fair to Apollo about what half a target means. Flagship funds routinely take a year and a half to two years to close, and Apollo set itself a record target, so this may be an ambition problem rather than a demand problem. The number that matters is not the 12 billion. It is that the firm has publicly floated the close slipping into 2027. That adds a third point to an arc this show has been drawing all week. Blue Owl's GP Stakes Fund Six is running roughly a year behind its own guided close. Advent is reportedly nearing a close near 26 billion dollars, helped by more than 12 billion of realizations this year. Apollo sits in between. The variable separating them is cash returned, not brand.

Now the other side of that trade, which is the anchor investor. CalPERS, the largest public pension in the country, cut large buyout strategies from 46 percent of its private equity portfolio in September 2022 to 33 percent by this past March. It did that mainly through secondary sales. Over the same stretch it lifted combined growth and venture exposure from 20 percent to 38 percent, even while raising its overall private-markets target. Those percentages come from a summary of CalPERS disclosures rather than a line-by-line read of the board deck, and this is a multi-year trend rather than news from this week. Read it as a beta decision, not a verdict on buyout returns. But the mechanism is the point. CalPERS chose to sell into the secondaries market rather than wait for its general partners to distribute. That is an anchor LP declining to underwrite the hold period on exactly the cohort carrying the longest holds and the heaviest fund-level leverage.

One short update on a deal we have covered. KKR's sale of USI Insurance to Aon is a 17-billion-dollar cash agreement targeted to close in the fourth quarter, so no money has changed hands yet. It carries two disclosed return multiples, and we used the more conservative one on Wednesday. KKR reports a 6.0 times return on the equity it invested back in 2017, against the 3.4 times on total balance-sheet capital we cited. They differ because follow-on capital went in later and at higher entry values. The 6.0 times is the fund-relevant denominator, and it works out to roughly 21 percent compounded over nine years, gross and before fees. That is a genuinely strong result. It also took nine years, and it is KKR's own disclosure on a deal that has not closed.

Pulling it together. Leverage in this cycle is migrating from the distribution line, where it was visible and policed, to the deployment line, where it is neither. The anchor limited partner is selling into the secondaries bid rather than waiting for cash. The fundraising clock, not a sale, is what the borrowing is really bridging. And none of this touches fee-related earnings, so the managers' own shares keep getting priced off the fee annuity while the risk accumulates inside the funds. Watch late October, when Blackstone, KKR, Apollo, Carlyle and Ares report third-quarter results. That is when we see realization pace, any confirmed Fund XI close, and possibly the first dollar figure on the NAV lending book that anyone can actually source.
