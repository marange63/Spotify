Good morning, this is The Throughline for Friday, October 2nd, 2026.

Today's headline: Anthropic's leaked IPO draft shows quarterly revenue more than doubling to 11.5 billion dollars, alongside a 2025 net loss five times its operating loss that nothing in the reporting explains.

Start with the macro backdrop. Strategic Power finds that private contracts, not policy, moved capacity this week: a state-linked Chinese lessor financed Nvidia B300 servers for a Ningxia computing hub, and Amazon signed a twenty-year nuclear power deal with Constellation at Calvert Cliffs. In both cases the headline overstates the physical change, with just 32 servers itemized in the chip filing and only about 190 genuinely new megawatts in the power deal.

From capacity to the cost of money. Capital Markets Cross-Asset Radar has the ten-year Treasury hitting a new multidecade high of 5.3 percent on Thursday, then giving ground within the day for the first time in this run. The desk calls that one crack, not a turn, and reads the dollar's year-to-date high as the tightening impulse spreading beyond bonds. September payrolls land this morning, after these briefings were prepared, and they're the next test.

Digital Money has bitcoin pinned just under the 84,000-to-85,000-dollar band of heavy long-term-holder supply, with a shrinking futures book whose remaining longs are paying more to stay in. The bigger story is one layer down. Binance bought 100 million dollars of Circle stock at a discount, and Circle now pays Binance a monthly fee that scales with USDC balances in one wallet channel, a rent whose size nobody has disclosed.

Staying with the money, Private Credit and the Corporate Credit Cycle carries a warning from inside the industry. Carlyle's credit arm cites a JPMorgan estimate that private credit will finance roughly a trillion dollars of the AI buildout, and argues that turns a diversification pitch into one correlated, procyclical bet. Meanwhile, Ares's easing redemption queue is mostly the same holders resubmitting, a backlog draining rather than confidence returning.

Private Equity and the Deal Economy finds exits reviving, but only where strategic buyers want the asset. Warburg Pincus has realized about 12 billion dollars this year, already matching its record 2025, yet its chief executive concedes software businesses "can't sell so easily." Meanwhile Bain Capital is bidding for an AI data-center developer, a sign that fresh buyout money is rotating toward AI infrastructure rather than clearing the software backlog.

That pull toward AI infrastructure brings us to the compute stack, where the scarce input is memory. Semiconductors and Compute Infrastructure finds Micron with 26 long-term agreements locking about 35 percent of its volume through 2030, reaching into commodity DRAM and NAND, not just HBM. Most of that priced revenue sits inside bands with a floor and a ceiling, so Micron is selling some upside to buy duration. SK hynix reportedly made the opposite bet with uncapped contracts, and the cycle will decide which was smarter.

Frontier AI Lab Competition carries the headline, with two caveats: the draft is leaked and unconfirmed, and the reported two-trillion-dollar valuation target is a hope, not a price. Anthropic, which makes the model behind this show, also took a public hit on its brand, as Meta's Yann LeCun called doom messaging "the worst marketing campaign you could imagine." The briefing's read is that safety has gone from a shared norm among the labs to an openly contested competitive dimension.

AI Products and Applications looks at OpenAI's Codex coding agent moving fully into the cloud, where tasks keep running with the laptop closed. That shifts the bottleneck from a developer's attention to their capacity to review unattended work. Meanwhile GitHub, now carrying OpenAI's new GPT-6.1 Sol, and Anthropic, with Sonnet 5.5, are both selling cheaper tasks rather than cheaper tokens, a price war fought entirely on vendor-reported numbers.

Enterprise Technology shows who carries the risk in that scramble. Akamai's filing discloses an 11.6-billion-dollar, seven-year Anthropic cloud commitment, won partly by handing Anthropic a warrant for up to 5 percent of Akamai's stock. Oracle reportedly got the opposite deal, with Tencent prepaying roughly 30 percent of a five-year chip lease, so when capacity is scarce the customer prepays, and when the supplier needs relevance, the supplier pays.

Now the applied frontier, where that same memory squeeze shows up inside a robot. Robotics and Physical AI has Elon Musk saying Tesla halved its AI5 chip's memory because it was "the only way to get enough volume for Optimus production," an admission that memory supply, not robot capability, gates the build. Agility Robotics, meanwhile, is going public by SPAC on an order from a related party, with a 140-million-dollar operating loss on 1.78 million dollars of revenue.

Space and the Orbital Economy tests the thesis that value in space is pooling around SpaceX, and finds it refined rather than reversed. AST SpaceMobile shipped three more satellites, and the FCC opened new satellite spectrum without picking a direct-to-cell winner. L3Harris still holds the missile-tracking sensor layer, though its order is roughly a tenth of SpaceX's reported Golden Dome awards.

AI in Biopharma R&D closes the tour with Insilico's rentosertib, whose Phase 3 in lung fibrosis is now recruiting as the first pivotal-scale test of an AI-designed drug. The same Nature Medicine paper that reported its Phase 2a signal notes AI-discovered drugs fail Phase 2 at a similar rate to conventional ones. So the demonstrated AI dividend so far is speed and lower cost per program, not better odds.

One thread does run through the day: memory scarcity, visible in Micron's contract book, in Akamai pre-buying memory for its Anthropic buildout, and now in Tesla's chip design for Optimus. After a week of tracking that squeeze in the chip briefings, today it reached a robot's silicon.

That's The Throughline. Have a good weekend.
