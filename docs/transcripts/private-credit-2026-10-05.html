Private Credit & the Corporate Credit Cycle

Transcript · October 5, 2026

Good morning. This is Cautious Optimism Briefings, private credit, for Monday, October 5th, 2026.

The tender result we have been waiting on is in, and it does not say what we expected. Blue Owl again limited withdrawals to 5% at two of its private credit vehicles. But the two vehicles moved in opposite directions. That is a queue sorting by sector, not a queue draining.

On October 1 we set a test. Third-quarter requests at the biggest non-traded funds had run from 10 percent to over 16 percent of shares. If Blue Owl's flagship landed in that range, liquidity mismatch was still the binding constraint. Blue Owl Credit Income Corp. landed at the top of it. Its requests fell from 18.8% to 16.8%. So the flagship is still gated, but it is not getting worse.

Be careful with what that easing means. A lower request rate could be returning confidence. It could also be fatigue, or holders who took a partial fill and stopped asking. The reported numbers do not tell those apart.

The break is at the tech-focused vehicle. Blue Owl Technology Income Corp., or OTIC, saw investors seek to redeem 39% of its shares, up from 38.1% the period before. On Friday we said the liquidity queues were draining, and that nothing that week contradicted it. OTIC does. These figures come from secondary reports and have not yet been checked against the tender filings, so treat them as reported figures.

Now the arithmetic, using only those reported numbers. A 5% cap against 39% of shares requested means roughly one in eight requested shares can get out this period. The rest roll forward. That is a multi-quarter queue, not a one-off.

There is also a caveat on the demand itself. The request figure includes resubmissions from earlier gated periods, and the net-new share is undisclosed. So 39% overstates fresh exit demand. It still tells you that holders in this vehicle want out at nearly eight times the cap.

Blue Owl says the results show a disconnect between the market's fears of AI disintermediating software and OTIC's resilient credit fundamentals. That is the manager talking its book. Nothing in the material I have shows OTIC's non-accruals, payment-in-kind income, or marks. So there are two readings. One is a sentiment overshoot on software. The other is that the vehicle's own holders are front-running marks that have not yet absorbed AI-disruption risk. Third-quarter filings should settle which one is right.

AI is showing up on the other side of the ledger too. On Friday we covered Carlyle's warning that private credit's AI financing is too concentrated. So the same asset class is being asked to finance the buildout while the owners of its existing software loans are heading for the exit. The takeaway is that the wealth channel is pricing software risk ahead of the marks.

Here is why that matters beyond one vehicle. Two second-order effects follow, and both are implications rather than reported facts. First, for a sector-concentrated book, gating stops being episodic and becomes a standing feature. That leaves the manager a hard choice. It can hold the 5% cap and pay for it in reputation and fundraising. Or it can sell assets or add leverage to meet more of the queue. Selling is the path that would force price discovery on exactly the marks in question.

Second, the dispersion story has moved. On September 30 it was dispersion between managers, with Goldman's GS Credit fund comfortably under its cap while the biggest funds were not. Today it is dispersion inside a single manager, sorted by sector. Other non-traded BDCs with heavy software books should expect advisors and holders to run the same sort.

And Blue Owl keeps turning up where the stress is. Eight days ago it was Loparex, a second-lien loan in Blue Owl's flagship BDC whose mark fell close to zero in six months. On Friday it was Project Jupiter, the New Mexico data-center campus Blue Owl developed for Oracle, which drew a force-majeure notice from Oracle. Now it is OTIC. That is three different problems with one name attached, so Blue Owl's earnings call is where the stress gets tested first.

That raises the question of what happens when someone forces the marks to catch up. Australia just showed us.

Metrics Credit Partners manages around A$40bn, according to press reports. Its auditor, KPMG, made different decisions on inputs and probability weightings from those used in Metrics' preliminary financial reports. The result, as reported, was a set of cuts to net tangible asset values across three listed trusts. The deepest was 12.16% at the Metrics Real Estate Multi-Strategy Fund. The shallowest was just under 2% at the Metrics Master Income Trust. Press reports put the writedown across the three at about A$169 million. Applications and redemptions were also suspended in the unlisted wholesale funds behind those listed trusts.

Three caveats matter here. First, every figure is secondary. The ASX announcement was not readable, so these numbers are press reports, not company wording. Second, the marked assets are property investments, not loans. As reported, the disclosures said greater weight was given to downside scenarios and less favourable outcomes. This is a property valuation story inside a private credit wrapper. It may also prove specific to one manager's weightings rather than a general flaw in mark-to-model practice. Third, nothing I have shows the audit is finished. KPMG could not meet its September 30 sign-off deadline.

So what does this tell us about US vehicles? The read-across is an implication, not evidence of contagion. US sponsor-backed direct lending uses different collateral and different auditors. But the mechanism is the lesson. The variance came from an auditor's judgment on valuation inputs and weightings, not from a payment default. And it reached past the listed trusts to freeze redemptions in the wholesale funds behind them. In retail wrappers, where NAV sets the redemption price, a mark can move well before a borrower defaults.

Recall the SEC staff's fair-value statement from last week, which said auditors should not accept less than persuasive evidence. Metrics is that principle applied by an auditor in another jurisdiction. On September 30 we said the first sign of stress might show up as NAV markdowns before it ever shows up as a default. Metrics is the first live case of that in our window. The bottom line is that marks can move before defaults do.

What this week did not bring is fresh default, non-accrual, or CLO data. I do not have a new rating-agency default study, and I have no current recovery rates. The last hard read on non-accruals is still the second quarter, and I will not restate those figures. So I am not changing the cycle call on the strength of this week's news alone.

Where does the cycle stand? Realized credit is a slow grind, and liquidity is sorting by sector. The biggest unresolved risk in private credit is that software-heavy retail vehicles are carrying marks that their own holders no longer seem to trust.

Three catalysts would confirm or refute that read. First, alternative-manager third-quarter earnings from late October. That is Blue Owl's first chance to back its resilient-fundamentals claim with numbers. Second, third-quarter BDC filings in November, where OTIC's non-accruals and payment-in-kind income will show whether the requests reflect a real credit problem. Third, OTIC's next tender, and whether its request rate keeps climbing. A fourth, outside the vehicles, is the completion of the Metrics audit and its ASX disclosures.
