Good morning, and welcome to Cautious Optimism Briefings on enterprise technology for Monday, September twenty-first, twenty twenty-six.

For most of last week, this briefing made one repeated complaint. Every checkable number on enterprise AI came from the party being graded. Vendors gave us agent counts, vendors gave us ARR, and nobody outside the vendor set was publishing dollars. That changed on September sixteenth, when Gartner published a new forecast. It says AI spending is accelerating, and it says so at the moment sentiment is souring.

Start with what moved. Gartner puts worldwide AI spending at two point seven trillion dollars this year, up forty-nine point five percent. Do not lean on that number. It bundles AI-optimized infrastructure with software and services, so it is largely a hardware figure, not a measure of enterprise software demand.

The marginal information is the revision. Gartner raised its growth forecast for AI application development platforms from twenty-eight percent to thirty-nine percent. That is an eleven-point change inside a single quarterly cycle. And a caveat belongs here. This is a model output from a research house with its own commercial incentives, not a filed result. It deserves weight, not deference.

Now the part that reads like a contradiction. Gartner also places generative AI firmly in the Trough of Disillusionment this year. Spending is revised up while disillusionment sets in. I think those are the same fact.

Gartner's own explanation is that enterprises are using simpler embedded AI features from their incumbent software providers to grow operational efficiency. Read that carefully. A bespoke generative AI project that disappointed does not end the budget. It redirects the budget into the suite the buyer already owns, because the embedded feature is the low-risk way to keep spending.

So the so-what is that money is consolidating toward whoever already holds the renewal. That is bullish for system-of-record vendors. It is bearish for AI-native point tools, which now sell against a capability the buyer has already paid for inside a suite. That caps their price at renewal, not just their share. And it is the opposite of the disruption story priced into application software.

If that is how enterprises are buying, the incumbent income statement should show it. Adobe is the cleanest test, and it has not come up on this briefing yet. The print is ten days old, so treat it as evidence in an argument, not as news.

Adobe reported third-quarter results on September tenth. Revenue was a record six point seven six billion dollars, up thirteen percent. On the call, chief executive Narayen said AI-first ending ARR is now exceeding six hundred fifty million dollars, growing more than one hundred fifty percent year over year.

Anchor that to the base. Adobe's total ending ARR was twenty-seven and a half billion dollars. So the AI-first line is roughly two and a half percent of the total, and a little more, because six hundred fifty million is stated as a floor. Triple-digit growth is not yet moving the aggregate.

The guidance points the same way. Adobe's fourth-quarter revenue target is six point eight to six point eight five billion dollars. The markets outlet Twenty-Four Seven Wall Street called that midpoint only a bracket around consensus, and the first visible crack. That framing is theirs, and I have not checked it against a consensus number. The stock fell about three percent to two hundred forty-one dollars early on September eleventh, then recovered to two hundred forty-eight, practically unchanged on the session.

There is a question the disclosures do not answer. Adobe does not define what counts as AI-first ARR. Nothing in the release or on the call says whether that line is new spend or existing Creative Cloud and Firefly revenue that now carries an AI label. That is undisclosed, not a finding against Adobe. The nearest datapoint is that Firefly ending ARR, across the app and the credit packs, grew forty percent quarter over quarter. That shows Adobe tracks a credit-based AI stream separately. It does not tell us whether the stream is incremental.

There are two readings. The bull case is that a small base growing this fast compounds into something material. The skeptical case is that embedded AI is defending revenue rather than adding it. A flat stock on a guide that only brackets expectations is the market saying it will not pay for the adjacent line. It wants acceleration in the core.

So Adobe's shape is consistent with Gartner's finding. The embedded AI line grows fast, the core does not speed up, and the data cannot yet distinguish that from slow early monetization. A chief executive handoff and an interim finance chief stack execution risk on top of the same test.

Now there is one category where AI is not showing up as a feature. That is security, and it is the exception the embedded-AI thesis has to survive.

You will remember the September fourteenth move, when CrowdStrike, Zscaler and Palo Alto Networks each jumped roughly eleven to twelve percent in a day, on a secondary market tracker's numbers rather than an exchange feed. What is new is the fundamental behind it. On September sixteenth this briefing set a test. After Zscaler put a dollar figure on AI-security demand, would CrowdStrike or Palo Alto match it? CrowdStrike has now answered, in its second-quarter fiscal twenty twenty-seven results, and the answer is a multiple. Its AI detection and response business, AIDR, saw ending ARR nearly triple versus the first quarter.

CrowdStrike chief executive Kurtz added a claim on the call. He said the AIDR business can be bigger than the EDR business, just given the pure number of agents.

Grade that honestly. A tripling with no disclosed base is unfalsifiable, and a multiple is not a dollar, so the test is answered only halfway. Kurtz's claim is a total-addressable-market argument, not a booking, and it rests on a count. There will be more agents than humans, so there will be more endpoints. That works only if agent security is priced per agent the way endpoints are priced per device. If buyers pay per workload or per gateway, the count does not translate into revenue. What would settle it is a dollar base next quarter, or a pricing unit disclosed on an earnings call.

The split still matters. In security, buyers are underwriting AI as a new line on top of the endpoint and identity stack. That favors the platform vendors who already hold that estate over the point tools launching agent-safety products this month, because the buyer adds a line to an existing contract instead of opening a new procurement.

It also creates the risk to the thesis. If security is the one additive AI line, it is now a crowded trade. Every coming print in the group is graded against a higher bar, and an in-line quarter becomes a negative catalyst.

One more implication follows from embedded AI. A feature inside a suite the customer already owns does not carry the integration project that a new platform does. That is a demand headwind for systems integrators billing by the hour, and it is the same point this briefing has now reached from several directions.

Let me close with Salesforce, because it is the purest form of the embedded trade. We have covered its Dreamforce launches. The thread we have not used is Koa, Salesforce's own CRM reasoning model, post-trained on NVIDIA's Nemotron. An application incumbent building its own model is the embedded-AI thesis in one move, and it is also how the feature gets metered. Koa is pilot-only, with no customer or performance data, so treat it as a strategic tell rather than a product. Post-training someone else's open model is also a thinner moat than the framing implies.

Now the arithmetic Adobe could not fully supply. Salesforce reported Agentforce ARR above one point five billion dollars in the quarter it announced on August twenty-sixth, against quarterly revenue of eleven point three billion dollars growing eleven percent. Annualize the quarter and Agentforce is about three percent of the base. After two years of the most aggressive agent marketing in enterprise software, three percent is either early compounding or a ceiling.

So here is where the argument lands. Salesforce and Adobe show the same shape. The AI lines grow at triple digits and sit at two to three percent of revenue, while headline growth runs eleven to thirteen percent. Gartner's revision says the money is coming. It does not say who keeps it.

Two things would break the stalemate. One is an AI line that gets a disclosed dollar base and a definition next quarter. The other is an incumbent whose core growth accelerates, not just its AI adjunct. Until one of those happens, a rising forecast and a flat core are the same story.
