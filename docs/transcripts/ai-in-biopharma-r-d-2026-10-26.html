AI in Biopharma R&D

Transcript · October 26, 2026

Good morning. It's October 26th, and this is your briefing on AI in biopharma R&D.

This week the contracts did the talking. One set of deals licensed data, another optioned design, and the difference tells you where pricing power in this industry actually sits right now.

Start with the lead. On October 23rd, Tempus AI disclosed, via an SEC filing, two real-world-data agreements worth up to $192.5 million combined. One is with Bristol Myers Squibb in oncology. The other is with a company called Pathos AI in neurology. This show has flagged repeatedly that the Tempus-BMS relationship carried no disclosed financial terms and no disclosed outcome. That relationship includes a five-program trial-design collaboration announced back in May. It finally has a dollar figure attached, though for a new data license rather than a disclosed price for that May work. Under the oncology agreement, Bristol Myers Squibb gets access to de-identified, multimodal oncology data through Tempus's Lens platform. It also gets the GPU infrastructure to train and run its own models on that data. Crucially, the 8-K specifies fixed payments: $137.5 million of them, invoiced quarterly, running through the end of 2031. That is cash for data access and compute, not for a molecule.

One nuance on how firm that cash is. The filing lets Bristol Myers Squibb terminate on 12 months' written notice, plus what it calls a substantial termination fee. The size of that fee is not disclosed. So these are fixed payments with a priced exit, not an unbreakable commitment. Still, it is a very different animal from a milestone. Back on October 16th, this show argued that the firmest claims on cash in this field belong to whoever owns real-world data at scale. That argument rested on Recursion's committed payments to Tempus. Bristol Myers Squibb is a second, larger payer on the same side of that ledger, and this time it's big pharma.

Here's the skeptical turn, and it matters. A price is not an outcome. Bristol Myers Squibb paying for access tells you nothing yet about whether models trained on this data improve target selection or the probability of technical and regulatory success. The May collaboration's result is still undisclosed, full stop.

And the headline $192.5 million is softer than it looks once you separate the two pieces. The Pathos AI portion, capped at $55 million, is entirely contingent. Tempus is paid $100 per delivered record, so the final number depends on how many records actually change hands. More importantly, the filing discloses that Pathos AI is a related party to Tempus. The two share overlapping executive leadership and equity ownership. That is why the deal needed approval from a special committee of disinterested directors. So a meaningful slice of this week's headline number is essentially intra-family. It is not outside pharma validating demand for Tempus's data. The real signal is the Bristol Myers Squibb piece alone. The market is paying for the data before anyone has proved the data pays off.

Why does that data command a price when extraction itself is getting easier? Consider a Cleveland Clinic study. Its publication date is unconfirmed, so treat it as context rather than this week's news. It found that an AI system parsing structured and unstructured electronic health records hit 96% accuracy assessing trial eligibility across nine criteria domains. But that result ran entirely inside one health system's firewall. It tells you that in-firewall extraction already works well. It is also accuracy against chart review, not faster enrollment or cheaper trials. What it doesn't solve is aggregation across institutions, which is exactly the scarce, priced asset Tempus is selling. The model travels to the data, and whoever holds the data at scale, across systems, sets the price. One more gap worth noting. The filing never specifies which HIPAA de-identification standard governs either dataset, Safe Harbor or Expert Determination. That question is simply undisclosed, not solved.

Now the other side of the ledger: what pharma pays the designers. On October 21st, AI Proteins signed a research collaboration and license agreement with Astellas to design novel miniprotein therapeutics. The headline value is more than $400 million in potential milestones, plus royalties. The upfront is explicitly undisclosed. AI Proteins applies its de novo design platform against targets Astellas selects. Once a candidate is nominated, Astellas takes over development, regulatory work, and commercialization. It is AI Proteins' second large-pharma deal of this kind, after one with Bristol Myers Squibb at the end of 2024. No candidates are named, and no preclinical data are disclosed. Miniproteins are also an early modality, with no AI-designed miniprotein clinical validation to point to. This is a discovery-stage option, not a pipeline asset. Structurally, it's the same pattern this show has tracked all week across insitro's AstraZeneca deal and other design-layer partnerships. A large milestone number sits on top of a small, or in this case unstated, committed payment.

That sets up a genuine tension worth testing. Yesterday, this show argued that molecule design itself is heading toward free, after an open-weight protein-design model shipped with its trained weights included. In the same week, Astellas signed a nine-figure headline deal with a design platform. Those two facts can coexist. Here's the likely reconciliation, and it's inference rather than confirmed fact. What Astellas is buying isn't model capability. It's program-specific execution against its own chosen targets. That means the wet-lab iteration that gets a design to a nominated candidate, and the resulting intellectual property. The model that generates candidate miniproteins may be increasingly commoditized. Running it against a real target, validating it in the lab, and owning what comes out is not. The undisclosed upfront is consistent with that reading. Pharma isn't paying much for access to the platform itself, because the platform alone isn't the scarce part anymore. Design is still paid for. It's just paid for as an option on an outcome, not as a purchase of capability.

So pull the two threads together. Bristol Myers Squibb committed fixed cash through 2031 for a data-and-compute relationship with a known price and a still-unknown payoff. Astellas committed a large contingent number, with no disclosed upfront, for a design platform's labor against its own targets. Data ownership is attracting the firmer money. Design is attracting the softer, option-shaped money. If that pattern holds, platform companies without proprietary real-world data of their own face a structurally thinner revenue mix. Their cash arrives slowly, gated by milestones, rather than as recurring licensing fees. Notice too what the structure implies about intent: sponsors want to own the models while renting the data.

For cloud and compute providers, the Bristol Myers Squibb deal bundles GPU infrastructure together with the data license. That means the training compute sits with the data owner, not inside the pharma company's own walls. Data licensors are becoming a compute channel as well. That is a meaningful shift in who controls the infrastructure layer as these relationships scale.

Three open questions to track. First, whether the Tempus-BMS relationship ever produces a disclosed outcome metric rather than just a price tag. Second, how much of AI Proteins' $400 million shows up as near-term cash once the upfront becomes known. Third, whether data licensors like Tempus capture the compute layer as a durable business, not just a one-time bundling choice.

On clinical success rates, the honest read is that data access targets the right lever: better go/no-go decisions earlier in the pipeline. But nobody has shown yet that it moves the number. For R&D budgets, the practical shift is that data access is becoming a recurring, calendar-driven line item, the way cloud spend did a decade ago. Discovery-stage AI partnerships, by contrast, remain lumpy and contingent on milestones that may never be hit. Those are two very different kinds of spending. This week is the clearest evidence yet of which one pharma is actually willing to commit to first.
