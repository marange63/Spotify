Today's story is a mismatch in the quality of evidence. At the infrastructure layer, the obligations backing the AI buildout are getting firmer. More of them now come with a named balance sheet standing behind them. At the application layer, the demand those obligations are meant to serve still hasn't been measured in public. Start with the firm side.

Bloomberg reported yesterday that the data-center developer Applied Digital is demanding multi-billion-dollar financial guarantees from large tenants before it will sign new leases. Secondary outlets carried the report. Get the roles straight. Applied Digital is the developer and landlord here. Oracle is the prospective tenant, and newer reporting confirms Meta is named alongside it. Oracle shares fell around four percent on the report, slipping below one hundred fifteen dollars. That drop came during a broader multi-day selloff in AI-adjacent names. So treat some of it as sector beta, not a verdict on Oracle specifically.

What's the guarantee actually for? According to The Next Web's account of Bloomberg's report, the money secures power for the sites. That makes the ask narrower than a blanket credit backstop. The question becomes less whether the developer trusts Oracle's balance sheet, and more who carries the cost of locking in power. No source puts a size on the guarantees beyond billions of dollars. Applied Digital's chief executive, Wes Cummins, spoke only to tenant mix. Per that account, the company prefers to do business with investment-grade tenants. He did not address the guarantee terms. Oracle declined to comment, and Meta had no comment. So this is a reported demand, not a signed deal.

Now the ledger that makes this bite for Oracle specifically. The bull case fits in one number. Oracle's cloud infrastructure revenue grew one hundred twenty-one percent year over year in the quarter that ended in August. That's Oracle's own figure, and it's a quarter old. The bear case fits in one comparison. Oracle spent fifty-five point seven billion dollars on capital expenditure in fiscal 2026, and it reported negative free cash flow for that year. Management says the heaviest spending is still ahead, with capex peaking in fiscal 2027 and 2028. So Oracle is funding triple-digit cloud growth with a business that isn't yet generating free cash. A guarantee demand lands right in that gap.

Here's the mechanism worth naming plainly, and it's inference, not confirmed fact. A lease backed by a parent guarantee starts to behave like debt rather than an ordinary operating commitment. Suppose that becomes standard practice across developers. Then more of Oracle's off-balance-sheet capacity commitments turn into contingent liabilities. Rating agencies and credit markets would become a gating factor on how fast Oracle's cloud, OCI, can build, not just on how fast it can sell. Pace matters here. Oracle added eight hundred fifty megawatts of data-center capacity in that August quarter alone. If each new lease needs a guarantee negotiated first, that build rate gets harder to hold.

But a competing reading deserves equal weight, and it points the other way. This ask may say more about the developer's constraints than about Oracle's credit. Start with who got asked. The demand reportedly went to Meta as well as Oracle. That looks more like a developer-wide policy than a judgment on one tenant's credit. Wes Cummins's stated preference for investment-grade tenants fits the same reading. Then there's the power. If the money secures power, the developer is asking tenants to carry a cost it would otherwise fund itself. That matters when data-center debt markets are tightening. On Sunday, this show's private credit desk covered Bloomberg's report that Blackstone-backed QTS cut a planned private debt sale from about eight hundred million dollars to about three hundred million. It shrank the deal rather than pay the higher yields buyers wanted. Seen that way, Applied Digital isn't signaling distrust of Oracle so much as protecting its own balance sheet. Both readings can be true at once.

This also extends a thread this desk has run all week. On Friday and Saturday, we followed risk landing on the lessors, with Microsoft reportedly handing back capacity it rented from Oracle. Today the direction reverses. A developer is pushing risk back onto the tenant's balance sheet. And Oracle sits on both sides of that squeeze. It is the landlord in that reported Microsoft arrangement, and the prospective tenant in the Applied Digital one. Stress at either end of the chain can reach it.

The so-what for enterprise buyers, again as inference. If you're negotiating a multi-year OCI capacity commitment right now, Oracle's financing durability belongs in that conversation alongside its growth rate. A vendor financing capacity this aggressively needs contracted demand to fill it. That gives large buyers real leverage on price and terms. And the question generalizes beyond Oracle. For any capacity you contract, ask who is standing behind the power.

Whether all that capacity earns its keep depends on enterprises actually running AI workloads in production. And that is exactly the number the application layer hasn't published. Take Salesforce, the vendor pushing agents hardest. A review page published by a vendor that sells competing AI agents claims twenty-nine thousand Agentforce deals, with only eight percent of eligible customers running it in production. None of that comes from a Salesforce filing or earnings call. Futurum Group's analysis of the quarter that ended in January found accounts with Agentforce in production up nearly fifty percent sequentially. That's a different measure, not comparable to the eight percent, and it points the other way. So read this as setup for Salesforce's next earnings report, not as news. If production keeps lagging deals, expect Salesforce to push pricing toward usage it can switch on or meter. The test is whether that report discloses production accounts, not just deal counts.

Put the two halves together, and the asymmetry is the story. At the bottom of the stack, obligations are getting firmer and more personal. Developers want tenants to put their own balance sheets behind leases and power. At the top of the stack, the demand evidence isn't soft so much as unmeasured. The vendor pushing agents hardest hasn't put a production number on the record. So the infrastructure risk is being written into contracts, with names attached. The usage meant to pay for it hasn't been publicly counted. For budget holders, that cuts one way. Committing to capacity now means accepting firm obligations against demand you'll only be able to verify later. Until application vendors disclose production numbers, every new guarantee is a bet on usage nobody outside can see.

A few things to watch, none of them settled yet. Anthropic, which makes the model behind this show, is expected to price its IPO this evening. That puts a market price on one AI vendor's story, but it won't measure enterprise production usage. Palo Alto Networks reports earnings after the close tonight. ING's auction of data-center loans has bids due Thursday, and this show's credit desk thinks it could set the first hard price on that debt. Watch whether Oracle signs a guarantee with Applied Digital, or responds publicly at all. And watch whether Salesforce's next report finally puts a production number on Agentforce.
