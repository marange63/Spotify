Good morning. It's Thursday, August 27th, 2026, and this is your briefing on robotics and physical AI.

For a week this show has asked the humanoid sector for one thing. Give us a commercial deployment count, not a shipment count. This week produced two candidate answers. They came from opposite sides of a trade wall, in currencies that turn out not to convert. And one of them, on closer inspection, is not this week's news at all.

Start with UBTech, the Shenzhen humanoid maker. Trade press reported on the 26th that UBTech has secured a 250-million-yuan contract for its Walker S2 humanoid, with deliveries beginning within the year. That sits on top of a roughly 100-million-yuan order in July, plus purchase orders from BYD, Foxconn, and the aerospace prime Airbus. On its face it reads as the first yuan-denominated proof that this cohort has moved from pilots to paid production. It is not that simple, and the reason matters more than the headline.

Dig into the 250-million-yuan figure and it traces back to a UBTech announcement from September of 2025. The contract was described then, in identical language, as signed with a leading domestic enterprise. The buyer was unnamed. The unit count and the per-unit price were undisclosed. This week's article reproduces the same value, the same product, and the same confidentiality language, with nothing to indicate a new and distinct deal. So the honest read is a year-old contract being recirculated by trade press as if it were fresh. It is not new order-book evidence from this week. And the buyer has never been named, in 2025 or now. That is not a reporting gap. It appears to be permanent.

Which kills the only derived number that would have mattered. Without a named counterparty or a unit count, the implied price per Walker S2 cannot be computed. No payback arithmetic against a loaded labor rate. No comparison to what an integrator pays for a fixed arm doing the same job. Quoting a headline contract value harder does not fix that.

What is audited is UBTech's own 2025 full-year result, announced April 4th. Humanoid-segment revenue of 821 million yuan, up from 35.6 million yuan the year before. Cumulative sales of 1,079 full-size humanoid units. That is a real base, filed, and growing more than twentyfold in a year. Now set the contract against it. Two hundred fifty million yuan is roughly thirty percent of that entire segment year. A deal that size would genuinely move the needle if it were new. That is precisely why the dating question is the story and not a footnote.

There is a correction owed here, and it runs against this show's own recent framing. On Monday we led with the finding that research and education customers were 73.6 percent of Unitree's humanoid revenue in 2025, with industrial applications around 9 percent. Across two briefings we let that generalize toward the category. UBTech's book is the counter-case inside the same cohort. Named industrial buyers, an aerospace prime, deliveries scheduled. So the laboratory-equipment read was always a fact about Unitree, not a law about Chinese humanoids. Today only partially breaks it, because UBTech's disclosure is thinner than Unitree's. But it breaks it.

The Airbus piece is the weakest link, not the strongest. Airbus's own language describes the relationship as being in the early concept-testing stage. The claim that Airbus has purchased Walker S2 units comes from UBTech, not from Airbus. Neither side discloses a unit count, a price, or a delivery date. So the marquee Western customer name is real as a sales credibility signal. It is not a production commitment, and it should not be spoken as one.

Now the American side of the same question. It arrives in a completely different currency: capability, not contracts. Figure livestreamed a demonstration in which its Helix 02 model ran humanoids through a continuous shift of more than seventeen hours. The robots sorted more than 22,000 packages. Figure and chief executive Brett Adcock called the run fully autonomous and at human performance levels. Duration and volume are exactly the two variables that separate a staged demo from something that could displace warehouse labor. This is the first Western figure that is even the right shape to answer the question.

Then read it the way this show reads every company-run number. It is Figure's own livestream and Figure's own measurement. No outside auditor. No named customer site. Figure did not disclose how many robots produced the 22,000-package total. It disclosed no intervention rate and no miss rate. So per-robot throughput, the one figure a listener could set against a human picker's rate, is not derivable from what was published. One of the Chinese robot makers actually running commercial deployments put the standard well at the Beijing conference this week. Keenon's chief operating officer said getting a robot to complete 50 percent of a task, even 80 percent, is relatively easy. Reaching 99.9 percent is what tests the engineering. Figure has published the volume and withheld the completion quality. That is answering the reliability question with trust us, which sits at the bottom of the evidence hierarchy this show has been building all week.

There is a supply-side data point in the same release worth banking. Figure says its BotQ factory is now building roughly one Figure 03 unit per hour, at end-of-line yield above 80 percent. Take that at face value and the Western humanoid ramp is not manufacturing-constrained. The constraint has moved to demand, and to naming a real customer. Which is exactly what Figure has not done. So the American side has capacity and a capability claim, and no buyer.

That gives us this week's actual finding. UBTech has named industrial counterparties, BYD, Foxconn, an aerospace prime, with essentially zero disclosed reliability data behind any of it. Figure has a specific, headline-grabbing reliability claim with zero named buyers behind it. Neither side has produced both a signed order and a checked performance number. A real humanoid business eventually needs both. And note which evidence is stronger. UBTech's 821 million yuan of humanoid revenue was booked from customers who actually paid, and it sits in an audited annual filing. Figure's 22,000 packages were counted by Figure. An audited revenue line tells you almost nothing about capability. It tells you a great deal about whether anyone was willing to pay.

Layer in the regulatory backdrop, which this show reported on the 22nd. The FCC's Covered List action from late July bars new US authorization, marketing, or sale of foreign-made humanoids and quadrupeds. The determination names no companies. The targeting of Unitree, AgiBot, and UBTech is a press reading, not the FCC's own text. It is also prospective, blocking new authorizations rather than pulling approved units. The upshot is structural. The cohort with the signed industrial orders is the cohort locked out of new US market access. The vendor with the capability claim is not selling into China either. So the two bodies of evidence sit in separated markets. They cannot correct each other through competition or comparable pricing. That is not a resolution to the deployment question. It is a structural reason the question stays open.

The second-order effect is the one to hold. If purchase orders and capability demos are priced in different markets, American humanoid valuations keep getting set by demonstrations, because the comparable transactions happen on the other side of the wall. That keeps this sector priced off what a robot was filmed doing rather than off what somebody paid for one.

The same missing-denominator problem shows up one layer down, at the model and data layer. Earlier this month LG said it is developing a next-generation bipedal humanoid built on Nvidia's Isaac GR00T platform, for a public unveiling in the first quarter of next year. That is a conglomerate name, a platform name, and a date. No specification, no cost, no use case, no customer. Nvidia now sits underneath almost every serious humanoid program, which is a genuinely good business regardless of who wins the hardware. But it also means the model layer is accumulating announcements on exactly the same terms the robot layer is. A logo is not a denominator.

So what would settle this. On the Chinese side, a named counterparty and a unit count behind the 250-million-yuan figure. Better still, UBTech's interim results, which its board was scheduled to approve on the 28th, and whatever they show for first-half humanoid deliveries. On the American side, any outside party willing to check Figure's shift against real intervention data, whether a named enterprise customer or an independent auditor. Until one of those arrives, this stays a category with plenty of announcements and remarkably little you could underwrite.

That's the briefing for today. Thanks for listening, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
