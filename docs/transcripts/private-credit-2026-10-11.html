Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Credit & the Corporate Credit Cycle

Transcript · October 11, 2026

Good morning, it's Sunday, October 11th. This is your private credit briefing.

Here's the thesis for today. Private credit's listed layer has gone from rationing liquidity to putting itself up for sale. On Friday, this desk argued that if stress was going to transmit anywhere first, it would show up as wider discounts in listed BDC shares, not as fresh gates in the semi-liquid retail funds. The reporting that landed around that call shows what the next step looks like. Three listed business development companies trading below net asset value say they are reviewing strategic options that could include a sale. And several of the largest alternative managers are circling. That doesn't prove the call across the whole asset class. It does say the listed tier is where the pressure is surfacing first.

Bloomberg reported on October 8th that Ares Management, Barings, BC Partners, and Churchill Asset Management have examined potential deals involving troubled funds in recent months. The targets are BlackRock's TCP Capital, WhiteHorse Finance, and Investcorp Credit Management BDC. All three have said publicly they are reviewing strategic options that could include a sale. Say the caveat up front, because it's load-bearing. In the reporting's own words, the talks remain at an early stage and may not lead to transactions. This is a signal about where pressure sits, not a completed restructuring of the industry.

Now, the timing is more interesting than the headline suggests, and it changes the read. TCP Capital's board engaged KBW back on August 6th to run its strategic review. Investcorp Credit Management BDC went further back still. It hired Houlihan Lokey as financial advisor on April 20th. Only WhiteHorse Finance's special committee is genuinely fresh, formed in mid-September. So the real news isn't that these three BDCs just put themselves in play. It's that the biggest managers are now visibly circling targets that have been quietly shopping themselves for months. The acquirer-side interest is what's new.

Why would a listed BDC sell rather than grow its way out? A fund stuck below net asset value can't issue new equity without diluting existing holders. So organic growth effectively stops, and fee economics erode from there. What's left with value is the management contract and the underlying portfolio. That's what makes these funds acquisition targets rather than survivors. For a manager like Ares, buying a listed BDC is a shortcut to permanent capital and an existing shareholder base, without the cost and time of raising a brand-new vehicle. The price of that shortcut is inheriting whatever credit problems pushed the target to look for a buyer in the first place.

How a deal gets done matters as much as whether it happens, because each structure the reporting describes moves risk differently. A full merger puts the target's loans, problem credits included, onto the acquirer's balance sheet, so the buyer's own shareholders now own that credit risk. A sale of the management contract is lighter. The new manager takes the fees and the oversight, but the existing shareholders keep the loans and any losses. An asset sale into an affiliated vehicle deserves the closest look. There, the price is set between related parties rather than by an open market, which is the same untested-mark problem in a new form.

TCP Capital's own filing adds one more path. Its board lists reinvestment, buybacks, strategic combinations, or an orderly realization of assets. That last phrase is a polite word for a wind-down. And a wind-down is the one outcome that forces every loan to be sold or repaid at a real price.

So is this a distress story? The credit evidence cuts against a simple distress read, but also against a simple all-clear. Start with TCP Capital. Its non-accrual rate fell to 1.6 percent of the portfolio at fair value as of June 30th, down from 2.8 percent the quarter before. That's the headline improvement. Measured at cost, though, non-accruals were still 7.4 percent, barely changed from 7.6 percent.

That gap between cost and fair value is the real signal. Problem loans worth 7.4 percent of the book at cost, but only 1.6 percent at fair value, are already marked down hard. So the fair-value improvement could reflect loans that were resolved, or simply deeper write-downs. The filing alone doesn't say which. What it does say is that TCP Capital's book isn't clean. Its losses are largely recognized already, and that's a different thing.

Investcorp Credit Management BDC is the sharper case. Its net asset value per share fell sixty cents in a single quarter, to $3.65 at the end of March from $4.25 at year-end. On our arithmetic, that's a drop of about fourteen percent, and it's the latest figure in our sourcing. WhiteHorse Finance is the blank. We have no verified non-accrual data for it. And for none of the three do we have a current share price we can verify, so don't lean on any specific discount figure today.

Put that together and these are not three identical stories. TCP Capital reads more like a fee-economics and scale exit, with its legacy losses already sitting in the marks. Investcorp reads like a portfolio under real pressure. WhiteHorse can't be placed yet.

That leaves two competing explanations for the wave, and the facts don't yet let us pick one. Call the first the scale-economics exit. Subscale managers are leaving a business where fixed costs and compliance now favor the largest platforms. Call the second a credit-distress sale. Here the discount reflects the market pricing losses that haven't fully shown up in reported non-accruals. Both are live, and they can coexist across different names.

Either way, the second-order effect is the same if deals close. Direct-lending assets concentrate further among the biggest managers. The asset class looks more orderly on the surface. Underneath, concentration risk rises. That is exactly the systemic-linkage question supervisors have already been asking about bank exposure to non-bank lenders, as we covered earlier this week.

That's the listed tier's exit. The institutional tier has a different one. Bloomberg reported this week that Blackstone raised a private-credit continuation fund. It's the same instrument the private equity desk flagged on October 8th, where single-asset deals were pricing close to net asset value on valuations anchored by the selling sponsor itself. A continuation fund lets limited partners who want out sell their stakes to new secondary buyers, while the manager keeps control of the underlying loans. We don't have a verified dollar figure, buyer list, or price relative to net asset value for this deal. So take it as directional. The mechanism matters more than the size.

There are two fair readings of it. The sympathetic one is that a continuation fund is a sensible way to give investors an exit without dumping loans into a weak market. The skeptical one is that it defers price discovery on ageing direct-lending vintages. Either way, the result is liquidity manufactured without ever testing the underlying marks against an arm's-length sale.

Put the three exits side by side and the pattern is the point. Retail holders in the big semi-liquid funds are still queuing behind the standard five percent quarterly gate, as covered earlier this week. Listed BDC holders may get bought out entirely. Institutional limited partners get a continuation fund. Which exit you get now depends on which wrapper you hold, not on what the underlying loans are worth. Only one of those routes, a completed BDC sale at a stated price, would force a transaction-tested mark on this part of the market. The other two let everyone avoid finding out.

So where does the cycle actually stand? There's no new default, non-accrual, or payment-in-kind print since our last briefing, so today's read rests on structure rather than fresh credit metrics. On that evidence, realized credit is still a slow grind, not a turn. But the industry's plumbing is adjusting faster than its credit numbers are moving, and that gap is the thing to watch. The biggest unresolved risk isn't a wave of defaults. It's concentration arriving before anyone has to price the weak books that get folded into the winners.

Watch three things from here. First and most important, whether any of these three strategic reviews produces a signed deal, and at what price relative to net asset value. That price would be a genuine transaction-tested mark for listed BDCs, and the rest of the sector would be compared against it. Second, third-quarter earnings from the big alternative managers starting in late October, where Ares and Apollo should get pressed directly on their appetite for this kind of acquisition. Third, November's BDC quarterly filings. Those should bring fresh non-accrual and discount data, including the first credit read we'd have on WhiteHorse.

That's the briefing. Talk tomorrow.
