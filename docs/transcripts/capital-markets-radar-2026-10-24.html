Good morning, it's Saturday, October 24th, 2026, and this is Capital Markets Radar.

The story this week has been the AI capex scare moving out of the stock price and into the plumbing of credit and positioning. Today it climbs one more rung.

Start with the ladder this desk has been climbing all week. First came distressed CoreWeave bonds. Then Oracle's record credit default swap and a widening BBB tier. Then, yesterday, Meta's free cash flow turning negative. Now it's a hyperscaler's own default-protection price making a fresh high. Per Bloomberg, citing ICE Data Services, Meta's five-year CDS rose as much as 8.9 basis points on Thursday, to around 119.3. That is the highest intraday level since those swaps began trading. Run the subtraction, and the contract started the day near 110.

And it didn't trade alone. The same Bloomberg report says protection on Oracle, Microsoft, and Broadcom also got more expensive that day. That matters because it rules out an idiosyncratic reaction to one earnings print. This is a sector-wide repricing of AI financing cost, hitting hyperscalers and chipmakers at once. One limit on that claim: the report did not name Alphabet or Amazon as widening on Thursday, so don't call it universal yet.

So what are protection buyers actually reacting to? Not the cash flow sign change. It's the commitments. Per press reports of Meta's release, total spending commitments, meaning leases plus cloud contracts, rose to 825 billion dollars, from 700 billion in July. That's 125 billion dollars of debt-like obligations added in a single quarter. Here's where the market may be misreading things. It may be over-reading the CDS print and under-reading the commitments. A CDS level is one day's read. The commitments compound.

Now the piece that wasn't available yesterday. Meta has created a new class of non-voting stock. According to Yahoo Finance's reporting, Meta can sell those shares for cash, or use them as pay and acquisition currency. The point is to fund the AI buildout without diluting Mark Zuckerberg's voting control. The board's advisers calculated that Meta could raise about 700 billion dollars in equity over several years before his voting power fell below fifty percent. Put that against the commitments. It's roughly 85 percent of the 825 billion dollar pile, from equity alone. Meta's CFO pushed back on reading that as a plan. The CFO called figures like it a theoretical maximum, and "not actually a description of our present equity financing needs or plans."

Now the skeptical turn. A record on this contract is still investment-grade pricing. The swap is young and thinly traded. And some of the move is probably hedging demand. That's our inference, not a reported fact. Counterparties exposed to AI projects may be buying protection on the most liquid hyperscaler name available. That's a hedge, not a verdict on Meta's own solvency. So the CDS can overstate Meta's standalone risk while correctly flagging system-wide financing anxiety. The bottom line on Meta: the market is pricing the financing path, not default. And on that path, Meta now holds a very large equity lever it hasn't pulled.

The same repricing is happening where there's no tape to watch. The Alternative Credit Council, private credit's own trade body, published research this week, as reported by Private Equity Wire. Its headline argument is that fears of stress in the asset class are overstated. Its own numbers cut against that framing. About one-sixth of surveyed funds report non-accrual rates above five percent. And for AI-exposed borrowers, lenders are demanding stronger covenants and higher spreads. In some cases they are refusing to extend maturities.

Treat the framing skeptically. The ACC is an industry body with every incentive to downplay stress. And a survey of funds is not a default rate. For scale, the Council puts global private credit assets at 4.1 trillion dollars in 2025, up 17 percent on the year. That's a very large pool of capital, now hesitating at the AI door. It also fits the exit-queue story this desk has tracked, with redemption requests at business development companies running well above their quarterly caps. So direct lenders are repricing AI-adjacent credit before it shows up in any index.

Here's the second-order point, and it's our inference from those two facts. The escape valves for AI borrowers are narrowing together. A company squeezed on private-credit terms would normally turn to public bond markets, or to a hyperscaler's balance sheet. But the price of protecting the biggest of those balance sheets just hit a record. When the alternatives tighten at the same time, refinancing risk stops being a single-name problem.

And the people trading against all of this have changed their posture. Earlier this week, this desk reported that Goldman's prime brokerage data showed hedge funds de-grossing AI by selling longs, not adding shorts. That pattern has broken. Per Bloomberg's reporting of a Goldman note, short sales have outpaced long purchases across global equities by about five to one this month. Short leverage in Goldman's prime book now sits at the 99th percentile of its five-year history.

Net leverage tells the rest of the story. Among fundamental long-short funds, it's near a three-year low, even as gross leverage has risen. In other words, funds are grossing up both sides of the book while cutting net exposure. It isn't working for them yet. Global fundamental long-short funds lost 2.9 percent in the week through October 15th. Asia-focused stock pickers were down 6.8 percent for the first half of the month. Losses like that can force funds to cut their books rather than press the shorts.

So the near-term risk has changed shape. It's no longer just further de-rating. It's squeeze risk. On any relief rally, the marginal buyer is short-covering, not fresh conviction. That bounce could be far more violent than fundamentals justify. But a squeeze wouldn't be a verdict that the capex worries were wrong. Positioning tells you the shape of the next move, not its direction.

That squeeze risk sits right next to a fragile bond rally. Thursday's flight into Treasuries took the 10-year to its first close below 5 percent since September 22nd. Last time, the yield jumped straight back to 5.11 percent the next day. One reversal is an analogy, not a base rate. But it's the right warning.

Friday didn't resolve the tension. The S&P 500 fell about half a percent, to 7,623.68. The Nasdaq Composite slipped about two-tenths of a percent. Both logged a second straight weekly loss. The 10-year stayed near, but below, 5 percent, though sources disagree on the exact close.

Now put the two threads together. Suppose equities get a squeeze catalyst, like a stabilizing CDS print or reassurance from the next hyperscaler earnings. That would likely drain the same flight-to-quality bid that took yields under 5 percent. Yields would back up as equities snap higher. Duration and equity positioning are unwinding the same trade from opposite ends. With short leverage at an extreme, that unwind could be fast.

Three things to watch into next week. First, the Fed meets Tuesday and Wednesday. The decision hasn't happened, so no forecast here. Watch whether the language calms or inflames this AI-financing anxiety. Second, watch whether Meta's CDS stabilizes, or whether protection on Oracle, Microsoft, and Broadcom keeps widening alongside it. That would confirm the move as systemic rather than single-name. Third, the next round of hyperscaler earnings is the likeliest trigger either way. Reassurance sparks the squeeze. Confirmation sends credit spreads wider still.
