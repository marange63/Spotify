Good morning. It's Monday, August 24th, and this is your strategic power briefing.

For five straight days this beat has been a story about the state as gatekeeper. Grid operators deciding which data centers get a connection. Washington deciding which chips can ship to China. Beijing deciding which of its own firms get to bring licensed hardware onshore. Every one of those items was about who gets to say yes. This week the state changed seats. It stopped only gatekeeping and started owning.

Here's the lead. Over the past several days the Trump administration signed letters of intent to take minority, non-controlling equity stakes in six more semiconductor and AI-supply-chain companies, converting up to $874 million of 2022 CHIPS Act money into equity. Those are letters of intent, set to be finalized in the months ahead. Nothing has closed. Separately, GlobalFoundries was awarded up to $300 million in new government funding this week, on top of $375 million announced in May, and that earlier tranche alone translated into a government stake of about one percent. Add it up and the portfolio now spans thirty companies, across chips, quantum computing, steel, nuclear and rare earths.

What that portfolio is actually worth, nobody in government can tell you. The figure in circulation is roughly $26.7 billion across thirty equity or quasi-equity deals, and it comes from a Fortune tally, not from a Treasury or Commerce disclosure. Fortune built the tally because no consolidated ledger of the government's holdings exists. The stakes sit scattered across at least four agencies. That absence is itself the story. The United States is now running a strategic equity book worth tens of billions of dollars, and there is no single document that adds it up, marks it, or reports it to anyone.

The reason the book keeps growing is sitting inside Intel. A year ago the government bought 433.3 million Intel shares at $20.47 apiece, a 9.9 percent stake, per Intel's own announcement. Since then Intel stock has hit record highs and that position is carrying an unrealized profit north of $70 billion. Unrealized is doing heavy lifting in that sentence. It's a paper mark on a stake the government cannot exit without moving the stock, and it was produced by the same AI equity revaluation that could take it back. Note also that the stake is 9.9 percent, sitting just under the ten percent line that trips a range of beneficial-ownership reporting rules. Nobody has confirmed that structuring was deliberate, but it is a precise number, not a round one. Politically, though, the paper gain is the entire argument. What looked in 2025 like a one-off emergency intervention now reads inside the administration as a repeatable, profitable model. That's the so-what: state equity is standing industrial policy now, not a rescue.

Before crediting any of it, sort the stages, because this week's news spans four of them and only one is money that has actually moved. Appropriated CHIPS funds converted into equity is real capital, disbursed. Letters of intent with six chipmakers is an announcement with a signature and no closing. A signed power purchase agreement, which we'll come to, is a contract for a future electron. And licensed-but-unbuilt enrichment capacity is a permit. Press coverage collapses all four into one impression of momentum. They are not the same asset, and they do not carry the same probability of ever existing.

There's a second-order effect here that will show up in equity dispersion long before it shows up in capacity. A company inside the portfolio gets something its competitors cannot bid for: cheaper capital, an implied federal backstop, and a procurement relationship with its own shareholder. That is a durable handicapping of private capital allocation across chips, quantum, steel and rare earths. If you're underwriting a private challenger in any of those four sectors, the question is no longer only whether the technology works. It's whether the government is sitting on somebody else's cap table.

Which leads to the governance problem, and it has nothing to do with returns. The same Commerce Department that now holds equity across the domestic chip complex is the authority deciding whether Nvidia's China-specific B30 chip, running at roughly eighty percent of standard Blackwell peak performance, clears for export. Regulator, customer and shareholder, in one building, on one industry. And resist the reflex that state ownership makes export policy tighter. A government holding equity in the American chip complex has a financial reason to approve sales, not merely a security reason to block them. Whichever way the B30 determination goes, it is no longer a clean call, and there is no mechanism inside the process to make it one.

So the harder question is whether ownership actually produces capacity. Rare earths are the first test. Two of the largest recipients of federal rare-earth backing, MP Materials and USA Rare Earth, sit on Beijing's dual-use export control blacklist, added in June. Prices in niche rare earth elements are reported to have jumped again in mid-August on fresh fears of tighter Chinese controls. The underlying math is what matters. As of 2024, China accounted for 61 percent of global mined rare earth supply and 91 percent of global refining and processing capacity. Washington can buy a position on those two balance sheets. It cannot buy the separation and refining step, which is where the leverage actually sits. And notice how cheaply Beijing is running this. The broader control round announced back in October 2025 is still paused, and the blacklist's operational bite on either company has never been established. China is pricing the threat without exercising it. The date to watch is November, when that pause is scheduled to run out.

Now the second test, and it's power. This beat has spent the week on grid operators moving to curtail data centers ahead of ordinary customers when supply runs short, which is precisely the pressure pushing hyperscalers to go procure their own generation. Google, Kairos Power and the Tennessee Valley Authority just delivered the clearest instance of that yet. TVA will buy 50 megawatts of electricity from Kairos's Hermes 2 plant at Oak Ridge, Tennessee, which the parties bill as the first purchase of electricity from an advanced Gen IV reactor by a US utility. It's the first deployment under a broader Google framework for up to 500 megawatts of new nuclear capacity by 2035. And Hermes 2 is scheduled to begin operating in 2030. Scheduled, not committed. This is a genuine capital commitment. It is not an operating watt, and it won't be one for four more years, on a first-of-a-kind design.

Fifty megawatts also doesn't power a hyperscale campus. A single one can draw several hundred. So what's load-bearing in this deal is the template, not the electrons. And the risk isn't Google's. Google is buying a cheap option on a technology it wants to exist. The exposure sits in the utility, independent power producer and uranium equities that have been re-rated on the premise that nuclear closes the AI power gap. That re-rating is built on announcements with a 2030 delivery date, which means four years of headline risk and no cash flow in between. First-of-a-kind reactors slip as a base rate. The first visible slip won't read as a project delay. It'll read as a repricing of the whole theme.

And there's an upstream constraint underneath all of it that almost nobody is tracking. In 1985 the United States held 64 percent of global uranium enrichment capacity. By 2024 that share had fallen to under one percent, while Russia's had grown to 43 percent. The domestic build-out is real but slow: Urenco's New Mexico expansion adds 2.1 million separative work units, taking its total above 7 million SWU by 2036. That's a decade past the capex cycle it's meant to serve. Be careful with the inference, because capacity share is not the same thing as physical dependence, and there are no import figures here to tell you how much Russian SWU American reactors actually burn today. But the direction is unambiguous. SWU capacity, not gigawatts announced, is the better number to track on the nuclear-for-AI trade through the early 2030s.

So across chips, rare earths and nuclear power, the same conclusion arrives three separate ways with three different pieces of evidence. An equity stake does not touch a refining share. A signed offtake contract does not produce an electron. And a reactor order is meaningless against an enrichment base this thin. Ownership is not capacity. It's a claim on someone else's capacity, and it can be bought far faster than the capacity itself can be built.

One last thread, and it's the funding side. The same week the government's equity portfolio reached thirty companies, the US national debt crossed a record $40 trillion, and gold printed near $4,634 an ounce on Friday, August 21st, up almost six percent on the week and 36.7 percent over the past year. Don't draw a straight line from the equity program to the gold bid. The mechanism cited for gold is rising debt and a soft dollar, not industrial policy. The narrower claim is the defensible one. A sovereign that is adding equity risk to its balance sheet in the same week it's issuing the debt that funds it is a different credit than one that only writes grants and collects taxes. That changes what a Treasury holder actually owns. Gold is currently the only market visibly pricing the change.

What to watch from here: whether any of those six letters of intent actually close, and whether Beijing lets its rare-earth control pause expire in November. The first tells you whether this week's ownership push becomes real capital. The second tells you whether it would matter much if it did.

That's the briefing. Have a good day.
