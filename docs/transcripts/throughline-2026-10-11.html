Good morning, this is The Throughline for Sunday, October 11th, 2026.

Today's headline: SpaceX reportedly agreed to buy roughly eight billion dollars of low-band spectrum, and T-Mobile and AT&T shares fell between seven and eleven percent intraday on a carrier threat whose satellites aren't built yet.

Ten briefings shipped today. Semiconductors and Compute Infrastructure and AI in Biopharma R&D did not publish. We'll start with the macro backdrop, move into the AI and compute stack, then close with the applied frontier.

Strategic Power finds Washington allocating scarce energy by decree rather than letting price clear it. Treasury licensed Russian diesel back into world trade to protect pump prices, and Georgetown sanctions expert Peter Harrell says that proves the new Graham sanctions law won't bind. Ukraine, meanwhile, vows to keep burning the refineries that make that diesel. At home, a new Minnesota law makes data centers of 150 megawatts or more cover the extra cost of serving them, and lets utilities curtail new large loads during load-shedding events.

From price set by decree to price set by the bond market. Capital Markets Radar notes that the odds of an October Fed hike collapsed, but a December hike is now priced at 86 percent. So the hike was deferred, not abandoned. On Friday, oil cooled and Treasury yields rose anyway. The desk reads that as a long end driven by the hike, and possibly by supply, not just an oil premium. In credit, the AI buildout prices at its weakest link: hyperscalers borrow a few dozen basis points over Treasuries, while SoftBank's record eleven-billion-dollar junk bond for its OpenAI commitments carries a 9.75 percent coupon on its longest tranche.

Digital Money finds a leverage story of its own in crypto. Bitcoin's leverage cycle rebuilt and unwound inside a single week, far smaller than last October's nineteen-billion-dollar crash, but running on the same mechanism. ETF selling has stopped without real buying returning, and the CFTC and SEC rulemakings now in motion target exchange options and token issuance, not the derivatives leverage that drove the flush.

From crypto leverage to the private credit market. Private Credit and the Corporate Credit Cycle reports that Ares, Barings, BC Partners and Churchill have examined deals for three listed business development companies that are reviewing options including a sale. The reviews themselves are months old, so the news is the big managers circling. If deals close, direct-lending assets concentrate among the largest managers before anyone has to put a tested price on the weak loans being folded in.

Private Equity and the Deal Economy asks who captures the recovery. In continuation funds, "super carry" above the standard 20 percent has more than doubled in share, to about 35 percent of first-half volume by Evercore's count. That means more of any gain flows to the sponsor, not to the limited partners or the secondary buyers funding the deal.

Now the AI and compute stack, where the fight is over who owns the customer, not just who has the best model. Anthropic makes the model behind this show, and it features in all three of these briefings, so weigh them accordingly.

Frontier AI Lab Competition leads with reports that Elon Musk's Grok Bot now routes its hardest tasks to Anthropic's Claude, though the whole public record is a single Musk post. The desk's read is that winning a routing slot is distribution, not ownership of the customer. Meanwhile, the first independent score, from Artificial Analysis, puts Anthropic's new small model, Haiku 5.5, above Mistral's new flagship on its Intelligence Index. Haiku costs ten cents per million input tokens, so the price of a given capability level keeps falling.

AI Products and Applications carries that fight up to the agent layer, where four companies are making four different bets. Google launched a "universal agent" that orchestrates across its own models and Anthropic's, with no price or general-availability date, while OpenAI keeps chat, work and coding as separate surfaces. Anthropic is bundling API credits into subscription seats, and Nous Research reportedly raised money on a pitch to keep agents and data in-house, yet none of the four has a named production customer on record.

Enterprise Technology shows what happens when the contracts get tested. Anthropic reportedly cuts a customer's discount the moment it exhausts its token allotment, while OpenAI gives a grace window and the hyperscalers are reportedly looser still. The desk's read is that commitments, not benchmarks, are now the battleground, as SAP buys and ServiceNow builds the layer that sits around the model.

Finally, the applied frontier.

Robotics and Physical AI has the clearest data yet on embodied AI. In Figure's own tests, a pretrained model succeeded on 56 percent of zero-shot trials against 9 percent for one trained from scratch, but no lab has published a field completion rate for real deployments. On volume, China built nearly 90 percent of last year's humanoids. And Waymo raised its first-ever debt against a geofenced robotaxi service that's already running. Robotaxis are the corner of physical AI that needs the least general intelligence, and they're the corner getting financed.

Space and the Orbital Economy closes the tour with the full story behind the headline. In 2025, T-Mobile sold this spectrum to the investment firm Grain Management. Now Grain is selling it to SpaceX, which is T-Mobile's own direct-to-cell partner, so T-Mobile sold the coverage layer that SpaceX will use to compete against it. The desk thinks carriers may lean harder on AST SpaceMobile, a separate satellite provider that partners with carriers instead of competing with them. And the threat is still years from scale. SpaceX's direct-to-device constellation is unbuilt, and Starship has yet to catch a ship.

One thread is worth carrying out. Three of today's AI desks land on the same point: Musk's router, Google's universal agent, and SAP's and ServiceNow's layer bets all treat the model as a swappable input. The leverage is shifting to whoever owns the routing decision.
