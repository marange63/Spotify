Good morning, and welcome to Cautious Optimism Briefings. It's Sunday, October 25th.

All week this show has watched software incumbents give artificial intelligence away for free, folded into the seat you already pay for. Yesterday's reporting broke that pattern. We'll look at what happens when the AI vendor itself sets the price, and the price becomes the gate. Then we'll ask where, if anywhere, genuinely new budget is showing up.

Start with Anthropic and Charles Schwab. Yesterday, the trade publication RIABiz reported the commercial terms behind the Claude deal the two companies announced in September. That announcement promised Claude directly to the sixteen-thousand-plus independent registered investment advisor firms Schwab serves. The terms are narrower. According to yesterday's report, Anthropic requires a minimum of twenty Enterprise seats. RIABiz puts the price at four thousand eight hundred dollars a year, without spelling out whether that is per seat or for the whole block. Either way, the binding constraint is the twenty seats, not the sticker. And industry consultant Tim Welsh, quoted in the same piece, warns that token usage on top of the seat fee could double, triple, or even multiply the cost tenfold, depending on how heavily a firm uses the tool.

Now set that floor against Schwab's own numbers. Schwab has disclosed that eleven thousand of its sixteen thousand RIA firms manage under one hundred million dollars in assets. RIABiz reads that as eleven thousand firms sidelined. That is the publication's inference from two separate facts, not a statement from either company. And assets under management is a proxy for headcount, not a measure of it. But the direction is plain. A firm with a handful of advisors does not have twenty people to put on a seat license. So the headline product, offered to all of Schwab's advisors, meets a floor that the small end of that base was never going to clear.

Why have a floor at all? Anthropic's stated reason is specific. It recommends Enterprise over its cheaper Team tier for advisors because Enterprise includes the audit logs that support regulatory recordkeeping. That deserves a fair hearing. A two-person advisory shop carries the same recordkeeping obligation as a two-hundred-person one. So the floor may be deliberate segmentation around compliance cost, not a go-to-market blunder. Still, Anthropic's go-to-market executive, Drew Parker, was candid about the gap in a webinar covered in yesterday's report. He said he is working to see if there is a way to lower the seat count for the Enterprise plan. Then, in the same breath, he said that for the time being there is no path to that. The honest state of play is an aspiration, not a fix.

Here is why the incumbents and Anthropic are behaving so differently. Each incumbent owns an installed seat, so it can give AI away to defend the renewal. On October 20th we covered Okta folding its Agent SSO feature into core Okta SSO at no additional cost. ServiceNow did the same with its new Arc agent. And SAP is paying for AI out of its cloud gross margin, which came in at seventy-four point three percent, a dip management tied to AI investment. Anthropic, selling direct, has no installed seat to defend. It has to charge for one, and the floor is its unit-economics guardrail. Both positions are rational. One inference follows, and I'll label it as mine. The long tail of small professional-services firms is exactly where seat-based enterprise AI pricing breaks. Whoever ships a compliant small-team tier is positioned to capture it.

Schwab's chief executive, Rick Wurster, has also reframed the scope. In remarks quoted in yesterday's report, he calls this a pilot, one that will expand to additional clients and additional large language models in the coming months. Note the plural: Claude is not exclusive here. The so-what: a partnership headline built on a platform partner's customer count is a reach claim, not an adoption claim. The AI vendor's own seat economics decide who actually gets served. Standing disclosure, as always: Anthropic makes the model behind this show.

Contrast that with the other way Anthropic is getting into enterprises. On October 13th, Workday and Anthropic announced that Workday's Sana agents and Claude now exchange tasks and context through the Model Context Protocol, an open standard. Sana handles the long-running HR and finance workflows. Claude handles individual reasoning steps inside them. Workday counts more than eleven thousand five hundred customer organizations, including over sixty-five percent of the Fortune 500. That is Workday's total installed base, not a count of who uses the integration. The integration is in early access, with general availability expected late this year, and no usage data exists. But notice the structural difference. Riding inside an incumbent's agent layer over an open protocol avoids the seat floor that direct licensing through a platform partner runs into. It's unproven today, but it sidesteps the exact friction that is throttling the Schwab deal.

If AI pricing is still being worked out on the vendor side, the next question is whether anyone is adding new budget, rather than moving dollars already earmarked for software. Yesterday's thesis on this show was that enterprise AI is redistributing spend inside incumbents, not expanding it. The one place the channel suggests otherwise is security.

Evercore ISI runs a quarterly survey of cybersecurity channel partners. This week's read, as reported by financial media, found a strengthening budget outlook across the sector. It named CrowdStrike and Palo Alto Networks as the top beneficiaries of AI-driven security spending. And it found Okta showing the strongest pipeline momentum of the vendors surveyed. That is the same Okta that gave Agent SSO away just days earlier. Now it shows the strongest pipeline in an independent channel read. That is consistent with bundling defending, and maybe even growing, the core franchise. One survey can't establish causation, but it's a thread worth pulling. The survey also landed two days after the October 21st disclosure of PHP2Shell, a pair of critical flaws in a language used on roughly seventy percent of websites. Nothing beyond timing links the two.

Here's the skeptical turn, because it matters. A channel survey measures how resellers feel about pipeline, not revenue that has been booked or recognized. And this one reaches us only through financial-media paraphrase. The stocks moved anyway. 24/7 Wall St. reported the surveyed names up about four percent on Friday morning. But these names already trade on broader AI-financing sentiment, so a one-morning pop on a sell-side note is momentum, not confirmation. The real test comes when CrowdStrike, Palo Alto, and Okta report, and either show that pipeline converting into bookings or don't. The so-what: security is this week's leading candidate for genuinely incremental AI-era spending, but it stays a candidate until the numbers print.

Two dates to watch. ServiceNow reports October 28th. It's the first incumbent print to test whether AI bundled free into existing seats shows up in renewals rather than marketing decks. Palantir reports November 2nd, the cleaner read on commercial AI demand. And on Schwab, watch whether Anthropic finds any path to a lower seat count, and whether Schwab adds other models to the pilot, as its chief executive says it will.

Put together: the incumbents are pricing AI at zero to protect what they already have. The AI vendor selling direct has to charge for what it doesn't have. And security is the one place this week where the money might actually be new.
