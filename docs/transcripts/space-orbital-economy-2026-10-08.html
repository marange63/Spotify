Cautious Optimism Briefings

Space & the Orbital Economy

Transcript · October 8, 2026

Good morning, it's October 8th, 2026, and this is your space and orbital economy briefing.

On October 2nd we asked a simple question on this show. Is Washington going to be a brake on SpaceX's orbital ambitions, or an accelerant? This week gave us the answer.

Start with October 6th. According to SatNews, the FCC granted SpaceX a new fifteen-thousand-satellite Very Low Earth Orbit constellation. It is dedicated to direct-to-device, satellite-to-cellular service, flying between 326 and 335 kilometers. KeepTrack reports that this approval and SpaceX's existing Gen2 authorization together cover about thirty thousand satellites. But the satellite count isn't the marginal story. The marginal story is a waiver. Per KeepTrack, the FCC also let SpaceX offer satellite wireless service without entering a spectrum-leasing agreement with a terrestrial mobile operator. The exact bands and conditions that waiver covers aren't yet clear from the public record, so treat its full scope as an open question. The direction, though, is not in doubt. SpaceX no longer needs a carrier's spectrum lease to sell satellite wireless. On October 2nd and again on October 7th, we told you the decisive lever in direct-to-cell was spectrum rules and carrier alliances. For SpaceX, the carrier-alliance half of that lever just loosened.

Don't read that as SpaceX now owning direct-to-cell. A waiver removes one partnership requirement. It doesn't supply handset spectrum depth, indoor coverage, or a retail channel, and the carriers still control those. For T-Mobile, AT&T and Verizon, the change is in the bargaining. A partnership with SpaceX becomes a negotiation over price and distribution, not a regulatory necessity. AT&T's John Stankey called one SpaceX plan "not a viable strategy" on October 2nd. That now looks more like negotiating posture.

So who loses leverage? The obvious candidate is AST SpaceMobile, the pure-play direct-to-cell operator whose model runs through carrier partnerships. AST's latest 8-K says it is preparing to initiate beta services with select strategic partners. Beta with select partners is not commercial service, and there's no subscriber or revenue number behind it. There are two readings of what SpaceX's waiver means for AST. In the first, carriers have less reason to lean on AST as a safety valve. That narrows AST's moat to spectrum depth and coverage quality. In the second, carriers facing a SpaceX that can go around them lean harder into AST, as a counterweight that keeps them in the deal. Both readings are live. What's clear is that AST's carrier-partner model now has to win on merit. It can no longer win on regulatory necessity, because that necessity just eased for its biggest rival.

Now, the same day the FCC acted, SpaceX told us how big it wants to go. SpaceX's newly published Space Safety page, as reported by KeepTrack and quoted widely on X, says the company plans to operate up to one hundred thousand Starlink satellites. It adds up to one million Starmind AI-compute satellites. Attribute that carefully. This is SpaceX's own forward-looking statement on its own site, not a new FCC filing granted this week. The only FCC paper trail behind either number is the January Starmind filing this show has already covered, and it remains pending. As of KeepTrack's October 2nd count, SpaceX had 11,156 Starlink satellites on orbit and 11,142 of them working. Set that against the hundred-thousand ceiling and you get, by our own arithmetic, roughly a ninefold gap. A planning ceiling is an option on orbital slots and spectrum. It is not a build plan or a capital commitment.

The Starmind side also got its first real architectural detail this week. KeepTrack quotes SpaceX vice president Michael Nicolls describing the compute satellites as tightly clustered groups. In his words, that's "ten-ish satellites connected with ten terabits or so of bandwidth between them." He said SpaceX needs clusters in tight formation "to get enough coherent compute to run AI models efficiently." That reframes the whole concept. The unit of compute isn't the satellite, it's the cluster. So a million-satellite ceiling implies something closer to a hundred thousand compute nodes. Again, that's our arithmetic, not SpaceX's. Compare Google, which already has a real TPU test satellite flying, as we covered yesterday. SpaceX, for now, has a cluster concept and no compute hardware in orbit.

Here's one inference worth flagging as ours, not reported. A hundred-thousand-satellite ceiling, plus a faster-moving FCC, raises the cost of not filing for every rival constellation. Expect matching claims to protect slot and spectrum priority. That turns the land grab into a filing race, decided in the end by who can actually launch.

And the regulator had cleared the path a week earlier. On September 30th, the FCC voted that space-based operations, including launch, deployment, and operating spacecraft, are not major federal actions requiring review under NEPA. That's the second leg of the accelerant pattern. First the FCC stripped environmental review from its space licensing. Six days later it cleared a fifteen-thousand-satellite direct-to-device system and waived the carrier-lease requirement. Note that the NEPA move is sector-wide, not a SpaceX favor. But here's the limit, and it's an important one. Per Via Satellite, launch activities remain subject to NEPA review by the FAA. So the permission bottleneck didn't disappear. It moved from the spectrum regulator to the launch regulator, and then to launch supply and capital. That is exactly where SpaceX's lead over everyone else is largest. Deregulation here compounds concentration rather than diluting it.

Which makes this fundamentally a rocket question. Starship's 14th flight, on September 28th, deployed all 26 Starlink V3 satellites it carried. SpaceX says each V3 satellite adds 1 terabit per second of capacity, or 26 terabits on that single mission. Now fill a hundred-thousand-satellite ceiling at 26 satellites per Starship flight. By our arithmetic, you land on thousands of flights. Treat that as an order-of-magnitude illustration only, since Falcon 9 also launches Starlink. Still, it tells you the real constraint is cadence and capital, not paperwork. And on Monday we noted that SpaceX's post-flight findings and the next flight date hadn't surfaced. Ten days after the flight, neither has appeared in our research.

Here's the capital-markets anchor. Per SpaceX's S-1, Starlink generated eleven point four billion dollars in 2025, about 61 percent of total company revenue. That's a 2025 figure, not a current quarter. Licenses are not revenue either. But it tells you what this week's approvals attach to. Every satellite authorization is capacity for the business line the SPCX equity is priced on. And the bigger Starlink's license stack grows, the more separable it looks. That keeps the Starlink spin-out question alive as a real capital-allocation lever, not just speculation.

So, pulling it together. The regulatory question is answered this week: the FCC is accelerating SpaceX, not restraining it. What's still paper is the hundred-thousand and one-million satellite ceilings, the Starmind cluster concept, and AST's beta service. And even the most mature part of this system can slip. On Monday we flagged the Space Development Agency's fourth Tranche 1 transport-layer mission, 21 Northrop Grumman satellites on a Falcon 9. It scrubbed on October 5th at about T-minus 58 seconds on an automated abort. The vehicle and payload were reported healthy, and the mission is now targeting October 8th. That's not a crisis. It's a reminder that permission is not the same thing as cadence, even on the workhorse rocket in the fleet.

Five things to watch. First, the next Starship flight date and its post-flight data. Second, any move by SpaceX to price or sell direct-to-cell service, or to renegotiate with carriers, now that it doesn't need a spectrum lease. Third, matching satellite-ceiling filings from rivals defending their orbital slots. Fourth, a launch date for AST's BlueBirds 14 through 16. And fifth, whether that Tranche 1 mission finally flies.

That's today's briefing. I'm wishing you clear skies, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
