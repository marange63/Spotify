Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Credit & the Corporate Credit Cycle

Transcript · August 23, 2026

Good morning, it's Sunday, August 23rd, 2026. This is your private credit briefing.

This show has spent five straight sessions on one question: are private marks stale, or just honest and slow. On Tuesday we looked at a Blue Owl loan sale that cleared at 99.7 cents on the dollar, declined to call it proof either way, and said watch for the next forced event. We got it this week. It doesn't show a drift. It shows a cliff.

The name is Loparex, a portfolio company inside Blue Owl's flagship business development company, ticker OBDC. In the first quarter, Loparex's second lien was marked at 63 cents on the dollar and its first lien at 99 — essentially par. By the second quarter, the second lien was at 5 cents and the first lien at 34.

Blue Owl gave the mechanism on its earnings call, and it's a transaction, not a slow deterioration. The company had been pursuing a recapitalization that would have brought in fresh equity, and that deal fell apart. Loparex then missed its June 30th interest payment on the second lien and skipped first-lien amortization. It entered a forbearance agreement with lenders, set to expire August 17th, with no resolution disclosed since.

OBDC's net unrealized losses for the quarter came to 99.4 million dollars, led by that one name. How much of that sits outside Loparex isn't broken out, so treat the rest of the book as unresolved, not clean. Net asset value fell to 14.26 dollars a share from 14.41 — a decline the company attributes primarily, not entirely, to that single markdown.

The lesson isn't that the mark was fake. A 65-point move in one quarter is arguably evidence the process works once new information arrives. The lesson is that loss recognition here is a step function tied to a transaction, not a continuous drift. A quarterly NAV can be honestly produced and still tell you nothing about the risk sitting between two report dates.

That changes what the maturity wall is. If marks reset on transactions rather than on the economy, NAV damage clusters around the refinancing and amendment calendar instead of around the cycle. The wall becomes a mark-recognition schedule. The timing of the damage is forecastable from the amendment calendar even when its size isn't.

Set that against the control case: Ares Capital, one of the two largest listed BDCs, is the slow-grind version of the same cycle. Its NAV eased to 19.35 dollars from 19.59, and non-accruals rose to 405 million dollars — 1.4 percent of the portfolio at fair value, against 1.2 percent a year earlier, so that's a year-over-year comparison. Core earnings held flat at 47 cents and the dividend stayed at 48. That's a manageable cycle: rising stress absorbed without touching the payout.

But watch the payout, because it breaks before the losses do. Blue Owl's BDC had already cut its base dividend 16 percent, to 31 cents, earlier this year. PIK income — interest taken as more debt instead of cash — ran at 10.7 percent of OBDC's investment income this quarter, which is this fund in this quarter, not an industry figure. With a shrinking asset base and lower new-money yields, the next cuts are arithmetic on the numerator rather than a response to realized losses. Distribution coverage leads loss recognition here; it doesn't follow it.

And the book is shrinking. OBDC funded 219 million dollars of new loans against 747 million of repayments, leaving the portfolio at 15 billion, down from 16.9 billion a year ago. Management said refinancing activity has, in their words, ground to a halt in this wider-spread environment. That's the 82 percent collapse in non-traded BDC sales we flagged Friday, now visible inside a filing rather than in an aggregate.

Before calling the asset class mismarked, though, the Fed just put out data that argues the other way. A staff note published August 11th sized the private-credit and leveraged-loan markets at roughly 1.4 trillion dollars apiece as of the end of last year. The borrower comparison is what matters. Private-credit borrowers carry a median revenue of 223 million dollars against 902 million on the leveraged-loan side, and 5.0 times debt to EBITDA against 3.2 times.

That's a real revision to how this show has been framing things. A default rate more than double high yield's isn't by itself evidence of concealment. It's largely a worse borrower pool. That's my inference, not the Fed's — the note documents the borrower gap, it doesn't adjudicate pricing. And it's a partial defence, not an acquittal: composition explains why the rate is higher, and says nothing about whether the spread compensates, or about the mark timing we just saw in Loparex.

The Fed also quantifies why a pullback here wouldn't stay contained. Roughly a third of borrowers have only ever used the leveraged-loan market, and another third have used both. Switching into private credit hit about 50 percent during the 2023 tightening. Software, at 21 percent of private-credit issuance, shows muted capacity to switch back. That's why the Fed's framing is that a pullback would mitigate, not eliminate, the broader financing effects.

That read gets one live test. Fitch's private-credit default rate — the more-than-double-high-yield figure we've cited before — is directional only across differently built indices, so hold it loosely. Technology software is now the lowest-default large sector at 1.2 percent, down from 2.3, which cuts against the AI-obsolescence story this show ran earlier in the week. Though it's also the sector with the least room to refinance elsewhere. And more than half of the quarter's default events were maturity extensions rather than the interest deferrals that drove the twelve months to April. So the forbearance instrument is rotating from the coupon to the maturity date, not disappearing.

Composition tells you how often these loans break. It doesn't tell you what you get when one does.

For that, look at First Brands. The auto-parts maker's Chapter 11 rescue loan sits first in the capital structure, and it's still quoted around 16 cents on the dollar — an indicative level, not a cleared trade on size. Compare that with the 99.7-cent Blue Owl sale from Tuesday. Two senior claims, two completely different prices, and the difference is whether a credible recovery process is running underneath.

The debtors' own plan, defended in closing arguments on August 7th, puts numbers on that gap. By their estimate, the litigation trust has to recover about 1.85 billion dollars before the rescue loan and administrative claims are even paid in full. At 3 billion of proceeds, unsecured litigation-trust claimants still recover only about 8.4 percent. Those are the debtors' figures, not court findings, and no confirmation ruling has been issued as of this recording.

Discount the 16 cents a little — part of that price is a liquidity discount from holders who can't warehouse a multi-year lawsuit. Seniority is worth what the estate is worth, and when the estate is a lawsuit, priority converts to almost nothing.

The same failure mode surfaced at the SEC this month. On August 18th it charged Tricolor's former CEO, CFO, and senior finance director with civil fraud. The complaint alleges subprime auto loans were pledged to multiple asset-backed deals at once, with performance metrics doctored to make defaulted loans look current. Those are allegations, and Tricolor is auto ABS rather than corporate direct lending — but it's the same failure as First Brands: collateral verification lagging origination volume. Expect third-party collateral audits to become an underwriting differentiator rather than a checkbox.

If working these credits out takes time and capital, the question is who funds the next book. CLO formation, the other channel that recycles leveraged-loan risk, priced 30.2 billion dollars across 65 deals last quarter — the weakest since the third quarter of 2023. Be careful with the cause. It's arbitrage economics, loan spreads too tight against CLO liability costs, which is a return problem for CLO equity rather than a buyers' strike on credit. Still, with the retail wrapper collapsing at the same time, two of the three replacement funding channels are contracting at once.

And the compensation is going the wrong way. Direct-lending asset yields fell below 10 percent for the first time in three years, per Bloomberg reporting, against a borrower pool running 5.0 times levered. Some of that is Fed cuts passing through floating-rate paper rather than spread compression. Either way, new vintages are being written at less yield than seasoned ones that are already producing non-accruals.

So where does the cycle stand. The marks aren't fake, but they resolve in steps tied to the deal calendar. The default gap over public credit is mostly a worse borrower, not a cover-up. The unresolved risk is recovery — nothing here has yet been forced to prove what a broken senior claim actually pays. Watch the First Brands confirmation, the next non-accrual and PIK prints, and whether third-quarter CLO formation and retail BDC sales stabilize or set another low.

That's the briefing. Talk to you next time.
