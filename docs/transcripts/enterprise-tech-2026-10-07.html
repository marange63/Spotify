Good morning, and welcome to Cautious Optimism Briefings for October 7, 2026.

Today's question is where control sits in enterprise software. The control point is moving above the individual application. Nobody has yet shown that customers will pay for that new layer.

Our lead comes from SAP. At SAP Connect on October 6, the company introduced Joule Work, a new AI user interface. Joule Work sits alongside Joule Desktop and the Autonomous Enterprise architecture, which SAP first showed at Sapphire in May. SAP calls this the beginning of a customer rollout, not a finished launch.

It helps to be precise about what Joule used to be. It was a conversational chat client. SAP is now repositioning it as a work layer. In SiliconANGLE's description, Joule Work provides the framework to bring AI assistants into customer workflows, working with both SAP and non-SAP applications. SAP's release adds that, through the Agent2Agent protocol, Joule can connect with third-party AI and agents. The release does not name a single partner platform.

Why does that matter? For two decades, the application suite won by owning the system of record. The ERP holds the ledger, the purchase orders, and the payroll. Seats followed the record. My read: if the agent layer sits above the applications, that logic weakens. Value and pricing power move toward whoever owns the place where the work gets done. That read is conditional. It only holds if customers actually route work through Joule across systems that SAP does not own.

So what evidence exists? Very little, and the release is candid about it. SAP says Joule Work is already live internally with 110,000 employees. The company reports a 20 percent productivity gain, which is its own figure, not an independent measurement. Those are SAP's own staff and processes, so they show internal use, not external demand. On the customer side, the release says only that a select group of customers also is using Joule Work in their business environments. It gives no count. It discloses no pricing, no licensing terms, no AI units, and no consumption metrics. Pricing is therefore an open question.

There is also a competing reading. The defensive interpretation is that Joule Work protects SAP's seats. If Microsoft Copilot Studio or Salesforce Agentforce becomes the layer where users work, SAP risks being reduced to a back-end database. A cross-application agent layer is one way to prevent that. The offensive reading is that SAP wants to be the orchestrator of the whole enterprise workflow. Both readings explain the same announcement. The evidence that would separate them is specific. We would need non-SAP customers using Joule Work in production, and we would need SAP to charge for cross-system work in a way it can report.

This matters beyond SAP, because it extends a thread the show has followed for a week. Palo Alto Networks has pushed agent permissions into hardware. IBM has pushed them into self-hosted deployments, and AWS onto the cloud host. SAP now brings that fight to the application suite itself. Every vendor in that list has announced. None has yet disclosed paid, production adoption at scale. That is the same gap, and it now runs through the application layer too.

So what does this mean for an investor or an enterprise buyer? Treat SAP's announcement as a product milestone with a credible strategy behind it. Do not treat it as an adoption result. The next useful evidence will come from SAP's next earnings call, where a customer count, an active-user figure, or a pricing construct would change the grade.

A second control fight is happening one layer down. Integrators are buying the identity and security software their engagements depend on. That is a different layer from SAP's, but it is the same contest.

On October 5, the government IT contractor SAIC said it had bought a small encryption and credential-software firm, Information Security Corporation, known as ISC. Executives call it a tuck-in. The terms were not disclosed, so the deal cannot be sized. ISC makes public key infrastructure, encryption, and credential management software. According to Washington Technology, ISC describes its technology as deeply embedded across the U.S. intelligence community and the Defense Department. That is ISC's own characterization, not an independent measure of market share. The government contractor SAIC gives zero-trust identity and post-quantum cryptography as its rationale. The post-quantum piece may be positioning ahead of federal migration requirements, rather than a reflection of current contract spend. There are no revenue or contract figures for it.

The larger version of the same move is Accenture's. In June, Accenture agreed to buy a majority stake in Dragos, and all of runZero and NetRise. The combined value is about 4.2 billion dollars, according to the trade site Consulting.us. All three are operational technology and industrial security firms. Accenture is assembling an industrial security stack it can bundle into consulting work. That gives Accenture a product to lead with, not just people to staff. My read is that the bet is on industrial clients buying the software and the services as one package. Whether every piece of the deal has closed is not confirmed. The split of the price among the three companies has not been disclosed.

Last Friday, October 2, we discussed Accenture's record mega-deal bookings. Our read then was that integrators had not yet been disrupted by AI. This is a different signal. The question now is not whether integrators win deals. It is whether they start to own the software inside those deals. That changes the economics. Owned software earns recurring revenue, while consulting revenue scales with headcount. The same logic applied to Schneider Electric's agreement to buy PTC, which we covered on October 6. Software assets are clearing to buyers that have an operating reason to hold them. An integrator that owns the credential stack, or the industrial security stack, does not need to resell someone else's product.

My read is that standalone security vendors face a real squeeze. Independent public key infrastructure and industrial security vendors may increasingly reach government and industrial buyers through integrator-owned stacks. That narrows their direct channel. It also creates a channel conflict for Accenture. By owning security software, Accenture now competes with some of its own technology partners. Whether that tension becomes a problem is an open question.

So what does the integrator segment tell us? Services firms are turning their bookings into owned software. The early evidence is deal-level, not revenue-level. Small deals like ISC show the direction. Large deals like the Accenture roll-up show the scale. The test that would upgrade it is an integrator reporting software revenue from these assets as its own line.

Let me tie the two halves together. SAP wants to own the agent layer above its applications. The integrators want to own the security and identity software beneath the services they sell. Both moves aim at the same prize, which is the control point in enterprise technology. Neither has yet proven that customers will pay for that control as a separate line item.

Two markers will tell us which way this goes. First, watch whether SAP discloses Joule or agent customer counts, usage figures, or a pricing construct on its next earnings call. Second, watch whether more security and identity software is bought by integrators rather than by other software companies. If both happen, the control point is moving. If neither does, the announcements remain announcements.
