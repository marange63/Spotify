Cautious Optimism Briefings

Frontier AI Lab Competition

Transcript · October 17, 2026

Good morning. It's Saturday, October 17th, 2026, and this is your frontier AI labs briefing.

This week's pattern is the distributors moving to make the labs underneath them replaceable. Microsoft is building its own substitute. Google is routing around any single lab. Meta is giving away the tier just below its best model. On Thursday, this desk said that for a player that owns the customer, the model is a swappable input. That was about Elon Musk. Today the same logic shows up at three much bigger distributors at once, so let's take them in turn.

Start with Microsoft, because it's the lead. On October 15th, Microsoft AI said it had finished pre-training MAI-Base-2. It's a mixture-of-experts model with two point eight trillion total parameters and eighty billion active. Microsoft's own blog states the training run plainly: sixteen thousand Nvidia GB200 GPUs, over thirty-eight days, in August and September. By frontier standards, that's a modest run. That supports the efficiency story. It also raises the question of how far a model trained this way gets from a good base to a frontier product. The model is still in post-training, with no release date, no price, and no public benchmark.

There are two separate claims here, and they carry very different weight. The one Microsoft actually published says its training recipe is, in its words, between four point two and twelve point three times more FLOPs-efficient than DeepSeek-V4-Pro-Base. Microsoft calls that the strongest open base model. That number is on the record. But it's Microsoft grading its own homework, on evaluations Microsoft chose. And every model it compares against in that post is open-weight. OpenAI doesn't appear in it at all.

The headline claim, the one making news, is much less solid. According to reporting relayed by 24/7 Wall St., AI chief Mustafa Suleyman told staff at an internal town hall that the new model, quote, "beats the one running OpenAI's GPT-5.6 while using about a quarter of the computing resources." That comes from a private meeting, not a publication. It has no public benchmark behind it. Neither Microsoft nor OpenAI has confirmed or disputed it. And even if it holds, it's a base-model comparison. Post-training, tool use, and serving decide what customers actually get. So Microsoft's documented record shows an efficiency edge over an open Chinese model. Its OpenAI claim is, for now, a rhetorical flex aimed at a negotiating table, not a verified result.

That negotiating table matters because this isn't a one-off. Across this show's briefings, it's the third step in a Microsoft insourcing arc. On Monday, our enterprise technology desk covered a report from The Information that Microsoft had cut its projected internal spend on Anthropic's Claude by more than a third. Yesterday, that desk covered Microsoft's own Maia 200 chip now serving production Copilot workloads. Now Microsoft has a frontier-scale base model of its own. Its stated plan is to license the weights to, quote, "trusted partners through Foundry." There's no date yet for Copilot or the API. Put together, Microsoft is building substitutes at the chip layer, the usage layer, and now the model layer, aimed at both OpenAI and Anthropic.

This is also a stated strategy, not an improvisation. On July 29th, the day of Microsoft's fiscal fourth-quarter earnings call, Satya Nadella posted on X that, quote, "every model is substitutable." He framed it as business continuity and resilience. MAI-Base-2 is the first concrete in-house model behind that line.

But this is leverage, not divorce. Microsoft still holds an OpenAI stake reportedly valued at a hundred and thirty-five billion dollars, plus rights to OpenAI's technology through 2032. A credible in-house model doesn't end that relationship. It changes who holds the upper hand inside it. My inference is that it weakens OpenAI's pricing power inside its single biggest distribution channel. And it lands in the same week that OpenAI's reported revenue baseline was marked down to roughly fifty billion dollars annualized. The takeaway: OpenAI's largest partner is turning it from a dependency into one supplier among several, on the strength of a claim no outsider has checked.

Microsoft's answer to lab dependence is to build. Google's answer is to route. At its Gemini at Work event on October 8th, Google Cloud announced what it calls the Gemini agent. That's older news, but it's the cleanest statement of the routing strategy. Google's own post says the agent runs each job on the model that fits best, orchestrating, quote, "across our Gemini family of models and Claude models from Anthropic today." The stated goal is, quote, "to deliver optimal quality and lower your costs."

A disclosure here: Anthropic makes the model behind this show, and its position runs through both the Microsoft and Google stories this week. Google's agent is in preview for financial services and legal. There's no general-availability date, no pricing, and no data on how often the router actually picks Claude over Gemini. A router that quietly defaults to Gemini can commoditize a rival while looking neutral. The takeaway: in Google's enterprise stack, a rival lab's model is a line item to be selected or not. Google is betting that owning the orchestration layer matters more than owning every model inside it.

Meta's answer is the third one: give away the tier just below your best model. On Tuesday, October 13th, this desk flagged a watch item. Would Meta publish weights for any Muse model, or had it stepped away from open weights for good? Midweek, Meta answered, with outlets dating the release to October 15th or 16th. It released downloadable weights for Muse Spark Open. That's a mixture-of-experts model with eight hundred sixteen billion total parameters, forty-two billion active, and up to a one-million-token context window.

That corrects our own framing from Tuesday and Thursday. Meta hasn't abandoned open weights. It's running two tiers. Its best model, Wonder Full, stays closed inside the paid Muse Code subscription. The strong-but-not-best tier goes to developers. Two cautions on the word open. First, nothing we've seen establishes the license terms, so call it open weights, not necessarily open source. Second, the logic is commoditizing the complement, not a reversal. Spark Open undercuts the layer where Chinese open-weight labs and API resellers make money, while Meta keeps its best model as a billable product.

Now be skeptical of the numbers Meta cites for Spark Open. Its win over Thinking Machines Lab's Inkling on JobBench, sixty-one point eight to thirty-one point one, comes from Meta's own evaluations against a non-frontier comparator. The more revealing number cuts against Meta. On Terminal-Bench 2.1, a coding-agent benchmark, Spark Open scores seventy-six point four. Moonshot's Kimi K3 scores eighty-five. Neither result has been independently reproduced. The takeaway: even Meta's largest open-weight release, by Meta's own reported numbers, doesn't take the open coding lead from a Chinese lab. That matches what the live DeepSWE leaderboard showed this desk on Monday.

Pulling it together by dimension. Distribution power is rising for Microsoft, Google, and Meta. When distributors build, route, or give away near-frontier models, the commoditizing pressure moves up the capability curve. Durable lab pricing power narrows to whatever sits clearly above what a distributor can replace. That squeezes OpenAI hardest, inside Azure and Copilot. On ecosystem control, Meta now plays both sides with its two tiers. On open-weight coding, Chinese labs still set the bar.

And on compute, the signal is two-sided. Microsoft's modest, efficient training run cuts against the idea that every competitive model needs a historic-scale buildout. But more companies training their own base models means more training runs in total. That's not simply a bearish signal for chip demand.

Three things worth watching. First, whether MAI-Base-2 ships with a public benchmark or a price, and whether it shows up inside Copilot. Second, what license Spark Open actually carries, and where independent evaluators place it once they test it themselves. Third, whether Google publishes any data on how often its router actually picks Claude over its own Gemini models.

That's the briefing. Stay cautiously optimistic.
