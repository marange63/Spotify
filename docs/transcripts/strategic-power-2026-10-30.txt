Good morning. It's Friday, October 30th, and this is your strategic power briefing.

This week the contests over strategic power were fought on paper, not on the ground. A spectrum proposal, a chip ban that leaks, a defense budget funded by shuffling money rather than raising it, a minerals declaration with no tonnage attached. In every case, the gap between the rule and the physical reality is where the real advantage sits. Let's start where that gap is widest.

Yesterday, Thursday, October 29th, the FCC voted to advance a proposal that would open an additional 482 megahertz of spectrum to satellite service beamed directly to ordinary phones. By the FCC's own description, that would more than double the spectrum available for this use and raise the number of eligible bands from five to thirteen. There's a small companion auction alongside it. None of this is final. Comments run for two to three months after the proposal is published in the Federal Register. So what happened Thursday is a proposal advanced for public comment, not a spectrum grant, and real service is still a long regulatory road away.

The incumbent carriers are fighting it hard. AT&T, Verizon, and T-Mobile, through the CTIA industry group, asked the FCC to pull nearly 300 megahertz of spectrum, the 2.5 and 3.45 gigahertz bands, out of the proposal entirely. Their technical case isn't vague hand-waving. Those bands use time-division duplexing, while the direct-to-device satellite systems in question generally use frequency-division duplexing. CTIA's argument is that satellite signals could therefore interfere with the terrestrial networks. T-Mobile has said that interference could wipe out up to 96 percent of a cell site's uplink coverage area. Treat that as the carrier's own worst-case claim, not an independent engineering finding. CTIA itself frames this not as opposition to satellite phone service, but as a fight to keep carrier consent and control over their own spectrum. And notably, even reporting published after the vote can't yet confirm whether those contested bands survived in the text commissioners actually adopted. That detail remains open.

Here's the tension worth naming. Verizon's CEO told investors on the company's October 26th earnings call that satellite service "just can't compete with a terrestrial network." If that's true, the fight to carve nearly 300 megahertz out of this proposal is hard to square with genuine indifference. Either the threat is more real than the public dismissal lets on, or the carriers are defending the principle of consent over their spectrum. My read is the second matters more. What the carriers really fear is losing veto power over who else can transmit on their frequencies.

Meanwhile SpaceX is stacking layers, not waiting on this proceeding alone. On October 8th, SpaceX agreed to buy Grain Management's nationwide 800 megahertz low-band spectrum portfolio, up to 14 megahertz of paired spectrum. SpaceX says that low band would help signals reach indoors. On October 23rd, SpaceX separately filed asking the FCC to add the 902 to 928 megahertz band to this same proceeding, arguing simply, "Doing so now saves time later." Individually, none of these moves is decisive. Together, they look like a company assembling every layer of mobile access it can reach before the regulatory outcome is settled. That means low-band for indoor reach, mid-band through this proposal, and its own constellation on top. The Grain purchase is also a quiet tell. SpaceX itself appears to see an indoor coverage problem that satellite mid-band alone doesn't solve.

The second-order effect, as inference, is on bargaining power. If SpaceX wins broad mid-band access, the carriers face a new counterparty, and towers in remote areas lose some of their strategic value. There's a security layer too. On Wednesday we covered Elon Musk's claim that SpaceX restricted Starlink service to Ukraine because military use broke its terms of service. In effect, one company's terms of service functioned as a military allocation decision. Now the FCC is deciding how much of the civilian mobile spectrum SpaceX can reach. So what matters here isn't who wins this particular rulemaking. It's that a private satellite operator is now a credible contestant for mobile spectrum, a strategic input that used to belong to a carrier oligopoly.

If spectrum is a rule being written, chip export controls are a rule already in force, and last week brought evidence on how well it holds. On October 23rd, it was reported that Israeli security firm Lava had identified 32 Nvidia Blackwell Ultra B300 GPUs through monitoring endpoints linked to China Telecom, a state-owned carrier. Those chips are barred from sale to China under US export controls. The method matters as much as the finding. Lava identified the chips remotely, using Nvidia's own Data Center GPU Manager Exporter, a telemetry tool that was reachable from the open internet. The China Telecom link is a network-address association, not proof of ownership or physical location. Nvidia's response was that diverted products make up "less than 0.5 percent of deployed inventory" in China. That's an unaudited company claim about an undisclosed base, so it can't be independently checked.

The real story is that enforcement here happened by accident. Nobody was hunting for these chips with a verification system. They were found because a monitoring tool was left exposed. That cuts two ways. As inference, it strengthens the case for building deliberate location verification into the control regime itself, turning an accidental discovery into a designed enforcement tool. That would raise compliance costs for Nvidia and its server partners. But the honest skeptic's read isn't that the ban has failed. Thirty-two chips are a rounding error against China's total compute base. A ban that leaks at the margin can still function if it denies China the scale needed for frontier training runs. So the takeaway is less about whether the ban works today, and more about how thin current enforcement is, and how much of it depends on luck.

Rules on paper versus capacity in practice is also the question for Europe's defense pledges. Around the UK's Autumn Budget on October 28th, Defence Secretary John Healey set 2030 as the target date for British defense spending to reach 3 percent of GDP, and reaffirmed NATO's 3.5 percent goal for 2035. Healey said he is, in his words, "fully funding the Defence Investment Plan, including through capital reprioritization across Whitehall." In other words, he closed a funding gap by moving money within government rather than borrowing more or raising new taxes for defense. The detailed roadmap to actually hit 3 percent is deferred to next year's spending review. So a date is now real. A funded multi-year path to it is not, at least not yet.

Fiscal constraint is shaping who builds what, and here it shapes a government's choice. Britain, a G7 economy, chose to reallocate rather than borrow for defense. Other NATO members face their own debt pressures and may need the same model. If they do, the alliance-wide 3.5 percent target is harder to fund at scale than the headline suggests. For European defense primes, a dated UK target supports order books on a horizon independent of US politics. But because it's funded by reshuffling, the near-term procurement uplift may be smaller than the percentage implies. And reprioritization means some other capital budget, possibly infrastructure, loses.

The same gap between declaration and capacity shows up in critical minerals. At an October 28th Tokyo summit, Japan and Germany issued a joint statement expressing "grave concerns on the increasing use of economic coercion, such as arbitrary export restrictions and retaliatory measures on critical minerals." The language is clearly aimed at Beijing without naming it. Three documents came out of that summit in total. None of them carried a tonnage figure, a funding number, or a delivery schedule. Two of the most magnet-exposed manufacturing economies in the world just agreed, in writing, to worry together.

And the coercion they're worried about has a clock on it. Beijing's one-year pause on its broader export controls, including on rare earths, is due to lapse around November 10th, eleven days from now. As of Monday, China had published no extension. Washington says the pause runs to January 10th, but Beijing has published no text that matches. So the Tokyo declarations land in front of a live deadline, and diplomatic alignment is accumulating faster than actual separation capacity.

Three things to watch into next week. First, OPEC+ meets November 1st. At its early-October meeting it held November output targets steady, and the outcome of this one isn't known yet. Second, watch the FCC docket for whether those contested 2.5 and 3.45 gigahertz bands actually stay in. Third, watch for any regulatory response to the B300 finding, since accidental discoveries like this one tend to get turned into policy.

The through-line across all four stories: this week, power moved on paper before it moved in the physical world, and the space between the two is exactly where the next fight will happen.

That's your briefing for October 30th. Stay cautiously optimistic, and have a good weekend.
