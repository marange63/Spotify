Good morning, it's Thursday, August 20th, and this is your private equity briefing.

This week gave us a case study in the gap between narrative and cash. On August 19th, Bloomberg reported that private equity buyers may finally be joining the broader M&A surge that has put three and a half trillion dollars of deals on the board this year, a surge so far dominated by corporate acquirers. That framing comes from Goldman Sachs' global co-head of investment banking, Matt McClure, who described the confidence behind dealmaking as "spreading" from corporate boardrooms into buyout firms. Note carefully what that is and what it isn't. Strategics have driven the surge, and Goldman says sponsors look ready to join it. There is no private-equity-specific dollar figure behind that claim, and no data confirming it yet. It's a banker's read on mood, not a booked number.

Set that mood against the number we actually have. US private equity exits totaled a hundred and two point six billion dollars in the second quarter, down roughly forty-six percent from the first quarter. Asset sales to corporate buyers, the exact channel Bloomberg says is about to pull sponsors along with it, fell sixty-three percent to thirty-eight and a half billion. These are not contradictory data points. They are two sides of the same stuck cycle: forward-looking deployment optimism sitting on top of a backward-looking collapse in realized cash back to limited partners. The thaw, if it's real, has not shown up in a single closed quarter yet, and there is no third-quarter print to check it against, because Q3 doesn't close its books until the end of September. So for now, treat "sponsors are ready to join" as exactly what it is: an expectation, not evidence.

And even the deal activity that does exist is concentrating, not broadening. First-half 2026 deal volume fell thirty-four percent year over year, but average deal size rose almost fourfold versus the same period last year. Capital is pooling into a handful of high-conviction megadeals rather than clearing a broad path through the backlog. That distinction matters more than it first sounds. The distribution problem isn't really about the five or six largest sponsors who can still command headline processes. It's about thousands of vintage 2018-through-2021 holdings sitting at mid-market funds that need ordinary-sized exits, not marquee transactions, to return cash to their LPs. A recovery built on bigger checks for fewer assets does very little to dent that backlog. What it does instead is widen the gap between the top-decile funds that can still transact and the median fund that can't. The dispersion between the best and the merely average manager is set to widen from here, and the squeeze lands hardest on the mid-market GPs who are shut out of both the deal window and the exit window at the same time.

Now the cover story for why deployment should pick up, the so-called wall of dry powder, is itself thinning. US dry powder peaked near one point three trillion dollars in December 2024 and had fallen to roughly eight hundred and eighty billion by last September, a drop of about thirty-two percent. That sits in real tension with the "near-record dry powder" framing that underpins the thaw narrative. I'm not going to resolve that conflict for you cleanly. The two figures come from different data providers, and neither is a confirmed current print, so I'll flag the discrepancy rather than pick a side. But the direction matters more than the decimal here. If the pile of committed, undeployed capital is shrinking rather than sitting at an all-time high, then the case for both continued high entry multiples and an imminent deployment wave gets weaker, not stronger. The wall of capital that's supposed to justify paying up is smaller than the marketing suggests.

Where liquidity is actually showing up is engineered, not organic. Secondaries volume topped a hundred and twenty billion dollars in the first half of the year, a twenty percent jump over the prior record, and full-year volume is tracking toward two hundred and fifty to two hundred and sixty billion by Preqin's estimate, though that's a projection, not a closed number. Single-asset continuation vehicles now make up sixty-two percent of GP-led secondaries, and here's the tell: they're pricing at an average discount to net asset value of just two point nine percent. That's essentially par. We've walked the mechanics of this before, so here's the one-line update and the caution that goes with it. This is the industry's liquidity valve of choice right now, and it is GP-controlled. The price is set by the GP's own chosen counterparties, often the same buyers who advise or co-invest alongside the sponsor, not cash cleared through a third-party sale to the broad LP base. Near-par pricing can mean genuine demand for quality assets, or it can mean the seller is also, in effect, the price-setter. And because a continuation vehicle lets a GP hold rather than force a sale, the more this mechanism institutionalizes, the longer hold periods structurally get. The liquidity valve and the lengthening-hold problem are the same instrument viewed from two ends.

Even inside the largest managers, capital is rotating away from traditional buyout. Carlyle's private equity assets under management actually shrank one percent year over year last quarter, while its AlpInvest secondaries platform grew sixteen percent and its credit business grew four percent. That's one firm's segment mix, not proof of an industry-wide shift, and I won't stretch it into one. But it's an early signal worth tracking, because it changes what a "private equity manager" even is. If the growth inside the majors is increasingly secondaries and credit rather than classic buyout carry, then the listed manager's valuation decouples further from the health of the buyout exit cycle. You can own the stock as a bet on fee-gathering and insurance float and still be right even if realized carry from portfolio-company exits stays soft for years. That's a coherent thesis. It is just a different thesis from "the private equity cycle is recovering," and the two are quietly being conflated.

So where does the cycle actually stand. The binding constraint is still the exit-and-distribution logjam, and this week finally attached a hard number to how wide that gap is: a forty-six percent quarter-over-quarter collapse in realized exits, landing in the same week a bullish deployment story broke. The catalyst that would genuinely confirm a turn is not another fee-related-earnings beat, and it's not a Goldman banker's sentiment read. It's a run of clean, broadly distributed third-party exits and a real, verifiable improvement in distributions-to-paid-in across ordinary vintages, not just the handful of megadeals grabbing the headlines. Until that shows up in a closed quarter, treat the thaw talk as a narrative running well ahead of the cash.
