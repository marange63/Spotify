We're leading with Strategy, the company formerly known as MicroStrategy, because its latest disclosure is the sharpest test yet of whether the bitcoin-treasury trade was ever permanent capital or just a leveraged bet on the price going up forever. In the week ending August 2nd, Strategy sold 1,638 bitcoin for $104.7 million, at an average price of $63,957 a coin. That price is below what the company paid for its bitcoin overall. A Strategy filing from June covering an earlier week put the company's average purchase price at $75,656 a coin, so while that specific figure predates this latest sale by about three weeks, it tells you the company's cost basis has been sitting well north of $75,000, and this week it sold meaningfully under that. After the transaction, Strategy holds 842,138 bitcoin.

Here's what makes this more than a rounding error. Of the proceeds, $52.4 million went straight to preferred-stock dividend payments, and another $52.3 million funded buybacks of STRC, Strategy's Digital Credit preferred security. That's the mechanism to flag. On August 4th we described Strategy's July selling as balance-sheet plumbing, not a change of conviction. This week's disclosure escalates that framing. This is at least the second straight week of below-cost sales, and it comes on the heels of an earlier tranche: between June 29th and July 5th, Strategy sold 3,588 bitcoin for roughly $216 million, also to fund preferred dividends and top up its dollar reserve. So we're no longer looking at a one-off. We're looking at a recurring, mechanical source of bitcoin supply, tied to a dividend calendar rather than to Michael Saylor's view on price.

Strategy has also quietly changed how it sets the STRC dividend rate. Instead of automatically raising the payout whenever the security trades below its $100 par value, the company now says it will weigh market price, competing yields, credit spreads, and bitcoin volatility before adjusting the rate. Management has said it does not intend to recommend cutting that rate below 12 percent until STRC trades consistently near par. Put plainly, the company has given itself discretion where it used to have a formula, and that shifts interest-rate risk onto the preferred holders rather than onto the balance sheet. If the market reads that as reduced downside protection, it's the kind of thing that can reprice Strategy's other Digital Credit securities, not just STRC itself.

Now, the honest caveat here, because this is exactly the kind of number that gets rounded into false precision. The total size of Strategy's annual preferred-dividend bill is widely reported in secondary coverage as exceeding a billion and a half dollars a year, but that figure has not turned up in a verbatim company filing in our research, and the underlying per-series dividend rates disagree across sources, with STRC's rate reported as either 11.5 or 12 percent depending on the filing you look at. So treat the size of that obligation as a directional estimate, not a precise number, and don't let anyone quote it to you as if it were confirmed.

And there's a real bull rebuttal worth stating plainly. The same week it sold bitcoin below cost, Strategy also raised $290.6 million by issuing about 3 million new shares, and it put roughly $250 million of that into its dollar reserve, which now stands at $4 billion. That's not a company being forced to liquidate. That's a company actively managing multiple funding levers at once. Whether that's prudent treasury management or a sign the flywheel that once ran one way, issuing equity to buy bitcoin, is now running in reverse, issuing equity and selling bitcoin to service the preferred stack, is the real question the market is pricing right now, visible in how far MSTR's premium to its net asset value has compressed.

And Strategy isn't the only balance sheet exposed to this math. A wave of corporate imitators built the same trade over the last two years, raising capital to hold bitcoin as a treasury reserve. Most of them have less balance-sheet flexibility than Strategy and no preferred-equity machine to lean on. If bitcoin stays below their acquisition cost, 2026 becomes the year we find out which of those treasuries were genuinely long-duration capital and which were leveraged bets on the price only ever going up. That's the second-order risk the market has to underwrite now, and it's why a single company's dividend calendar reads as a sector signal, not an idiosyncratic one.

That question hands off directly to the ETF side of the ledger, because the price level matters. Spot bitcoin has been trading in the low-to-mid $60,000s, meaningfully below where Strategy's cost basis sits. And the ETF tape backing that price has turned genuinely two-sided. US spot bitcoin ETFs shed $61.53 million over the week ending around August 3rd, that's the net figure. Within that week, July 31st alone saw $265.4 million walk out the door, reversing a $233 million inflow just the session before, with BlackRock's IBIT losing $122.7 million and Fidelity's FBTC losing $54.8 million.

But here's the wrinkle that actually breaks the arc we've been running all week. July as a whole closed positive, with $172.4 million in net inflows, the first positive month after two straight months of outflows. So this isn't a market in freefall. It's a fragile, barely-positive tape sitting on top of daily volatility that swings by hundreds of millions in either direction. The so-what: with bitcoin trading below the largest corporate holder's cost basis, and the ETF wrapper only marginally net-positive, the buyer base absorbing Strategy's scheduled, dividend-driven selling is thinner than it was during 2024 and 2025's one-way inflow regime. Scheduled supply meeting a fragile bid is a different market structure than scheduled supply meeting a one-way flow.

Let's close with the counterweight, because institutionalization isn't collapsing, it's splitting into two stories. While the leveraged corporate-treasury bet deleverages, the collateral-utility side of digital finance keeps growing straight through the drawdown. Tokenized US Treasuries have crossed roughly $15.2 billion on-chain, led by BlackRock and Circle. Circle's own USYC product alone holds about $2.67 billion, largely structured for non-US investors. That growth happening in the same weeks that Strategy is selling bitcoin at a loss tells you something: demand for on-chain collateral efficiency looks structural, not just a speculative-cycle side effect. It's a small number next to the $52 million Strategy funneled into dividends in this single week, so don't treat it as an offset. Treat it as evidence that not every part of this ecosystem is levered to bitcoin's price.

So here's where institutionalization actually stands today. The part of the trade built on leverage and a permanent-capital story, Strategy's treasury, is now visibly running its flywheel backward, selling the core asset below cost on a recurring schedule to service preferred obligations it didn't have five years ago. The part of the trade built on genuine collateral utility, tokenized Treasuries and on-chain cash management, keeps compounding regardless of price. Those are two very different bets wearing the same institutional adoption label, and this week is the clearest evidence yet that the market is starting to price them separately.
