Good morning, and happy Thanksgiving. It's Thursday, November 26th, 2026, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

This week the biggest counted AI dollars in enterprise tech sit in a hardware order book, not a software line. That shifts the question. It's no longer whether AI infrastructure demand exists. It's whether the buyers placing those orders can actually pay for them.

Start with Dell, which reported fiscal third-quarter results on Tuesday. AI-optimized server revenue came in at 22 billion dollars for the quarter, up 291 percent from a year ago. Total revenue and adjusted earnings per share both beat the LSEG consensus by a wide margin. This is the first time this desk has weighed the print, and it changes the scale of the week's evidence.

Set it against the rest of the week. In Wednesday's briefing, Workday showed that AI dollars in seat-priced software are real but still small. HPE showed AI order volume surging while it guided its AI segment margin lower. By our arithmetic, Dell's AI server revenue this quarter is close to nine times HPE's AI systems revenue in its latest quarter. So the counted AI money this week is infrastructure money, and it is concentrating at the largest box-builder.

Now the backlog, which deserves the most scrutiny. Dell's AI server backlog, meaning orders still being worked through, stands at 132.3 billion dollars. By our arithmetic, that's about six quarters of the current AI server revenue rate. Backlog is not revenue. When it runs that far ahead of shipments, the binding constraint may be supply rather than demand. Chip allocation, power, and data-center readiness can all hold up conversion.

Dell did raise its guidance. Its earnings release shows the current-year AI server forecast moving from 74 billion dollars to 78 billion dollars. That's a raise of Dell's own guide for this fiscal year, not a projection for next year. But with only one quarter left, our reading is that most of the backlog ships after this fiscal year ends. So treat the backlog as a conversion question, not a demand verdict.

Then there's the question of who's paying. Dell's earnings release says every AI order booked this quarter was, in the company's words, "rigorously vetted for customer quality and access to capital, land and power." That's the most important sentence Dell published. It speaks directly to the financing worries that have hung over AI infrastructure all month. Those include vendor financing, neocloud credit risk, and circular deals. In Sunday's briefing, this desk covered Oracle defending its own funding in public. Now the box-builder is vouching for its buyers.

Read the sentence for what it concedes. Land and power are on Dell's own screening list. So site readiness is a constraint Dell itself checks for, not just an outside worry. Then read it for what it leaves out. Dell says more than 7,500 customers are deploying what it calls AI Factories. But it gave no split between neocloud, hyperscaler, sovereign, and enterprise buyers. It gave no concentration figures, and no financing or prepayment terms. So Dell answered the credit question in words, not numbers. Our inference is that buyer quality is becoming the variable every vendor asserts and none quantifies. Buyers with weaker balance sheets may face tighter terms or longer queues.

Management also said, "We are now a different company," with the Infrastructure Solutions Group driving the vast majority of profit and growth. That's a statement about profit mix, not proof of durable platform adoption. On margin, Dell gave a segment number, not an AI-server number. The Infrastructure Solutions Group combines AI servers with traditional servers and storage. That segment's operating margin was 17.0 percent this quarter, up from 12.4 percent a year ago.

That leans against the bear case that AI servers are thin-margin commodity hardware. It also points the opposite way from HPE's guide. But the figure is blended across the whole segment. It doesn't prove the AI server line itself is driving the gain. Call it directionally encouraging, not conclusive.

One more confidence signal. Dell authorized a 25 billion dollar buyback. What it actually spent on repurchases and dividends this quarter was 4.9 billion dollars. An authorization signals intent. It is not a cash commitment. The Dell takeaway is simple. The volume is real, the margin trend is encouraging, and the buyer question is answered only by assertion.

If the question is who ultimately pays for those servers, one answer arrived in a regulatory filing. A standing disclosure first: Anthropic makes the model behind this show. Trade press reports that a filing shows Anthropic raised its compute commitment to Microsoft Azure. Neither company has confirmed it directly. The previous filing, dated November 2nd, listed a minimum spending commitment of 44.9 billion dollars.

The reported new range is 47.2 to 49.2 billion dollars. That still matters. It shows lab-to-hyperscaler commitments escalating, even through a month when AI infrastructure stocks wobbled hard.

By our arithmetic, the firm floor rose by about 2.3 billion dollars. The rest of the reported increase is contingent, which is why the headlines say "up to." Treat it as one tranche, and don't stack it on Anthropic's other compute deals, because the reported figures overlap in ways the coverage doesn't untangle.

And a commitment is not consumption. For Microsoft, it is contracted backlog. For Anthropic, it is an obligation. It becomes Azure revenue only as Anthropic draws it down. That drawdown rests on Anthropic's own revenue and capital. So the top of the demand chain is still committing more. But it is committing on paper, against a lab's balance sheet.

Compute commitments are the floor of Microsoft's AI business. The control plane sitting on top of them is where Microsoft wants its margin. And here I owe you a correction. In Sunday's briefing, I said Microsoft had disclosed no adoption figures for Agent 365. That was wrong. At last week's Ignite conference, Satya Nadella said 50 million agents are already managed through the platform. We missed that figure, and it belongs on the record.

Here's what that number is not. It's a keynote claim, not an audited or filed metric. It's also undefined. "Managed" could mean every agent registered or discovered across Microsoft's install base, dormant ones included. It isn't a count of active production agents. And it isn't paid seats or revenue, neither of which Microsoft has disclosed for Agent 365. One alternative read is that the number says more about how easily Microsoft enrolls agents across its suite than about enterprise adoption.

What's more concrete is a product you can buy today. Microsoft Perception is now generally available. It's an agentic security system. Red agents simulate attacks, blue agents detect them, and green agents remediate. Around it, Microsoft showed an MCP firewall and Intune tools for discovering unapproved agents on managed devices. Treat that surrounding stack as preview, not deployed. Still, the direction is clear. Microsoft isn't just issuing agent identities anymore. It's selling agent security into the security budget.

That puts Microsoft in contention with CrowdStrike. On Monday, CrowdStrike, the endpoint security vendor, announced it will buy Zscaler, the network and zero-trust access specialist. It's an all-stock deal valued at 43.9 billion dollars, which this desk covered in Tuesday's briefing. The point of the deal is to put endpoint and network security under one vendor. CrowdStrike's chief executive, George Kurtz, framed it as building, in his words, "cybersecurity's operating system: a single control plane to deploy AI, control AI." That's a chief executive's framing, not proof of capability.

But it means two vendors now claim the same control plane from opposite directions. One is a security pure-play buying its way from the endpoint into the network. The other is the productivity-suite owner bundling security down into its own licensing. And timing sharpens the contest. CrowdStrike doesn't expect the Zscaler deal to close until the second half of its fiscal 2028. Microsoft's Perception is on sale now. Our inference is that if agent governance rides inside Microsoft's licensing, the budget line behind the CrowdStrike and Zscaler rationale gets contested before the combined company even exists. And neither side has disclosed what revenue that control plane generates today.

Pull the spine together. The counted AI dollars this week sit in hardware backlogs. The commitments funding those backlogs sit in regulatory filings, not earnings disclosures. And the software layer meant to monetize it all is being claimed by agent count, not by revenue, on both sides of the fight. Three things to watch, as calendar items only. First, AWS re:Invent, starting November 30th. Second, Snowflake's earnings, scheduled for December 2nd. Third, whether Dell or its peers ever put a customer mix behind those AI orders.
