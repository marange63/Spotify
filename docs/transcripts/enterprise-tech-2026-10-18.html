Good morning, this is your Cautious Optimism Briefing for enterprise technology, October 18th, 2026.

Here's the thesis for today. This week, the moves to buy enterprise AI and security capability came from a private-equity-owned platform and two AI labs. ServiceNow, the one public platform vendor that came up in deal talk, got rewarded for declining to buy. That split tells you something about where consolidation is actually happening right now.

Start with the one signed transaction. Proofpoint, which is owned by Thoma Bravo, agreed on October 15th to acquire Varonis Systems for forty-nine dollars and seventy-five cents a share, all cash, about six point three billion dollars in total. The companies say the combined business will serve more than sixteen thousand enterprises. Hold that number loosely. It's a customer count, not a revenue figure.

The premium tells two different stories depending on which date you anchor to. Against Varonis's close on June 22nd, the last full trading day before sale rumors surfaced, the offer is fifty-two percent higher. Against the September 1st close, after the market had already priced in a sale, it's seventeen percent. So most of the takeout value was already in the stock by September. The real new information isn't the size of the premium. It's that a sponsor-owned platform was willing to pay cash for a data-security point product at all.

Varonis's own second-quarter results give us a number to size this deal against. Total SaaS annual recurring revenue, including contract conversions, was seven hundred twenty-six million dollars as of June 30th. Strip out the conversions and the underlying growth rate was twenty-five percent. Divide the six point three billion dollar price by that ARR figure and you get roughly eight point seven times trailing SaaS ARR. That's my calculation from two disclosed numbers, not a multiple either company stated. But it's the clearest read we have on what Thoma Bravo is paying for twenty-five percent organic growth.

Two details in Varonis's merger filing, its 8-K, sharpen the read. First, there's no go-shop period. Varonis accepted a customary no-shop restriction instead, so it isn't out testing the market for a better offer. Second, the termination fees are lopsided. Varonis would owe about two hundred twelve million dollars in specified cases, such as walking away for a superior bid. Proofpoint's parent would owe a reverse fee of about four hundred twenty-three million, twice as much, under its own specified circumstances.

One reading of this deal is defensive. On that view, Varonis is cashing out before the big platforms squeeze its category. The fee structure cuts against that. The buyer is carrying more of the deal-failure risk than the target, which suggests Thoma Bravo wanted this asset enough to backstop it. Don't over-read the gap, though. A bigger reverse fee also partly prices the risk that naturally sits on the buyer's side of a sponsor deal.

Now the timeline, which runs longer than the headline suggests. Proofpoint's press release targets a close in the first half of 2027. The merger agreement's outside date is July 15th, 2027. There's a three-month extension available if regulatory approvals are still pending. So the contractual window could stretch into October of 2027. My inference is that this matters most for Varonis customers. A deal that's signed but not closed is a classic pause point for renewals and upsell. That gives rival data-security vendors, such as CrowdStrike or Microsoft's Purview, a months-long opening.

Frame all this against what resellers told Jefferies, which we covered on October 13th. Buyers are consolidating security spend around fewer suppliers and integrated platforms. Proofpoint and Varonis is that consolidation showing up as M&A, with data security and access controls folded into an email and threat-protection platform. The notable part is who's doing the consolidating. It's happening under private-equity ownership, not through a public platform vendor expanding its own balance sheet.

The other would-be buyers this week weren't sponsors, they were labs. Earlier this week we covered OpenAI's reported talks to acquire the security auditing firm Trail of Bits. Those talks are still unconfirmed and could fall apart. The new piece is Cohere. According to The Logic, Cohere signed a non-binding letter of intent to acquire Aisle, a startup that uses AI to find and help fix software vulnerabilities. Cohere also plans to stand up its own security division. It disclosed no price, no structure, and no closing date. Still, two labs moving the same direction in the same week turns a single rumor into a pattern of intent. It is not yet a pattern of product, because nothing has shipped.

My read, as it was with Trail of Bits, is that labs want to harden and audit their own agent products in-house. They are not lining up to fight Palo Alto Networks or CrowdStrike on firewalls. If that's right, the first spend at risk isn't a platform vendor's. It's the independent audit and vulnerability-research work that labs would otherwise buy from outside firms. That's a pricing and channel question for security services before it's a threat to the platforms.

Now the public-market side of the same ledger. Business Insider reported that Decagon, an AI customer-service agent startup, had hired a bank to gauge acquisition interest. Decagon was valued at four and a half billion dollars earlier this year. The report said it held preliminary talks with ServiceNow, among other potential buyers. ServiceNow's president and chief operating officer, Amit Zavery, denied it. He called the reports misinformation and said the company has no intention of buying Decagon. Sources differ on whether that denial came Tuesday or Wednesday. Then, on Thursday, October 15th, 24/7 Wall Street reported that ServiceNow stock had risen about five percent after the denial, to one hundred forty-six dollars and ninety-three cents in midday trading. Because the denial and the move are a day or two apart, treat the link between them as plausible rather than proven.

With that caveat, it's still worth sitting with. In the same week a sponsor and two AI labs were shopping for capability, ServiceNow's shareholders appear to have rewarded it for saying it would not buy. There are two readings, and the facts in hand don't let us pick one cleanly. It could be relief that ServiceNow is avoiding dilution and integration risk on a four and a half billion dollar target. Or it could be a genuine preference, at least for now, for building agentic AI organically rather than buying it. Either way, a denial is not a strategy disclosure. "No intention" today doesn't rule out a deal later.

The bigger implication is for the startups. Set the ServiceNow reaction next to Modal's private funding round, which we covered yesterday at roughly sixteen times run rate. Private appetite for AI infrastructure is clearly strong. Appetite for a standalone agent-application company that's shopping itself to strategics looks less certain. My inference is that if public platforms get rewarded for not buying, the exit path for venture-backed agent startups like Decagon narrows. It tilts toward sponsors, labs, or an IPO. That puts pressure on agent-application valuations, and it makes the labs a more important buyer of last resort.

Last, a quick test of a distinction that matters for budgets: AI as a feature built into an existing suite, versus AI as genuinely new infrastructure demand. On October 15th, Adobe and NVIDIA expanded a partnership that puts NVIDIA models into Photoshop, Premiere, and other tools customers already pay for. That's AI as a feature. The same day, Akamai said AI training and inference are driving more use of its cloud services. That's a claim of new infrastructure demand. Neither attached a dollar figure, so both are announcements, not revenue.

What to watch from here. Watch whether the Proofpoint deal for Varonis clears inside the first half of 2027, or runs toward the July outside date and the extension. Watch whether Trail of Bits or Aisle converts from talks and a letter of intent into a signed, priced deal. Watch whether ServiceNow's late-October results say anything about its M&A posture. And watch whether Adobe or Akamai eventually puts a number on the AI demand they're describing.

That's the briefing. Have a good day.
