Good morning. It's Monday, August 3rd, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

The memory story has entered a new phase. All last week the question was whether the HBM shortage was real. It is settled now. This week the question is who wins the next generation. SK hynix began mass shipments of HBM4 in the second quarter and is ramping through the second half of the year. That is the marginal news — not the pricing, which we already know is extraordinary, but the generational handoff. Industry analysts estimate SK hynix holds sixty to seventy percent of Nvidia's HBM4 allocation for the coming Vera Rubin platform, with Samsung around twenty-five to thirty percent and Micron the remainder. Note that split is an analyst estimate. Nobody — not Nvidia, not the memory makers — has disclosed an actual number, so treat it as directional, not precise.

Samsung is not conceding that split. On its own Q2 earnings call, Samsung's memory management said it expects to bring HBM market share up to roughly its overall DRAM market share, and that it will manage HBM and conventional DRAM in a balanced way to get there. That is a materially bigger ambition than the twenty-five to thirty percent analysts currently pencil in, because Samsung is the largest DRAM producer in the world. Aligning HBM share with total DRAM share would mean something close to parity, not a distant third place. Samsung also told investors HBM4 will make up well over sixty percent of its total HBM revenue mix in the second half, with HBM4 sales growing more than threefold quarter over quarter in Q3. That is not a slow transition. It is Samsung trying to skip straight to the new generation, where the qualification race is still open, rather than keep fighting over an HBM3E allocation it already lost.

Here's the crucial caveat. All of this is qualification and ambition, not settled volume. Nvidia's own Jensen Huang has said all three suppliers — Samsung, SK hynix, and Micron — are now qualified and in production for Vera Rubin. Qualification is the entry ticket, not the prize. The actual share is decided by who yields at volume, quarter after quarter. So the near-term allocation numbers are still SK hynix's to lose. But Nvidia deliberately triple-sourcing this generation is a hedge against exactly the single-supplier pricing power SK hynix has been enjoying. A seventy-six percent operating margin, which is what SK hynix posted last quarter, is the kind of super-normal profitability that historically invites a capacity response. Samsung's own words suggest that response is already underway. If Samsung qualifies at scale into 2027, the premium baked into that seventy-six percent margin is the first thing that compresses.

What's pulling all of this HBM4 volume is hyperscaler capex, and that demand is now running into real investor skepticism. Combined 2026 AI capex across Amazon, Google, Meta, and Microsoft is tracking to roughly seven hundred twenty-five billion dollars, up about seventy-seven percent from around four hundred ten billion last year. That aggregate is a secondary estimate, not summed filings, so hold it loosely — but every component that is filed points the same way. Alphabet raised its guidance to a range of one hundred eighty to one hundred ninety billion dollars, and Google Cloud revenue grew sixty-three percent year over year to twenty billion. And yet the stock fell on the print. Amazon raised its own capex outlook to two hundred twenty billion, with AWS growing thirty-seven percent, its fastest pace in eighteen quarters. So you have record cloud growth being punished by the market anyway.

That reaction is the signal worth decoding, because it separates two very different kinds of demand. Amazon's growth acceleration reads as compute-constrained — capacity simply cannot keep up, so every incremental chip gets sold. The broader selloff reads as investors questioning whether that capex is converting to cash fast enough to justify the pace. Both things are true at once. One says order more HBM4 as fast as it can be built. The other says the market will only reward the hyperscaler that can prove the tightest link between spending and revenue. That tension is exactly what governs how quickly the 2027 HBM4 order book actually fills, and it is why the memory makers cannot simply extrapolate today's allocation into next year's volume.

Now, who else can actually build at the leading edge besides the incumbents. Intel posted sixteen-point-one billion dollars in second-quarter revenue, up twenty-five percent year over year — its strongest growth rate in over fifteen years — and said on the call that 18A yields are tracking ahead of expectations, with 18A-P now in risk production. Intel Foundry revenue was five-point-eight billion, up thirty-one percent year over year. That is real technical and commercial evidence, the first concrete sign in over a year that Intel's foundry roadmap is holding. But management gave no specific yield number, so "ahead of expectations" is Intel's own characterization, not an audited figure. And Intel Foundry still lost two-point-one billion dollars in the quarter, only three hundred forty-eight million better than the prior quarter. So the technical story and the financial story have not converged. Process execution is necessary for Intel to win outside customers away from TSMC. It is not sufficient. External customers underwrite a foundry on economics as much as on yield, and a division losing over two billion dollars a quarter is not yet a business they can bet a product line on. That gap is the whole question for Intel Foundry, and this quarter narrowed it only at the margin.

That brings the whole complex back to cost. The memory supercycle isn't staying contained to AI accelerators — it's taxing everything downstream. Qualcomm's chief executive told investors the company will raise chip prices across the board starting September 1st, specifically to offset higher input costs. That's a direct read-through from the same DRAM and NAND inflation driving SK hynix's and Samsung's record memory margins, now showing up in mainstream mobile and automotive silicon pricing. And this is where the supercycle stops being an AI story and becomes an everyone story. The cost has already walked from HBM to server DRAM to graphics memory, and now to the mobile and auto chips inside ordinary devices. If other suppliers follow Qualcomm with similar increases, that cost either gets absorbed by chipmakers' margins or passed to device makers and, eventually, consumers — pressuring handset and auto unit demand right as OEMs already face rising bills from every other angle. A broad price increase can destroy demand as easily as it lifts margin, if the buyer at the end of the chain simply refuses to pay.

So the throughline across all four of these. The same shortage funding record memory margins is now a tax on almost everything built with silicon, and the capital funding the buildout that created that shortage is facing its first real skepticism from the market it's supposed to be pleasing. Watch two things next. Whether Samsung's HBM4 qualification actually converts to volume, and whether hyperscaler capex holds when the market is no longer applauding it.
