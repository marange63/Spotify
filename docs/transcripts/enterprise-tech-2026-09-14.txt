Good morning. It's Monday, September 14th, and this is your Cautious Optimism enterprise technology briefing.

Days before its own annual conference, Salesforce stopped selling an agent platform and started selling seven employees with names. Casey handles customer service across voice, text and chat. Hunter does outbound sales. Marshall runs supply chain and back-office work. Six of the seven are generally available right now. Hunter is the exception. It is still in pilot, with general availability targeted for November. And no price accompanied any of them.

That absence is not a minor omission, and it is worth sitting with. None of the seven names appears anywhere on Salesforce's own Agentforce pricing page. What is published there is the old menu. Two dollars per conversation. Flex Credits at five hundred dollars per hundred thousand. Or per-user licenses running from five dollars a month to five hundred and fifty. None of those units is tied to a specific named role. So the roster does not merely lack a price. It sits entirely outside the pricing structure Salesforce itself publishes.

Here is why that matters commercially. When you sell a product called Agentforce, the buyer compares it to last year's software contract. When you sell Hunter, the buyer compares it to a fully-loaded outbound sales rep. That is a far bigger number, and a far better place for a vendor to stand in a negotiation. The packaging move is the news, not the technology. It shifts the comparison off the software line and onto the headcount line. But a headcount comparison with no rate attached cannot actually be evaluated by the buyer it is aimed at. Salesforce published the roles. It did not publish the rate card.

There is a reason a vendor might take its time with that. Salesforce's installed base is priced per seat, per human seat. Metering the same work as a digital role means choosing a number that either undercuts the CRM licenses those customers already buy, or prices above them and invites the buyer to do the arithmetic out loud. So give yourself two checkpoints. The first is whether any dollar figure attaches to a single one of the seven at the conference this week. The second is whether Hunter, the agent that maps most cleanly onto a paid human job, actually reaches general availability in November as stated. The most aggressive claim in the announcement is the one not yet shipping.

What Salesforce did publish, in the launch article itself, were customer outcomes attached to named companies. Salesforce says ninety percent of core shopper journeys at the retailer Hibbett are now handled by Carter, the commerce agent in that roster, live within six weeks. It says seventy-nine percent of the support conversations at Anthropic are resolved autonomously by Fin, the customer-service agent Salesforce bought this month for three point six billion dollars. And it says sixty percent of Perk's sales pipeline is built by Hunter, the outbound agent still in pilot. Those are attributable, and naming the customer is more than most vendors do. Salesforce lists six such wins in all, one customer apiece, chosen by the vendor, with no standardized sample size or timeframe across them. They tell you these particular installations work. They do not tell you what a typical enterprise should expect.

The only figure with scale behind it is Agentic Work Units. Three point two billion in a single quarter, out of seven billion cumulative across Agentforce and Slack. This beat took that unit apart a few days ago. It is Salesforce-defined, carries no dollar value, has no denominator, and spans two products priced completely differently. A bigger number does not change the critique. It is an adoption proxy, not a revenue signal.

One correction while we are here. A few days ago this beat read Salesforce's purchase of Fin as a hedge, something the company was running alongside Agentforce rather than folding into it. Fin now appears as one of the seven named roles. That is absorption, not hedging.

Now the mechanism that connects this to everything else today. Six of these seven agents are live, acting across voice, text, messaging apps, e-commerce and back-office systems. Every one of those actions runs on a credential into a production system. Salesforce's published documentation on that point covers the general service-agent architecture, where an agent runs under an auto-generated identity that starts with a nearly empty permission set. That is a real least-privilege design. But it describes in-Salesforce data access, not these seven roles specifically, and it says nothing about how access is scoped when an agent reaches outside to a third-party messaging or commerce platform. That is a documentation gap, and it sits exactly where the risk showed up elsewhere this week.

Anthropic published a threat intelligence report describing an actor who pursued access to a pre-release Claude model across more than a dozen avenues. A follow-on campaign from the same infrastructure hit roughly thirty AI companies in about four days. Here is the detail that matters. The route in was not the model. Anthropic states that the keys involved were customers' keys, stolen from customers' own environments, and that its own systems were never compromised.

Name the conflict plainly. This is a lab reporting on attacks against itself, and the sentence that clears the lab is also the most load-bearing one in the report. No outside party has confirmed it. Take it as a claim, not a verdict. But if it holds, it inverts the standard vendor-risk conversation. Enterprise buyers have spent a year diligencing model providers' security posture. This says the failure point was not the vendor. It was the buyer's own key hygiene. That changes what procurement should actually ask about. Not the vendor's certifications, but how long your own API keys live, how narrowly they are scoped, and how fast they rotate.

There is a collision buried in that, and it is worth naming. If labs respond to stolen keys and copied capabilities by tightening the API layer, with harder rate limits, key restrictions or output fingerprinting, the friction lands first on the highest-volume legitimate customers. Those are precisely the agent deployments the Salesforce launch is about. Note that these are not opposing camps. Anthropic is one of the named customers in Salesforce's own launch material. The security response to agentic AI constrains the commercial case for agentic AI, and both halves this week came from partners.

The one piece of evidence no vendor or lab wrote is an infostealer dump posted to a Telegram channel on August 2nd. Roughly seven gigabytes of unexpired authentication tokens for major AI and cloud services, scraped off nearly six thousand infected machines. It reaches us through researcher analysis rather than a directly verified primary page, and it is one dump, not a measurement of enterprise exposure. Still, rank the week's three sources honestly. Salesforce describing its own product, with no price attached. Anthropic describing attacks on itself, with a conclusion that happens to clear its own name. And a criminal dump, produced by the attackers, with nothing to sell. Only the third has no stake in the answer. And it says plainly that AI governance built around what a model might say is aimed at the wrong failure mode. The weaker link is a live token sitting on an employee's laptop, replaying straight past multi-factor authentication.

Last, briefly. GitHub's own changelog says that from October 1st, new Copilot Business and Enterprise seats will require payment before the user gets access. Call it what it is, an account-vetting and provisioning change, not a price increase. Then set it against something this beat reported a few days ago. JetBrains, surveying more than fifteen thousand professional developers, found Copilot's adoption falling from twenty-nine percent to twenty-one over the past year, despite bundled distribution inside the dominant developer platform. So this week, the control that actually tightened was not the credential. It was the invoice.

That's your enterprise technology briefing. Have a good day.
