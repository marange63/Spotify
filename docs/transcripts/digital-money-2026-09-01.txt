Good morning. It's Tuesday, September 1st, and this is your digital money briefing.

On Friday this show named two things that would falsify its skeptical read on August's rally. One of them was Strategy resuming net purchases of bitcoin. On Monday, Strategy disclosed that it had been buying. So the trigger this beat set on air has fired, and the honest thing to do is walk through what it actually shows — because the disclosure both confirms the test and undercuts it.

Start with the headline. Strategy, the company formerly known as MicroStrategy, bought 4,603 bitcoin for roughly 370 million dollars between August 24th and August 30th, at an average price of about 80,318 dollars. That is its first purchase since late June, ending a pause of about ten weeks, and it takes total holdings to 845,050 bitcoin. Read at that level, the largest corporate holder in the world has stopped being a seller and gone back to being a buyer. That is the version that will travel.

Now look at how it was paid for, because the funding is the story. Strategy sold about four and a half million Class A shares during the week and raised 602.8 million dollars in net proceeds. Bitcoin got 369.7 million of that. In other words, well under two thirds of the raise went into the asset the company exists to accumulate.

The rest went to the balance sheet. Roughly 200 million dollars of that 600 million dollar raise was spent defending a preferred security — most of it retiring STRC preferred shares, the remainder funding the dividend on them. That defense ran alongside the bitcoin purchase, not instead of it. The company also says its net leverage is zero. But that is the company's own characterization, relayed through secondary reporting of the filing rather than a quoted audited line, so treat it as claimed rather than confirmed.

Then size the purchase against what preceded it. Across four disclosed tranches from late June to early August, Strategy sold roughly 7,000 bitcoin. The last of those sales, 1,690 coins, went out at an average of 64,262 dollars. So the company bought back materially fewer coins than it sold. And it paid roughly a quarter more per coin than it received on its most recent sale. Putting those two disclosures side by side is my arithmetic across separate filings, not a net figure the company states anywhere.

So the plain read is this. This is not accumulation resuming at scale. It is a partial, more expensive retracement of the company's own selling, executed with fresh equity, while an almost equivalent sum goes to protecting the credit profile of the preferred stack. Saylor's framing is that the buyer is back. The disclosure says the buyer is back on much tighter terms.

The second-order effect matters more than the headline. Funding purchases with equity rather than debt does remove the thing critics feared most, which was a forced seller in a drawdown. But it does not remove reflexivity — it relocates it. If the bid is financed by issuing stock, then the pace of accumulation becomes a direct function of what the equity market will pay for the shares. Buying capacity expands when sentiment is strong and the stock is rich. It contracts exactly when sentiment breaks and coins are cheapest. The marginal corporate bid has been converted from a leveraged, always-on buyer into a pro-cyclical one. For anyone pricing Strategy's preferred or convertible paper, that is arguably the better outcome, because the credit is being actively defended and the leverage is not being added back. For anyone who owned the equity as a permanent, price-insensitive bid under bitcoin, it is a downgrade.

If the corporate bid is now capped by what equity markets will fund, then the growth in this sector has to be coming from somewhere else. It is coming from the plumbing.

Circle's second-quarter results, reported August 5th, contained a divergence worth sitting with. On-chain USDC transaction volume was 14.8 trillion dollars, up 151 percent year over year. USDC in circulation grew only 19 percent, to 73.3 billion. That is roughly an eight-to-one gap between how fast dollars are moving across the network and how fast the float backing them is growing. The float is the part Circle earns rent on, and that rent is compressing — reserve returns fell 66 basis points over the year even as average circulation grew. Yesterday's briefing made that point in full, so take it here only as setup for the question it raises. If the rent shrinks, what replaces it?

Circle's answer is regulatory and infrastructural. It has reportedly received final approval for a federally chartered national trust bank, which would put it among the first stablecoin issuers holding that status. It also has a settlement network called Arc, built with institutional partners including DTCC and BlackRock, with a launch scheduled for September. Both of those are reported rather than confirmed against a primary release in this pass, so hold the specific dates loosely.

Here is the skeptical version. Volume growing eight times faster than float reads as much like churn as adoption. USDC is disproportionately the dollar of exchanges and trading venues rather than of end users. This show reported last week that Coinbase alone was sitting on an average USDC balance of 20 billion dollars, more than thirty percent of everything in circulation. Turnover inside a handful of trading venues is not the same phenomenon as payment adoption. And the economics make the point cleanly. All of that on-chain flow sat alongside 701 million dollars of total revenue for the quarter, because Circle monetizes the balance it holds, not the volume that crosses it. Profitability is finally there — net income from continuing operations swung by more than half a billion dollars year over year into positive territory — but it came from scale and cost on the float, not from the flow.

The charter and the network are the strategic answer to that, and both are promises rather than revenue today. What they would change, if they work, is competitive geometry rather than market size. A federally chartered US issuer competes against offshore Tether specifically in the regulated use cases — bank settlement, custody, tokenized money-market reserves — which are the channels institutional money would actually have to arrive through. So the charter matters more for who takes share than for how much share exists. And there is a broader implication for anyone reading this market. Total stablecoin supply is becoming a progressively worse proxy for on-chain dollar activity, and the issuer model is drifting from a rate-linked annuity toward fee and network economics. That is a lower-multiple, more competitive business than the one these companies were priced as.

Both of these stories are, in the end, bets on the front end of the rate curve. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh's Jackson Hole speech on August 28th cited twelve-month PCE inflation at 3.7 percent and the six-month rate at 4.1 percent, both well above target. If underlying inflation is not clearly moving toward two percent, he said, the Fed has "work to do." In the week that followed, CME-tracked odds of a September hike moved from roughly 40 percent to a majority likelihood. Trackers disagree on the exact number, so take the direction rather than a decimal. Bitcoin opened Monday near 77,695 dollars, about seven tenths of a percent lower. That same session also carried an oil-driven risk-off bid after US airstrikes on Iranian rocket launchers, so the bitcoin move is confounded and proves nothing on its own.

But the mechanism cuts both ways, and that is the part worth banking. A hike would raise the reserve income that issuers like Circle earn on their Treasury holdings. The very same hike would raise the opportunity cost of holding non-yielding bitcoin, and raise the cost of the equity Strategy now has to issue in order to buy it. One rate outcome, opposite signs on the two halves of this briefing.

Which leaves a single clean observable. Watch whether Strategy's next disclosed week is another purchase or another sale. And remember that the September 15th and 16th FOMC meeting, which has not happened, is the binary sitting behind that choice.

That's the digital money briefing for September 1st. Have a good one.
