Strategic Power, Energy, and Institutional Change

Transcript · August 19, 2026

Good morning. It's Wednesday, August 19th. Across power markets, chip policy, and the Federal Reserve this week, the same question keeps showing up: the state and the grid operator both wrote new rules to control a strategic resource, but neither has actually made anyone comply yet. The gap between the mechanism and the binding is where the real story is.

Start with the grid, because it's the sharpest example. PJM Interconnection, the operator covering the mid-Atlantic and much of the industrial Midwest, filed a proposal with federal regulators on August 13th that does two things. It creates a public registry tracking every site pulling at least 50 megawatts, which in practice means every hyperscale data center. And it says any new large load that isn't bringing its own generation or otherwise locking in supply by June 1st, 2027, goes to the front of the line for power cuts, ahead of general emergency load-shedding. We've been tracking this arc for a few days now: first PJM's state-level cost-allocation fight, then Sunday's disclosure that its most recent capacity auction cleared 6,831 megawatts short of the reliability requirement PJM is supposed to meet. This filing is the escalation. It's PJM converting a paper shortfall into an enforcement mechanism with a deadline attached.

The scale problem behind it is real. PJM's own board materials put new large-load demand at 70 additional gigawatts by 2038, against roughly 15 gigawatts of generation retired since 2022. And the price signal backs that up: the capacity auction for the 2026/27 delivery year cleared at $329.17 per megawatt-day, the highest clearing price in PJM's history and a 22% jump, $59.22, over what was already a record the year before. That's back-to-back record years, not a one-off spike. So the message to hyperscalers is blunt: bring your own power, or you're first to go dark when the grid gets tight.

But be careful not to treat the filing as delivered enforcement. It's a proposal awaiting a ruling from FERC, not an approved rule, and two things remain genuinely unresolved. First, nobody has stated publicly, including PJM itself, whether data centers already in the interconnection queue before this filing are grandfathered out of the June 2027 test, or whether "new" just means anyone not self-supplying by that date regardless of when they applied. That single definitional question determines whether this rule bites on a handful of future projects or on a meaningful share of the pipeline already announced. Second, there's no confirmed FERC timeline for a decision, so the deadline pressure is real for developers but the regulatory clock itself is still unknown. So what does this mean practically? Expect an acceleration in hyperscaler deals for behind-the-meter gas turbines and small modular reactors, because that's the only way to guarantee you're not the load PJM cuts first. Expect some siting decisions to migrate out of PJM territory entirely, toward Texas's ERCOT or the Midwest's SPP, where the rules aren't yet this explicit. And expect other grid operators to watch closely, because if PJM's rule survives FERC review, it becomes the template.

That same lag, between the rule that's supposed to control a strategic resource and the physical reality on the ground, shows up abroad too, in a very different form. Bloomberg reported this month that the Commerce Department's enforcement arm has opened a formal review into how Chinese AI companies are accessing Nvidia chips they're barred from importing, by renting compute time in third-country data centers rather than shipping hardware into China directly. The trigger was a run of Chinese frontier model releases that shouldn't have been possible under the chip restrictions. Moonshot AI published the full weights of its Kimi K3 model on July 27th, a 2.8 trillion parameter system that's the largest open-weight model ever released. Alibaba followed with a comparably large model of its own.

Here's the structural problem for Washington: export-control law was built around the physical transfer of hardware. Renting time on a chip sitting in a data center in a third country, without that chip ever crossing into China, doesn't clearly meet the legal definition of an export under the current rules. So the Commerce Department may not actually have the statutory authority to close this loophole through enforcement alone. It would likely need new rulemaking, or an act of Congress, to extend export-control jurisdiction to cloud rental arrangements, and that's a much slower and more legally contested path than issuing an enforcement notice. So what does this mean? It undercuts both sides of the standard Nvidia-China argument at once. It weakens the case that restricting chip sales is actually slowing Chinese capability, since capability is showing up anyway. And it weakens the case for selling more chips to keep the global standard American, since the compute gap is apparently being closed through rental arrangements Washington can't yet touch. Watch whether this review turns into an actual rule or just stays a fact-finding exercise, because that answer will tell you whether the control regime catches up to the workaround or falls permanently behind it.

Now the same test of institutional teeth, but domestically, and arguably more consequential for markets. The White House has renewed its effort to remove Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook. This follows the Supreme Court's June ruling in Trump versus Cook, which blocked an earlier removal attempt and held that Fed governors can only be fired "for cause" during their statutory terms, not at presidential discretion. A letter dated August 5th gave Cook formal notice that the president is considering her removal, citing what it calls "a distinct nexus between a basic level of trustworthiness and care in financial matters and the duties of a Member of the Board of Governors," pointing to allegations about mortgage paperwork on properties in Michigan and Georgia. Cook has until August 26th to respond before any final decision. Her attorneys call the allegations baseless, and note they're the same claims made a year ago.

This is worth taking seriously not because the underlying allegation is new, but because of what it tests. The Supreme Court preserved "for cause" protection as a doctrine in June. What's being probed now is whether that protection can be hollowed out procedurally: if the administration can run a plausible-sounding cause claim through the newly required notice process and make it stick, the practical independence the Court preserved on paper may not hold up in practice. So what's the market read? Prolonged uncertainty over Fed board composition heading into the September 16th policy meeting is itself a source of volatility in rates and the dollar, independent of how the Cook matter ultimately resolves. And it feeds a standing narrative: erosion of confidence in institutional independence is part of why gold and Bitcoin keep getting bid as hedges against monetary and institutional credibility risk. None of that requires assuming Cook is actually removed. The uncertainty itself is the input markets are pricing.

Three different arenas, one thread. A grid operator wrote a curtailment rule but hasn't defined who it actually covers. A chip-control regime wrote restrictions built for physical hardware that don't reach the rental arrangements actually closing the compute gap. And a constitutional protection for the Fed was affirmed in June but is now being tested by a notice-and-response process that could hollow it out without ever overturning it. In every case, watch the mechanism, not the announcement. That's where the balance of power actually gets decided.
