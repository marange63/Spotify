Good morning. It's Friday, October 23rd, and this is your AI products briefing. One quick disclosure before we start: Anthropic makes the model behind this show, and one of today's items is Anthropic's own.

The thread running through today's briefing is plumbing. Three different platforms just standardized a different layer of the agent stack: where agents are allowed to buy things, what knowledge they carry with them, and where they actually run. In every case, the door opened before the lock was installed. Let's start with the door that matters most.

Agentic commerce just crossed from assisting a shopper to acting for one. On Monday, we covered Google's Universal Cart, now live for US users. But it still required you to assemble your own cart. Google opened a protocol while keeping the shopping surface for itself. Walmart just went further. CEO John Furner announced an Agent Gateway at the Masters of Scale Summit. It's built on the Model Context Protocol, or MCP. It lets approved third-party agents connect to Walmart and Sam's Club inventory, and the named examples include ChatGPT, Gemini, and Meta's Muse. In the words of the reporting, those agents can buy for the shopper. Sam's Club access doesn't even require a Walmart Plus membership.

The safeguards are real but not yet complete. According to TechCrunch's account, customers will control how purchases get approved. They can require approval for every transaction, or set a spending limit an agent can act within. The reporter compares it to adding an authorized user to a credit card. A profile is created the first time you use a given agent through the Gateway, and you can delete it at any time. Connected agents can't access your payment information directly. Agents that violate Walmart's terms get blocked from the site. And Walmart's returns process applies even when an agent, as the company puts it, doesn't act as intended. That's the safety net. But here's the catch: those Agent Profiles, with the actual spending caps and approval settings, are rolling out next month. They are not live today. So what's live right now is the open door, and the controls are still a promise.

One more caveat on sourcing. Every mechanic here traces back to secondary coverage of a conference remark, not a Walmart press release or developer documentation. Treat the specifics as reported, not confirmed by Walmart directly. And there are no volume or adoption figures at all. This is a launch, not proven usage.

The contrast with Amazon sharpens the stakes. Amazon began blocking Meta's Muse from transacting on its marketplace back on September 20th, about a month before Walmart's announcement. Amazon's position is that continued access by an unauthorized agent violates its conditions of use. An Amazon spokesperson framed it as a principle: third-party shopping apps, in the company's words, should operate openly and respect service provider decisions about whether to participate. So you now have three distinct bets on who owns the moment a customer decides to buy. Google keeps the cart on its own surface while opening the protocol underneath it. Amazon keeps the surface closed entirely. Walmart opens the surface itself, betting that being the agent-friendly store wins share that a defensive posture would cost it. None of those bets is proven yet. This is one week of positioning, not a verdict. But if agents become the actual front end for shopping, a retailer's own app, loyalty program, and on-site advertising all lose some claim on that decision moment. That's my inference, and it's the risk Walmart is choosing to take.

Walmart chose MCP for the checkout. The same week, the standard's sibling moved to neutral ground. Anthropic announced yesterday, October 22nd, that it's donating Agent Skills, its open standard for packaging specialized knowledge and workflows for agents. The recipient is the Agentic AI Foundation, a Linux Foundation project that already governs MCP, and that Anthropic co-founded alongside Block and OpenAI. In Anthropic's own words: "Today, we are donating the standard to the Agentic AI Foundation under the Linux Foundation." The company commits to keeping it, as it says, "open, community-driven, and vendor-neutral." That's a commitment, not yet a governance track record.

Think of it as the packaging layer joining the transaction layer under the same kind of governance. If MCP is how an agent connects to a retailer's inventory, Agent Skills is how an agent carries the specialized know-how to use that connection well. On Monday we watched Perplexity give away its sandbox to own what gets built on top of it. This is the same move, with one new element: the foundation receiving it was co-founded by OpenAI. That makes it as much a neutrality signal as a licensing move. My inference is that neutral standards for both tools and skills cut the switching cost between agent platforms. That pushes differentiation away from integrations and toward trust, data, and distribution.

Now the skeptical read. Donating a standard is the easy part. Nobody has described a security review process for third-party skills yet. That's exactly the supply-chain risk that package registries like npm have struggled with for years. A neutral foundation doesn't automatically solve that problem; it just relocates the question. There's also a reading where Anthropic designed this standard and is simply trading nominal neutrality for faster adoption. Both things can be true at once. And the adoption numbers circulating online haven't been confirmed against Anthropic's announcement, so I'm leaving them out.

If MCP covers where an agent acts, and Agent Skills covers what it knows, the last piece of this stack is where it actually runs. Cursor, the AI coding tool, shipped a beta Environments API on October 16th. It lets developers programmatically create and manage cloud environments for coding agents, rather than spinning one up by hand inside an editor. In Cursor's own changelog: "The Environments API is in beta, so you can create and manage cloud agent environments programmatically." The same release raised the ceiling to 300 repositories per environment. Cursor says large environments now load five times faster.

That's a meaningful shift in who buys this. If a coding agent's workspace is something you provision through an API, the way you'd provision a CI runner, the purchasing decision moves. It goes from an individual developer picking a tool to a platform team budgeting infrastructure. That's an inference, not a confirmed trend, but it's the direction the release points. The caveat is straightforward. Faster loading and higher repo limits are plumbing improvements, not evidence that agents complete bigger work correctly. Cursor's own changelog is the only source for the five-times figure, with no stated baseline. And there's no completion-rate data here at all. I'd also set aside the shareable canvases Cursor added alongside this. They're a nice-to-have for showing a teammate an agent's output, but the lesser half of the release.

Not every agent needs an open door, though. Google's approach this week runs the other way. Starting Tuesday, Gemini's Personal Intelligence feature began rolling out to Android Auto. It already draws on your Gmail, Calendar, Maps, and search history. Google's own built-in car software follows over the coming months, with no firm date. No protocol handshake is required, because Google already holds the data. So it can tell a driver navigating to the airport that their flight is delayed, pulling straight from the calendar. It's opt-in. And it's the same pattern we've flagged from Google all week: open the rails for others, but keep your own station running on data you already control.

So here's where that leaves the agent stack. This week, three different layers, the checkout, the skill, and the runtime, all moved toward shared, open plumbing. Google shows there's still a competing path built on first-party data alone. And in every open-door case, the actual controls are trailing behind. Walmart's spending caps are next month, Agent Skills has no published security review, and Cursor's environments are still in beta.

Four things to watch from here. First, whether Walmart's Agent Profiles actually ship next month, and whether Walmart discloses any agent order volume. Second, whether Amazon's block holds. Third, whether the Agentic AI Foundation publishes a security process for skills. And fourth, whether Cursor's Environments API graduates out of beta.

That's the briefing for October 23rd. Have a great weekend.
