Good morning, this is The Throughline for Tuesday, October 6th, 2026.

Today's headline: the ten-year Treasury closed Monday at 5.31 percent, and the two-year rose right along with it, which Capital Markets Radar reads as the market pricing a tighter Fed rather than just heavier bond supply.

Ten briefings shipped today, so we'll take them in three groups, starting with the macro backdrop.

Strategic Power says the oil chain is being fought over by force again, with a third tanker in two days struck near Hormuz and Brent above 105 dollars on Monday. The latest flow data predates those strikes, so the desk reads oil above a hundred as a contested chokepoint with no reliable meter on it. And in Wisconsin, secondary reporting links Oracle's nuclear power deal to a proposed 176 million dollar rate hike, which puts AI power costs in front of ordinary voters.

That oil shock lands on a bond market already under strain, which brings us back to the Capital Markets Radar. Over the three sessions to Monday, real yields rose as much as nominal ones, so breakevens on the desk's own arithmetic stayed flat and this leg is about real rates, not inflation. The so-what is that even Friday's weak jobs report did not make long bonds a hedge this week.

Digital Money asks who is actually buying Bitcoin, which traded around 86,200 dollars. Spot ETF inflows of 241 million dollars last week ran at well under half of September's weekly pace, and futures open interest fell. So the desk sees a de-levered rally on a thin ETF bid, and the next leg needs either ETF money to return or the other spot buyers to persist.

On the credit side, Private Credit and the Corporate Credit Cycle leads with KKR's retail credit fund, K-FITS, which received repurchase requests for 5.06 percent of shares and chose to pay every one. Apollo and Ares are still rationing against queues two to three times their 5 percent cap, so the desk's point is that the gate is a manager's choice, not a wall. It also finds that liability management exercises now make up 66 percent of leveraged-loan default activity by count, so the headline default rate captures only about a third of the action.

Private Equity and the Deal Economy sees the liquidity problem moving into the wealth channel, where Partners Group plans to split its capped Global Value fund into a sleeve that sells older assets and a sleeve for new investments. The plan needs shareholder approval, and the test is whether those asset sales clear near carrying values. Buyout executives are also reportedly starting to concede that returns on 2017 to 2021 vintages will fall short, though the desk stresses that is a change in tone, not a markdown.

Now the AI and compute stack, where the question that keeps coming up is who pays.

Semiconductors and Compute Infrastructure leads with Reuters' report, from Anthropic's IPO prospectus, that Broadcom has agreed to lend Anthropic up to 42 billion dollars toward its TPU lease commitments. The desk reads that as vendor finance and customer lock-in, which turns part of Broadcom's AI backlog into a credit position. Every figure is secondhand, and Anthropic makes the model behind this show, so weigh the read accordingly.

Frontier AI Lab Competition corrects two of its own earlier calls, and the first is that Google's Argon matches Opus 5.5 per token once its launch promotion ends. Per task, though, Argon is still cheaper, because it used fewer tokens and ran one benchmark suite for about half Opus 5.5's cost. The second is that the open-weight challenger the desk said hadn't shown up is DeepSeek's V4.1 Flash, at a dollar twenty per million output tokens, which sets a floor under the mid-tier while flagship prices hold.

AI Products and Applications covers Amazon Bedrock Managed Agents powered by OpenAI, now in public preview, which changes where the agent runs and whose governance it travels under, not what it can do. The desk's warning is that vendors are selling agents that need less human oversight faster than they publish rules for what those agents may touch. That leaves the control burden on the buyer.

Enterprise Technology covers two deals that both bet on the system of record. Schneider Electric agreed to buy PTC at an enterprise value of 23.7 billion dollars, or 21 times PTC's estimated 2027 adjusted EBITA, a price that only works if synergies arrive, chiefly cross-selling revenue. Supabase raised 150 million dollars and says about 70 percent of its new databases are now created by agents or AI tools, but the desk's caution is that creation is not payment.

Last, the applied frontier, where Robotics and Physical AI says the money for physical AI is moving onto corporate balance sheets. AMD agreed to buy World Labs for about 8.2 billion dollars in stock, and Tesla lined up 30 billion dollars of committed but undrawn credit. Yet the operating evidence is small, with Tesla's registered Cybercabs in Austin at 169 against a Waymo fleet of roughly 4,000.

One change from the last few days is worth marking. On Monday, this show relayed the Radar desk's read that the ten-year rose on a weak jobs report because of term premium. Today the same desk says the near-parallel move across the curve fits a tighter policy path better, though it concedes three sessions is a small sample. September CPI, due October 14th, is the next test.

That's The Throughline. Talk to you tomorrow.
