Good morning. This is Cautious Optimism Briefings for Tuesday, November 10th, 2026.

Rocket Lab reported yesterday, and it answers the question this desk has carried all week. Buyers are now paying more for a rocket that still has no flight date.

Start with the base business, from the third-quarter release filed with the SEC on November 9th. Revenue came in at a record 268 million dollars, up 73 percent from a year ago. Fourth-quarter guidance is 270 to 290 million dollars. So management expects the base business to keep growing into year end, whatever Neutron does.

Now the order book. Backlog hit a record of roughly 3.6 billion dollars, up 52 percent since the end of the second quarter. By our arithmetic, that puts the prior backlog near 2.4 billion. So backlog grew by about 1.2 billion dollars in a single quarter. That is the frame for everything that follows. Rocket Lab is converting demand into contracts at a rapid clip, while one of its core products has yet to leave the ground.

Here is the piece everyone was watching. Rocket Lab says it secured over 515 million dollars in Neutron launch contracts from constellation operators in the third quarter. On several of those missions, it lifted the base launch price to 70 million dollars. That is pricing power, or it looks like it.

But set the Neutron number against the backlog math. By our arithmetic, 515 million dollars is under half of the quarter's roughly 1.2 billion dollar increase. And that increase is net of the revenue Rocket Lab recognized in the quarter, so gross new business was larger still. The release itself points to where the rest came from. About 470 million dollars of bookings were signed for Space Systems. And the company says nearly a billion dollars of that backlog comes from new launch contracts. Neutron is roughly half of that launch figure. That implies several hundred million dollars of new launch work outside Neutron, presumably mostly Electron. So the record backlog is not a Neutron story. Neutron is the loudest part of it, not the biggest.

Now the catalyst this desk has flagged since last Thursday. Would Rocket Lab finally name a quarter for Neutron's debut? It did not, in the release or on the earnings call. The release says only that first launch is "now targeted for no earlier than 2027," with no quarter attached. That is the same year the company set after its October dome defect, so this is a reaffirmation, not a new slip. The quarterly filing gives the company's own account of that defect. Rocket Lab found what it calls "a manufacturing process issue that resulted in voids within portions of a composite propellant tank dome," during manufacturing of first-stage composite structures. The filing ties the 2027 timing to the time needed to investigate and validate that process, and to build a replacement forward dome. It gives no testing timeline for the new dome. So the news today is the absence of a date. The only quarter anyone has put on Neutron still belongs to the sell side. As we reported last Thursday, Citizens and Cantor Fitzgerald both model late fiscal second quarter 2027. That is analyst modeling, not company guidance.

What does that mean for the money? Neutron backlog becomes revenue only when Neutron launches. So none of that 515 million dollars converts until the vehicle flies. The 73 percent growth came entirely from businesses that already fly or ship: Electron launches and Space Systems hardware. Rocket Lab's equity story has effectively split in two. One half is a fast-growing base business. The other half is a single vehicle debut that the market is pricing as an open-ended option. The stock had run up more than 15 percent last Friday into the print, as we noted on Sunday. After hours yesterday, it fell. Reports of the size of the drop conflict, so I won't give you a number. The market's read was plain enough. It weighted the missing date over the record backlog.

There are two honest ways to read a buyer paying more for a rocket that has not flown. The first is pricing power. Operators that want medium lift at scale from someone other than SpaceX have almost no flying option today. So they pay up to reserve a slot. The second reading is cheap optionality. If the contracts carry generous deposit, rebooking, or termination terms, the higher price may cost buyers very little. Neither the release nor the 10-Q discloses those terms. Rocket Lab also has not disclosed the old base price, so we cannot size the increase. And its total launch backlog of 117 launches is not split between Electron and Neutron. Both readings can be true at once, and today's disclosures do not let us choose.

Either way, the second-order effect is the same. Every constellation operator that signed for Neutron still needs satellites in orbit on its original schedule. With Neutron undated, those operators are carrying Rocket Lab's schedule risk inside their own replenishment plans. Their near-term launches most likely default back to Falcon 9. Rocket Lab is monetizing scarcity on paper. SpaceX is still monetizing it in flights. It is the same scarcity that pushed AST SpaceMobile to build its own rocket, Blacklift, which we covered on Saturday and again yesterday. When the non-SpaceX bench stays grounded, customers either pay a premium for a seat that does not exist yet, or they build their own.

The contrast is China. A Long March 12 put up another batch of Guowang satellites on November 8th. That came five days after the record Qianfan batch on Kinetica-2, which we covered last week. Last Thursday, this desk said the tell was whether batch sizes and cadence held over the next few launches. One more batch, from the second program, says cadence is holding so far. I won't put satellite counts on it today. The tracking sources disagree with each other by double digits, and the November 8th batch size isn't reported. And as we said then, deployed satellites are not subscribers. But the direction is the point. China's state-linked programs keep turning launch capacity into deployed hardware, while the Western challenger turns demand into contracts. Orbital slots and spectrum priority reward whoever actually flies, not whoever signs.

Which brings us back to the incumbent. SpaceX is reportedly easing the growth of its Falcon 9 cadence, shifting engineering and range resources toward Starship. Starship Flight 15 is targeted for this Friday, November 13th, pending FAA approval. It has not flown. We covered the mission profile in detail yesterday, so just the stakes for today's thread. That resource shift makes the next leg of Western launch supply depend on a vehicle still in test. If Starship starts converting into operational deployment capacity, the premium Rocket Lab is charging on an unflown Neutron gets squeezed from the other side too. If Starship stumbles, that premium holds for longer.

So where does the orbital economy actually stand? The inflection is real on the demand side. Constellation operators are willing to commit up front, in contracts and in price, for non-SpaceX lift. And China's state-backed programs are showing that sustained cadence, not announced ambition, is what claims orbital real estate. The parts still at demo stage are just as clear. Neutron has booked contracts but no flight date. Rocket Lab attributes the latest push into 2027 to a manufacturing process, not the vehicle's design. But it has given no timeline for proving the fix. Starship remains a test vehicle, and even a clean Friday would be one flight, not a cadence. The open question for Rocket Lab is a race between two clocks. Can it turn more than half a billion dollars of Neutron orders into launches before its customers' replenishment needs push them back to Falcon 9 for good? Watch Starship Flight 15 this Friday. And watch for the first time Rocket Lab's management puts a quarter, not just a year, on Neutron's debut.

That's the briefing. Talk tomorrow.
