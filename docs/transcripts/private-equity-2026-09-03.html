Private Equity & the Deal Economy

Good morning. It's September 3rd, and this is your private-equity briefing.

The deal tape has been quiet for the past few days, so today we're going somewhere different. We're going into the managers' own numbers. This is where the argument this show has been making all week — that private-equity liquidity is being engineered rather than realized — finally gets a hard ratio attached to it. These second-quarter prints are six weeks old. We're coming back to them for the ratio, not for the headline.

Start with the fee-related-earnings machine, because it is working extremely well. Blackstone's assets under management reached one point three five trillion dollars in the second quarter, up eleven percent year on year, and its fee-related earnings climbed twenty-two percent, to one point eight billion dollars. KKR's fee-related earnings rose thirty-seven percent, to just over one point two billion, on thirty-four billion dollars of new capital raised in the quarter alone. Ares raised a record thirty-six point four billion dollars in the quarter, the highest quarterly total in its history. And Apollo posted a record seven hundred eighty-five million dollars of fee-related earnings, up twenty-five percent year on year. The so-what here is simple. This is a fee business compounding on fundraising and distribution, not on exits. None of that growth requires a single asset to be sold.

Now the hinge, and it's the number that carries this whole briefing. Blackstone is sitting on seven and a half billion dollars of net accrued performance revenue. That's carried interest booked on paper, thirteen percent higher than a year ago. In that same quarter, its net realizations — the carry actually converted to cash — were four hundred fourteen million dollars. Be careful about what those two figures are. The seven and a half billion is a balance, a stock, carried on the books over time. The four hundred fourteen million is a flow, what moved in three months. They are not directly comparable in scale, and that is exactly why the gap matters. The pile of paper carry sitting on Blackstone's balance sheet dwarfs what its investors are actually being paid this quarter. Record fee income does not tell you the cycle is healthy. It tells you the manager gets paid regardless.

That gap is not uniform, though, and the honest version of this story has to include the exception. Carlyle's second quarter ran the other way. Its fee-related earnings were a record too, three hundred fifty-eight million dollars, up eleven percent. But its net accrued performance revenue actually fell seven percent quarter over quarter, to two point four billion dollars from two point six billion, while its net realized performance revenue rose more than fivefold over the same period. That is carry converting to cash, not stacking up unrealized. So the accrued-versus-realized gap anchoring this lead is acute at Blackstone. It is not an industry-wide law. Keep both facts in view. The fee machine is compounding everywhere, but whether accrued carry actually turns into distributions depends on which manager's book you're looking at.

Now the line itself is moving, which is the genuinely new item here. KKR changed how it reports realized performance fees earned by its K-Series retail private-equity vehicles. This is KKR's own stated reporting treatment, as summarized from its second-quarter disclosure. Those fees used to sit in realized performance income, where compensation ran seventy to eighty percent. Starting this quarter, KKR moves them into Fee Related Performance Revenues instead, where the compensation margin is only fifteen to twenty percent. The mechanics are the whole story. More of that retail-vehicle revenue now lands in the line the market rewards with a higher multiple, the fee-related-earnings line, rather than in the line investors think of as cyclical carry. We can't assign intent here, because KKR hasn't stated a motive. But it is a checkable, structural choice about presentation. And it matters beyond one manager's quarter. As semi-liquid and evergreen vehicles keep pulling retail and wealth-channel dollars into these funds, the boundary between fee and carry increasingly becomes a reporting choice rather than an economic fact.

So if cash isn't coming back to investors at the pace the paper carry implies, why do they keep committing? Look at the allocator side. CalPERS reported a preliminary net investment return of fourteen point eight percent for the fiscal year ended June 30th, comfortably above its six point eight percent assumed rate. Its private-equity sleeve did even better, a preliminary seventeen percent. That's a mark, not a distribution. The release doesn't say how much of it was cash versus unrealized appreciation. But it is the mechanism that keeps the machine clearing. When the marked return beats the assumed rate by that much, the allocation gets defended internally, and commitments keep flowing even while realizations lag. Layer onto that CalPERS' formal move to a Total Portfolio Approach, replacing siloed asset-class targets with whole-portfolio construction. That is a structural change to how the largest U.S. public pension sizes and paces its private-equity commitments. What it will actually do to that pacing is not settled. It's a question general partners now have to price into their own fundraising timelines.

Now the deal tape, briefly, because it shows what an exit looks like when the buyer is another sponsor rather than a strategic acquirer or the public market. Thoma Bravo agreed to take insurance risk-exchange platform Accelerant private, an all-cash deal with an enterprise value of more than four billion dollars. Shareholders get twenty dollars and twenty-five cents a share, a forty-nine percent premium to the prior day's close. Don't over-read that premium. A premium is measured against wherever the share price happened to be trading, and we have no entry multiple and no exit multiple to judge this against. The more telling detail is the vote. Altamont Capital Partners, holding roughly eighty-two percent of the voting rights, had already agreed to vote yes before the deal was announced. The public market never really priced this asset. The seller and the buyer set the number between themselves. That is the same complaint this show has aimed at continuation vehicles all week, showing up here in a take-private wrapper. And the deal isn't closing this year. It's expected in the first half of 2027, with a six-percent annual ticking fee if insurance-regulatory approvals slip.

That closing date is worth dwelling on, because it's a pattern now, not a coincidence. Kelvion, the exit this show led with yesterday, also settles in the first half of 2027. Two of the cleanest sponsor exits currently on the tape both pay out next year, not this one. Announced is not delivered, and distributions-to-paid-in for 2026 will not get a lift from either deal.

So where does the cycle actually stand. The general partners' earnings and the investors' cash returns are being driven by two different businesses now. One compounds on fundraising. The other depends on exits that keep slipping a year out. Marked returns, not cash returns, are what's keeping commitments coming in. Watch three things from here. First, how September quarter-end marks land in late-October earnings. Second, whether Apollo's and Carlyle's next prints show the same realization gap Blackstone is showing, or extend the Carlyle exception. And third, whether any announced deal actually converts into a 2026 distribution, rather than another first-half-2027 close.
