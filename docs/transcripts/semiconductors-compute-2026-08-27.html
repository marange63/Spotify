Good morning, it's Thursday, August 27th, 2026, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

Four straight briefings this week ended the same way: pointing at Wednesday night's Nvidia print and saying, wait for it. It landed after the close on August 26th. And the headline number is not actually the story.

Start with the headline anyway, because it sets the context. Nvidia reported fiscal second-quarter revenue of $96.2 billion. That is up 106 percent year over year, against a consensus estimate of about $92.2 billion. Non-GAAP earnings came in at $2.22 a share, above the $2.06-to-$2.09 range Wall Street was modeling.

Set that against the positioning. The stock had fallen for seven straight sessions into the print, its first losing streak that long since September 2022. So the market went in defensive, and the number was not defensive at all.

Now the part that actually matters, which is composition. Data center revenue was $89 billion, and for the first time Nvidia broke it into two pieces. Hyperscale came in at $48.7 billion. The other bucket, which Nvidia calls ACIE — AI clouds, industrial, and enterprise — came in at $40.3 billion. That means roughly forty-five percent of Nvidia's data-center book is now sold to buyers outside the four balance-sheet hyperscalers everyone assumes are driving this cycle. Those are Nvidia's own segment definitions, by the way, not standard industry ones.

Read that two ways. It could mean demand for AI compute is genuinely broadening beyond Microsoft, Amazon, Google and Meta. Or it could mean the revenue mix is shifting toward AI clouds and neoclouds whose purchases are financed rather than paid out of operating cash flow — the same private-credit-funded layer this show has been documenting all week. Here's why that second reading has teeth. If a chunk of that financing gets more expensive, it doesn't just dent those customers' equity. It transmits straight into Nvidia's order book. Revenue durability at Nvidia is now partly a credit story, not purely a capex story.

The print also does not settle the question this show posed on Tuesday: is this revenue growth coming from more chips shipped, or from higher prices per chip? A single revenue line cannot separate the two. But Nvidia's own release does give us the number that had been missing. Gross margin for the quarter was 75.0 percent, on both a GAAP and a non-GAAP basis. Guidance for the current quarter is 74.0 percent, plus or minus fifty basis points. That is a full point of sequential compression on record revenue. Margin compression alongside record top-line growth is what you would expect if the mix is shifting toward lower-margin ACIE business and Blackwell Ultra ramp costs, or if a more expensive memory bill of materials is being passed through. We'll come back to why that memory cost is already locked in for the rest of the year.

On the guide itself: the number, straight from Nvidia's own release, is $108.0 billion for the current quarter, plus or minus two percent. That's roughly twelve percent sequential growth off the $96.2 billion base. It's a materially stronger trajectory than some early secondary reporting on this print suggested, and it is the figure to anchor to going forward.

Here's the subtraction that may matter more than any of the addition. Nvidia states directly, in its own outlook language, that it is not assuming any data-center compute revenue from China. And the footnote in its reconciliation table shows the H20 China charges have already gone quiet: a $4.5 billion charge in the first quarter of last fiscal year, a $180 million release in the second quarter of last fiscal year, and an insignificant net figure in the quarter just reported. So China went from a swing factor to a rounding error inside Nvidia's reported numbers, not just in the forward guide.

That is a strange thing to see land in the same week as reports that Nvidia has resumed some H200 shipments into China, with ByteDance and Tencent each said to have received roughly ten thousand units. I want to flag that rather than resolve it. Those shipment figures come from secondary reporting that could not be verified against a primary source or any published export notice, and Nvidia's own release doesn't address H200 at all — only data-center compute revenue in general. Layer on a separate, better-sourced thread: US officials are reviewing how Chinese firms access restricted Nvidia compute indirectly, by renting capacity from data centers in Thailand, Malaysia and Japan rather than importing chips. No rule has been proposed or published. But that is a harder problem to police than a shipment ban, because it targets compute access rather than hardware ownership. Put it together and export controls have stopped being a swing factor in Nvidia's numbers and become something closer to a call option: the downside is already written down to zero, and any policy reversal is upside nobody is modeling.

There's a quieter supply-side consequence too. Capacity that would have gone to China-compliant parts is freed for Blackwell Ultra and Rubin, which slightly loosens the packaging and high-bandwidth-memory queue this show has repeatedly called the binding constraint. Nvidia publishes no allocation figures, so treat that as directional only.

If demand is answered and policy is a coin flip, the next question is who else can physically make this silicon. That brings us to Samsung. On August 23rd this show said Samsung Foundry's 2-nanometer yield wasn't sourceable with any real confidence. There is now a number: roughly 60 percent, against the roughly 70 percent yield threshold that customers generally require before committing large production runs. Trade press has it improving through this year. Hold the caveat firmly, though — these are analyst and trade-press estimates, not figures Samsung discloses, and the 70 percent threshold is a rule of thumb, not a published customer specification. Samsung's utilization is running 70 to 80 percent, with some reports pointing to full capacity by year-end.

So that ten-point gap is a commercial timing problem, not an architectural one. The question is when Samsung crosses it, not whether it eventually does. And if it does, the first effect isn't share loss at TSMC. It's leverage. Hyperscaler ASIC programs today negotiate against a single qualified leading-edge source. Reporting puts Google, Tesla, Qualcomm and AMD among the companies evaluating Samsung's 2-nanometer process, though none of them has confirmed it and evaluating a process is several steps removed from taping out on it. A credible second source changes foundry pricing structure well before it changes foundry market share.

Last, briefly, the cost side that explains the margin line we started with. Micron reported back in late June — this is standing context, not new news — that it has completed price and volume agreements covering its entire calendar-2026 high-bandwidth-memory supply, including HBM4. Its own guidance for that quarter put gross margin around 86 percent, and that is a guide, not a reported result. What it means is that the split of AI-era profit between the memory supplier and the accelerator vendor is already contracted for this year, not spot-priced. So further AI demand strength in 2026 won't show up as memory price spikes the way past cycles trained investors to expect.

Two things follow from that. Any margin surprise at the accelerator vendors this year has to come from end pricing or mix, because the memory input is a fixed, known cost. And the renegotiation risk lands in 2027 — the same window Lam Research's own fab count put the next capacity wave in, which this show dated on Sunday.

So where this leaves us: the demand question is answered, decisively. The question of who captures the value that demand creates — hyperscalers versus AI clouds, Nvidia versus its memory suppliers, TSMC versus a still-catching-up Samsung — is not, and this print, for all its size, didn't close it. And be honest about the rest of today's read. Outside Nvidia's audited release, it rests on unverified shipment reports and trade-press yield estimates that no company has confirmed.
