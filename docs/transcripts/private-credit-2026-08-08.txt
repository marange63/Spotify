Good morning, it's Saturday, August 8th, and this is your private credit briefing. This earnings season is splitting the industry into two tiers. At the vehicle level, marks are eroding. At the manager level, the biggest platforms are deploying record capital into the same dislocation. Today's prints tell you exactly how wide that gap has become.

Start with Blue Owl's flagship listed BDC, OBDC. This is the publicly traded vehicle, not the interval funds — Credit Income Corp and Technology Income Corp — that hit their five percent redemption caps in yesterday's briefing. Different structure, different investors, same manager. OBDC reported Tuesday that net asset value per share fell to fourteen twenty-six, down from fourteen forty-one in the first quarter. Management attributed the decline to, quote, "markdowns on a small number of names, partially offset by over-earning the dividend and accretive share repurchases." Here's the tell. While the NAV got marked down, non-accruals at fair value actually improved, to zero point eight percent from one point zero percent. Non-accruals at cost moved the other way, worsening to two point eight percent from two point zero. We flagged this exact pattern at Blackstone's BXSL just yesterday and asked whether it would repeat across the sector. It just did, at a second flagship BDC. That makes it a lot harder to call this one manager's idiosyncratic conservatism. Two readings are still open. Either managers are getting ahead of a real deterioration and marking first-lien books down before the non-accrual data catches up — that's bullish, the market pricing risk correctly. Or the non-accrual accounting itself is lagging, and defaults are coming that the marks are only now anticipating — that's bearish. The dossier doesn't tell us which. Dividend coverage is still intact, with a nine point six percent annualized return on adjusted net investment income, but that's the metric to watch if the markdowns keep compounding. So what matters here: private marks may finally be catching up to reality, and the next few weeks of BDC earnings will tell you whether this is a two-name story or a whole-book one.

Now the other side of that trade. While the BDCs mark down, the managers themselves are leaning in. Apollo posted a record quarter on Tuesday: total assets under management near one point zero five trillion dollars, fee-related earnings of a record seven hundred eighty-five million, up twenty-five percent year over year. It originated seventy-four billion dollars in the quarter — a record — alongside sixty billion of organic inflows, also a record, with CEO Marc Rowan crediting, quote, "record earnings across Asset Management and Retirement Services." That origination figure excludes the thirty-five billion dollar Broadcom and Anthropic AI-chip financing, whose fees get recognized later. The more interesting move is infrastructure, not scale. Apollo confirmed it went live on July 1st with, quote, "an estimated daily net asset value for its entire investment grade fixed income suite of products," and that by October 1st it, quote, "expects to have daily pricing for all its credit assets." That's the plumbing behind retailization — daily pricing is what has to exist before a 401k plan can practically hold illiquid private credit. But be skeptical of what daily pricing actually solves. These assets don't trade daily. A daily mark on an asset that trades quarterly, or not at all, doesn't remove the liquidity mismatch — it just dresses it in a more convincing wrapper. That's the valuation-opacity risk this whole asset class carries, now being built directly into the retail on-ramp.

The most aggressive read on the dislocation comes from KKR. On its earnings call this week, KKR said its credit business has grown from eighty billion dollars to three hundred billion since the Global Atlantic acquisition — close to a fourfold scale-up funded substantially by an annuity balance sheet. Capital committed to the credit business but not yet earning fees is up thirty-three percent year over year, a forward deployment signal. And management's framing of the current volatility was blunt: quote, "volatility we're seeing, we think is helpful for us." That's a well-capitalized lender talking its book, and if the read is wrong, today's aggressive deployment becomes tomorrow's bad vintage. But the structural point matters beyond KKR's confidence: an annuity book, funded by retirement savers, is now the engine behind a major slice of private credit growth. That's the systemic linkage regulators — including the Fed's Bowman, in the thread we've been tracking — say they still can't fully see into. So institutional capital, funneled partly through insurance liabilities, is buying exactly what retail vehicles are gating.

One more data point, on whether lenders are actually getting paid for the risk they're taking. Ares Capital disclosed that new senior loan commitments this quarter priced twenty basis points wider than the fourth quarter of last year, with upfront fees up fifty basis points over the same stretch. Non-accruals at cost held at two point four percent, still below Ares' roughly three percent historical average since the financial crisis. June was, in management's words, quote, "one of our strongest months for new transactions reviewed in the past two years." That's a genuine, if modest, tightening in terms after years of spread compression from too much dry powder chasing too few deals. The caveat: this repricing applies to new originations only. It doesn't re-rate the existing book that OBDC and BXSL are marking down, and it's not yet clear whether twenty basis points of widening is market-wide or specific to Ares' upper-middle-market niche, where fewer lenders have the scale to compete.

Pulling it together: the credit cycle right now looks less like a single verdict and more like two markets moving in opposite directions inside the same asset class. Vehicle-level marks are eroding and retail capital is gating redemptions, while the largest managers deploy record sums into the identical dislocation on wider, better-compensated new-issue terms. The unresolved question is whether the BDC markdowns are an overdue reset finally catching up with credit quality, or conservative first-lien marking that gets proven right. Watch three things from here: whether the rest of BDC earnings season repeats the OBDC and BXSL inversion, whether dividend coverage at these funds starts to crack, and whether Apollo actually hits its October 1st daily-pricing target on schedule.

That's the briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
