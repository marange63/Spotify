Good morning. It's July 28th, and this is your briefing on robotics and physical AI.

The theme today is a pricing dispute, not a pricing calm. Embodied AI's valuations are supposed to be escalating across the board right now — but this week the sector's most technically respected humanoid brand just changed hands at what looks, on the surface, like a flat price. Dig one layer down, though, and that flat price turns out to be contested rather than settled. That's the story worth unpacking first.

On July 16th, Hyundai Motor Group agreed to buy SoftBank's remaining stake in Boston Dynamics, about ten percent, for three hundred twenty-five million dollars, becoming the sole owner. That price implies a valuation of roughly three point three billion dollars — the same number Hyundai paid for its original eighty percent stake back in 2021. Five years of humanoid-market froth everywhere else, and on paper Boston Dynamics hasn't moved.

But that three-point-three-billion figure isn't a market bid. It traces to a put-and-call option written into the original 2021 agreement, triggered because Boston Dynamics stayed private past a mid-2026 deadline. Hyundai's own statement confirms the deal simply executes the terms of that existing contract — it wasn't renegotiated. So the real question isn't whether Boston Dynamics is worth three point three billion. It's whether that contractual price still means anything five years later.

Here's where it gets genuinely contentious, not calm. Multiple analysts cited in Korean financial press now peg Boston Dynamics' actual current worth at somewhere between twenty billion and one hundred billion dollars, given how far the physical-AI boom has run since 2021. One outlet flatly called the gap between that estimate and the put-option price "a massive gap with market expectations." A separate report even described the transaction itself as valuing the company at "over twenty billion" — directly contradicting the three-point-three-billion figure used elsewhere. The sourcing doesn't let us resolve which framing is correct. What it does tell us is that this isn't a case of the market calmly marking the category leader flat while everyone else overheats. It's a case of a contractual price and independent market judgment pulling six to thirty times apart, with nobody agreeing on which one is real.

For scale, put that next to Figure AI, whose own release confirms it raised over a billion dollars in a Series C at a thirty-nine-billion-dollar post-money valuation, up from two point six billion just eighteen months earlier. That's an actual negotiated, disclosed market price for a rival humanoid platform. Against that backdrop, a three-point-three-billion put option looks less like evidence of market discipline and more like an artifact of a stale contract that Hyundai now gets to benefit from — and that's worth watching for blowback. If Boston Dynamics really is worth twenty billion or more, Hyundai's own minority shareholders have grounds to ask why the company's robotics unit changed hands at a fraction of that. So what this really tells the listener is: don't use Boston Dynamics as your flat-market anchor point this cycle. Use it as evidence that even sophisticated participants can't agree on what a leading physical-AI platform is worth right now.

Strategically, Hyundai isn't treating Boston Dynamics as something to sell externally anyway. It's folding the company fully into its own manufacturing stack, targeting Atlas humanoid deployment inside its Georgia metaplant by 2028, with a stated capacity goal of up to thirty thousand units a year and Nvidia and Google DeepMind as compute and model partners. That's a captive, vertical-integration bet — an auto OEM building its own robot workforce rather than selling a platform — and it stands in contrast to Figure and Physical Intelligence, which are pricing themselves as sell-to-anyone infrastructure. Treat the thirty-thousand-unit target as roadmap language, not a signed production order; nothing here is delivered yet.

Now, if Boston Dynamics gives us a valuation argument with no settled answer, Figure AI is where we actually get hard operating numbers this week — not a mark, but manufacturing data. Figure's own account of its BotQ factory reports a production-rate jump from one Figure 03 unit per day to one per hour, a twenty-four-times throughput increase achieved in under one hundred twenty days. The company has delivered over three hundred fifty units, with end-of-line first-pass yield above eighty percent, a battery line running at ninety-nine point three percent first-pass yield having shipped more than five hundred packs, and over nine thousand actuators produced across more than ten distinct part numbers.

That's a real automotive-supplier-style yield curve, and it's the closest thing the humanoid sector has produced to evidence that manufacturing at volume is becoming tractable. But notice what's missing. Figure hasn't disclosed cost per unit, price to its customers, uptime once deployed, or the intervention rate when its robots are actually working. BMW's own confirmation that Figure 02 spent eleven months on the Spartanburg line, supporting production of more than thirty thousand X3 vehicles before Figure 03 took over logistics-sequencing work at the same plant, tells you the deployment is real and repeatable. It doesn't tell you whether the economics pencil out. So the honest read is that Figure has solved a manufacturing precondition — you can now build these things with rising yield — while the number that would actually prove the unit economics, cost against labor displaced, remains undisclosed. Yield without a cost number isn't a business case yet; it's a very good factory.

There's also a supply-chain tell buried in that actuator count. Nine thousand actuators across more than ten part numbers is a demand signal in its own right, and the high-strength magnets inside those actuators still run through a rare-earth supply chain that China dominates. So even a humanoid designed, built, and assembled in California inherits the same component dependency that shapes the rest of this sector. The faster Figure ramps its build rate, the faster it scales that exposure too. That's the quiet catch under the yield curve: manufacturing progress in the West doesn't buy its way out of an Eastern bottleneck.

That same split — deploying versus still speculative — shows up inside a single company this week too. Tesla's robotaxi service expanded from Miami into Tampa and Orlando, and on last week's earnings call executives said the fleet is already running early builds of FSD version fifteen, carrying roughly ten times more parameters than the current build and already at about forty percent of its planned improvements. That's real commercial expansion, even if it's still regulator-gated market by market — Tesla's own Bay Area fleet still requires a human safety driver under California rules. Meanwhile Musk himself warned that Optimus faces a "long and flat" initial production ramp, citing roughly ten thousand unique parts on an entirely new Fremont line. So inside one company, the driving side of physical AI is iterating toward scale while the walking side is still pre-production. That's a useful discipline to apply everywhere else in this sector too.

Pulling the threads together: this week didn't hand us a calm, flat valuation to anchor against the froth. It handed us a valuation dispute at the very top of the humanoid market, sitting right next to the first real manufacturing-yield data the sector has produced, and a reminder from Tesla that deployment and speculation can coexist inside the same balance sheet. The catalyst to watch is whether Figure or anyone else finally discloses a cost-per-unit or intervention-rate number — that's the figure that turns yield into an actual investment thesis. Until then, price embodied AI on evidence, not on marks, and treat every valuation you hear this year as provisional.
