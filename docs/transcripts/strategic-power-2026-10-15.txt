Good morning. It's Thursday, October 15th, 2026.

Here's the thesis up front. The energy ceasefire between Russia and Ukraine is over. The more revealing story isn't that it broke. It's whom Washington leaned on to try to hold it together.

Start with the resolution. Yesterday, one of the items this desk told you to watch was whether Kyiv would give up waiting on Moscow and resume strikes on Russian energy targets. It just did. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy told the Financial Times today that Ukraine will now resume attacks on Russian energy targets. One line of setup, because we covered the announcement in depth yesterday. President Trump posted on Sunday that "there is an ENERGY CEASEFIRE in the War between Russia and Ukraine" and that "both parties have agreed," without saying what it covered. Zelenskyy said at the time he had not heard Russia agree. Four days later, that skepticism looks correct. One caution before going further. "Will resume" is declared intent. The record so far documents no resumed strike, so treat the tempo as unproven.

What's new today is the leverage mechanism, and it points in an uncomfortable direction. On Wednesday, Ukraine's military Starlink and Starshield terminals were restricted without warning. This is not a bandwidth throttle. It's a velocity limit. A terminal moving faster than about 160 kilometers an hour loses service. That is why it lands on the links Ukrainian forces use to fly interceptor drones against Russian missiles and strike drones. Elon Musk, whose SpaceX runs the system, put the control question on record himself. In his words, "Any limits on speed or usage are decided by the US military and ultimately the President of the United States." That is a direct claim of government control, not corporate control. It doesn't settle the question, though. A separate US official reportedly told Axios the administration had nothing to do with this particular restriction. Those two American accounts contradict each other. The White House has neither confirmed nor denied Musk's statement, and the Pentagon didn't respond to press inquiries. Notice what nobody on the American side is claiming, which is a technical malfunction. So this isn't malfunction versus deliberate action. By every on-record account, it's a deliberate setting with disputed authorship. One fair caveat. A velocity ceiling like that can also work as an anti-misuse setting, so the restriction alone doesn't prove coercion. It's the timing that led a Zelenskyy adviser to say it "cannot be a coincidence."

Now layer on the reported motive. Sources told Reuters, as reported by Axios, that the US threatened to cut early-warning intelligence unless Kyiv halted strikes on Russian oil refineries. That comes from anonymous sources, and the US government has not confirmed it. But it fits the shape of the week. The reported pressure was aimed at the refinery campaign specifically. Nothing in the record shows comparable pressure on Moscow, whose forces kept hitting Ukraine's power system after the announcement. A Russian ballistic missile strike on Kyiv this week killed at least three people in the capital, per Radio Free Europe, and knocked out power. Other outlets report higher tolls, so treat three as a floor. Zelenskyy said Kyiv again received no advance warning from its Western partners. No source names the United States as the party that withheld it, so hold that as an open question.

Here's the so-what. If this week's pressure ran toward the partner rather than the adversary, that tells you what the ceasefire was protecting. It wasn't mutual restraint. It was the flow of Russian diesel that Washington licensed last week, which reporting ties to cooling fuel prices before the midterms. Kyiv's announcement cuts directly against that goal. Twice this month, the diesel risk premium came out on a license and a declaration rather than on barrels. If refinery strikes resume at scale, that premium has a mechanism to come back. The tape hasn't confirmed that yet. One market report has Brent down about one and a half percent in Thursday trading. No source ties that move to Kyiv's announcement, so treat any price reaction as unconfirmed.

The next test is three days out. On October 18th, the Graham Act's tariff authority against buyers of Russian energy goes live. A week spent leaning on Kyiv rather than Moscow makes it less likely that authority gets used at all. So the question for Sunday is simple: is anyone actually targeted?

Now step back from the war for a moment. A sovereign military just lost satellite service on its fast-moving platforms mid-war, whoever ordered it. That is a live case study for every government relying on one foreign-controlled provider for both civilian and defense connectivity. The inference is straightforward. Allied defense ministries now have a concrete argument for sovereign or non-US low-earth-orbit alternatives, and for backup links they control themselves. It also politicizes SpaceX's widening infrastructure footprint. Every government contract SpaceX signs now carries the question Kyiv faced this week, which is who holds the off switch. So what: single-provider dependence just went from a theoretical risk to a documented one.

Europe read the same week very differently. On Monday, the EU's Foreign Affairs Council adopted what foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas called the largest single expansion of its Russia sanctions list since the full-scale war began. The Latvian foreign ministry puts it at nearly 1,600 individuals and entities. Kallas said more than half of the new listings are tied to ballistic missile production. That's the same capability Russia used against Kyiv this week. Now the caveat. A listing is not enforcement. Designating 1,600 parties doesn't tell you how many hold assets or supply chains Europe can actually reach. And years of earlier packages haven't stopped Russia from firing ballistic missiles at Ukrainian cities, as this week showed. So don't read this as evidence that Russian missile output will fall. Read it as a signal. Brussels is tightening on Russia in the same week Washington reportedly leaned on Kyiv to ease off. That transatlantic split is the real news in the package.

And Beijing noticed the gap. China's Commerce Ministry said today that Beijing "is willing to continue to provide facilitation for the approval of rare earth export licenses to the EU." Per CNBC's reporting, that holds whether or not the US-China trade truce is renewed. Last Saturday, this desk said Beijing's rare-earth lever runs on its own instrument, separate from the broader truce calendar. Now Beijing is running a separate clock for Europe too. The catch is the ask attached. The ministry said it "hopes the EU will offer facilitation for the reciprocal resolution of key licensing cases concerning China." In context, those are dual-use licensing cases. So this isn't a gift. The likelier reading is rare-earth access priced country by country. For Europe, the price is easing its own dual-use controls, which means drifting away from Washington's export-control line.

Two caveats keep that in proportion. Facilitation is a promise of process, with no committed volumes. And the underlying 2025 Chinese export controls are suspended, not repealed. For magnet supply chains in EVs, defense electronics, and wind turbines, the tail risk is reduced, not removed. The bigger second-order effect is on alignment. If Europe secures its own rare-earth channel, its incentive to coordinate with Washington on chip tools and dual-use controls weakens. So what: rare earths have become a divisible lever that Beijing can price separately for each customer.

Put the three together. Washington pressed its partner. Brussels escalated against the adversary. Beijing offered Europe a side deal decoupled from Washington's clock. That's US leverage fragmenting in three directions in a single week, not compounding. What would prove this reading wrong? Europe tightening its own dual-use controls instead of loosening them for Chinese magnets would do it. So would a credible US account of the Starlink restriction that reconciles Musk's version with the administration's denial. Watch October 18th for the Graham tariff authority. Watch October 22nd, when the Lukoil wind-down license expires. And watch whether Ukraine's refinery strikes actually materialize, with a diesel reaction to match.

That's the briefing for October 15th. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
