Capital Markets Cross-Asset Radar

Transcript · September 5, 2026

Good morning, it's Saturday, September 5th, and this is your Capital Markets Radar.

Since the start of this month, one scenario has stayed on the table here. It was an energy shock arriving on top of a softening labour market, the stagflation setup nobody was being paid to hold. On Thursday we fed it the first labour-market evidence we had, ADP's soft private payroll count, and said in the same breath that one ADP reading sits inside the error bars and settles nothing. It settled nothing. The Bureau of Labor Statistics published the number that actually counts on Friday morning. Nonfarm payrolls rose 162,000 in August, against a consensus of roughly 56,000.

The revisions did more damage to our read than the headline number did. June and July were revised up by a combined 55,000 jobs, and July flipped from an outright decline to a gain. So the month this beat treated as the first crack in hiring now reads as growth. The softening-labour-market leg of that scenario is gone. That is not a marginal beat, it is a falsified call, and we are scoring it against ourselves rather than quietly moving on.

Look at the internals. The unemployment rate held at 4.1 percent, and average hourly earnings grew at the same contained pace they have run at for months. So this is a labour market that is firm in the level of employment, not one generating a fresh wage impulse. Nothing here says a wage-price spiral restarted.

Here is why that is hawkish rather than reassuring. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh built his Jackson Hole case a week and a half ago on the breadth of price increases, and said explicitly that the summer's softer inflation readings did not show meaningful improvement. Now watch what a firm jobs number does to that argument. It does not strengthen it, because Warsh never argued from labour slack in the first place. What it does is remove the one objection that could have stayed his hand, which is the fear of tightening into a visible slowdown. That distinction is what the good-news-is-bad-news equity reaction was actually pricing.

Market pricing moved accordingly, and the pattern in that pricing is more interesting than the Fed itself. Implied odds of a hike at the still-pending September 16th decision came into Friday near fifty-fifty, after a single conditional dovish sentence from Governor Waller earlier in the week. They came out of the jobs report back in the high fifties to low sixties. Treat that as a range rather than a figure, because desks disagree, and as pricing ahead of a decision that has not happened. Step back over the week and a half. Those odds have travelled from the mid-thirties before Warsh spoke, up to roughly two-thirds, back down to even, and up again, with no policy action and no inflation release in between. The volatility is living in the pricing, not in the policy. That is a positioning problem as much as a macro one.

Where did the pain actually land? The S&P 500 slipped about four tenths of a percent on the print. The Russell 2000 dropped 1.40 percent. This was not a broad growth scare. It was a discount-rate event, and it was absorbed by the most duration-sensitive, highest-beta, most floating-rate-exposed block of the equity market. If you are long small caps here, you are short the front end whether you meant to be or not.

Gold confirms the same read from the other side of the book. The metal is down roughly 22 percent from the record it set in late January, and it slipped again on the day of the payroll print. That is happening while a live Strait of Hormuz conflict runs in the background. Gold falling anyway, during a shooting war near the world's most important oil chokepoint, tells you real yields rather than geopolitics own the hard-asset complex. Earlier this week this beat called gold's bounce noise and said the drawdown was the durable fact. That call held through the most hawkish data event of the week.

Which brings us to oil, the other inflation leg, and the reason the stagflation framing is not dead so much as transformed. Brent crude is trading near $95 a barrel, up about 8 percent on the week as the Hormuz conflict flares again. The oil price is not the news here, because the energy leg of the call was never the part in doubt. The combination is the news. An energy shock landing on a labour market that just printed hot is a different policy object than the same shock landing on one that is cracking. A shock on a cracking labour market splits the Fed's mandate and forces a choice between inflation and jobs. A shock on a firm labour market does no such thing. It lets the Fed tighten into resilience, which is straightforwardly hawkish rather than stagflationary. So the inflation prints, not the labour data, are now the binding constraint on the September decision.

Now from the cost of money to the question of who is actually buying the front end. Yesterday this beat described regulated stablecoin reserves as a price-inelastic bid for Treasury bills, and then admitted on air that no dollar figure for the size of that pool survived our checks. Bloomberg supplied the magnitude on Friday, and it cuts the other way. The stablecoin float sits at roughly $300 to $310 billion and has stopped growing, as crypto trading volumes slow. Set that against the pitch attributed to Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, as reported rather than in his own words, of stablecoin issuers becoming a trillion-dollar-scale buyer base under the GENIUS Act's reserve rules. That is not a shortfall a good quarter closes. It is a gap of several times the entire current float.

Give the other side its due. A stalling float during a crypto-volume slowdown is cyclical evidence, not a structural verdict on what a regulated, bank-issued coin eventually does to bill demand. The bank consortium announced last week does not launch a product until 2027. The bull case here is deferred, not dead. But one line inside the stall is genuinely compounding, and it is tokenised Treasury collateral. Circle's USYC has now overtaken BlackRock's BUIDL as the largest such vehicle. So the mechanism is real, the collateral use case is growing, and the payments use case, the one that was supposed to buy the bills, is not. For front-end demand that is a mild headwind, and it arrives exactly when the Fed may be adding to financing costs itself.

Crypto's own price action makes a related point about lenses. On Thursday we told you daily ETF flow data is the wrong frame for judging a structural bid. The monthly number backs that up. August was the best month of 2026 for US spot Bitcoin ETFs, at $3.52 billion of net inflows, which pulled year-to-date net outflows down to roughly $1.77 billion. Meanwhile the daily series did exactly what a daily series does. The month opened with a $236 million outflow, then reversed to two straight days of inflows that more than erased it.

But the monthly lens is not vindication. August's inflow arrived alongside a 25 percent price gain in the same month, which is at least as consistent with flow chasing price as with a durable allocation shift. BlackRock's IBIT drove both the outflow and the reversal, so these are aggregator tallies of one distribution channel, not a measure of broad-based demand. The genuinely interesting fact is what Bitcoin itself did through the repricing, which is essentially nothing. It held between roughly $78,000 and $80,000 across the biggest macro event of the week, while gold fell. Two assets that have spent months trading as paired hard-money hedges have stopped moving as one trade. That is a pattern break worth watching.

Last, the price of protection. The VIX sat around 14.32 on Friday. That is within two tenths of a point of the 14.13 low this beat reported before the entire hawkish repricing began, and it is well below the 16 handle it carried midweek. Hold the intraday timing loosely, because a falling volatility print and a down equity session on the same day suggests a clock mismatch between sources. The level is the point, not the daily change. The cost of owning volatility into a genuinely two-sided decision has fallen back to the cycle lows while the dispersion of outcomes has widened. That is an asymmetry available to buy. It is not a forecast that it pays.

The calendar does the talking from here. Producer prices land on the 10th, consumer prices on the 11th, and the Fed decides on the 16th. None of those outcomes are known. So here is the falsifier for everything you just heard. If those inflation prints come in cool, a strong labour market stops looking hawkish, the binding constraint disappears, and this whole round trip runs again in reverse.

That's the Radar for today. Trade safe, and we'll see you next time.
