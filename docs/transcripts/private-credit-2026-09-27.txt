Good morning, it's Sunday, September 27th, 2026. Here's where the credit machine stands.

The frame for this one is a split screen. The exit queue in private credit's wealth-channel vehicles is easing, and not just at one manager. At the same time, the market's own price of BDC risk sits at a record gap over investment-grade corporates. That's liquidity healing while credit isn't.

Start with the queue. On Friday we told you Apollo's Debt Solutions BDC saw redemption requests fall to 14.7% of shares from 16.8%. We also flagged an open question. Was Apollo a one-off, or a sector pattern? This week's tender disclosures from BlackRock's HPS-branded funds answer it, though less cleanly than the headline suggests.

HPS Corporate Lending Fund, known as HLEND, saw third-quarter requests of 11.5% of shares, down from 13.3% the quarter before. That is still more than double its 5% ceiling. HLEND's own 8-K confirms it was capped exactly like Apollo, repurchasing 5% of shares, or about 600 million dollars. So HLEND is a genuine second data point for the capped-but-easing pattern.

Two other funds in the same family tell a different story. At BlackRock Private Credit Fund, about 4.58% of shares were tendered, under its 5% offer. HPS Corporate Capital Solutions Fund came in lower still, at around 1.9%. On the mechanics, neither tender should have been rationed this quarter, though neither source says so in as many words. Exit demand at those two had already fallen below the level where the cap binds. So easing is not one state. HLEND is still gated. Those two funds are not.

The bullish read comes from an analyst quoted by Reuters, who called the clearing backlog "another encouraging data point on direct lending sentiment in the wealth channel." That view deserves a fair statement before we test it.

Here's the test. Falling request rates fit two very different stories, and this data can't separate them. One is that sentiment is genuinely recovering. The other is that the queue is converging on a shrinking stock of gated holders. That's the read this show gave you last Sunday, when North Haven, Cliffwater and BCRED all showed flattening request rates. The newest filings raise a third possibility. HLEND's disclosures say unfulfilled requests don't roll over automatically. Holders must resubmit in the next window. So some of the decline could be fatigue, with holders giving up on the queue rather than changing their minds. The point to bank is this. The exit door is no longer jammed, but we can't yet tell whether fewer people are pushing because they're confident, because they've already left, or because they've stopped trying.

Apollo, for its part, is marketing its own number to the people still in that queue. By Apollo's figures, the Class I shares of Debt Solutions have delivered an 8.2% net total return since inception, as of August 31st. Apollo puts that at 177 basis points over leveraged loans and 377 over high yield. Take it for what it is. It's a manager-reported return, earned through a stretch of benign realized defaults, and it is not loss-adjusted. So here's what the market thinks of the risk behind that pitch.

The data comes from Moody's probability-of-default series, as analyzed by the research outfit Private Markets Insights. Start with the backdrop. Average one-year default probability for US listed companies fell to 7.6% in July, from 8.5% a year earlier. On that surface, the cycle looks benign.

BDCs are moving the other way. Default probability for publicly traded BDCs has risen 44% since the third quarter of 2025. For Baa-rated corporates over the same stretch, it's up just 9%. In July, public BDCs sat at 0.51% against 0.27% for Baa, which Private Markets Insights calls the widest spread on record. Be clear about what this measures. It's a market-implied probability, not a realized default rate. The absolute levels are small, so the signal is the ratio and its direction.

The obvious rebuttal is that this is just equity-market gloom about BDC shares trading below NAV. The private-BDC number undercuts that. Default probability for privately held BDCs, which have no traded shares to sour on, is up 29% over the same period. That's more than three times the Baa move. The rebuttal doesn't fully die, because model inputs could still pick up sector sentiment. But it gets much weaker.

The realized side is starting to corroborate. Morningstar data shows the BDC non-accrual share held in a narrow band of 1.3 to 1.5%. Then, in the first quarter of 2026, it jumped 52 basis points to 1.9%. The takeaway is that the market is pricing a deterioration that reported numbers, and the private marks built on them, are only beginning to show.

So how does a gap like that actually close inside a private book? Not gradually. It closes in a jump.

Blue Owl's flagship BDC, OBDC, held a second-lien position in specialty-materials maker Loparex at roughly 88 cents on the dollar at the end of 2025. By the end of March, that mark was about 63 cents. By the end of June, it was roughly 5 cents. In between, a refinancing effort fell through, and Loparex missed a second-lien interest payment in June. OBDC has moved the exposure to non-accrual, and Loparex is operating under a forbearance agreement through this month. This is a second-quarter mark disclosed in early September, not this week's news. But its catalyst is live right now.

Keep it in proportion. OBDC's total non-accrual exposure is still only about 0.8% of fair value, so this is one name, not a system. There's a fair defense of Blue Owl, too. It cut the mark within a quarter of the missed payment, which is arguably the process working. The real critique is the 88-cent year-end mark on a credit that missed a coupon six months later. Last Sunday we said the next move in tail marks would be a jump, not a slope. Loparex is the first clean single-name instance of what that jump looks like.

The more alarming number isn't the second lien, though. OBDC also held a first-lien Loparex loan that had been carried close to par. It's now marked at around 22 cents. That's a senior-secured claim losing nearly four-fifths of its value on paper. If Loparex files, that becomes a hard recovery datapoint for post-2020 direct-lending structures, and a direct test of the senior-secured recovery assumptions built into them. The lesson is that a private mark reflects the last test event, not continuous price discovery. Most positions in this market simply haven't been tested yet.

One more thread ties this together, and it's about what the default count leaves out. S&P Global Ratings counted 62 corporate defaults globally through July, fewer than the 71 at the same point in 2025. That's another tick for the benign-cycle story. But 27 of those 62 were distressed exchanges, the single largest cause this year. And a 2023 S&P study found that 35% of distressed exchanges led to a repeat default within four years. That study describes past cohorts, not this one. Still, if anything like that rate holds, part of this cycle's defaults haven't been avoided. They've been rescheduled into 2027 and 2028. A distressed exchange is a queue for defaults, the way a tender is a queue for cash.

So where does this leave the cycle? Liquidity in the wealth channel is genuinely improving, and the redemption queues are draining. Credit quality is drifting the other way, by the market's own pricing of BDC risk and by the non-accrual data underneath it. And private marks, when they finally move, move the way Loparex just did. The biggest unresolved risk is mark integrity in gated retail vehicles. Tender pressure is one of the few forces that makes a manager sell loans and confront a real price. The same easing that calms the queue also removes that pressure, so the marks face less discovery just as the credit signals turn.

Watch three things. First, Blue Owl's Credit Income Corp tender closes September 30th, against an 18.8% request rate last quarter. A similar number would say that queue isn't draining. Second, whether Loparex's forbearance ends in court or out of it. Third, the third-quarter BDC filings in November, the first real test of whether this quarter's easing holds, and whether marks like Loparex's are surfacing elsewhere.

That's the state of the machine. Thanks for listening, and we'll see you next time.
