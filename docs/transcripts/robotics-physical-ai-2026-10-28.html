Cautious Optimism Briefings

Robotics & Physical AI

Transcript · October 28, 2026

Good morning, this is Cautious Optimism Briefings for Wednesday, October 28th, 2026.

Here's the spine for today. Physical AI keeps growing its footprint faster than it proves its economics. We'll look at two versions of that gap, one in a Southern warehouse and one on the Shanghai exchange. Then we'll close with the one corner of the sector where scale has actually turned into paid volume.

Start with the lead. GXO and Agility Robotics announced on Monday that Digit, Agility's humanoid, is expanding to two new U.S. e-commerce sites. They are in Statesville, North Carolina, and Lebanon, Tennessee, and deployments are expected to begin in early 2027. Both sites will start on the current Digit 4. They move to Digit 5 only once it becomes commercially available. So the near-term expansion runs on the generation Agility is already trying to replace.

This is the second piece of Agility customer news in about a week. Last Friday this desk covered AGCO's planned 2027 pilot, and called it a logo, not revenue. GXO is different, because it comes with a track record. At GXO's original site in Flowery Branch, Georgia, Digit has moved more than 100,000 totes. Across all of Agility's commercial deployments, the robot has logged more than 65,000 hours of real-world operation. Credit where it's due. That is the first humanoid program this desk has covered with a genuine cumulative operating history, not just a press-release pilot.

Now the limits of those numbers. The 65,000 hours cover every Agility deployment, not GXO's alone. So don't divide the Flowery Branch tote count by them and call it a throughput rate. Neither company disclosed how many robots run at any site, or what GXO pays. That leaves four inputs missing for a payback calculation: robot count, per-robot throughput, uptime, and price. So the operating record is real, but it can't yet be turned into economics.

Here's the tension. GXO's own CEO, Patrick Kelleher, spoke to investors on the company's second-quarter call in August. He said, "We have done 45 pilots on humanoids so far." And then: "We have not achieved ROI on humanoids yet. I think we are a couple of years away from that." The transcript of that call goes further. Kelleher said, "In production, zero will be humanoids this year. We have a number of humanoids deployed in pilot." And on when production use itself arrives, he said, "I think in production, we are probably two years away from that."

Two cautions on those quotes. First, Kelleher was describing GXO's whole humanoid pilot book. He gave no count of vendors, so this is not a verdict on Digit specifically. Second, he named no cost, throughput, or reliability figure as the blocker. He spoke only in general terms about progress with partners on how well the technology works. Our read, and it is inference, is that the open variable is task performance under real operating conditions, not just hardware price. That matters, because it means cheaper robots alone may not close the gap.

So why expand into two new sites with no ROI in sight? The rational reading is that this is a paid option, not proof the technology has arrived. The relationship has been reported as a robots-as-a-service arrangement, a structure in which the vendor typically carries much of the early cost. GXO buys learning and positioning for when the curve bends. Kelleher's "couple of years" puts payback beyond the early-2027 start of the new sites. So the expansion is a bet on the curve, and that is consistent with his words, not a contradiction of them.

The timing isn't neutral either. Agility's merger with its SPAC partner goes to a shareholder vote in early November. This news lands right in the window where deployment headlines help the vendor's listing story. Investors in that deal will have to price breadth of deployment against economics nobody has disclosed. The takeaway: Digit has real operating hours, but operating data and unit economics are not the same thing.

If a customer with 45 humanoid pilots says the technology doesn't pay yet, Shanghai's market is pricing the opposite. According to BigGo Finance's coverage of Shanghai trading data, Unitree's shares gained about two-thirds over seven trading sessions through Monday's close.

That close put Unitree's market value at about 271.8 billion yuan, roughly 40.8 billion dollars. The same coverage reports that 23 A-share robot stocks hit their daily limit-up that day.

That is an escalation of a number we've been tracking. On October 23rd this desk reported Unitree back above 200 billion yuan, and the driver then was Beijing's policy guidance. This time the coverage ties the run to an industry event Unitree co-hosted, the World Physical AI Conference in Hangzhou on October 22nd. The fundamentals, meanwhile, haven't moved in anything we can verify. The last hard figure on revenue mix, from the pre-IPO period, had research and education at 73.6 percent of revenue. In other words, roughly three-quarters of Unitree's sales went to labs and classrooms, not to factories or warehouses. Nothing in this week's reporting updates that.

The counterpoint comes from the company's own founder. Wang Xingxing told the conference, "In as little as a year, or as long as three, large models will break through into physical AI." Read that carefully. He's saying the breakthrough hasn't happened yet. The market is treating a forecast one to three years out as a reason to re-rate the stock today. Limit-up rallies in A-shares often run on retail momentum and policy narrative rather than confirmed demand. So this is capital pricing an inflection, not evidence that one has occurred.

Wang's line is also the only model-layer signal in this week's reporting. We found no verifiable new release from the robot foundation model labs this cycle. So the general-purpose robot brain is still a forecast. Our read is that task-specific engineering is doing the work in every deployment in today's briefing, from tote moving to driving.

Set that next to GXO and you can see the link in what the market is pricing. A valuation of roughly 40 billion dollars on a mostly lab-and-classroom revenue base only works if industrial demand arrives. Kelleher's couple-of-years horizon is the best read we have on when that kind of demand pays, and it isn't this year. So what: valuation is running well ahead of the capability timeline, on both sides of the Pacific.

There is one corner of physical AI where scale has already turned into paid volume, and it's on the road. Waymo said on Tuesday it is growing its driverless service area by nearly 250 square miles over the next week, across five metros. That sits on top of an operation Waymo says already runs more than 500,000 paid rides a week across 15 U.S. metros. Square miles are geography, not demand. The paid-ride figure is the number that carries the argument, because it measures customers paying for a unit of work.

Tesla, meanwhile, widened its Houston robotaxi geofence on Monday. That's growth inside a metro Tesla already serves, not new territory. Now compare the mileage. Waymo logged 3.9 million rider-only miles a week in the second quarter. A third-party tracker, not Tesla itself, puts Tesla's driverless fleet at 558,000 miles in its latest reported week, mostly in Austin.

That works out to roughly a sevenfold gap. One caveat: it compares a second-quarter average with a single recent week, so treat the multiple as directional, not precise. There's also a fair counter-reading. Tesla's vision-only approach on consumer-built vehicles has a different cost structure, and it could scale faster once validated. A mileage snapshot can't settle that.

But the gap fits a pattern. On Sunday this desk noted that Ashok Elluswamy, who runs Tesla's AI software, put unsupervised self-driving for customers at "somewhere around Q2 next year." That was a slip from Musk's earlier end-of-this-year guidance. Today's mileage gap is the operating evidence behind that slip. So what: in robotaxis, Waymo is converting footprint into paid rides, while Tesla is still widening neighborhoods.

So, pulling the threads together. Robotaxis are the one part of physical AI where scale has demonstrably become paid volume. Waymo's half-million weekly rides are a real number, not a narrative. Humanoids are accruing real operating hours, which is progress. But nobody has published the paid-units-of-work figure that would let you judge them the same way. And Shanghai is pricing a breakthrough that Unitree's own founder places one to three years out. Waymo's paid-ride number is the disclosure bar humanoids will eventually be held to: paid units of work per week, not sites, pilots, or hours.

Watch three things. First, Agility's SPAC vote in early November. Second, whether the Statesville and Lebanon sites open on schedule in early 2027, and on which Digit. Third, whether Waymo's next disclosure moves that 500,000-ride figure. Until a humanoid vendor publishes something comparable, footprint and economics remain two different stories.

That's the briefing. Stay cautiously optimistic.
