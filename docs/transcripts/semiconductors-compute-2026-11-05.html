Good morning. This is your Cautious Optimism semiconductors and compute briefing for Thursday, November 5th.

Yesterday we closed on one line: the toll on the route around Nvidia gets paid in HBM, not avoided by it. Today's reporting pushes that idea one layer deeper. The question is no longer only who pays for the memory. It's who builds the logic die at the bottom of the memory stack. And on fresh reports, the two biggest GPU vendors are answering that question in opposite directions.

Start with what's being reported, and by whom. Korean trade press, relayed by TrendForce and Digitimes, says Samsung's second fab in Taylor, Texas, a 2-nanometer facility, is already fully booked. TrendForce's language is specific. In its words, "Samsung has secured orders covering the entire planned production capacity of Taylor Fab 2." The lead customer is reportedly AMD, ordering HBM base dies. TrendForce adds that "the facility's capacity could be stretched even by AMD's orders alone." The same report says Nvidia has asked all three major HBM makers, Samsung included, to outsource production of its next-generation base die to TSMC instead.

Now weight that properly, because the timeline is long. Full-scale construction on Taylor Fab 2 is only scheduled to begin this month. TrendForce puts mass production in 2030. Samsung has confirmed that timing on the record. A company spokeswoman said in late October that construction begins in November, with the fab planned to be operational by 2030. But a Samsung official declined to name any customer, saying the company "cannot confirm customer-related details." We found no on-record comment from AMD or Nvidia either. So the only company-confirmed facts are a building and a date. Everything about who ordered what is trade press citing unnamed sources, four years ahead of output. "Fully booked" at this stage is a reservation, not a shipment. It's a claim to watch, not a market-share shift.

So why lead with a base die at all? Because the base die is increasingly a logic-process part rather than a DRAM-process part. That blurs a line that used to be clean. Memory makers built the stack, and foundries built the GPUs. If the base die moves to a foundry, the memory maker cedes one layer of its own product. And the GPU vendor gains a say in how that layer gets built.

On this reporting, Nvidia and AMD are choosing opposite strategies for that layer. Nvidia is reportedly pulling base-die production toward TSMC. That's the same foundry that already makes its GPU die and packages it in CoWoS. AMD's orders, meanwhile, are reportedly anchoring a new Samsung fab on US soil. One vendor concentrates further inside Taiwan's dominant foundry. The other diversifies toward Texas.

It's worth being direct that the two reports don't fully agree. Digitimes frames Samsung as making base dies at Taylor for AMD and possibly Nvidia. TrendForce frames Nvidia as steering its base dies to TSMC, away from Samsung. Both can't be fully true for the same Nvidia product. We tried to pin down which HBM generation, which Nvidia platform, and which process node the orders cover. We came up short. We couldn't access the original Digitimes report, and no company has said on the record where Nvidia's base dies will be made. So treat that conflict as open. Be skeptical of anyone stating either version as settled fact.

One primary source does cut against the easy reading of this story. SK hynix's own newsroom says it used proprietary technology to make base dies "up to HBM3E." For the next generation, it plans to adopt "TSMC's advanced logic process for HBM4's base die so additional functionality can be packed into limited space." That's framed as a node migration, and the stated reason is technical, with no mention of any customer request. So for SK hynix at least, Nvidia's reported ask may describe a move already under way. That narrows the story. The ask is a real change mainly for suppliers not already headed to TSMC. And we have no on-record source for where Samsung or Micron build their base dies today.

That sets up two readings of Nvidia's motive. The leverage reading says Nvidia wants the memory makers to control less of the stack it buys from them. The integration reading says a TSMC-made base die sits more cleanly beside a TSMC-made GPU, inside TSMC's own CoWoS line. That's an engineering argument, not a power play. Our lean, and it is judgment, tilts toward integration as the main driver. The only primary evidence we have is SK hynix's, and its reasoning is technical. Leverage may still matter at the margin with Samsung and Micron. But on today's evidence, that's an inference, not something any source shows.

Now score it by competitive dimension, rather than naming one winner. Samsung Foundry gets a reported customer relationship and a US manufacturing anchor at 2 nanometers. If it holds, that's a rare win against TSMC. But it says nothing yet about production leadership, which is four years out. TSMC, if Nvidia's ask holds, extends its reach by one more layer. It would hold the GPU die, the CoWoS package, and part of the memory stack. The memory makers, as a group, stand to lose some end-to-end control of their own product. That bears on the pricing power this desk has tracked since late October, but only if the leverage reading is right. On Taiwan concentration, the reported choices diverge. Nvidia's route deepens reliance on a single island. AMD's route puts a piece of its memory supply chain in Texas. That's our inference from the reports, not a strategy either company has stated. The takeaway: the contest over memory has moved to a layer that memory makers like SK hynix used to build themselves.

But that's a 2030 fight. The squeeze is now, and it runs on a different clock. TrendForce is forecasting another 10 to 15 percent rise in conventional DRAM contract prices for the fourth quarter. That's commodity DRAM, not HBM. It also expects tight supply to persist into 2027. Separately, TeamGroup's chief executive said on November 2nd that the tightest stretch runs from this quarter through the second quarter of 2027. Two flags on sourcing. Both points reach us through secondary reporting, not TrendForce's own release, so we can't date that forecast precisely. And TeamGroup makes memory modules. It buys DRAM rather than fabricating it. So that's a buyer's read on a market it has every incentive to call scarce.

Here's the part that cuts against the consensus this desk has carried. On its third-quarter call, SK hynix described its DRAM and HBM volumes as effectively sold out through 2029, and we've leaned on that framing. If TeamGroup is right that the squeeze is tightest by mid-2027, that's a dated peak. And a dated peak implies a top. That's a more cyclical read than a multi-year wall. We'd flag that tension rather than resolve it. The practical point is that the market may now have a specific date to watch for when the squeeze starts to ease.

So why can't supply simply catch up sooner? Go back to ASML's October 14th results. They're three weeks old, so this is context rather than news. ASML's management said planned lithography supply for 2027 and 2028 "will not fully meet the demand" of its customers. It added that some customers want to place orders for 2029 and even 2030. Discount that a little. An equipment vendor saying demand exceeds supply is also setting up its next round of pricing talks. But the mechanism holds. New memory and logic capacity is gated by tools, not just by capital. ASML said nothing about Samsung or base dies. Our inference is that tool scarcity fits Taylor Fab 2's 2030 output date. It also helps explain why the base-die fight is being booked four years out.

Put the two clocks side by side. Relief on today's memory squeeze, if TeamGroup is right, runs on a 2027 clock. Control of who builds the logic inside the memory stack is being contested on a 2030 clock. Neither clock speeds up the other, so don't read news on one as news on the other.
