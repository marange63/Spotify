Private Equity & the Deal Economy

Good morning, it's October 22nd, 2026, and this is your private equity briefing.

For a week now, this show has been counting capital going into take-privates. On the other side of the ledger, there's still no signed sale of a sponsor-held company to a genuine third-party buyer. Today brings the biggest entry yet, and the first one two mega-sponsors visibly fought over.

EQT has agreed to take Qiagen private at forty-six euros fifty a share in cash. That's an equity value of roughly nine point six billion euros, or about ten point eight billion dollars. The buyer is EQT's eleventh flagship fund. Affiliates of the Abu Dhabi Investment Authority and Canada Pension Plan Investment Board are participating in the offer alongside it.

This was a real contest, not a sole-source negotiation. On October 20th, Bloomberg reported that EQT had made a binding offer of more than fifty dollars a share, and that KKR had also bid. KKR's price was never reported. The signed price works out to about fifty-two dollars twenty-two. So competition visibly lifted the number between the binding offer and the signature.

Now the detail that changes how you read the deal. Some secondary reports put the premium at just eight point six percent above Qiagen's last close. On that number, this looks like a sponsor paying almost nothing over a stock that had already run up on takeover chatter. That framing is wrong. The joint EQT and Qiagen release puts the premium at forty point three percent. It measures that against Qiagen's XETRA close of thirty-three euros fifteen on July 8th. The companies identify that as the last trading day before takeover reports surfaced. So the small headline number was measured off a base that speculation had already lifted. Forty point three percent sits in the same neighborhood as the software take-private premiums this show covered last week. The lesson is simple. Check the date a premium is measured from before you trust it.

The terms are built for certainty, with a long runway. The release says the deal is not subject to any financing condition. It needs acceptance from fifty percent of outstanding shares plus one, along with shareholder meeting resolutions and regulatory clearances. That bar matters at this company, because Thermo Fisher's 2020 attempt to buy Qiagen failed on its acceptance threshold. Closing is expected in mid-2027. If regulatory approvals are still pending after ten months, Qiagen may pay shareholders an interim dividend of up to forty-seven cents a share. A provision like that suggests both sides expect serious regulatory review, possibly including foreign-investment screening given the sovereign money in the stack. The debt and equity split is undisclosed, so take no view yet on leverage.

Alongside the deal, Qiagen disclosed preliminary third-quarter sales of five hundred forty-eight million dollars and adjusted earnings of sixty-five cents a share. It has suspended its full-year outlook while the transaction is pending.

So what does Qiagen add up to? Two of the largest buyout firms fought over a scarce, cash-generative healthcare asset, and competition pushed the price up. That cuts against the industry's talking point that entry multiples have reset to attractive levels. Full-price entries also push realizations further out for the vintage deploying now, and they raise the bar for its eventual exit. The co-investors matter too. ADIA and CPPIB are writing direct checks into a chosen deal. That echoes CPP Investments sitting beside Blackstone in AirTrunk, which this show discussed yesterday. The largest allocators are still deploying deal by deal, even as the broad base of limited partners reports a cash squeeze. And remember, this is signed, not closed. Qiagen's public shareholders will get paid. Not one dollar goes back to a private equity limited partner.

Which raises the obvious question. Why are sponsors fighting to put money in while limited partners say they're short of cash? Because the money is concentrating at the top, and the cash isn't coming back. PitchBook's second-quarter fundraising report, as summarized in secondary coverage, says nearly five trillion dollars of net asset value now sits in funds seven years or older. It pairs that with three straight years of negative net cash flow to limited partners. Keep in mind that five trillion is a mark, not cash. What it's worth depends on clearing prices that this show has argued sit somewhat below the marks.

Yet raising hasn't stopped. PitchBook has private equity fund closes rebounding to a hundred sixty-five point three billion dollars in the second quarter. PwC adds that first-half dollars are concentrating in established managers. So this isn't a fundraising collapse. It's concentration into brand-name managers, layered on a three-year cash drought. Those are two different problems, and the second one is the binding constraint.

The dry powder data completes the picture. Per PitchBook figures cited in first-half market coverage, dry powder fell ten percent from the start of the year. The capital overhang multiple is now close to one times. You might expect shrinking dry powder to cool deal competition. At the top of the market, it does the opposite. Fewer, larger pools are competing for the same handful of quality assets. That is exactly what produced the Qiagen contest.

So if sales to third parties aren't producing the cash, where is it coming from? Increasingly, from vehicles the selling manager controls. This is first-half data, and the record total itself isn't new to this show. Evercore, as reported, puts secondaries volume above a hundred twenty billion dollars. What's new is the composition. GP-led deals made up fifty-three point seven percent of that volume. Single-asset continuation vehicles alone accounted for thirty-four billion dollars, more than half of all GP-led volume.

There are two ways to read that. The bull case is a deep, functioning liquidity market, where limited partners get a real choice to sell or roll. The bear case is about price discovery. When most secondaries liquidity runs through vehicles the selling GP structures, the GP also shapes the price that validates its own mark. The only independent check is what the secondaries buyer is willing to pay. That favors the managers with the scale to run these deals. It leaves the long tail of aged holdings untested.

Limited partners themselves are drawing a line on how manufactured that liquidity can get. A Rede Partners survey of limited partners, as reported, found broad support for NAV loans that fund add-on acquisitions or refinance existing debt. Money-out loans are different. These borrow against the portfolio to fund distributions, and limited partners rejected them by sixty-two percent to thirty-eight. The survey's date and sample aren't verified, so treat it as one read rather than consensus. Still, it adds a fourth engineered channel to the ones this show has logged: continuation vehicles, consent-driven take-privates, and dividend recaps. It's also the one investors trust least, because it looks most like leverage dressed up as a realized return.

And the arm's-length exit route isn't picking up the slack. The Financial Times reported on October 21st that CVC is exploring a take-private of Verisure, the recently listed European home-security group. The process is early, there is no guarantee of a deal, and both companies declined to comment. No price is known, and exploring is not bidding. On its own, this could simply mean a sponsor sees value the market is missing. But it lands a day after Blackstone-backed AirTrunk reportedly pushed its listing into 2027. Two consecutive days of the public exit route deferring or running backwards is worth flagging, even before either story is confirmed.

Pull the threads together and the private equity cycle is running at two speeds. Deployment is competitive, fully financed, and willing to pay up for quality, as Qiagen just showed. Distribution is still mostly engineered. It runs through continuation vehicles, NAV loans, and dividend recaps rather than third-party sales or durable public listings. The biggest unresolved risk is that pile of nearly five trillion dollars in aging net asset value. The question is whether it can clear through a secondaries market where the seller increasingly sets up the buyer too.

The next test arrives this morning. Blackstone reports third-quarter results before the open, after this briefing's research closed, so there are no numbers here. The question is unchanged. Did realizations come from genuine third-party and public-market sales, or from engineered channels that never test the mark? Ares, Apollo and KKR follow over the next few weeks. And on Qiagen, watch the acceptance level as the offer runs toward its mid-2027 close.
