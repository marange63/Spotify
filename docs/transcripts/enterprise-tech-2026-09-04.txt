Good morning. It's Friday, September 4th, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

On August 30th, this show named Snowflake as the next real test in the current earnings cluster. The question was whether anyone would finally attach a hard AI number to a growth line that was actually moving, instead of a flat one. Snowflake reported after the close on September 2nd. The answer is more interesting than the headline beat.

Start with the number that carries the argument. Snowflake's product revenue was one point four nine billion dollars in the second fiscal quarter, up thirty-seven percent year over year. Total revenue grew thirty-five percent, so the product line is running ahead of the company. And Snowflake's own release goes further than that comparison. It states that this is the third consecutive quarter of product revenue growth acceleration, driven, in the company's words, by strength in the core data platform and a meaningful step-up in AI revenue. That is a verbatim company claim about the direction of its own growth rate, not our inference from two data points. Every other vendor in this cluster has offered an AI number attached to a growth rate that was standing still.

Sitting next to that growth line is CoCo, Snowflake's AI coding agent. CoCo surpassed nine thousand one hundred accounts, adding more than two thousand of them in this quarter alone. A related agent, CoWork, reached five thousand eight hundred accounts. So the adoption story and the revenue story are pointing the same direction at the same time, which has not happened yet in this earnings cycle.

Now the skepticism, because it belongs here rather than as an afterthought. That account count is an activation metric. It carries no attached revenue, no usage depth, and no attribution. It is exactly the unit this show has already discounted twice: Workday's fifty-five hundred agent customers on August 30th, and Salesforce's six thousand paying Agentforce customers on September 1st. A company that switched on one agent in one department counts identically to one running agents across the business. Snowflake publishes no CoCo revenue line at all. So the link between CoCo's account growth and the thirty-seven percent product revenue number is an association inside one press release. It is not demonstrated cause and effect, and the inference is ours, not a disclosure.

What makes Snowflake different from its peers is not the count. It is the line the count sits next to. On August 30th, Workday reported that more than a quarter of its new contract value carried an AI label, while its subscription growth stayed pinned near thirteen to fourteen percent. Same shape of disclosure, opposite result. When the growth rate does not bend, an AI attach number is at best a labeling convention. When the growth rate does bend, the attach number at least becomes worth arguing about. That is the whole distinction, and Snowflake is the first name in this cluster on the right side of it.

But the same release carries its own counterweight. Total remaining performance obligations, the multi-year backlog, grew thirty percent year over year to nine billion dollars. That is slower than the thirty-seven percent product revenue growth sitting beside it. Net revenue retention was one hundred twenty-six percent. So current in-quarter consumption is running hotter than the long-duration backlog is being replenished. Read that as the price of consumption-flexible contracting rather than a contradiction of the growth story. Enterprises are committing to shorter, usage-based AI workloads instead of locking in multi-year deals. The vendor trades backlog visibility for near-term usage, and the buyer keeps its optionality.

Worth naming what Snowflake does not publish here, because a deep dive on this print went looking for it and came back empty. Snowflake discloses only total remaining performance obligations. It gives no separate figure for the slice expected to convert within twelve months, which is the number that would tell you whether near-term bookings are carrying the quarter. So an outsider cannot check the duration story either way. The test for next quarter is simple: does that gap between backlog growth and revenue growth widen or close.

One more thing from the same release cuts in Snowflake's favor. Non-GAAP operating margin was fifteen point three percent for the quarter, and management raised the full-year operating margin guide to fourteen and a half percent, up from thirteen and a half. The full-year figure sits below the quarter because it blends in softer periods, so those two numbers are consistent rather than contradictory. The point for the listener is that Snowflake is not buying this growth with margin, on its own guidance. Full-year product revenue guidance was raised to six point zero seven billion dollars, which is thirty-six percent growth. On our arithmetic, that implies growth roughly holding rather than the acceleration continuing.

Which raises the obvious question. If buyers are increasingly metered on usage rather than locked into seats, somebody is carrying the underlying compute bill. That shows up a day earlier, in Palo Alto Networks' fiscal fourth quarter, reported September 1st.

Palo Alto's headline numbers were strong on their own terms. Next-generation security annual recurring revenue, the company's platformized product line, grew sixty-three percent year over year to nine point one billion dollars. Remaining performance obligations grew thirty-four percent to twenty-one point two billion. Quarterly revenue grew thirty-four percent to three point four one billion dollars. On any normal reading, that is a platformization strategy working.

Two other figures are the actual story. Gross margin came in at seventy-four point eight percent, down one hundred basis points from a year earlier. That figure comes from secondary reporting of management's commentary rather than the filed exhibit, so take it as reported. Management pointed at cloud hosting costs, which it expects to grow faster than revenue in fiscal 2027, and at memory and storage costs staying elevated. Then the guidance. Palo Alto guided next-generation security ARR growth to twenty-two to twenty-three percent for fiscal 2027, down from this year's sixty-three. Growth guided to fall by roughly two-thirds while the vendor's own unit costs rise is the story here, not the quarterly beat.

That is worth sitting with, because it is a specific, quantified sign that AI infrastructure cost inflation has crossed a boundary. The memory and hosting story has been running through the semiconductor beat all week as a hardware and capex problem. It now appears inside a software vendor's own cost of goods sold, not just in its customers' capital budgets.

Resist reading the ARR deceleration as pure conservatism, too. Part of that sixty-three percent reflects platformization through acquisition rather than pure cross-sell into the installed base, and Palo Alto's release names another agentic-workflow acquisition on top of it. Margin compression makes funding further deals more expensive at exactly the moment growth is guided down. So watch whether security consolidation slows from here, or shifts toward stock-funded deals.

One contrast is worth drawing carefully, because this is not yet a sector-wide law. CrowdStrike, covered here on August 30th, reported annual recurring revenue of five point eight four billion dollars, up twenty-five percent, off the same argument that AI adoption creates attack surface and therefore security demand. It disclosed no comparable hosting or memory cost pressure. But be precise about what that means. As this show noted on August 29th, CrowdStrike publishes no cost-to-serve metric at all. So the honest read is that the difference between these two companies is currently a difference in disclosure, not a proven difference in economics.

Step back and connect the two prints, because they are one story told from opposite ends. The industry-wide flight from seat-based pricing hands the compute bill to whoever is doing the metering. Snowflake sits on one end, charging customers by usage while absorbing the underlying infrastructure cost itself. Palo Alto sits on the other, watching hosting and memory costs outgrow revenue inside its own margin line. The further the industry moves from the seat, the more of the AI cost curve lands on the software vendor's income statement rather than the buyer's budget. For enterprise buyers, that argues for negotiating multi-year price protection into consumption contracts now, before vendors carrying this cost start passing it through wholesale.

One line of context before we close. Salesforce's Agentforce run-rate, above one and a half billion dollars and up more than two hundred forty percent, remains the largest single AI-attach number in this cluster. It has aired repeatedly this week and nothing in it changed.

Here is the disclosure arc worth naming directly. Snowflake put a product-level adoption number on the table. Palo Alto put a cost number on the table. Neither published the attribution that would let an outsider connect either one to revenue. What has shifted for high-multiple enterprise software is not the growth rate anymore. It is whether that growth can survive its own gross margin.

That's the briefing. Have a good weekend.
