Good morning. It's Monday, November 16th, and this is your digital money briefing.

Yesterday this desk said the bull case was missing two confirmations. One was an end to the bitcoin ETF outflow streak. The other was long-side funding falling back inside its normal range. After the weekend, the second one has arguably arrived. The evidence is messier than a single headline suggests, though, and the macro pressure behind the selloff is still building.

Start with the flush itself, briefly, since you heard the headline yesterday. Bitcoin printed a low of $61,946 on Coinbase in the early hours of Sunday, UTC. Price then bounced about three thousand dollars off that low.

Roughly two billion dollars of liquidations hit around the move, mostly long positions. That's the same event yesterday's tally sized near $1.9 billion, so don't add the two together. It's one flush.

Now the new information: what happened to the price of leverage afterward. It's genuinely contested. CoinGlass data, relayed by ChainCatcher, puts bitcoin funding at a small positive 0.0020 percent. A second report, from crypto.news, says funding turned negative for the first time since March. Both describe a rate close to zero. Neither describes longs paying up with confidence. So treat "funding has reset" as directionally true, but don't lean on the sign. The desks tracking it in real time don't agree on one.

The sign does matter for what comes next. If funding is negative, shorts are now paying to hold their positions. Yesterday's briefing flagged that as a later-stage setup: new shorts piling in at the lows, vulnerable to a squeeze. If the negative read is right, that setup is now in view. If the small positive read is right, the book is simply balanced. Either way, the premium longs were paying all last week has been flushed out.

But funding measures what leverage costs, not how much of it there is. A book can carry heavy open interest and still show flat funding, if it's balanced or hedged through basis trades. So the real question is whether the levered book actually shrank. There's a partial answer. A CryptoQuant contributor, cited by crypto.news, has open interest in coin-margined bitcoin-dollar contracts falling from one point five five billion dollars to one point one billion during the slide. By our arithmetic, that's close to a thirty percent contraction in that segment.

That slice matters more than its size suggests. Coin-margined contracts are collateralized in bitcoin itself, so their margin erodes as price falls. That makes them the most reflexive part of the book, and the first place you'd expect forced selling to bite. So the contraction confirms the flush did its mechanical work. It says nothing about the stablecoin-margined book. And the coin-denominated aggregate series this desk cited at a two-year high last Wednesday has no post-flush reading in anything we have. The takeaway: one piece of the levered book got smaller, not just cheaper, but the aggregate size is still unmeasured.

If longs have stopped paying up, the next question is who holds the rest of the supply. Here the picture is steadier. CoinGlass data as of November 11th showed long-term holder supply flat at 16.24 million bitcoin. That reading predates the weekend low. So it can't tell you what long-term holders did into sixty-two thousand. But going into the selloff, conviction holders weren't selling. That's the other half of a classic shakeout signature. Weak hands and levered hands take the loss, while long-term holders sit still.

With positioning at least partly reset, turn to the pressure that started this. It hasn't eased, and it's macro, not crypto-native. In a November 13th note, Coinbase Institutional had futures pricing 64 basis points of Fed hikes over the following year. At the start of November, that figure was 37. By our arithmetic, that's a near doubling in about two weeks.

Two cautions keep that honest. Priced hikes are not delivered hikes. And a market that repriced that far, that fast, can reverse just as quickly. Still, the mechanism is straightforward, and this is our inference rather than a quoted claim. A non-yielding asset, and a non-yielding wrapper around it, both get costlier to hold when cash is priced to pay more for longer.

That links the rate tape to the ETF tape. TFTC's November tracker shows $2.3 billion of net outflows from US spot bitcoin ETFs so far this month. The only inflow day was November 4th, and it was a small one. The opportunity-cost story fits that pattern, though nothing we have shows it's the sole cause. Today's session is the first chance for the seven-day streak to break.

Layer onto that the tech-tape shock. Nvidia announced last week that Jensen Huang had resigned as president, CEO, and board member, effective immediately. CFO Colette Kress is serving as interim CEO. Nvidia reports earnings Tuesday, the first print under Kress. No results exist yet, so treat it purely as a forward catalyst. How much each driver contributed, whether rates, ETF flows, or the Huang selloff, is interpretation, and we won't rank them.

Here's why that print matters for crypto. Last Wednesday this desk cited bitcoin's correlation with the Nasdaq at a top-percentile reading. We don't have a fresh measurement for the Huang session or the week since. So treat that link as last week's reading, not this week's proof. But if it holds, a weak Nvidia print lands on a crypto book that has reset its funding and may not have cut its size. The takeaway: the marginal price-setter has rotated. It's less about who's levered, and more about the Fed path and the AI-equity tape.

That rotation carries a cost for allocators. Part of the case for a strategic bitcoin sleeve was that it diversifies an equity book. A positioning reset that leaves price hostage to rate futures and a chipmaker's earnings weakens that pitch further. The floor then depends on the Fed, not on crypto-native demand.

The same rate repricing reaches the issuers behind the on-chain dollar. Circle's third-quarter reserve income, the interest it earns on the assets backing USDC, fell 8 percent to $658 million. That's the cleaner number to watch, since the headline revenue beat leaned on a one-time token sale. This desk has framed Circle's core business as squeezed from two sides, by compressing yields and by a shrinking float. That framing assumed rates were heading down. The futures market is now pricing more hikes, not fewer. On its own, that should eventually lift reserve yield per dollar held.

But here's the offset, and it's our inference, not disclosed fact. Higher-for-longer pricing also raises what it costs holders to sit in a zero-yield on-chain dollar instead of a T-bill. Last week this desk linked exactly that pressure to the shrinking stablecoin float. So the rate leg of the squeeze flips, while the float leg may worsen for the same reason. Every dollar that leaves the float is also a dollar of reserve assets the issuers no longer hold, which is the read-through to short-bill demand. For CRCL holders, the hike repricing is not the clean tailwind it looks like. The rate hedge in issuer economics may partly cancel itself.

Now the strongest bull case, built only from what's established. Funding has compressed toward zero by either measure. Coin-margined open interest shrank materially. Long-term holders were steady heading into the low. And the market bounced off it. That's a real shakeout signature. What it still lacks is a broken ETF outflow streak, and any sign that rate pricing is easing.

The competing read deserves equal airtime. Near-zero funding right after a flush is mechanical. The longs who were paying that premium are the ones who just got liquidated. On its own, it says nothing about fresh demand. If dip-buyers re-lever this week and funding rebuilds, the reset was a pause, not a clearing.

Three things to watch. First, today's ETF session, and whether the outflow streak reaches eight days or breaks. Second, whether funding holds near zero through the week, or climbs back as longs re-lever. Third, Tuesday's Nvidia print, and what rate pricing does around it.

The through-line: positioning has reset, but the macro pressure that forced the reset has not. So bitcoin's next move depends less on its own levered book, and more on the Fed and the AI-equity tape.

That's today's digital money briefing. Have a good Monday.
