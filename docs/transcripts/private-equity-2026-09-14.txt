Good morning, it's Monday, September 14th, and this is your private equity briefing.

Start with the close, not the announcement. EQT completed its acquisition of Coller Capital on August 31st. The deal was signed back in January, so the news is the closing itself, not new terms. Here are the terms, stated once and precisely. The base consideration is three point two billion dollars, paid in about eighty million EQT shares, roughly seven percent of EQT's own share count. On top of that sits up to five hundred million dollars of contingent earnout running through March of 2029. That is why some coverage of this deal carries a three point two billion dollar headline and some carries three point seven. Those are not competing numbers. One is the base, the other is the base plus the maximum earnout, and the earnout has to be earned.

Notice what EQT chose to pay with. This is stock, not cash. That seven percent is a permanent transfer from existing EQT holders to Coller's owners, settled on day one, and it does not come back if the acquired business underperforms. The earnout is the only part of the price that stays contingent on delivery, and it is the smaller part. So the shareholder bought a fee stream at a fixed share count cost, and took the performance risk in exchange for not putting cash on the balance sheet in a market where cash is the scarce thing. That trade tells you something about how EQT is thinking, and it is worth pausing on before we get to what the money bought.

What EQT says it bought, measured at combined scale, is three hundred and forty-one billion euros of assets under management, of which one hundred and eighty-six billion euros is fee-generating. That second number is the one that matters. Fee-generating assets are what the management-fee annuity is charged on. Headline assets under management include a lot of capital that is either not yet called or no longer paying full freight.

We can now also measure the price against Coller on a standalone basis, which is the test the acquirer usually prefers you skip. EQT's January signing announcement disclosed Coller's own figures as of year-end 2025: nearly fifty billion dollars in total assets under management, with thirty-three billion of that fee-generating. Those figures were disclosed at signing and not restated at close, so treat them as eight months stale rather than current. The same January release set a target for the combined Coller EQT unit of one hundred and seventy-five to two hundred million dollars of fee-related EBITDA in 2026. Put the three point two billion dollar base price against that range and you are paying roughly sixteen to eighteen times the fee earnings being targeted. That is a real multiple to underwrite against rather than a leap of faith, and it is broadly where listed alternative managers trade on fee earnings. The catch is that the target is EQT's own forecast for a unit it now controls and reports.

There is a sharper piece of arithmetic sitting inside EQT's own disclosure. EQT said the transaction is expected to be mid-single-digit accretive to its fee-related earnings. It is also, on the same page, roughly seven percent dilutive to the share count. Put those two together and on the company's own numbers the deal is close to a wash on fee-related earnings per share in year one, possibly slightly negative. That does not make it a bad deal. It does mean the case for it is not 2026 earnings. The case is that secondaries fee income grows faster from here than buyout fee income does, and that EQT is buying a growth rate rather than a current number. Shareholders are being asked to underwrite that specific claim.

Now the justification EQT itself offers for entering this business at all. Its release cites a record first half in secondaries, more than one hundred and twenty billion dollars of volume, up roughly a fifth year over year. Handle that figure carefully. Two days ago this show withdrew a first-half secondaries number of that same magnitude, because it traced only to low-provenance content aggregators with no named data source behind it. That number now has an author. The author is EQT, in the press release announcing its own purchase of a secondaries firm. That is not the same failure mode as an anonymous statistic, and arguably it is a worse one. An interested party is sizing the market it just paid real equity to enter. Use it as an attributed claim about what EQT believes the market is worth, not as a verified market statistic.

Now the turn, and it is the real story here. This show has argued that a price set by rival bidders is a different kind of evidence from a price a general partner sets against its own mark. Coller Capital was one of a small number of large independent secondaries buyers with the capital and the mandate to underwrite a fresh price against a general partner's reported net asset value, rather than simply validate it. Coller now belongs to a general partner, EQT, that sponsors continuation vehicles itself.

Be disciplined about what that does and does not mean. EQT's releases, at signing in January and again at closing on August 31st, describe Coller's origination, underwriting and investment decision-making as remaining independent. But neither release contains any commitment addressing the one conflict that actually matters here, which is whether Coller will bid on, or price, continuation vehicles that EQT itself sponsors. The independence language on the page is generic operational autonomy, how the unit runs day to day. It is not a firewall. Nothing disclosed says Coller stops bidding independently. What changed is that its owner now has a direct interest in where those bids land. The plain version is this. The buyer of last resort, the counterparty capable of pricing against a sponsor's own marks rather than confirming them, just got absorbed by the sell side.

Two second-order effects follow. First, expect more general-partner-on-general-partner consolidation aimed at the liquidity layer rather than the fundraising layer, and every such deal removes another independent counterparty capable of checking marks. Second, it creates a live diligence question for any limited partner evaluating a continuation vehicle from here. Who owns the buyer sitting across the table, and does that owner sponsor continuation vehicles of its own. That question is new, and there is no standard disclosure that answers it.

So why is this rational for a listed manager at all. Look at Blackstone's most recent reported figures, for the six months ended June 30th. This is six-week-old background, not fresh news, but it is the cleanest read on the underlying logic. Blackstone's fee-related earnings for that half were one point three billion dollars, up nearly four hundred and eighteen million from the same period a year earlier. That is the annuity the market pays a premium for. Carried interest stays hostage to an exit environment that has not cleared. Fee-related earnings compound every quarter regardless. Buying a durable fee-generating base with equity pushes a manager further toward being a fee annuity and further away from being a carry business, which decouples its own share price from the exit problem straining the funds underneath it. That decoupling is the trade the listed managers have been making all year.

So where does that leave the cycle. The industry is now spending real primary equity to own the machinery that prices its own unsold inventory, rather than waiting for that inventory to sell. The unresolved risk is the one this show has tracked all week. Do marks reflect what assets are actually worth, or what general partners need them to be worth to avoid crystallizing a loss. Watch three things from here. EQT's first full reporting period that includes Coller, which will show whether that fee-related EBITDA target holds. The late-October third-quarter prints from the large managers, for any sign that realization pace is improving rather than just fee income growing. And whether any named independent data provider, rather than an acquirer's own press release, publishes verified first-half secondaries volume. Until one does, treat the size of this market as a claim made by the people buying their way into it.
