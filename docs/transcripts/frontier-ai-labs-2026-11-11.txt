Good morning. It's Wednesday, November 11th, and this is your frontier AI labs briefing.

One disclosure up front, because it runs through most of today's show. Anthropic makes the model that generates this briefing. Today's lead is Anthropic's own IPO roadshow, partly in Anthropic's own words. A later item involves a rival publicly praising an Anthropic model. I'll keep that material tight and numerical, and I won't editorialize in Anthropic's favor.

Here's the thesis. The frontier race is starting to get priced in public. Buyers are reportedly lining up for the one lab that has opened its books. Voters and a state regulator are setting the price of permission for everyone else. And the most opaque contender, xAI, is the one conceding ground.

Start with the lead. Anthropic launched its IPO roadshow yesterday. Its own release sets the terms: eight hundred twenty-five million Class A shares, at an expected seventy to seventy-eight dollars a share, listing on Nasdaq under the ticker A-N-T. CNBC reports that Anthropic plans to price on November 18th and begin trading November 19th. At the top of the range, on a fully diluted basis, CNBC says the company could be worth up to one point four zero five trillion dollars. That's the largest possible headline, not the market cap at listing. The anchor is the nine hundred sixty-five billion dollar valuation Anthropic set in its May funding round. By my arithmetic, the top of the range sits about 46 percent above that mark.

Now, the pattern break. On Monday and Tuesday, this desk told a story of labs pricing below their own opening narratives. Anthropic's reported range had drifted down from early investor talk near two trillion dollars. The filed range cuts against that. Its top end sits above the lower, Journal-sourced band we cited yesterday, though still well short of that early two-trillion talk. And CNBC reports the order book is already covered at the seventy-to-seventy-eight range. Its sources also say institutional demand is stronger than it was for SpaceX at the same stage of that company's process. That halts the drift-down story, but it doesn't vindicate the earlier high talk. And it comes about a week after the chip selloff this desk covered, which makes the demand more notable, not less.

A word of caution on that demand color. It comes from unnamed people familiar with the matter, relayed by CNBC. And "covered on an allocable basis" is standard banker phrasing, not a disclosed oversubscription multiple. There are three ways to read it. One is real conviction in Anthropic's enterprise and coding franchise. Two is a scarcity premium. Anthropic would be the only listed pure-play frontier lab, so investors who want this exposure have no substitute. That says more about access than about relative quality. Three is ordinary momentum-building ahead of pricing. The November 18th price against the range, and the November 19th first trade, will separate those stories. Roadshow chatter won't.

Now the size of the deal. Eight hundred twenty-five million shares at seventy to seventy-eight dollars works out to roughly fifty-eight to sixty-four billion dollars. That's my own arithmetic from the filed terms, before the underwriter option and before fees. Anthropic's release also says it won't receive any proceeds from shares sold by selling stockholders, so not all of that is new money for the company. Even so, it's a capital-access channel no private rival can open on this timeline.

Set that against OpenAI. Bloomberg reported on November 6th, via TechCrunch, that OpenAI's latest round values it at one point two five trillion dollars. That's the round this desk covered Monday, which came in well short of its original target. So OpenAI's latest reported mark sits below the top of Anthropic's range. Treat that as directional. OpenAI's figure is a private mark, for a round that hadn't closed. Anthropic's is a top-of-range, fully diluted ceiling the market hasn't confirmed.

Here's why next week matters beyond Anthropic. A strong November 18th pricing becomes the yardstick for OpenAI's private marks, and for its own eventual listing. It would turn capital access, the dimension this desk called binding on Monday, into an advantage for the disclosed lab. A weak break would be read as a sector-wide de-rating of frontier-lab multiples. And as we argued Monday, the chip and cloud suppliers' backlog depends on the labs' ability to keep raising, so a covered book makes that backlog look sturdier. The so-what: the first public mark on a frontier lab is now dated and, on early evidence, firm.

Public buyers are pricing the lab. Voters are pricing its buildings.

Last week, this desk noted voters restricting data centers in nearly every ballot contest. OpenAI has now put its response on paper. On November 5th, it published a national community compact and compute-campus policy blueprint. The trigger, per CNBC's count: voters backed restrictions or rejected pro-development measures on all but one of forty-one local data-center ballot measures, across ten states. What's genuinely new is three commitments. OpenAI will solicit community input. It will publish construction plans. And it will stop using non-disclosure agreements with public officials. What's not new is the power pledge. CNBC describes OpenAI's promise to pay the full cost of powering its data centers as a reaffirmation. The coverage we reviewed also doesn't tie the compact to any named site, or make clear whether it reaches sites OpenAI leases rather than owns. So the concessions so far are disclosure, not dollars.

One competing read is that this is a cheap, pre-emptive move. It gets ahead of grid-cost legislation and the next round of ballot fights, without changing OpenAI's cost structure. My inference: permission is becoming a cost line in this race. Whoever can absorb published plans, open dealings with officials, and full power costs gains on whoever can't. Right now, that cost is being paid in transparency, not cash.

And New York has started pricing the paperwork. Per TechCrunch, the only source we reviewed on this, the state opened its RAISE Act registration portal on Tuesday, November 10th. Large frontier developers, including Anthropic and OpenAI, must register by January 1st, 2027. Penalties for failing to register run up to one million dollars for a first violation, and three million for later ones. Against trillion-dollar valuations, those fines are immaterial. The real significance is that a dated, state-run registration regime now exists, and it lands squarely inside Anthropic's roadshow window.

Now the lab furthest from any of that scrutiny. Repeating the disclosure: Opus 5.5 is an Anthropic model, and Anthropic makes the model behind this show. Elon Musk posted that xAI has, in his words, dumped JAX and SGLang and rewritten the entire reinforcement-learning and inference stack from scratch in C and C++. That's for the upcoming Grok 4.8. He said pre-training is done, and that evals in reinforcement-learning training are, in his words, starting to climb. He also called Opus 5.5 a masterpiece, and said Grok 4.8 is not as good. Those quotes come from Musk's own posts, relayed by TeslaNorth. No xAI technical documentation or independent eval backs them.

A rival's CEO ranking a competitor's model above his own is rare, and there are two ways to read it. One is a genuine admission of a capability gap. The other is expectation management before a Grok reveal, which lowers the bar for 4.8 and sets up a leapfrog story. No third-party score exists for Grok 4.8, so the ranking is unverified either way.

Now connect it to two threads from this desk. On November 6th, we relayed Bloomberg's report that Anthropic is a major compute customer of SpaceX, which has owned xAI since February. On November 8th, we covered Musk saying Grok Bot's serving cost was unsustainably high relative to revenue. So the Musk camp is at once Anthropic's landlord through SpaceX, a public admirer of its model, and a rival rebuilding its serving stack under cost pressure. My read is that a full stack rewrite this late in a model's cycle looks less like a capability leap than a cost-to-serve and throughput fix. It also carries real schedule risk. The so-what: the concession can't be verified, but the rewrite says xAI is fighting on cost to serve.

Quickly, by competitive dimension. Capital access: Anthropic, pending the November 18th print. Social license: OpenAI moving first, mostly on disclosure. Regulation: New York's clock has started. Capability: rival-asserted this week, not independently verified. Chinese open weights: no verified release surfaced in this window. Google: no verified move surfaced in our research.

Three things to watch. First, where Anthropic prices against that seventy-to-seventy-eight range, and how the stock trades on November 19th. Second, whether OpenAI's compact ever attaches to a named site. Third, whether Grok 4.8 ships with any benchmark score nobody at xAI wrote.

That's today's briefing. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
