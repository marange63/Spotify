Good morning, this is Cautious Optimism Briefings for Sunday, November 22, 2026.

This week the money behind the orbital economy looked smaller and further away than the headlines suggested. The one thing that got more concentrated was the ride to orbit. Start with Washington.

Yesterday we framed the Senate's 9.4 billion dollars for Golden Dome as a big addition to a base of roughly 400 million dollars. By our arithmetic, against what the administration actually asked for, it is about half.

According to Washington Technology, the administration sought roughly 17.5 billion dollars for Golden Dome. Nearly all of it was tied to a reconciliation measure, not to the annual defense bill. That roughly 400 million dollar base was barely 2 percent of the ask.

On that roughly 17 billion dollar ask, AEI budget analyst Todd Harrison was blunt. In his words, "The chances of Golden Dome getting the full 17 billion dollars requested are now almost zero."

This is not a clean story of appropriators slashing a program, though. The administration routed almost its entire request through reconciliation. So the Senate draft can be read two ways. One reading is a haircut to the ask. The other is appropriators pulling program money into the annual bill, where they control it.

Either way, the gap to the full request is really a bet on a funding vehicle. It closes only if a second reconciliation measure materializes. If it doesn't, the appropriations bill is effectively the whole program budget for next year.

Keep the two fiscal years apart, too. Golden Dome's program office said this week that its fiscal 2026 money is fully obligated. That says nothing about how large fiscal 2027 turns out to be. And this is only the Senate committee's draft. We don't have a House figure yet, and the conference number could differ.

So government is still the most durable buyer in this sector. It is just a smaller buyer, for now, than the administration wanted. Our read is that new hardware awards will now be paced by legislative mechanics rather than by program demand. For the space segments of the defense primes, award timing is now the variable to watch.

Private capital sent the same message as Washington this week, for different reasons. According to a Yahoo Finance recap, Firefly led the space sector's losses, down 23 percent for the week through Thursday. AST SpaceMobile, Rocket Lab and Redwire all fell double digits.

SpaceX was the exception. The same recap has SpaceX, trading as SPCX since its June IPO, slipping just half a percent. Another outlet reported a steeper decline, so treat it as roughly flat.

So what drove the selloff? The reporting points to a tech-wide de-risking trade. It gathered pace on Monday, ahead of Nvidia's earnings and Anthropic's IPO. On Thursday alone, the Nasdaq Composite fell 2.3 percent.

AST SpaceMobile took a sector-specific hit on top of that. Its shares fell about 12 percent on Monday, per the same recap. The trigger was a launch failure that destroyed Amazon's satellites, not AST's. That failure was the week's biggest operational event.

Last Sunday, ULA's Vulcan rocket failed on the way to orbit. All 40 Amazon Leo broadband satellites aboard were lost. Amazon Leo is the constellation formerly called Kuiper. As we reported on Tuesday, the launch was the first total failure in ULA's history. It also came on what was effectively Vulcan's requalification flight.

The cause is narrowing but not settled. ULA's preliminary finding, which we covered on Friday, points to inadequate fuel supply to the engines, a problem on the vehicle side. We have seen no named component and no return-to-flight date.

As we reported Wednesday, the largest block of Amazon's launch bookings sits on Vulcan. So much of its manifest now waits behind a fix nobody has scoped. Amazon was already behind its FCC deployment milestone before Sunday. Vulcan didn't create that gap. It makes the gap harder to close.

For ULA and its parents, Boeing and Lockheed Martin, a failed requalification flight costs credibility with constellation customers, by our read. And the Space Force had already shifted national-security missions off Vulcan to SpaceX after an earlier booster incident.

Now put that next to Blue Origin. Its New Glenn heavy lifter has been grounded since a hot-fire test accident in May damaged its launch pad. With Vulcan and New Glenn both on the ground, SpaceX is, for now, the only working American heavy-lift provider. Rocket Lab's Neutron hasn't flown yet. So the Pentagon and Amazon are left leaning on Falcon, and Falcon belongs to Amazon's direct broadband rival. The plain takeaway is that SpaceX's lead widened this week on the launch pad, not just in the stock market.

Back to the equity selloff. At Rocket Lab, at least, it is not explained by weak operating results. As reported by Satellite Today, Rocket Lab posted record quarterly revenue of 268 million dollars. That was up 73 percent on the year.

Rocket Lab also built a record backlog of 117 missions. Booked missions are backlog, not recognized revenue. None of it stopped the stock from falling double digits.

AST SpaceMobile is the harder case. Its third-quarter revenue came in at 42.3 million dollars. TipRanks had tracked about 45.7 million as the expected figure, so this was a genuine miss. AST is a name with real growth but no commercial service yet. It is still far from funding itself.

That leaves three competing explanations, and the evidence doesn't settle them. The first is macro. Space pure-plays traded as a high-beta growth basket in a week of broad tech de-risking. The second is company-specific, meaning AST's miss plus fresh launch risk after Vulcan. The third concerns SpaceX itself. Its resilience may reflect a thin post-IPO float and index-style holders, not a market verdict on its fundamentals.

That third reading matters because of something we flagged on Friday. SpaceX's credit market has been pricing more risk since the IPO, even as its equity barely moved. So SpaceX's equity and its credit are telling different stories about the same company.

Our read is that the market is starting to price who can finance the next two years, not who booked the most missions. If pure-play equity keeps trading this way, the cost of capital rises for the cash-hungry names just as they need to raise. That widens SpaceX's scale advantage, which the groundings have already made operational.

The one AI-shaped demand signal of the week shows how far out that payoff sits. Google and Blue Origin announced a launch services agreement. It books New Glenn to fly Project Suncatcher. That is Google's research effort testing satellites that carry its AI chips in orbit.

That is a real contract with a named vehicle, not a rendering. But the mission is years away, and it is one research flight, not a space data-center business. There's no constellation plan behind it yet.

It also rides on New Glenn, still grounded after that pad accident. Blue Origin CEO Dave Limp has said, in his words, "We just said we're going to get back to flight this year." Treat that as the company's stated aim, not a date on a manifest. For Blue Origin, an AI-adjacent anchor customer is welcome. It also raises the bar for New Glenn to prove cadence first.

Now set that beside what we reported on Friday. SpaceX is financing a large purchase of Nvidia AI chips through a leasing vehicle. The Financial Times, as relayed by TipRanks, reported that the lenders sought a clause barring those chips from going to space. Their concern was recovering the asset in a default. So orbital compute drew a real launch contract and real lender skepticism in the same week. For the AI and data-center story, Suncatcher is an option on orbital compute. It is not yet relief for terrestrial power constraints.

Pulling the threads together. What's genuinely inflecting is SpaceX's grip on American heavy lift, which is now a near-monopoly by default. Government demand for missile defense is real too, and its fiscal 2026 money is on contract. But its near-term scale is visibly smaller than requested.

What's still unresolved? Golden Dome's fiscal 2027 size is one question. The financing picture for pure-play launch and connectivity names is another. What's still a demo is orbital compute, which remains a research line, not a business.

Watch four catalysts. First, the House's Golden Dome number, and whether a second reconciliation vehicle actually appears. Second, ULA naming a failed component and a return-to-flight plan for Vulcan. Third, whether New Glenn returns to flight on anything like Blue Origin's stated timeline. And fourth, the next batch of Amazon Leo satellites, slated for a European Ariane 6, as we flagged on Friday.

That's the briefing. Stay cautiously optimistic, and we'll talk next time.
