Good morning. It's Monday, September 7th, and this is your private credit briefing.

Start with a number. New money into non-traded BDCs fell 82 percent year over year. We've spent the past week watching investors try to get out of these vehicles. Today's number is about the investors who never showed up in the first place.

That forces a correction on air. On Friday, this briefing argued that retailization's binding constraint was the liquidity promise, not fundraising appetite. The flow data says appetite turned too. But be precise about what this 82 percent figure is and isn't. It comes from the reporting around Blackstone's redemption news, not from a fundraising database we've seen. It covers non-traded BDCs specifically, not the asset class. And the reporting doesn't specify the base period, so treat the magnitude as directional, not exact.

One more distinction, because it does most of the work today. This is a flow statistic, not a stock statistic. Falling inflows into one wrapper is not falling assets under management. It is not shrinking dry powder. And it is not evidence of credit deterioration. It's evidence about one wrapper, and as we'll get to, the other wrappers are not doing the same thing.

Fold in where that leaves Blackstone's flagship fund, BCRED, without re-litigating ground we covered on Friday. Blackstone again capped redemptions at 5 percent of shares against 10 percent requested, the identical outcome to the prior quarter. What's new is the shareholder letter's own arithmetic. Investors who asked to redeem over the past two quarters got back only about 75 percent of their money within roughly 90 days.

That's Blackstone's own disclosure, and it's the number to sit with. It converts gating from a binary event — you're in or you're out — into a measured time cost on capital. Getting three-quarters of your money back in three months isn't a failure of the structure. It's the structure's price, quantified for the first time. So the allocator's question isn't whether the gate is scary. It's whether the illiquidity premium on offer compensates for that delay. Blackstone claims a 9 percent annualized since-inception return, by its own account roughly three points above leveraged loans — self-reported, with no net-of-fee or net-of-loss breakdown disclosed. Two quarters is a pattern, not a trend. But the channel is narrowing at both ends: new capital slowing to a trickle, and capital trying to leave paying a real, now-measured toll.

The antecedent here is Moody's. Back on April 7th, five months ago, the rating agency cut its outlook on the roughly $400 billion BDC sector from stable to negative. It cited rising redemption requests at non-traded BDCs, with one rated BDC seeing requests above 20 percent of shares outstanding, alongside elevated leverage at the publicly traded names and weakening access to unsecured debt and equity markets. It flagged the sector's first-ever net outflow. Five months on, the flow data puts a magnitude on that warning. And there's a loop inside it. Weak inflows tighten the same funding channel Moody's was worried about, which raises the cost of capital, which makes the vehicle less attractive to the next dollar. Worth noting what Moody's also said: asset quality had so far remained largely benign. This is a liability-side story about funding, not yet a story about loan performance.

Now, if that's the money going out, look at what it was supposed to fund — and here's where the picture gets more complicated than a simple retreat.

Private credit has positioned itself as the marginal financier of the AI buildout. Morgan Stanley's research projects private credit will supply roughly $800 billion of a $1.5 trillion global data-center financing gap through 2028. Treat that as directional. It reaches us through secondary reporting aggregating Morgan Stanley's work, not a sentence we could verify in the bank's own publication, and it is a projection rather than committed money. The template deal is Blue Owl's joint venture with Meta, named Beignet: an 80 percent stake sold to Blue Owl for about $2.5 billion, with the venture raising $27 billion of external debt in October of last year. That one is hard and dated. And in August, Nvidia announced partnerships with six global investment firms aiming to mobilize more than $500 billion for dedicated AI-compute financing platforms — an announced target, not committed or deployed capital.

Here's the analytical turn. This is a different risk than the cash-flow lending direct lenders built their track record on. Sponsor-backed middle-market loans are underwritten against diversified EBITDA. Data-center debt is long-duration, hard-asset-backed, and concentrated in a handful of hyperscaler counterparties. The same managers are writing both books — and as we noted on Saturday, those same managers also hold the loans of the legacy software companies the buildout is disrupting. That isn't diversification. It's one technology thesis expressed twice in the same fund. Whether data-center lending is priced to the same return targets as traditional direct lending is an open question. The evidence doesn't tell us either way, so we won't assert an answer.

Which brings us to the funding-source question, and this is where a deeper look complicates the tidy story. If the retail wealth channel is genuinely closing while the AI lending pipeline runs into the hundreds of billions, common sense says the replacement has to be capital with no redemption right — institutional drawdown funds and insurance balance sheets. That would matter. Retail money that can request redemption exerts discipline through the tender queue. Capital that can't leave removes that discipline and lengthens the duration of the liability sitting behind the loan.

But look at the institutional data, and the 82 percent retail collapse doesn't look like a broad retreat. Closed-end direct lending is the institutional analogue to the non-traded BDC. It raised $16.25 billion in the second quarter of this year. That's up sharply from just $1.3 billion in the first quarter, and reporting citing Preqin data calls it the strongest quarter for the category in two years.

Handle that comparison carefully. It's quarter over quarter within 2026, not year over year the way the 82 percent figure is, so the two statistics sit on different bases and shouldn't be netted against each other. But the direction is unambiguous. Institutional appetite for direct lending just had its best quarter in two years at almost exactly the moment retail appetite collapsed. So the honest read isn't that private credit's funding is drying up. It's that the retail wrapper and the institutional channel are moving in opposite directions. On the insurance leg of that argument, we don't have the number. There's no dated 2026 figure we can quote for annuity balance-sheet capital flowing into this lending, so the insurance-substitution story stays an open question rather than a claim.

One more data point on why a buyer might walk away from the retail vehicle in the first place. Fitch's monitored portfolio of 302 privately rated borrowers recorded a record 9.2 percent default rate for calendar 2025, up from 8.1 percent in 2024. That's 38 defaults among 28 borrowers, concentrated in floating-rate structures carrying minimal interest-rate hedges and in the smallest issuers, those under $25 million of EBITDA. Two warnings on that number. It's a March report covering last year, so it isn't new this week. And it's a different measurement basis than any BDC's non-accrual percentage, so don't blend the two. What it gives you is the demand-side backdrop: the lower middle market, increasingly funded by newer and less-tested vehicles, is where the stress is concentrating.

So where does the cycle actually stand? The payment layer is deteriorating slowly, and from a level that, per Moody's, still counts as largely benign. The layer under real stress this week is funding — but it's stress with a rotation inside it, not a uniform freeze. Retail money is retreating from the semi-liquid wrapper faster than expected while institutional direct-lending capital just posted its best quarter in two years. The biggest unresolved question is whether that institutional rebound is durable enough, and large enough, to fund a buildout measured in the hundreds of billions with no redemption right attached. If it isn't, the AI financing gap gets filled by someone else, on different terms. Watch three things from here: the fourth-quarter redemption prints across the non-traded BDC complex, the November BDC filings for non-accrual and payment-in-kind trends, and the next round of alternative-manager earnings calls, where institutional fund-close disclosure is now the number to read first.

That's the briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
