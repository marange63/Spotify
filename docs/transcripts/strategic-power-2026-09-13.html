Cautious Optimism Briefings

Strategic Power, Energy, and Institutional Change

Transcript · September 13, 2026

Good morning. It's Sunday, September 13th, 2026, and here's where power is actually shifting.

For two sessions this beat has described China's US-facing rare-earth lever as an option Beijing was holding rather than using. November 10th was the date to watch — when a broader suspension of export controls is set to expire. That framing just broke. Something has been happening since early August that doesn't need November 10th at all. And it never touched a rule.

Here are the facts. Reuters reports that a handful of Chinese rare-earth suppliers have refused to ship material to US companies since early August. The refusals began after Beijing sanctioned the Responsible Business Alliance. That's the US-based supply-chain monitor whose due-diligence framework many Western buyers use to vet Chinese suppliers. No license was denied. No new regulation was issued. No notice went out. A supplier simply calculated that cooperating with a now-sanctioned American auditor was a legal risk not worth taking, and stopped shipping. US officials have flagged the resulting friction as a live item on the planning agenda ahead of Xi Jinping's state visit to Washington. Trump has said that visit begins September 24th.

Now, why this form of leverage is different. China's licensing architecture is a two-layer system. A rule from April 2025 requires individual export licenses for seven medium and heavy rare-earth elements — samarium, gadolinium, terbium, dysprosium, lutetium, scandium and yttrium — along with their metals, oxides, alloys and magnet materials. That licensing requirement is still fully in force. A separate, broader expansion of controls announced last October was suspended under the post-summit truce, and that suspension runs to November 10th. The August stoppage sits outside both. Nobody invoked the active licensing rule. Nobody touched the suspended one. It is a third channel entirely, running on corporate fear rather than on law.

That matters because deniable leverage has no counter-instrument. Export-control law, sanctions, WTO complaints — all of them target state measures. Each one reaches a license denial or a published rule, something a government can point at and negotiate against. None of them reaches a private supplier's decision to protect itself. And the deniability cuts both ways. An effect with no author is also an effect Beijing cannot credibly promise to reverse at the summit. There is nothing for Xi to instruct anyone to stop doing, because officially nobody started.

On scale, be honest about what isn't known. Reuters, the fullest wire account of this, states outright that it was unable to determine the total number of Chinese suppliers involved. There is no customs or USGS figure quantifying the volume, or the share of US-bound trade this touches. A handful of suppliers is not a rounding of some known number. It is genuinely unquantified, and that absence of scale is part of the story rather than a caveat to hedge around. Worth noting too: China's Ministry of Commerce didn't respond to requests for comment. The only official word came from the Foreign Ministry, a boilerplate line about being committed to global critical-mineral supply chains.

There is a competing reading here, and it is a serious one. Beijing has framed its sanctioning of the Responsible Business Alliance as retaliation for a string of FCC actions since December against Chinese electronics testing labs, drones, routers and other equipment. On that account, this is tit-for-tat over an unrelated tech-enforcement dispute, not a minerals policy at all. That cuts against reading the stoppage as a coordinated pre-summit rare-earth play. The effect on the ground is identical either way, which is precisely what makes it hard to price.

So what does it mean for pricing? On Thursday this beat asked whether magnet-exposed defense primes and EV makers were showing any option premium into September 24th and November 10th. If they still aren't, the market is assuming a clean renewal of the suspension. It is now also ignoring a channel that bypasses that suspension entirely. Same mispricing question, with a second and uncounted channel added to it.

There is a slower consequence too, and it runs against Beijing. Direction only here — nothing in the evidence supports attaching a number to it. Every month of unexplained friction strengthens the case for non-China refining and magnet capacity. A published control is something a buyer can model, appeal and wait out. An unexplained refusal is something a buyer engineers its way around permanently. That is the strategic cost Beijing pays for using an instrument it cannot be seen using.

Now the mirror image, running the other direction. Washington's own control regime has the identical dependency. On Wednesday this beat priced one version of the leak: a US server maker one-third owned by a blacklisted Chinese firm, sitting comfortably under the fifty percent ownership threshold that would have caught it. The other half of that gap is re-export, and Taiwan is the one jurisdiction that could close it. Bloomberg reported in June that Taiwan authorities were weighing much stricter legal tools against the diversion of advanced Nvidia-based servers to China, because unauthorized re-export isn't currently treated as a crime there. That was a policy under consideration, and three months on there is no confirmed movement. The inaction is itself the finding. The US regime, like China's rare-earth lever, now turns on what private firms voluntarily choose to do, because the statute was never finished on either side.

The concession Washington actually has to offer at the summit is the chip file. Since January 15th, Commerce's Bureau of Industry and Security has treated exports of Nvidia's H200 and AMD's MI325X chips to China as case-by-case review rather than presumptive denial. That comes with a 25 percent tariff, a 50 percent volume cap and third-party testing requirements. It is a real, quantified lever. It may also be a cheap one to offer. Nvidia's own stated posture, as reported, is that it is not counting on China data-center revenue, given Beijing's restrictions on state-funded data centers using foreign chips. A concession that costs Washington little to make is also one Beijing can decline cheaply.

Put that together. Eleven days out from the Xi-Trump meeting, both sides are bringing behaviors to the table, not rules. That is exactly why nothing agreed on September 24th will be verifiable in the way a tariff schedule or a license count would be.

The same substitution — process standing in for a deliverable — shows up in Ukraine this week. US envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner met Vladimir Putin in Moscow on September 5th, then flew to Kyiv to meet Volodymyr Zelensky. Both sides called those talks substantive, though that is the participants' own word, not an independent finding. Kushner proposed reviving the trilateral US-Russia-Ukraine negotiating format used roughly six months earlier. Zelensky agreed in principle. He said Ukraine would pursue trilateral talks, or trilateral talks plus Europe, but added, "we'll see what Russia agrees to." No ceasefire emerged. No territorial or security-guarantee framework was announced. A White House official said only that the two sides discussed substantive plans for next steps, to be announced in the coming weeks. A format proposal is the cheapest possible output of a senior diplomatic visit. Reviving a framework that was tried and dropped six months ago reads at least as much like regression as momentum. So there is no catalyst here to unwind the war-risk premium sitting under European defense spending and gas prices. That premium holds until there is an actual framework rather than a format.

Zoom out. For the past week this beat has graded state and corporate announcements against what actually gets delivered — queues, auctions, task orders, backlogs. Today inverts the test. The most consequential action of the week was never announced at all, on either side of the Pacific, which is precisely why it cannot be verified, priced or countered by anyone. Watch two dates. September 24th, when Xi arrives in Washington. And November 10th, when China's broader suspension is due to lapse. Knowing now that neither date controls the leverage that matters most.

That's the briefing. Have a good day.
