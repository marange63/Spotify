Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Credit & the Corporate Credit Cycle

Transcript · September 15, 2026

Good morning, it's September 15th. This is your private credit briefing.

Start with the number that breaks the argument this briefing has run all week. Fitch's private credit default index tracks roughly 1,300 borrowers. It put the trailing-twelve-month default rate at 6.3 percent at the end of August, up from 6.1 percent in July. One sourcing note before we build anything on that. Fitch's own report is not public, so these figures reach us through press coverage of it rather than off Fitch's release. Every reading of the index this year has set a new all-time high, from 5.7 percent at the end of the first quarter to 6.3 percent now. August itself logged 14 default events, the highest monthly count since the series began in 2024. Eleven of those were new borrowers and three were repeat defaulters.

On Saturday this briefing pointed at a very different default gauge — the one tracking losses inside broadly syndicated CLO collateral — sitting near a four-year low. We used it to argue that defaults right now are elective, deferred by an open refinancing window rather than a genuine turn in the cycle. The Fitch print is the number that disagrees. Two measures of the same lending cycle, pointing opposite ways, in the same week. The interesting question isn't which one is right. It's why they diverge.

The first answer is population. Fitch's index reaches deep into the lower middle market, to borrowers who never touch the syndicated loan market at all. The CLO number only sees credits large enough to be broadly syndicated and packaged into a structured vehicle. So part of the gap is simply sampling. Fitch is measuring companies the CLO series was never built to see.

The second possible answer is definitional, and here I want to be straight about what we don't know. Default definitions vary. Some count only missed payments and bankruptcy filings. Others fold in distressed exchanges and liability management exercises. We went looking for Fitch's own methodology language on that point and could not find it in any retrievable source. So treat the definitional question as open rather than settled. The population difference is the more defensible explanation for the gap. We can't rule out that definitions are doing some of the work too.

Now the payload inside the number, because this is where the record stops being scary and starts being informative. Fitch breaks the rate out by sector, and the dispersion is stark. Healthcare providers and industrial and manufacturing borrowers both sit near 9.9 percent. Software companies sit at 0.6 percent. Direction matters as much as level here. Healthcare was at 6.9 percent a year ago. Industrial was at 5.2 percent a year ago, so it has nearly doubled in twelve months. Software has moved the other way, down from 2.0 percent a year ago to 0.6 percent today.

That cuts directly against a narrative circulating right now — that private credit stress is concentrated in software borrowers whose revenue is being eaten by AI. On the data available, software is the cleanest sector in the index and getting cleaner. The deterioration is running in operationally exposed businesses instead. The honest caveat is that a trailing-twelve-month rate is backward-looking. If AI-driven software distress is only now beginning, this measure wouldn't show it yet. So the claim isn't disproven; it's unsupported by the table we actually have. The so-what is this: the deterioration is real, but it is concentrated, and it is not concentrated where the headlines say it is. For an allocator, that changes the first due-diligence question from what spread a fund is earning to what sectors sit inside its book.

I also want to flag where this briefing's own working thesis doesn't hold up. We have argued that the smallest borrowers are driving the record rate, because they have the least refinancing optionality. The one verifiable figure we could find complicates that. For borrowers with less than 20 million dollars of EBITDA, the default rate was almost 4 percent by borrower count. That is below the 6.3 percent aggregate, not above it. It's the opposite of what the small-borrower thesis predicts. It may be a measurement convention, by borrower count rather than by exposure, or a threshold that doesn't map onto the sector data. Either way, treat the size story as unconfirmed. What the data confirms is dispersion by sector, not by borrower size.

Turn from who is defaulting to who is lending, because the two connect. Direct lending fundraising fell 35 percent year on year in the first half of 2026 by fund count, even as the funds that did close were roughly twice as large on average. Those are trade-press tallies rather than primary data from a PitchBook or a Preqin, so hold the decimals loosely. Concentration is sharpening at the deal level too. The top ten North American managers completed 38 percent of all direct lending deals in 2025. And the way those managers source new business is shifting, toward refinancings, add-ons and repeat transactions with borrowers they already hold, rather than brand-new platform originations.

Read that against the default data and a real argument appears. A lender whose new deal flow is mostly incremental credit to borrowers already in its book is running amend-and-extend at the point of origination. That mechanically holds its own measured default rate down, because it keeps choosing which borrowers stay current. The benign read deserves equal weight, because the data can't separate the two. Repeat lending to a known borrower is better information, not necessarily adverse selection, and add-on financing for sponsor buy-and-build is ordinary, healthy activity. What the concentration does establish is narrower but still useful. Any single large manager's default statistic increasingly describes a population that manager selected itself. It also means fewer independent underwriters are pricing the same paper, at a moment when the industry's own marks are the main evidence of value. Fitch's 1,300-borrower index, by contrast, includes credits with no such optionality. That's a reason to trust the aggregate over manager-level statistics, not a reason to assume the worst about any one manager.

Capital is still arriving through all of this. Arini Capital Management is in the final stages of a roughly 4 billion dollar debut European direct lending fund, with more than 3 billion already secured and a target close before year-end. It has not closed yet, so treat the size as a target rather than a result. The structure is the interesting part. It runs on a sourcing agreement with Lazard that gives Arini access to Lazard's European advisory and origination network for middle-market borrowers. That is an advisory bank renting out its deal flow for economics, rather than committing its own balance sheet to lending the way JPMorgan has. British Columbia Investment Management anchored the fund with 200 million dollars and committed at least another 400 million to co-investments. Note the geography too. The record default rate is a US index. This capital is going to European middle-market borrowers, a different book entirely.

The fund reports a net internal rate of return of roughly 13 percent since inception, inside its own 12-to-14 percent target range. Press that number before you trust it. It's a since-inception return on a young and largely unrealized book, self-reported by the manager. Landing neatly inside a marketed target range is exactly what an untested mark tends to do. Fundraising momentum and a healthy-looking IRR are statements about LP appetite. They are not statements about loss experience. Both can be true in the same week as a record default rate.

So where does the cycle actually stand? The deterioration is real and it is concentrated — in healthcare and industrial borrowers, not across the asset class, and not in the software names getting the attention. The two aggregate statistics disagree because they measure different populations, and possibly under different definitions we could not confirm. The binding risk from here is reflexive. Capital is consolidating into fewer, larger platforms that increasingly re-lend to borrowers they already own. The borrowers outside that circle have the least refinancing optionality, and they are the ones losing lender interest. That is the channel through which an elective default statistic stops being elective. Three catalysts to watch: tomorrow's Federal Reserve decision, which I treat strictly as scheduled with no outcome assumed; September's Fitch index print; and third-quarter non-accrual and payment-in-kind disclosures from the BDCs in November.

That's the briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
