That is against a pre-war baseline of 130 ships a day. And on August 19th, Lloyd's List Intelligence put its read on the diplomacy bluntly: US-Iran diplomacy, in its words, has effectively collapsed. On Thursday we called that track opaque rather than dead. It is now, on the record, dead.

Here's the non-consensus point. If you only watched the oil price, you would never know any of that happened. WTI printed $86.64 a barrel on August 22nd, up less than a percent on the day. Six months into a chokepoint running roughly ninety percent below its pre-war traffic level, and there is no spike. Two explanations, and the evidence does not yet pick one for you. Either non-Gulf supply and rerouting have genuinely absorbed the shortfall, so the barrels are reaching market some other way. Or the transit collapse overstates the real loss of flow, because a meaningful share of what is still moving is Iranian-linked tonnage that the count captures as traffic without capturing as market-accessible crude. Watch the non-Iranian-linked share specifically. That is the series that would settle it.

Note what this does to the spine we have been running all week. Every item on this beat for four days has been an announced number overstating a delivered one. Requested gigawatts. Licensed chips. Promised reactors. Hormuz runs the other way. The physical disruption is far worse than the price implies. The gap between the headline and the reality is not always in the direction of hype.

Because the toll here is real. It is just being collected somewhere else. Middle East Gulf-to-China tanker earnings have spiked above $520,000 a day. And DP World is spending roughly $100 million a month just to keep its Jebel Ali port ready for a reopening that has not come, with container throughput still near ten percent of normal. That is not a price shock at the pump. That is a freight and insurance tax, and it reaches consumer goods with a lag, through logistics rather than through energy prices. Which is precisely the kind of inflation a central bank handles badly. It does not show up in the oil complex the models watch, and it does not respond to the policy rate.

There is a strategic read underneath this too. Six months in, with the world's dominant navy on station, navigation through this chokepoint still has not been restored. That is worth sitting with if you are pricing a Taiwan Strait contingency. The lesson is not binary, open or closed. It is that a determined adversary can hold a strait in a half-closed state for quarters at a time, and markets will eventually stop pricing it as a crisis and start budgeting for it as a cost structure. That is exactly what is happening here.

Now from a lane a state cannot reopen to a connection a state has decided to stop granting. Texas.

We cited ERCOT's interconnection queue on Friday, sourced to trade-press reporting. This week the number arrived in a primary document. Governor Abbott's own release states that ERCOT is considering, in its words, "approximately over 474 gigawatts of requests to connect to the Texas grid, more than five times Texas' record peak electricity demand." Data centers are roughly ninety percent of those requests. And Abbott's directive, issued as a letter to the Public Utility Commission and to ERCOT rather than as an executive order, calls for an audit of between 250 and 300 projects. It states plainly that any project failing to comply will be, quote, denied connection to the Texas grid.

This matters because Texas was the release valve. When PJM turned restrictive on large-load interconnection, Texas was where the hyperscalers were supposed to go instead. Now the state everyone routes toward to escape restriction is the one running an audit with denial power attached. Track the escalation across five days on this beat. Friday it was Wood Mackenzie estimating that only about 28 percent of requested load ever gets committed, and Exelon voluntarily cutting its own interconnection pipeline by nearly 40 percent. Today it is a state government auditing the queue directly. Estimate, then self-correction, then regulatory filter.

But apply the same skepticism here that we applied to the oil price. The same ERCOT running this audit missed its own August 7th study deadline for the first batch of interconnection reviews. It went to the Public Utility Commission for a good-cause exception ahead of the August 20th open meeting. As of the most recent filings available, that request is pending, not resolved. An operator that cannot hit one study deadline is now expected to verify hundreds of projects.

And there is a deeper gap than capacity. The governor's letter asserts the denial outcome. But neither the letter nor the law-firm analysis around it points to a specific statute or Commission rule that empowers ERCOT to deny an already-filed interconnection request on audit grounds. An order to audit is not audit capacity. A stated intent to deny is not tested legal authority. So hold a second reading alongside the headline one. This may function less as a filter than as an amnesty, a process that lets speculative filers withdraw quietly. That produces a smaller queue number without changing how much of that load was ever real.

Which brings us to where the money is already going rather than waiting to find out. Eagle Point Credit Management has closed roughly $1.3 billion of mezzanine private-credit financing for a data center campus in Hubbard, Texas, south of Dallas. That slice sits inside a project-finance package reported at roughly fifteen to sixteen billion dollars, for a 2,900-acre site with Anthropic as primary tenant. Keep the scale honest: first-phase permits cover about 491,000 square feet of compute space, and the multi-gigawatt ambitions are stated plans rather than commitments. But the critical detail is the power. The campus will have its own gas-fired plant. It never needed to enter the queue Abbott just paused, because it was never planning to rely on the grid connection in the first place.

That is the prediction this beat made on three consecutive days, now arriving with a capital structure attached. And it carries a trade-off worth naming. Behind-the-meter generation buys speed by giving up the grid's shared reserve margin. A campus with its own turbines is exempt from curtailment, and it is also uninsured against its own outage. Meanwhile the fixed cost of maintaining Texas's shared capacity falls increasingly on the ratepayers who stay connected to it, which turns an interconnection question into a state-level political one over time. And note where the risk now sits. Mezzanine credit behind a single-tenant, single-purpose compute campus has no mark-to-market and no public disclosure. If AI capex decelerates, the first visible damage shows up in project-finance layers, not on a hyperscaler balance sheet.

So here is the spine tying today together. Three gatekeepers, three different tools. Tehran controls a shipping lane and is using ambiguity rather than closure. ERCOT controls a grid connection and is using an audit whose legal teeth are untested. And private credit is underwriting the exit from both, one campus at a time. In each case the price of the rationing is being paid somewhere other than where the market is watching. In tanker charter rates, not the oil price. In ratepayer bills, not the interconnection fee. In mezzanine spreads, not the hyperscaler balance sheet.

What to watch from here. First, whether the Public Utility Commission rules on ERCOT's exception request, and whether any denial under the audit ever gets tested by a developer with standing to challenge it. Second, whether weekly Hormuz transit counts break out of their recent seventy-to-ninety range in either direction. That is the number that will tell you whether the market's calm is justified or just tired.
