Cautious Optimism Briefings

Robotics & Physical AI

Transcript · October 20, 2026

Good morning. It's Tuesday, October 20th, and this is your robotics and physical AI briefing.

Two things happened in the past week that come at the same question from opposite directions. A frontier AI lab says it wants to own the robot body, not just supply the brain. And the venture money that moved last week went mostly to data capture and to robots that are already working, not to another generalist humanoid story. Both point the same way. The value in physical AI is shifting toward whoever controls the deployment and the data, not toward whoever has the slickest model demo.

Start with OpenAI. Asked directly whether OpenAI will build a humanoid robot, Sam Altman answered, "We will," according to Engadget. His argument is that a human-shaped body fits a world built for people, and that the body matters less than the brain that controls it. In a separate interview with Fortune, he put a date on it: "I think we'll have a cool demo in 2027." He also cautioned that robots walking the streets are still a few years further out. Those remarks come from two different occasions, not one coordinated announcement. OpenAI has published no blog post, spec sheet, price, partner, or manufacturing plan. This is interview color, not a launch.

But it's interview color worth sitting with, because of an independent result this desk flagged last week. A disclosure first: Anthropic makes the model behind this show and competes with OpenAI. On Artificial Analysis's independent robot-arm benchmark, published October 14th, two OpenAI models took the top two spots. Those were general-purpose frontier models, not systems built for robotics. If a model sold for everything else can top a physical-control test, a capable robot brain may simply come standard with frontier AI. In that world the brain is a commodity, and the value moves to whoever owns the body, the deployment site, and the field data. OpenAI's decision to build the body fits that reading. A lab confident that the brain alone captures the value would license it to hardware makers and collect the rent. Building the body says OpenAI doesn't think licensing is enough. As for Altman's line that the brain is the hard part, read it as positioning from a lab with no hardware track record, not as evidence that actuators and manufacturing are solved. So what: if the brain is commoditizing, the most exposed names in the stack are the startups that sell the brain as the product, Physical Intelligence, Skild AI, and Figure with its Helix program.

Their best defense is to lock in customers and field data before OpenAI ships anything. As we'll see in a moment, one of them is already doing exactly that.

If OpenAI is betting on owning everything, last week's private money bet on something narrower. Dealroom reported that five physical-AI startups raised a combined $510 million in the week of October 12th. The largest was ICON, the construction 3D-printing company, with a $200 million Series D. ICON has already won more than $370 million in government contracts. No valuations were disclosed for any of the five rounds, so this week tells us where capital is going, not what it's paying.

The most telling round was at the data layer. Mecka AI closed a $60 million Series B led by Sequoia. Mecka pays people to demonstrate everyday activities on camera in real-world settings, and turns that human motion into robot training data. The company says its annual run-rate revenue was above $100 million as of June. It says it expects to reach $300 million by year-end. That's a claimed tripling in six months. It's self-reported and unaudited, and no customer names or methodology have been disclosed anywhere. A company already at $100 million of run-rate that raises only $60 million is either unusually capital-efficient, or it defines run-rate generously. Treat it as a claim, not a fact.

Mecka also fits a data-layer race this desk has been tracking. Nvidia's latest GR00T release added pretraining on human video. Mecka is a different answer to the same bottleneck: paid, egocentric human capture. So venture money is now funding training data as its own layer of the stack.

The deployed verticals got paid too. MMI, a surgical robotics company, raised a $125 million Series D led by BioStar Capital. According to the company, as reported by MedTech Dive, its Symani system has performed about 3,500 microsurgical procedures. That's on an installed base of about 50 systems. Those are company figures, not regulator-confirmed ones. And 50 systems is a small base next to the surgical-robot incumbents, so deployed is not the same as scaled. But it's real, revenue-generating clinical use.

Then there's Ultra, which builds two-armed warehouse robots that pack and sort orders. Ultra raised a $50 million Series A. It leases its hardware monthly, with software supplied through a partnership with Physical Intelligence. That's the mirror image of OpenAI's bet. Instead of owning the brain and the body, Ultra licenses a third party's brain and leases out the hardware. Leasing moves capital cost and utilization risk onto Ultra's own balance sheet rather than the customer's. Ultra hasn't disclosed its lease rate, so the payback math stays private for now. It's also Physical Intelligence locking in a deployment channel and field data, which is the defense we just described. So what: last week's capital rewarded verified usage and data infrastructure over the generalist humanoid pitch. And it put two competing answers on the table for who captures the value.

If the brain and the data are where the money is going, the body itself is getting cheap. Faraday Future and its robotics affiliate FFR launched FF Nova, a bipedal humanoid with 20 degrees of freedom. It's priced at $2,490, with a $390 developer package on top. Phase One is capped at 100 units, with the first 20 to be delivered immediately. This is a developer and education seeding play, not a labor product. Twenty joints on a body that cheap almost certainly means small, low-torque servo-class actuators. Faraday Future also has a long history of production and solvency trouble in its EV business. So a list price and an immediate-delivery claim are not the same as a fulfilled order book. If it fails to ship, it becomes a cautionary tale for every cheap-humanoid price promise. So what: degrees of freedom are getting commoditized fast, but reliability and payload are not, and that gap is where the real cost still lives.

Now to the names with tests on the calendar. Texas registration data puts Tesla's Austin robotaxi fleet at roughly 740 vehicles. That's still just a vehicle count, with no disclosed ride volume or utilization. The one new detail is that Ashok Elluswamy, Tesla's VP of AI software, said Dallas could be next. No date is attached. Tesla's third-quarter call lands tomorrow, October 21st. It's the real test of whether Tesla will finally disclose driverless miles, utilization, or an Optimus production number.

In defense, Leonardo DRS won a counter-drone sensing contract under JIATF-401, the Joint Interagency Task Force. It carries a $500 million ceiling over three years. That's a ceiling, not committed revenue. It's also a sensing deal, not an autonomy or kill-chain one. Still, it fits the pattern. In the West, the autonomy dollars that turn into fielded hardware keep coming from defense budgets.

Pulling it together. What's genuinely inflecting is capital discipline. Investors are paying for verified deployments and data infrastructure over roadmap promises. Defense demand keeps showing up as real contracts. What's still a claim is most of the rest: OpenAI's humanoid, which is a 2027 demo at best, Mecka's revenue projection, and whether Faraday Future can actually ship a hundred units. The biggest open question is whether frontier labs integrating into hardware squeezes the robot-brain startups before they lock in their field data.

Watch four things. Intuitive Surgical reports today, and that print hasn't happened yet. It's the scale benchmark that MMI's 50 systems are measured against. Tesla's call is tomorrow. The RoboBusiness and World Physical AI conferences open this week. And in November, Agility Robotics' shareholders vote on its SPAC merger to go public. That gives a Western humanoid maker its first daily public price, and a read-through for every private humanoid mark.

That's the briefing. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
