Good morning. It's Tuesday, November 3rd, and this is your private equity briefing. The thread running through today's news is simple: at every level of this machine, the biggest holders are choosing to stay in private paper instead of taking cash.

Start with Global Payments. Per the Financial Times on October 30th, CD&R and GTCR are exploring a joint take-private of the card processor at roughly forty billion dollars including debt. New since Monday, the trade outlet FinTech Futures reports that GTCR would roll its stake rather than sell. On Monday we told you that hadn't been reported, so that question now has an answer, at least from one outlet. We've found no formal proposal, no price, and no board response since. GTCR became Global Payments' largest outside shareholder through the Worldpay deal, and William Blair sizes that position at roughly fifteen percent of the shares. William Blair calls the take-private report credible. But credible isn't the same as likely. The firm puts the odds of a completed deal comfortably below fifty percent. The reason is leverage. Global Payments already carries nearly twenty-two billion dollars of net debt, which leaves little room for a traditional buyout to layer on fresh borrowing.

That's exactly why the rollover matters. Rolled equity substitutes for capital that debt markets may not supply. It only goes so far, though. GTCR's roughly fifteen percent covers about that slice of the equity. CD&R, plus any fresh money from GTCR, would still have to buy out the other eighty-five percent of the shares, on top of a balance sheet that's already heavily borrowed. So the roll makes the deal more financeable, not easy.

There are two ways to read GTCR staying in. One is conviction. The best-informed holder thinks the business is worth more than any price it could get selling into the market today. The other is necessity. A fifteen percent block can't be sold into the market without crushing the stock, so rolling avoids the markdown a block sale would force. Either way, a roughly forty-billion-dollar buyout, if it happens, returns no cash to GTCR's own limited partners on that stake. So what looks like a marquee deal is, for one of its two sponsors, not an exit at all.

GTCR isn't alone, and the next deal tells you more about price. Bain Capital owns about thirty-nine percent of Surgery Partners. It has agreed to roll that stake into a take-private led by HealthSpan Partners, an affiliate of the nonprofit health system Bon Secours Mercy Health. Public shareholders get twenty-three dollars and fifty cents a share in cash. That's two dollars and twenty-five cents below the twenty-five seventy-five that Bain itself proposed, non-bindingly, in January of 2025. Unlike Global Payments, this one is signed. It still needs shareholder and regulatory approval, with closing expected in the first quarter of 2027.

Here's why Surgery Partners is the sharpest data point in the episode. HealthSpan is the one arm's-length buyer in this set. Its bid is a genuine outside price rather than a sponsor's own number. And that outside price came in below what Bain said the business was worth twenty-one months ago. Bain stayed in anyway. Be careful with that comparison, though. The January figure was a non-binding opening bid, not a valuation mark. Bain's cost basis isn't disclosed, so we can't say whether Bain is underwater. There may also be governance value or strategic logic in the new structure that isn't in the headline price. But the narrower point holds. The only outside price we have is lower than the sponsor's own earlier number, and once again the sponsor's limited partners get no cash. There's a quieter cost too. Bain's stake has had a share price every trading day. After closing, it will carry a model mark, and Bain's LPs lose that daily market check. Listed, observable stakes are becoming private, model-marked ones.

Why would sponsors rather hold than sell? Look at how the biggest manager in the business gets paid. On its third-quarter call, Blackstone reported fee-related earnings up six percent from a year earlier. In the same quarter, net realizations fell twenty percent from the second quarter. That's the split that matters. Fees keep running while capital stays invested, and in Blackstone's perpetual and wealth vehicles they're charged on the marked value itself, whether or not any cash comes back to limited partners. Carry waits on realizations, which are lumpy and right now depressed.

Two fair qualifiers on Blackstone. Management flagged the realization drop in advance, so read it as timing, not collapse. And these are firm-wide numbers, spanning credit, real estate and infrastructure as well as buyouts, so the fee growth isn't a pure buyout read. Some of it comes from those perpetual vehicles. Still, the incentive is plain. A manager can keep growing its steadiest earnings stream while the cost of waiting for cash falls on its LPs. Blackstone is guiding to a significant step-up in fourth-quarter realizations, with contracted carry alone expected to exceed the third quarter's entire total. That's the pending test, and it stays a promise until the January print. So for this week's manager earnings, read the realization line before the fee line.

LPs are supposed to be the brake on all of this. The biggest one just pressed the accelerator instead. CalPERS' private equity allocation stood at a hundred forty-five point six billion dollars as of September 30th. That's twenty-two point four percent of total assets, well above the board's seventeen percent target. Per the Financial Times, the pension cut its directly held Magnificent Seven positions by twenty-six percent in the first half of the year, and it is shifting further toward private equity. That reverses the inference we drew on Monday. We said an over-target pension would have less room and would likely slow new commitments. CalPERS is doing the opposite. The reporting doesn't say the board changed its target, so treat this as positioning, not a formal policy shift.

The counterweight is the return record. Private Equity Wire cites the S&P 500 returning about sixty-five percent including dividends over the three years to this past March. A Cambridge Associates U.S. private equity index returned about twenty-four percent over the same stretch. That's one three-year window, and it's an index, not CalPERS' own book. It's also not a formal public-market-equivalent comparison. But the gap is wide. CalPERS is leaning harder into the asset class that badly lagged. There's a fair counter-case. Cutting concentrated mega-cap tech exposure is reasonable risk management on its own terms. And the relative-value bet could prove right. Even so, adding to an overweight on that argument is not self-evidently sound allocation logic. If other large pensions copy this rotation, flagship fundraising can keep running on narrative while distributions stay thin. That defers the reckoning rather than resolving it.

One market exists to put an outside price on all this held paper, and that's secondaries. The latest full data covers the first half, reported in July. Volume set a first-half record, somewhere around a hundred twenty billion dollars across the major advisors. But Jefferies says dedicated secondaries dry powder fell to two hundred ninety billion dollars at the end of June. That's down from a record three hundred twenty-seven billion at the end of last year. Part of that is simply capital being deployed fast, which is a sign of demand. But it also means the buyers who test marks against a real bid are drawing down capacity while the supply of stakes keeps growing. If that gap persists, discounts could widen and force a real repricing of the marks. That's a conditional to watch, not a forecast.

Pull it together. On this week's evidence, the private equity cycle isn't so much frozen as choosing duration over cash. Sponsors are rolling rather than selling. Managers are paid whether or not realizations arrive. And CalPERS is adding to an overweight instead of demanding distributions. In these deals, at least, the exit logjam looks partly like a choice, not purely a market constraint. The generous reading is that insiders and allocators genuinely believe private values will beat public ones. The test of that belief is whether cash eventually comes back at or above today's marks. The catalysts are close together. Watch whether Global Payments actually gets signed and financed. Watch Apollo's and Carlyle's prints this week, and KKR's on November 9th, and read them for realizations, not just fee growth. Then comes Blackstone's promised fourth-quarter step-up in January, and whether Surgery Partners closes on schedule in the first quarter of 2027. Any one of those could confirm this read or break it.

That's the briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
