Good morning. This is Cautious Optimism Briefings, Enterprise Tech, for September 17th, 2026.

Let's start with Salesforce's own numbers, because they answer a question this beat has been chasing all week. On September 16th, Salesforce published its second Agentic Enterprise Index. It is aggregated telemetry from its own customers running Agentforce, the agent platform whose seven named job-ready agents we covered on Monday. The headline finding is speed. The average number of agents activated per organization is up nearly three times over the past year. And organizations now deploy an agent an average of two days after they provision it.

A callback, because it frames what follows. Yesterday on this beat we ranked the major enterprise AI vendors by the unit each one chooses to measure its AI business in, from contracted annual value at the top down to percentage claims with no customer attached at the bottom. The Index adds a new rung underneath that ladder, and it is the cleanest example yet. It counts agents. It does not count dollars.

That matters because Salesforce does have dollars to report, and chose not to put them in this document. On its second-quarter earnings call on August 31st, Chief Financial Officer Robin Washington and Chief Executive Marc Benioff both said Agentforce annual recurring revenue had reached one and a half billion dollars. President Miguel Milano added a second disclosure on that same call. Half of the quarter's bookings, he said, came from existing customers "refilling the tank" on usage-based Flex Credits. He also said Salesforce added two thousand new paying customers into production, up 70 percent quarter over quarter.

Hold onto that last figure, because it is the one the Index most conspicuously lacks. A paying customer in production is exactly the production-versus-pilot split that an activation count cannot give you. Salesforce has that number. It published it three weeks ago, to investors. It simply did not carry it over into the marketing document.

So this is not a vendor that lacks a monetizable metric. It is a vendor making a choice about audience. The dollar figure goes to the people who can check it, and the agent count goes to the market it wants to convince. Two other limits are worth stating plainly. The Index is Salesforce's first-party usage data, not an audited or third-party study, and Salesforce explicitly disclaims the whole report as, in its words, "not indicative of Salesforce performance." And the inclusion rule is thin. The methodology note requires only that a business have activated agents in production every month across the analysis window. It does not say what share of that business's agents those were, and it does not disclose how many organizations are in the sample at all.

There is one more thing about the window itself. Salesforce describes the analysis as running from February 2025 to April 2026. So the "nearly three times" growth figure, published in the middle of September, measures a period that closed roughly five months ago. That is not disqualifying. It does mean this is a historical study being presented as a read on the current state of enterprise agent adoption.

Then there is the retail claim, which is the softest thing in the report. Salesforce says retailers deploying agents saw online sales growth of 8 percent year over year, against 2 percent for those that didn't. That is a four-times gap, and it is presented as a result of deploying agents. But it is a comparison of two self-selected groups inside a single vendor's own customer base. The more obvious reading runs backwards. Retailers already growing at 8 percent are the ones with the engineering bench and the budget to deploy agents in the first place, not the other way around. Causation here is asserted, not shown.

Here is the "so what," and it is the genuinely useful finding buried under the marketing. Creating an enterprise agent has become nearly free and nearly instant. Salesforce's own telemetry puts agent build time down 53 percent over the year, and the gap between provisioning an agent and running it in production at two days. Whatever you make of the source, the direction is not in dispute. That removes the constraint everyone assumed was the bottleneck. Building the thing was never going to be the hard part for long. What it relocates the bottleneck to is everything that has to happen before an agent touches a production system. Permissioning. Identity. Audit. Every two-day deployment is another non-human principal holding a live credential into a system of record.

And notice the timing mismatch that creates. Enterprise security budgets are set annually and contractually. Agents are now provisioned in days. The surface that needs governing is expanding on a two-day clock, inside a spending cycle that refreshes once a year. That gap is the actual commercial opening for the security vendors. Nobody has published a price for closing it.

Which brings us to the one vendor now trying to sell exactly that. On September 3rd, Cloudflare extended its OpenAI partnership into an early-access vulnerability discovery and remediation service. It pairs a cyber-specific OpenAI model with Cloudflare's own network telemetry. The combination does code investigation and automated patch generation at the edge, delivered inside Cloudflare's Managed Defense product.

One number explains why this is a real category rather than a press release. The National Vulnerability Database had logged 60,475 vulnerabilities by September of this year. That is already above the 48,185 recorded across all of 2025, with a quarter of the year still to run. Triage at that volume is not a staffing problem anyone solves by hiring.

But be precise about what has and hasn't happened. This is early access, not general availability. There is no customer count and no contract value disclosed. That puts it in the same category as the Salesforce Index we just went through: a capability described by the company selling it.

The interesting question isn't whether the model works. It is who owns the distribution. Automated detection and patching is the same pitch the endpoint security incumbents already sell as their core AI differentiator. Cloudflare's edge here is not the model, which anyone can license. It is that Cloudflare already sits in the traffic path. If that incumbency holds, this is a genuine moat extension into a category Cloudflare didn't previously own. If it doesn't, the capability commoditizes, and differentiation reverts to what it has always been in security, which is telemetry coverage and where you sit in the path.

There is a second exposure worth naming. If automated patch generation actually works at scale, it compresses the market for manual vulnerability management and penetration testing. That work sits today with systems integrators and security consultancies, on a billable-hours model. That is the margin pool most directly in the line of fire, and it is a bigger number than anything Cloudflare will book from this service next year.

So Cloudflare is the first vendor to put a product where that governance gap is. It still has no named customer and no published price.

One item this week did arrive with a customer, a deliverable and a date. On September 1st, the Army Contracting Command moved Palantir's TITAN tactical intelligence program from prototype into production. It awarded Palantir and Anduril a combined deal reported at roughly 190 million dollars, for eight ground-station systems over 18 months. Treat that figure as directional, because sources disagree. One outlet puts Palantir's share alone at 127 million. And remember what a production award is. It is a delivery obligation, not revenue recognized today. It converts over a year and a half.

Put it together. What a vendor is willing to put a number on tells you where its business actually stands. This week the AI-native software vendors gave us agent counts up threefold, a build-time percentage, and a correlational sales claim from a window that closed in April. The one hard, contracted dollar figure in the whole set came from a defense delivery order, not from an agent platform. So here is the thing to watch next quarter. Does Salesforce attach a consumption figure or a contract value to Agentforce agents specifically, or does the agent count simply grow again in its place. The second outcome would tell you the monetization unit still isn't settled.
