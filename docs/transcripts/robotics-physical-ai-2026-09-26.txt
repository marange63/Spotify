Good morning. It's September 26th, 2026, and this is your robotics and physical AI briefing.

All month this beat has asked for numbers other than demo success rates. This week production rates finally showed up. The open question is whether the money is following them.

Start with Tesla's Optimus line. Electrek, crediting a same-day report from The Information, says Tesla went from a few dozen units a week during small-batch testing in the second quarter to several hundred a week by August. For scale, CBS put Tesla's entire 2025 humanoid shipments at "several hundred or fewer." So if the new rate is right, Tesla is now building in a single week roughly what it shipped in all of last year. That comparison is this desk's arithmetic on two relayed estimates, so treat it as an order of magnitude, not a ratio.

That is a real jump in manufacturing output. But look at where the bottleneck sits. According to the same report, the hand and forearm still contain more than a hundred screws and small components that workers assemble by hand. That is not a model problem. It is a bill-of-materials problem. On Thursday this desk argued that the constraint on humanoid scale is the bill of materials, not the brain. Tesla's own reported bottleneck now says the same thing. The value-capture read follows, and it is ours rather than a reported trend. If hand actuation stays the binding constraint, near-term margin in the humanoid stack goes to dexterous-hand and actuator suppliers, not to the model layer. A better model does not remove a single one of those screws.

Now look at where the units go. Electrek reports that most of them are used internally for testing, training and data collection. The ones working inside Tesla's factories are confined to tightly controlled, supervised areas. Tesla's own shareholder update for the quarter ended June 30th backs that up in the company's words. It says the initial Optimus builds will be used in what it calls its Optimus Academy, for training data collection and further functionality development. So the buyer for these robots, for now, is Tesla. Hundreds of units a week is a factory-floor number, not a demand number.

One caveat covers all of this. The weekly figure rests on a single press account relaying a paywalled report, not a Tesla filing or call. And a rate observed in one month is not a stable run-rate.

The timeline adds a second layer of skepticism. Tesla's fourth-quarter 2025 shareholder deck said it planned to unveil the Gen 3 version of Optimus in the first quarter of this year. That did not happen. Elon Musk separately promised a reveal by mid-2026. It is now late September, and that demo still has not happened either. So any new Gen 3 date would be the third version of a promise already missed twice. The same deck is the source of an eventual design capacity of one million robots a year. That is an ambitious number attached to a roadmap with a live credibility problem. So what: this is genuine progress in manufacturing legibility. But treat every date on the Optimus calendar as provisional until Tesla itself puts it in writing and then meets it.

Volume without a paying buyer is also, reportedly, the question Beijing is asking of its own humanoid makers. Technology.org reported around September 21st that Chinese regulators are slowing humanoid IPOs after Unitree's Shanghai listing, reportedly to examine how much revenue comes from state-backed robot data-collection contracts rather than organic commercial demand. This desk could not verify that account against the full article, and it names no regulator, company or percentage, so treat it as reported, not confirmed.

If it holds, it rhymes with Tesla's Optimus Academy, and that says something about the model and data layer. In both cases, early humanoid output appears to be going to data collection. Tesla's buyer is Tesla itself. The Chinese buyer, reportedly, is the state. This desk's reading is that real-world robot data is still scarce enough that builders are putting hardware into the field partly just to generate it. That makes early humanoid fleets look more like data-gathering capital than deployed labor. Their payback runs through a better model later, not through work sold today. That is exactly the revenue an investor, or a regulator, should discount. So what: revenue quality, not unit volume, is becoming the gating question on both sides of the Pacific.

Which brings us to the one physical-AI operator that has actually put a revenue line next to its production line. Aurora Innovation held its investor day on September 23rd. The figures here are company guidance and targets, relayed by Yahoo Finance's coverage.

First, the setup. On Thursday we reported that Aurora runs twenty driver-out trucks, with a stated goal of two hundred by year-end. That gave us a simple test. At twenty trucks, its per-mile operating-cost claim is a claim. At two hundred, it's a P&L line. The investor-day coverage we have gives no updated fleet count. So the fleet half of that test is still open. What investor day did supply is a build rate and a revenue line.

Start with the safety base. Aurora says it has accumulated six million total miles, including nearly half a million fully driverless. By this desk's arithmetic, that means under a tenth of its driving has happened without a driver. The safety record is thinner than the headline mileage suggests.

Now the build rate. Aurora is targeting a production rate of twenty trucks a week in the fourth quarter. That is a weekly build rate, not a fleet size, and it should not be confused with the twenty trucks on the road today. By this desk's arithmetic, holding twenty new trucks a week for about nine weeks would take the fleet from twenty to two hundred. So the year-end goal is reachable on paper. But that only works if the rate arrives early in the quarter and every new truck enters driverless service, and Aurora has disclosed neither.

Now set the revenue line beside it. Aurora guided to fourteen to sixteen million dollars for all of 2026, with more than half of that expected in the fourth quarter. So the build ramp and the revenue ramp are back-loaded into the same three months. That makes fourth-quarter production execution the single swing factor for the 2026 guide. A slip in the build rate shows up directly in revenue. And even if everything lands, fourteen to sixteen million dollars is small for a company aiming at a two-hundred-truck fleet. The composition of that revenue is also undisclosed.

The lever that would close the gap is cost. Aurora says its second-generation system cuts hardware costs by more than fifty percent. That is the mechanism that would turn the per-mile cost claim we covered Thursday into actual margin. But Aurora has not disclosed the base cost. Without it, that reduction cannot yet be converted into a payback period. So what: this is the clearest look yet at the gap between a physical-AI operator's build rate and its revenue, and for now the revenue curve is well behind. Two checkpoints will tell you which curve is winning. Does the build rate actually reach twenty a week? And where does the fleet land in December against two hundred?

Pull the threads together. The producers in this space are finally publishing numbers that can be checked against each other, rather than against a demo reel. That is real progress in legibility. But output is not demand, and a robot built to collect data is not yet a robot doing paid work. What's still missing on every side is paid demand, disclosed unit cost, and reliability data from outside a controlled test area. Three questions heading into year-end. Does Tesla put a production figure or a Gen 3 date in a primary disclosure, rather than a press report? Does a Chinese regulator ever name the revenue test it's reportedly running? And does Aurora's driverless fleet get anywhere near two hundred trucks by December? Those three answers, more than any new demo, will tell you whether physical AI is compounding or just getting louder.

That's the briefing. Have a good one.
