Good morning. It's Tuesday, July 28th, and this is your strategic power briefing.

All week this feed has argued that the binding constraint on the AI buildout was physical, not financial: turbine order books, grid interconnection queues, export-control law. Money was abundant; the bottleneck was concrete, steel, and permits. Today that thesis broke. The market moved the constraint back onto the balance sheet.

Alphabet shares fell more than seven percent on Thursday, their worst single session in over a year. The broader Magnificent Seven index tumbled four point eight percent the same day, its worst day since April of last year, and the group is now down for 2026 after three straight years of gains. The trigger was capital spending. Alphabet's own earnings materials confirm it raised full-year 2026 capex guidance to a range of 195 to 205 billion dollars, up from a prior 180 to 190 billion. That guidance increase, not a miss, is what investors punished. Alphabet's own numbers also show second-quarter free cash flow went negative, by 5.9 billion dollars, driven directly by that capital spending. Some press reporting calls that Alphabet's first negative free cash flow since its 2004 IPO — but that historical claim comes from secondary coverage, not from Alphabet itself, so hold it as reported, not confirmed. Reporting also puts combined 2026 capital spending across Alphabet, Microsoft, Amazon and Meta at roughly 724 billion dollars, rising toward 950 billion in 2027. That aggregate is a press estimate, not a company or analyst-house figure, but directionally it captures the scale investors are now recoiling from. As one fund manager put it to reporters, the market used to reward more capex; now it rewards less.

Two things to hold apart here. The capacity being financed — the data centers, the chips, the power contracts — is real. What's cracking is the market's tolerance for financing it. This is anticipatory repricing, not a confirmed slowdown. Microsoft and Meta report earnings tomorrow, Apple and Amazon Thursday, all after this briefing airs, so the market is front-running guidance it hasn't seen yet. The so-what: the cost of equity capital for the entire AI buildout just rose, before a single hyperscaler has actually cut a dollar of spend. If that discipline holds through this week's prints, expect more of the buildout to migrate off hyperscaler balance sheets and into private credit, joint ventures, and special-purpose vehicles — shifting who actually bears the risk, and echoing the private-credit stress this show has already flagged in semi-liquid vehicles elsewhere.

Now set that repricing against the physical reality it's supposedly disciplining, because the power side of this buildout was already running behind the announcements. A Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists analysis this week is the sharpest evidence yet of the gap between nuclear press releases and operating nuclear capacity. Amazon's deal with Energy Northwest isn't a supply commitment — it's a purchase right, an option to buy from the first four-module project, which is expected to generate 320 megawatts. TerraPower's commercial operating date, meanwhile, has slipped from the Department of Energy's original five-to-seven-year target set in 2020 to 2031, per the company's own November statement. And the dollars going into these developers are a fraction of what a plant actually costs: X-energy has raised roughly 500 million dollars, TerraPower roughly 650 million, against an estimated 9.4 billion dollar price tag for a single 345-megawatt Natrium plant. None of that indicts the whole nuclear-for-AI push evenly — more mature large-reactor restarts, like the Three Mile Island unit under Microsoft's power agreement, are further along. But for the small modular reactor wave specifically, the paper is years ahead of the pour. So what: if capex discipline restrains hyperscalers just as signed nuclear capacity keeps slipping toward 2031, the near-term power gap through the rest of this decade gets filled disproportionately by gas turbines and behind-the-meter gas generation — supporting gas-turbine order books over uranium and SMR-adjacent equities that may be pricing in timelines more aggressive than what's actually under contract.

Who pays for that power is now moving from a voluntary pledge to binding law. We've tracked the Ratepayer Protection Pledge, the March agreement in which Amazon, Google, Meta, Microsoft, OpenAI, Oracle and xAI committed to cover the grid-upgrade costs their data centers create. That pledge has now cleared its first legislative hurdle: the House Energy and Commerce Committee voted 52 to nothing to advance the Ratepayer Protection Act, which would direct state utility commissions to require data-center loads of 100 megawatts or more to cover the full cost of the grid upgrades they trigger. The administration says the underlying pledge now covers eighty percent of power delivered to U.S. homes and businesses. A unanimous committee vote is real bipartisan signal, but it isn't enacted law — it still needs full House and Senate passage, and the bill's language leaves much of the actual enforcement to state commissions. So what: this closes off a political tail risk — an electricity-price backlash against the AI buildout — before it fully materialized.

Now pivot to the other side of the board. While U.S. markets are disciplining their builders, Beijing is moving the opposite direction. China's Commerce Ministry has held talks with Alibaba, ByteDance and the startup Z.ai about limiting overseas access to their flagship AI systems — Qwen, Doubao, and GLM-5.2 — under a formal licensing regime that would extend China's existing rare-earth and semiconductor export restrictions to cover finished AI chips and the models trained on them. That's a meaningful shift: Beijing moving from purely defensive controls on inputs it dominates to offensive controls on its own frontier AI outputs, treating advanced models as a state asset to hoard rather than diffuse. It's not enacted — no rule text or effective date exists, and analysts expect any restrictions to hit future high-performance systems before today's open-weight models already circulating. But it complicates the assumption that China floods the world with cheap open models indefinitely, and it sharpens the live question this briefing keeps returning to: does market-imposed capex discipline on democratic, market-financed builders cede buildout momentum to a state-directed rival, even as that rival simultaneously tightens its own grip on what it's willing to export?

One more enforcement gap worth a line. Senator Elizabeth Warren has invoked her statutory authority under the Export Control Reform Act, demanding the Bureau of Industry and Security turn over records by August 7th on a specific loophole: when the administration rescinded the Biden-era AI Diffusion Rule, it also stripped the license requirement underpinning the Foundry Due Diligence Rule — potentially letting foundries like TSMC once again export advanced chips to unvetted third-country intermediaries without the vetting that rule required. This is an oversight letter, not a Commerce admission, and there's no public evidence yet the gap has actually been exploited. But it's a concrete, dated procedural action against a real structural hole in the export-control architecture, at the exact moment China is tightening its own.

Three things to watch. Microsoft and Meta report tomorrow, Apple and Amazon Thursday — the actual guidance behind this week's selloff. Watch whether China moves from consultation to a published AI-model licensing rule. And watch whether BIS actually produces the records Warren demanded by August 7th.
