Cautious Optimism Briefings

Robotics & Physical AI

Transcript · October 9, 2026

Good morning. It's Friday, October 9th, 2026, and this is your briefing on robotics and physical AI.

Three times this week, on Monday, Wednesday, and yesterday, this desk told you it found no fresh evidence from the robot-brain layer. That's the model layer that is supposed to turn humanoids and manipulators from demos into deployed labor. This week that silence broke. But it broke in a boardroom and on term sheets, not in an audited performance number. Here's what that means, and why it should make you more skeptical, not less.

Start with the lead. Boston Dynamics has named Rohit Prasad as its chief executive, effective October 7th. He is the company's third CEO. Bloomberg broke the news on October 6th. Prasad spent twelve years at Amazon, where he was senior vice president and head scientist for Alexa and artificial general intelligence. He later led development of Amazon's Nova foundation models. He is not a roboticist. Boston Dynamics says only that he will speed up its "Physical AI" strategy, which combines its robots with advanced AI. One sourcing note: the reporting is secondary, and no company release text has surfaced.

Put this in the context of the week. Last Friday we reported that Boston Dynamics looked unlikely to list next year, because Atlas, its humanoid, isn't deployed at scale. On Monday, its under-two-year payback pitch leaned on Spot, its quadruped, not on Atlas. On Tuesday, we covered its new four-fingered Atlas hand, with thirteen degrees of freedom but no customer behind it. Each of those was a hardware story. Today's is not. A company whose identity is mechanical and locomotion engineering just handed the top job to a large-model executive. This desk's reading is that Hyundai, which owns Boston Dynamics, is conceding the bottleneck on Atlas is the model and the data, not the actuators.

Hold that reading loosely, though. Three interpretations compete, and the reporting doesn't let us pick one. The first is the plain one: the model is the scarce input, so you hire a model builder. The second is a narrative hire. A company angling for an eventual listing benefits from an AI-pedigree CEO, whatever changes on the factory floor. The third is that a build-or-buy decision on a robot foundation model is coming, and Prasad is there to run it. There's also a competence gap to flag. Conversational models at Amazon are a different problem from closed-loop control under physical contact. A model pedigree is not evidence of robot-policy skill. And a CEO hire is not a product, a partnership, or a deployment. What it does change is the bar. The next Atlas disclosure will be expected to be a model or a partnership, not another hardware spec. So what: Boston Dynamics now says, out loud, that the next move happens in the model layer.

If the model is the scarce input, what does a leading robot brain actually deliver on a real line? That's where Skild AI comes in. In mid-September, Skild said its S1 model is being deployed with Foxconn and Nvidia on dual-arm manipulators for assembly, running on Nvidia Blackwell systems. In Skild's own testing, S1 hit an average per-step success rate of about 66 percent on unseen tasks. An unnamed baseline system managed 9 percent.

Seven times the baseline sounds like real progress, and it probably is evidence of genuine generalization. But per-step is not per-task. Take a job with ten sequential steps, each succeeding independently at 66 percent. The odds of finishing the whole job unaided come out under 2 percent. That's this desk's arithmetic, not Skild's number. It assumes the steps are independent, and retries or recovery could change it. Skild hasn't disclosed either. Nor has it said whether the Foxconn line is a pilot or a production deployment. So the honest read: real evidence of generalization, not yet evidence of a deployable completion rate.

A benchmark is one kind of evidence. An operating record is another. On Tuesday we covered Figure retiring most of its Figure 02 humanoid fleet, and asked whether depreciation, not sticker price, drives humanoid payback. Figure's own June announcement shows what that generation produced first. Over an eleven-month deployment at BMW's Spartanburg plant, Figure says its 02 robots loaded more than 90,000 parts across more than 1,250 operating hours. Figure 03 has since taken over that line.

Figure frames the task, part sequencing, as one that, in its words, "cannot be solved reliably with a fixed series of hard-coded motions." That's the company's argument that its Helix vision-language-action model is doing real work, not running a script.

Now run the numbers yourself. Ninety thousand parts over 1,250 hours is roughly 72 parts per operating hour. And 1,250 hours over eleven months is under four hours a day, if that figure describes a single robot. Figure doesn't say whether those are single-robot hours or hours summed across a fleet. If they're fleet hours, per-robot utilization is even lower. These are Figure's own numbers, and BMW hasn't confirmed them.

There's a bigger gap. Figure gives no split between autonomy and teleoperation, and no intervention rate. That's the same gap we flagged on Monday in Nucleus's self-reported 60 percent autonomy figure. So this is the best fielded humanoid record on the tape, and it still can't be priced against labor cost. We don't know what fraction of it was a human driving the robot remotely.

Capital isn't waiting for that answer. FieldAI, the Nvidia- and Bill Gates-backed robotics-AI startup, has reportedly signed a term sheet to raise $700 million at a $10 billion valuation. Its last valuation was $2 billion, set in August of last year. That's a five-fold step-up in roughly fourteen months.

Now the discounts. The round hasn't closed. The report rests on a single anonymous source, and no lead investor is named. FieldAI says its revenue and customer contracts now top $135 million. But that figure mixes recognized revenue with contract value. It's an order book dressed up as a sales number.

The peer marks deserve the same scrutiny. Skild AI's valuation of more than $14 billion stands on firmer ground. That was a closed $1.4 billion Series C, led by SoftBank, reported by Bloomberg in January. Physical Intelligence is often cited near that range too. But that figure traces to a single report of financing talks, never confirmed as closed, so we won't treat it as a settled mark.

The repricing shows up in acquisitions as well. Late last month AMD agreed to buy Fei-Fei Li's World Labs, a company valued at $5 billion just seven months earlier. This desk's inference: if hardware owners conclude the model is the scarce input, they become buyers of the independent brain labs. That supports those labs' private marks through strategic optionality, not demonstrated performance. So what: the brain layer is being priced ahead of any audited completion rate.

So where does that leave us? What's genuinely inflecting is conviction. Management and capital are now betting explicitly on the model and data layer, with named deployments to point to. What's still a demo is the performance evidence. Every number on today's tape is a per-step success rate, a parts count with no autonomy split, or revenue mixed with contract value. Those are three incompatible units. Nobody has yet published an intervention rate on a paying deployment. Whoever does first sets the benchmark everyone else gets measured against.

One correction from yesterday. We framed November 10th as the date China's magnet export lever comes back into play. In fact, China's April 2025 licensing regime on heavy rare earths was never suspended. So November 10th is a step inside an existing regime, not a cliff. And the reported extension of the broader truce to January is still unconfirmed by any formal Ministry of Commerce notice.

Three catalysts to watch. First, Prasad's first strategic move or partnership at Boston Dynamics. Second, whether FieldAI's round actually closes, and at what price. Third, Tesla's October earnings call, for a primary Optimus production number.

That's the briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
