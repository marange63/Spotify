Semiconductors & Compute Infrastructure

Good morning. It's Monday, August 31st, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

For a week now, this show has called memory the one input cost in this industry that refuses to slide while every revenue commitment moves right. Today brings the first supply-side answer to that constraint, and it is not the answer anyone was hoping for. It is a share fight inside the high-bandwidth-memory duopoly, not new capacity. And the pricing evidence says the relief case does not hold up.

Start with Samsung. TrendForce reporting from earlier this month puts Samsung's HBM4 yield at roughly 80 percent, up from below 60 percent when mass production began in February. Samsung is guiding third-quarter HBM4 revenue to more than triple sequentially, with HBM4 expected to exceed 60 percent of its total HBM sales in the second half. The company is also reported to be targeting around 38 percent of the overall HBM market by year-end. Set that against SK hynix, which holds an estimated 50 to 60 percent share of HBM3e, the generation Samsung is trying to leapfrog past. So the headline is real. Samsung is closing a yield gap that looked much worse six months ago. But triple-digit sequential growth off an unstated base is not the same as qualified volume landing at the GPU makers, and nobody outside Samsung has disclosed what that base actually is. This is share moving inside a fixed pool of suppliers, not more bits arriving in the market.

Before going further, settle one thing the coverage keeps treating as open. HBM4 qualification is not the contest. NVIDIA's Jensen Huang confirmed on June the fifth that Samsung, SK hynix and Micron had all passed certification to supply HBM4 for the Vera Rubin platform. That was nearly three months ago, and it means all three are already cleared to ship. The live contest is allocation share among vendors who are already qualified. That makes Samsung's yield climb more consequential, not less, because the remaining variable is execution and negotiating leverage rather than whether Samsung can play at all.

Here is where this show set itself a test on Saturday. We said NVIDIA's supply-constrained guidance had shifted the swing factor off hyperscaler budgets and onto memory and packaging output, and we named HBM pricing as the thing to watch. This is the first read on that test, and it is a mixed one. A yield number at the number-two vendor is not the same as more bits reaching the accelerator makers. And Micron disclosed back in late June that its entire calendar-2026 high-bandwidth-memory book, including HBM4, was already price- and volume-contracted. This show covered that on Thursday. So whatever Samsung does this quarter cannot reprice anything already locked in for this year. The earliest point where a supply shift could show up in pricing was always going to be 2027.

Except the 2027 evidence does not cooperate either. Analysts cited by the Korean outlet Bloter now project HBM contract prices rising more than 50 percent in 2027 versus this year. UBS is projecting something sharper still, roughly a 79 percent increase in HBM average selling prices next year. Both are analyst estimates responding to a reported 15 percent NVIDIA server price hike, not company guidance, so treat them as directional rather than contracted. But they point the same way, and it is the opposite of relief. Samsung converting yield into share does not mean falling prices even a year out. It means a tighter allocation fight inside a market where 2027 supply is reported to be largely spoken for already. That last point is secondary reporting, not a company filing, so hold it loosely. The honest read is that the constraint is not loosening. It is changing which supplier profits from it.

That pricing structure matters beyond the memory makers, because HBM4 is not sold at one price. Trade reporting from earlier this month put HBM4 costs for NVIDIA's GPUs around 31 to 32 dollars per gigabyte, against roughly 17 to 18 dollars for the outgoing HBM3e generation. That comparison has been on this show before, so take it as the setup. What has not aired is the tier underneath it. The same reporting puts HBM4 costs for other GPU makers and custom ASIC buyers meaningfully higher, in the 35 to 36 dollar range. These are cost estimates from trade research, not disclosed contracts, but the direction is the point. Memory procurement is now its own competitive dimension, separate from chip architecture and from software ecosystem. And right now it runs the same direction as every other advantage NVIDIA already holds.

That tiering lands hardest on the vendor whose pitch depends on carrying more memory per chip. AMD's MI455X, the flagship of its coming Instinct MI400 family, is specified with 432 gigabytes of HBM4 and up to 23.3 terabytes per second of memory bandwidth. That is a genuinely large memory footprint, built for the biggest language and multimodal workloads. If AMD is paying anywhere near that higher non-NVIDIA tier, the premium compounds on exactly the spec AMD is using to differentiate. To be clear, AMD's actual contracted memory pricing is not public, and there is no confirmed unit volume behind the part. AMD's gigawatt-scale commitments are standing context from prior months, not news this week. Anthropic signed for up to two gigawatts of MI450-series GPUs, with the first gigawatt beginning deployment in the first half of 2027. OpenAI's broader six-gigawatt agreement includes a one-gigawatt MI450 buildout. Gigawatts describe power capacity, not chip counts and not revenue. So the conclusion is not that AMD's margins are compressed today. It is that AMD can win the architecture argument and the customer-commitment argument and still be negotiating from behind on the one input that increasingly decides system cost.

Which brings us to what the incumbent is doing with the advantage that procurement power generates. Bloomberg reported this month that NVIDIA is in early talks with Rebellions, a Seongnam-based Korean designer of inference-focused chips, over three possible structures: a technical partnership, an equity investment, or an outright acquisition. Nothing is agreed, the talks are early-stage, and neither company has confirmed anything beyond the reporting. Rebellions has raised roughly 850 million dollars from investors including SK hynix, Samsung Ventures and Arm. It was last valued around 2.3 billion dollars, and it is separately preparing a listing on South Korea's main exchange for late this year or early next. This follows a similar reported approach to Hugging Face that this show covered on Saturday. That is twice in under two weeks that NVIDIA has answered a competitive threat by moving to absorb the adjacent layer rather than out-ship it. Two data points do not make a strategy, but they are worth naming. And the Rebellions angle in particular plants NVIDIA inside the same Korean memory complex, SK hynix and Samsung Ventures, that is setting HBM4 allocation and pricing right now, at the exact moment that allocation is the industry's scarcest resource.

So where this leaves things. Today's clearest numbers are a yield figure no memory maker itself disclosed, a set of per-gigabyte costs no supplier confirmed, and a deal structure nobody has signed. What is solid is the direction. Samsung is genuinely closing ground on SK hynix, and that fight is happening inside a supply pool whose pricing is trending up, not down, into next year. The forward test is whether Samsung's HBM4 shows up as shipped, allocated volume at an accelerator vendor, because share moving inside a duopoly changes who profits long before it changes what anyone actually pays.

That's the briefing for August 31st. Have a good day.
