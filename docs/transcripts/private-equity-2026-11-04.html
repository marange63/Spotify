Private Equity & the Deal Economy

Good morning, this is Cautious Optimism Briefings for Wednesday, November 4th. Here's the state of the private-equity machine.

Apollo's third-quarter print landed yesterday, and it's the test this desk flagged in each of our last three episodes. In yesterday's briefing we said to read it for realizations, not fee growth. The answer is that both showed up, and they have to be read separately.

Start with the fee-growth side, because the records are real. Origination hit a new high of $114 billion in the quarter, per Apollo's release. Over the trailing twelve months, that activity exceeded $350 billion. Origination is lending, so these are overwhelmingly credit numbers, not buyout numbers. They tell you about deal-financing capacity, not about private-equity exits.

The capital-gathering numbers point the same way. Clients entrusted Apollo with $270 billion of inflows over those same twelve months. Total assets under management now sit at roughly $1.07 trillion. Neither figure is performance, and neither is cash returned to anyone.

Apollo's dry powder confirms the tilt. The release puts it at $82 billion, and says approximately 65 percent of it sits in credit. So the asset-gathering engine at Apollo is healthy, but it's mostly a credit engine wearing an alternative-manager name tag.

Now the realization side, which is the part that actually answers yesterday's question. Realized performance fees came in at $362 million for the quarter. That's up about 80 percent from a year earlier, and nearly triple the $130 million booked in the second quarter. Apollo's release says those fees reflect realizations from private equity. That complicates the purely skeptical read. Real private-equity monetization showed up this quarter, not just fees and inflows.

One caveat keeps that honest. Realized performance fees are Apollo's share of the gains, not a measure of what its limited partners received. Carry generally crystallizes when exits return cash, so the line implies distributions did happen. But we found no figure for cash returned to limited partners, and that's the number allocators actually need.

Next comes the split between the metric managers prefer and the one accountants require. Apollo reported record fee-related earnings of $802 million. On a GAAP basis, diluted earnings per share swung to a loss of $1.78, from a gain of $2.15 last quarter. Apollo attributes that swing primarily to unrealized mark-to-market losses on certain Retirement Services investments, resulting from higher interest rates.

That deserves skepticism in both directions. It's a real headline loss. But it's a paper mark on the insurance balance sheet. It isn't a cash loss to the firm or its limited partners, and it can reverse as rates move. There's an irony here too. The one place marks visibly hit Apollo's results is where they track interest rates, not where private-equity holdings are valued by model. The market may be over-penalizing a number that says more about rate sensitivity than about the franchise.

The deeper point is who bears the cost of a slow exit market. When a manager's growth runs on origination and inflows, its fee earnings decouple from the exit cycle. Apollo's fee line set a record in the same quarter its GAAP line went negative. If exits stall again, that fee base keeps compounding. The cost of waiting falls on limited partners holding paper marks, not on the manager.

So what does the Apollo print tell us? The capital-gathering machine is unambiguously strong, and it's a credit story. But for the first time this week, we also have a quoted signal that private-equity realizations contributed to a quarter. That's one manager and one quarter. It shouldn't be read as the exit logjam clearing economy-wide. But it's the first real data point cutting against the capital-in, no-cash-out framing we've been running.

Apollo isn't only gathering capital this week. It's also hunting for places to put it. A market-news aggregator reports that Universal Music Group held preliminary, non-process talks with Apollo, TPG and Blackstone about a take-private. No valuation is attached. And preliminary, non-process is the weakest tier of deal news. It may say more about sponsor appetite for trophy assets than about an executable transaction.

It does extend a pattern. At the end of October, funds managed by Apollo affiliates supplied all the financing commitments for Thrive Holdings' take-private of ADT. Now Apollo turns up as a suitor for a trophy asset. Record origination capacity suggests megadeal financing can come from a manager's own vehicles rather than a bank syndicate. That makes take-private ambitions more feasible. It also concentrates single-name risk inside those vehicles, where the manager's investors hold it. So the arc across the week is Apollo as the balance sheet behind deals, not just the fundraiser.

Meanwhile, the reported Clayton Dubilier and Rice and GTCR approach for Global Payments still has no signed agreement or regulatory filing that we can find. Both situations are capital going in, not cash coming out. That's the distinction that matters for limited partners waiting on distributions.

The capital to do those deals keeps arriving, but not evenly. Two details from Blackstone's October 22nd earnings call haven't made it into this briefing yet, and they sharpen the picture. Both come from a published call transcript. First, Blackstone's energy transition fund hit its $8.7 billion hard cap after quarter-end. Blackstone says that's the fourth major drawdown fund at the firm to hit its hard cap this year. Set that beside Advent's flagship close last week, and Blue Owl's warning that its GP Stakes fund's final close may slip into next year. Fundraising strength is concentrating at the very top of the manager hierarchy, and even there, clearing is uneven.

The second detail matters more for exits. Blackstone's dedicated secondaries flagship has raised over $16 billion toward a target of at least $22 billion. That's a raise in progress, not a close. But it is refilling buyer capacity for continuation vehicles at the top of the market, just after we flagged yesterday that secondaries dry powder was shrinking. The trade-off is real. More secondaries capital makes GP-led liquidity easier to execute. But GP-controlled continuation vehicles are the weakest form of price discovery we have. So easier liquidity can defer the test of today's marks rather than pass it.

Pulling the threads together. The fundraising machine is healthy and concentrating at the top, and fee earnings are compounding whether or not exits arrive. The exit-and-distribution logjam is still the binding risk for the asset class broadly. But Apollo's quarter put a real, quoted number behind the idea that realizations can thaw selectively before the broader logjam clears. Where's the market complacent? In reading record inflows at the giants as proof the cycle is healthy. Where might it be over-discounting? Possibly in Apollo's rate-driven GAAP loss.

Now the catalysts. Watch Carlyle's results, expected this week. Watch KKR's too, and here's a correction. We've previously given you November 9th as a firm date. Sources actually disagree between this Friday and next Monday, so don't hold us to either. Watch whether Blackstone's promised fourth-quarter realization step-up actually shows up in its January print. And watch whether Global Payments or Universal Music Group turns from reported talks into a signed deal. Those will tell us whether this week's thaw was real, or one manager's good quarter.
