Good morning. It's Saturday, October 24th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

On October 17th, we told you to watch for one thing: any chip supplier, anywhere, trimming a forecast. Yesterday we said none had. That turns out to need a correction, and not the kind you'd expect. Lam Research reported on October 21st, and it held back part of its own guidance. The reason it gave was supply, not demand. The bottleneck in AI infrastructure has now reached the companies that build the machines that build the chips.

Start with what Lam actually reported. Fiscal first-quarter revenue was a record eight point two two billion dollars. Non-GAAP earnings per share came in at two dollars twenty, and both beat estimates, according to 24/7 Wall St. For the December quarter, Lam guided to eight point six billion dollars, plus or minus four hundred million.

Here's the line that matters. On the call, CFO Doug Bettinger said that guide "would have been a bit higher if not for some near-term supply chain constraints." He described this year as "primarily clean room constrained." Analysts pressed him on which components or suppliers were involved. His answer was, "I'm not going to get into specifically where it is, except I would tell you that we're managing it, just like we always do." He added that a lot of Lam's suppliers are well-capitalized and can manage this on their own financially. And on duration, management said things are going to continue to be constrained into next year by the same factors.

The most useful detail is about timing. Bettinger said the shipments held back from December aren't lost. In his words, "we will ship that in the March quarter." So read the midpoint correctly. Eight point six billion against the eight point two two billion just reported is still sequential growth. This is a cap on upside, not a cut, and not a contraction.

Lam also put a bigger number on demand. CEO Tim Archer said calendar 2026 wafer fab equipment spending is tracking in the "mid one fifty billion range." Lam's preliminary view of 2027 is "more than two hundred billion dollars" of spending. Bettinger called that "our initial starting point," to be refined later. That's roughly thirty percent more spending next year than this year, on Lam's own estimate. So the constraint is sitting on top of rising demand, not falling demand.

Now the skeptical turn, because it deserves real weight. "Supply chain constraints" is the most flattering possible explanation for a guide that underwhelmed. Management chose that framing. It refused to name the bottlenecked part. And the duration claim is Lam's own characterization. At least one secondary report described the constraint as resolving in the near term, which conflicts with what management said on the call. We also couldn't confirm from a named outlet whether the guide landed above or below Street consensus. So the competing reading stands: this could be an ordinary deceleration after a record quarter, dressed in the least damaging language available.

What tilts the evidence is the combination. A company softening its tone to hide weak demand doesn't usually name a specific quarter for the deferred shipments. It also doesn't usually hand you a 2027 number above two hundred billion dollars in the same breath. That doesn't settle the question. But either way, this is the first guide in the cycle to name the tool tier itself as a bottleneck.

The market didn't wait for that nuance. Lam closed down ten point two six percent on October 22nd, at two hundred thirty four dollars and six cents, according to the Korean outlet JKN. Applied Materials and KLA were reported down sharply in afternoon trading too, but Lam's is the only confirmed closing move we have. That selloff lands on a sector already down more than twenty percent from its June peak, as we reported on Thursday.

Here's the tension worth naming. The tape took a supply-side data point and priced it like a demand-side warning. Mark this as inference, because we have no backlog figure. If shipments are deferred to March, and Lam's 2027 demand outlook is above this year's, then revenue is moving between quarters, not vanishing. Extrapolating a cyclical top from one capped guide may be the wrong read. For equipment-maker valuations, the trade looks like less near-term upside in exchange for potentially longer visibility.

Now to the buyer of those same tools, TSMC. Through the first nine months of the year, TSMC's capital spending totaled forty point six four billion dollars. The full-year target is sixty to sixty-four billion. Subtract the nine-month total from each end of that range, and the implied fourth quarter is roughly nineteen to twenty-three billion dollars. That's a residual from the guidance, not a disclosed quarterly plan. But it's a sharp step up from the thirteen point eight four billion TSMC spent in the third quarter alone.

To be fair to TSMC, its capex often runs back-loaded, so a big fourth quarter isn't unusual by itself. The point is the timing. That spending lands in the same quarter Lam says it can't ship everything it could. On its call, TSMC management also said capital spending from 2026 through 2028 will be "even more significantly higher" than the prior three years. It still won't put a number on it. We flagged on October 18th that TSMC hadn't given a 2027 capex figure. It still hasn't.

Put Lam and TSMC together, and you get what we'd call the conversion gap. Approved capex dollars and installed, running capacity are two different things. This next part is inference, because nothing in the record ties Lam's constraint to TSMC's orders specifically. But if the toolmakers are short on cleanroom space and unnamed components into next year, the gap between a capex budget and a working fab can widen without anyone cutting a check. Leading-edge capacity would then stay scarce for longer, even where budgets are approved. And pricing power would stay with TSMC.

ASML's numbers point the same way. Its SEC filing shows third-quarter net sales of eleven point seven billion euros, with the fourth quarter guided to fourteen point two to fifteen point two billion. That's a big sequential step up, on top of the supply shortfall ASML flagged for 2027 and 2028, which we covered last week. The toolmakers are guiding higher, and still describing demand they can't fully serve.

One brief counterweight, on the demand side. SpaceX is reportedly in talks to raise around forty billion dollars in debt to buy Nvidia chips. The Financial Times reported the split as ten billion in bank loans and thirty billion in investment-grade bonds. Bloomberg calls the talks early-stage, and says they could end without a deal. Neither company has commented. Yesterday we described vendor balance sheets as the financing layer of this buildout. The new wrinkle is that Nvidia already holds a stake in SpaceX worth roughly twenty-one billion dollars. So the vendor holds equity in the leveraged buyer. When capacity is capped at several tiers at once, scarcity tends to favor whoever will borrow or prepay to get to the front of the queue. That raises the credit stakes under this entire buildout.

So where does that leave the picture? The binding constraint in AI infrastructure keeps migrating up the supply chain. It was wafers and advanced packaging. Yesterday it was substrates, interposers, test and optics, with Nvidia paying to expand them. Now it has reached the equipment makers' own cleanroom space and component suppliers. That argues against treating any single choke point, whether CoWoS or HBM, as the whole story. Today's evidence leans toward a supply-capped cycle rather than a demand slowdown. The company closest to the problem put a bigger number on next year's demand while capping its own near-term guide. What would change that read is simple. Watch for a named component, a named supplier, or a March quarter where the deferred shipments don't show up.

The watch list for the week ahead. SK hynix reports October 28th. Samsung's divisional breakdown and Intel both land October 29th. For Intel, the real question is whether Intel Foundry can finally name an outside leading-edge customer. AMD reports November 3rd. And keep an ear out for any toolmaker willing to name the part that's actually constrained, and for TSMC's first real 2027 capex number.
