Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Credit & the Corporate Credit Cycle

Transcript · November 20, 2026

Good morning. It's Friday, November 20th, and this is your private credit briefing.

The price this desk has been waiting on all week didn't come from the ING data-center auction. It came from Fullscript, the hung loan that private credit took off Morgan Stanley's hands.

A quick callback, since we've tracked this since syndication stalled. L Catterton and co-investor Atlas Partners needed $1.3 billion to fund their buyout of the healthcare platform Fullscript. A Morgan Stanley-led bank group underwrote it, and the syndicated loan market couldn't find enough demand to clear it. On Wednesday this desk said the final terms hadn't been disclosed. In fact, Bloomberg's account of the close, dated the 17th and citing people familiar with the deal, carried them.

Here they are. Direct lenders Antares Capital, Blue Owl, Carlyle and Golub Capital took the $1.3 billion at 400 basis points over SOFR, issued at 96 cents on the dollar. Morgan Stanley kept a $230 million revolver for itself. How the four lenders split the loan isn't disclosed. A follow-up check adds one more piece. According to a Seeking Alpha report, the banks' original commitment capped pricing at four points over the benchmark rate. So the spread landed exactly at the banks' own ceiling.

That ceiling explains the form the compensation took. The spread went to the cap and no further. Whatever more the lenders wanted came as discount. Original-issue discount front-loads a lender's return without moving the number everyone quotes. On this desk's own arithmetic, assuming a three-to-four-year life, four points of discount is worth roughly 100 to 130 basis points a year on top of the spread. That's an assumption, not a reported yield.

Two things we still don't know. The first is whether the 96-cent price sat inside or outside the banks' original flex. That decides who absorbed the four points. It was either the banks, through fees or losses, or the direct lenders demanding it at issuance. The second unknown is a clean benchmark for where comparable syndicated or unitranche paper is clearing right now. Neither is public. So I won't tell you whether four points up front is rich or cheap. And I won't tell you the banks escaped clean, because avoiding a hung loan is not the same as avoiding a loss.

The practical test comes next quarter. Whichever listed BDCs sit on those four lenders' platforms and hold a piece of Fullscript will have to carry it in their fourth-quarter schedules of investments. Which vehicles hold it isn't disclosed, so that part is inference. But the market now has a price at origination to check those marks against. One deal is a data point, not a market-clearing level. Still, the takeaway is that bank-to-private substitution now has a printed price, and that price is waiting to be marked.

If private lenders are going to keep catching paper like this, the next question is how much capital is lined up to do it. BlackRock's HPS Investment Partners began marketing Specialty Loan Fund VII this week. It is targeting $9.5 billion of initial investor commitments, with a first close aimed for the first quarter of 2027. The predecessor, Specialty Loan Fund VI, closed in 2024 at $21.1 billion. Don't read that as demand halving. The $9.5 billion is an opening target, and the $21.1 billion was the predecessor's final close. Those numbers describe different stages of the same process.

The fund is pitching a 10% yield target over three years, lending to companies with at least $75 million of annual EBITDA. That 10% isn't disclosed as gross or net of losses and fees, so treat it as a sales pitch, not a return. The real question is deployment. A $75-million-plus EBITDA floor puts this fund squarely in the upper middle market. That's the same segment where, as this desk reported on Monday, banks have been winning deals back on price. Large fresh commitments landing in a contested segment push on terms long before that pressure shows up in a default rate. There's a quieter risk too, and it's this desk's inference. If deep private funds reliably catch what syndication can't clear, banks may start underwriting as if that backstop were guaranteed. So watch covenants and leverage on HPS's next few deals, not the fundraising headline.

Behind the funds sits the other source of patient capital: the insurance balance sheet. A recent S&P Global Market Intelligence analysis, as relayed in secondary coverage, found Bermuda's life and annuity reinsurers holding an estimated $435.7 billion in private credit. Athene, Apollo's insurance arm, is the single largest holder, at roughly $74 billion. That's about a sixth of the total.

Here's the detail that complicates the easy story. Direct loans make up only about $9.35 billion of that book. The biggest category is corporate private placements, at about $120.9 billion. Private asset-backed securities follow at about $70.3 billion. So the offshore annuity money isn't mostly bankrolling sponsor direct lending. Most of it sits in placements and asset-backed paper.

Then the valuation point, stated honestly. S&P found 60.1% of these holdings valued with Level 3, model-based inputs, against just 3.6% for public credit. That is not proof the marks are wrong. Private placements and private ABS land in Level 3 by construction, because there is no observable trading price. The real issue is that most of a book this size has never been tested by a market. That fits the Fed naming life insurers among the holders of the riskier AI-buildout debt slices, which this desk covered yesterday. So the biggest pool of offshore insurance credit is mostly not in direct loans, and most of it carries a model mark rather than a market one.

Which is exactly where supervisors are now pointing. The ECB published its 2027-to-2029 supervisory priorities yesterday. They include a new campaign of on-site inspections covering bank fund financing, asset-based lending, and exposures to private equity, private credit and tangible-asset funds. That follows an earlier ECB exercise that found concentrated exposures to below-investment-grade counterparties. No exposure figures or inspection timeline were disclosed, and these are supervisory priorities, not binding rules.

ECB supervisory chief Claudia Buch said euro-area banks' direct exposures are relatively small. That sits in real tension with launching a campaign, and there are a few ways to read it. One is pre-emptive supervision, getting in before the exposures grow. Another is that the worry is indirect, running through fund financing rather than direct loans. A third is that Frankfurt is matching the attention already coming from the Fed and the Financial Stability Board. The named targets fit the second reading best.

Compare the Fed's approach. The Dallas and New York Feds ran a voluntary, explicitly non-supervisory pilot survey of the private credit market, and it closed on Wednesday. Aggregate findings aren't due until the first quarter of next year. One side of the Atlantic is still collecting data by request. The other just committed to walking into the banks and looking.

Here's why that matters for everything above, and it's this desk's inference about mechanism, not a prediction. Fund financing is the plumbing that connects banks to the direct lenders, including the four who just took Fullscript. If inspections tighten the NAV and subscription lines those lenders borrow against, their cost of leverage rises. That pressure reaches the exact bid that absorbed Fullscript's hung loan. Regulators have moved from asking questions to going in to look.

One more line on the item that didn't deliver this week. Bids were due Thursday in ING's roughly $500 million data-center loan auction, the test this desk has flagged as an early public price on AI-linked credit. No clearing price has surfaced as of this recording, so that test is still outstanding. No speculation on where it lands.

So where does the cycle actually stand? Substitution from banks to direct lenders is clearing, and this week it cleared at a visible price. That's new information. There's no default turn in anything we've seen this week. The unresolved risk is what sits underneath. Paper acquired at a discount is landing in fund books, beside a far larger insurance book that is mostly marked to model. And the lenders doing the buying lean partly on bank lines that supervisors now intend to inspect. Watch four things. First, the ING result, whenever it surfaces. Second, Fullscript's fair value in fourth-quarter BDC filings. Third, whether HPS VII's first close lands anywhere near its $9.5 billion target. Fourth, the Fed survey findings due in the first quarter, and whatever the ECB inspections turn up.

That's the lending machine for November 20th. Stay cautiously optimistic, and have a good weekend.
