Good morning. It's Tuesday, October 20th, and this is your digital money briefing.

Bitcoin starts the week down about 8 percent on the year, and more than 35 percent below its roughly 126,000-dollar record from last October. Traders are still paying to be long it. Funding on bitcoin perpetuals ran at 0.01 percent every eight hours on Saturday, the highest among the major tokens. So today's question is whether anyone actually wants to own bitcoin as a hedge, rather than rent it with leverage.

Since October 13th, this desk has argued that bitcoin trades as rate-sensitive risk beta, not digital gold. Last Thursday brought the strongest evidence yet against that view, from the derivatives research firm Block Scholes. Its data puts bitcoin's 90-day rolling correlation with gold at 51 percent as of October 15th. That's the highest level since November 2020. The relationship actually peaked at 55 percent in mid-September, which Block Scholes calls the highest reading in its historical dataset. One sourcing note covers today's episode. Most of these figures reach us through press accounts rather than original documents, so treat them as directional.

The other half of the Block Scholes picture matters more for allocators. Bitcoin's correlation with the S&P 500 and the Nasdaq-100 has weakened to roughly 40 percent this month, down from 62 percent in February. And since mid-August, bitcoin has tracked gold more closely than US stocks. For a cross-asset book, the fading equity link is the consequential part. Diversification value comes from what an asset stops moving with, not from what it starts to resemble. So let's say it plainly. On the slow statistics, our rate-beta framing looks weaker today than it did a week ago.

Before anyone calls that a regime change, Block Scholes itself won't. The firm describes an emerging relationship rather than a definitive change in bitcoin's investment characteristics. And it says outright that the findings do not show bitcoin has replaced gold as a safe haven. That caution is the key to reconciling this data with what we've reported. A 90-day correlation of daily returns and a single-event reaction measure different things. Last Wednesday, September's CPI print came in a tenth of a point hot. Implied odds of an October 28th rate hike roughly doubled within hours, and bitcoin sold off below 81,000 dollars. That was rate-sensitive risk behavior, and it happened inside the same window that produced the gold correlation. Both can be true at once. Bitcoin may be drifting gold-ward on slow statistics while still trading like leveraged risk when a macro shock lands.

Correlation is also not protection. Go back to the number we opened with. Bitcoin is down about 8 percent this year, while gold-like co-movement was supposedly building. That co-movement has not made anyone who held bitcoin this year whole. So the hedge label, for now, describes a statistic, not a P&L.

There's also a simpler explanation for the gold correlation, and it's a shared macro driver. Consider the one clean same-window price comparison available. In the week after August 19th, when the US Treasury expanded its bond buybacks, bitcoin gained more than 20 percent. That was its largest weekly advance since March 2024. Gold rose about 5 percent over the same week. Both rallied together on a liquidity catalyst. That's consistent with a common driver, not necessarily hedge demand flowing into bitcoin. CryptoQuant data, on a different window, adds a humbling footnote. The dollar, equities and gold together explain only about a third of bitcoin's daily moves. So most of what bitcoin does still isn't captured by any of these correlations.

If a gold-style allocator base were actually arriving, the ETF wrapper is where you'd expect to see it. Instead, US spot bitcoin ETFs are net sellers for October. TFTC's tracker puts net outflows at about 617 million dollars month to date, through October 16th. That reverses the steady accumulation story bulls leaned on for most of the year. And the selling has spread beyond bitcoin. Solana spot ETFs posted their tenth straight trading day of net outflows on October 16th. Taken together, the statistics have moved toward a hedge profile, and the money hasn't followed. No new owner has shown up to buy the correlation shift.

The bull case deserves its hearing here. A sustained gold-like correlation could eventually draw a different buyer base. That means macro and sovereign allocators, rather than the equity-correlated risk-on crowd. That kind of buyer moves slowly, and it wouldn't show up in two weeks of flows. But it would need custody it trusts. And that plumbing moved last week, administratively rather than legislatively.

The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency reviewed nine large national banks, covering 2020 through September 2025. It found the banks had restricted digital-asset businesses, often citing financial-crime concerns. That's backward-looking evidence of friction. It doesn't by itself change any bank's current policy. But it's the backdrop for a charter queue that's getting more interesting.

On October 16th, the payments fintech Ramp applied to the OCC for a national trust bank charter. The trust bank would offer digital-asset custody and fiduciary investment management, explicitly without taking deposits or making loans. An application is not an approval, and this one is pending. The pattern is still instructive. What's being built is custody without a balance sheet. A trust charter puts crypto custody on federally supervised rails. It doesn't deliver the deposit and payments access that the debanking review found banks restricting. And the applicant is a payments fintech, not a crypto native. That convergence is the signal worth watching. So the institutional on-ramp is real, but narrower than the headline suggests.

The same gap between regulatory comfort and actual demand shows up in the dollar layer underneath. Total stablecoin supply sits around 302 billion dollars, and Tether and Circle together hold about 85 percent of it. Over the past 30 days, Tether's USDT edged up 0.09 percent, while Circle's USDC fell 1.43 percent. Those are small moves, and the float as a whole is flat. But the direction matters for this story. Circle is the bank-adjacent, US-regulated issuer, and Tether is the offshore one. If federally supervised rails were what users prized most, you'd expect USDC to be gaining share. Instead it's slipping. That suggests distribution and offshore demand still drive stablecoin share more than US regulatory comfort does. And a flat float means stablecoins aren't adding to Treasury bill demand right now, a point from yesterday that still holds. So the onshore plumbing hasn't yet pulled the dollar layer toward it.

So where does that leave the thesis? For allocators, if bitcoin's equity correlation keeps fading while its gold correlation holds, the sizing case shifts. It moves from a risk-budget position toward something closer to a diversifier sleeve. That's an inference from statistics, not a conclusion backed by flows, and October's ETF data argues the other way for now. For custody markets, a federally chartered trust run by a payments fintech would compete with crypto-native custodians for institutional mandates. That would change the counterparty risk allocators underwrite, though it's an implication, not a measured outcome. And for regulation, the institutional stack is advancing through OCC reviews and charter applications while market-structure legislation sits stalled. That makes this build-out more reversible than a statute would be.

The one-line version is this. Bitcoin's statistics are starting to look like gold, its flows and its shock reactions still look like rented risk, and the plumbing for a real hedge buyer is being built through charters, not price.

Watch two things from here. First, whether ETF flows turn as the correlation evidence builds. Second, the Fed's October 28th decision, which hasn't happened yet. It's the next real test of whether bitcoin's rate sensitivity or its gold-like drift wins out.
