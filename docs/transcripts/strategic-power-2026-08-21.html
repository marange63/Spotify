Cautious Optimism Briefings

Strategic Power, Energy, and Institutional Change

Transcript · August 21, 2026

Good morning. It's Friday, August 21st, and this is your strategic power briefing.

Start with a number that should reset how you think about the entire AI power story. Data center developers across the United States have asked utilities for 1,066 gigawatts of electricity. Wood Mackenzie now projects grid operators will actually commit to only about 28 percent of that, roughly 298 gigawatts. The other 768 gigawatts, call it three quarters of the headline number, will never be drawn from the grid.

That's not a one-off estimate. Back in April, Wood Mackenzie ran the same exercise and found a firm-commitment rate of roughly 23 percent. Two reads four months apart, in the same range, is a rate, not noise. And it isn't just one research house. Exelon, whose utilities serve a big slice of PJM territory, has already cut its own data-center interconnection pipeline by nearly 40 percent, down to an 11 gigawatt queue it now considers credible. In Texas, ERCOT is tracking 474 gigawatts of data-center connection requests, more than five times its own record peak demand. And a separate forecaster, Rapidan Energy Group, independently puts realistic commitment at 20 to 30 percent, which brackets Wood Mackenzie's number from an unrelated angle.

The mechanism is simple and entirely rational. A developer files the same project with five utilities across five sites at once, then keeps every option open until one pencils out. Grid operators themselves say they cannot reliably tell which requests are real. So this isn't fraud, it's optionality: filing a request is nearly free, and the developer who files five of them is behaving sensibly. But it means the number the entire AI-power trade has been built on, the gigawatts hyperscalers are said to need, is mostly a free option rather than a commitment.

Now the honest counterweight, because this cuts both ways. Two hundred ninety-eight gigawatts of genuinely committed load is still an enormous amount of new demand for a system that is retiring generation faster than it adds it. Phantom requests also consume real interconnection-queue slots, real engineering studies, and real utility planning attention even when they never energize. And one caveat to state plainly rather than bury: nobody has published Wood Mackenzie's exact test for what counts as committed, whether that's a signed supply agreement, a deposit, or some other financial milestone. Treat 28 percent as a well-corroborated estimate with a methodology question attached, not a precisely measured rate.

Still, it forces a correction to how this beat has framed things all week. We've spent four days arguing that announced power supply is falling behind real demand. Georgia Power's roughly four gigawatts of approved capacity against dozens of gigawatts in proposals. PJM's capacity auction clearing short of its own reliability requirement. TerraPower's reactor that doesn't break ground until 2027. That supply-side gap is real. But the demand half of the equation now needs revising: if only about a quarter of requested load is ever committed, the aggregate scarcity is less severe than the headline gigawatt figures imply, even as the specific, financed projects stay genuinely capacity-constrained.

That distinction is where the money is. The AI-power-scarcity complex — uranium, gas-turbine OEMs, transmission developers, independent power producer equity — has largely been underwritten against headline request volumes, because that's the only number anyone publishes. A 28 percent conversion rate is a materially different underwriting case than a 100 percent one. It doesn't kill the trade; it splits it. The developer with contracted, financed load and dedicated generation behind the meter is worth more than the one selling into the aggregate. The utility that built rate base against a queue full of options is worth less. Expect dispersion within the theme rather than a clean unwind of it.

Which raises the question of who actually verifies the real number, and that's exactly what PJM is now trying to build. We led with this filing on Wednesday: PJM, the largest US grid operator, went to federal regulators on August 13th with a framework creating a Large Load Registry, and in emergencies it would let utilities curtail data centers before cutting off residential customers. The new piece today is what that filing is actually for. PJM attributes 30 of its 32 gigawatts of forecast demand growth through 2030 to data centers. But if Wood Mackenzie's math holds, a chunk of that 30 gigawatts is the same duplicative, non-binding request the phantom-load research describes. PJM may be justifying its own enforcement regime with inputs drawn from the exact measurement problem the regime exists to solve.

The registry, plus a self-supply deadline in June 2027, is essentially an attempt to convert a paper metric into a verified one. Federal regulators haven't acted, and PJM only asked for a 60-day window, so this is a proposal, not a rule. States also keep siting and rate authority, so implementation isn't PJM's to guarantee. But watch what it would do if it survives. The genuinely committed loads get pushed toward behind-the-meter gas and dedicated nuclear, because that's the only way to avoid being first curtailed. The marginal, speculative ones get pushed out of PJM territory or out of existence. Either way the phantom share becomes visible, and Exelon's voluntary 40 percent haircut is the live precedent for what that looks like. The first honest census of American AI power demand is going to come out of an enforcement process, not a forecast.

The same gap, between what a control regime counts and what actually moves, shows up in chip policy too. Reporting this week describes Chinese AI firms renting access to restricted Nvidia chips through cloud data centers in Southeast Asia and the Middle East. It works because US export controls govern the physical transfer of chips, not remote access to them. The specific case: Michael Kratsios, the White House science and technology policy director, has alleged that Moonshot AI reached banned, Blackwell-generation GB300 chips through a facility in Thailand to build its Kimi K3 model, running an internal distillation platform against US models and switching between access methods to avoid detection. Separately, Bloomberg has reported that Moonshot's Kimi runs in part on a 20,000-GPU Nvidia cluster provisioned through Alibaba, which owns 36 percent of the company. That last figure matters because it puts a number on what had been a vaguely described loophole. Moonshot has not publicly responded, so hold the Thailand allegation as an unverified US government claim, not an established fact, and keep it separate from the broader cloud-rental story.

The legislative fix has a name: the Remote Access Security Act, which would extend export controls to cloud-based remote access. It passed the House back in January. It's still stuck in the Senate, with no clear path. That answers the question this briefing left open on Wednesday, when we said closing this loophole would likely require an act of Congress. It does, and Congress hasn't moved. The asymmetry is unpleasant either way. If the bill stays stalled, compute denial keeps degrading at the margin while remaining fully costly to Nvidia's China channel. If it passes, US export-control jurisdiction reaches into offshore cloud capacity itself, which raises compliance risk and cost for Amazon, Microsoft, and Google data center footprints across Southeast Asia and the Gulf. Controls built to count shipments cannot bind a capability that arrives as a rental.

Last point, and it's the cost of getting any of this wrong. The 30-year Treasury yield touched 5.327 percent this week, its highest since June 2007, before the Treasury announced it would at least double the size of its long-bond buyback operations, from two billion to at least four billion dollars per operation, running September 9th through November 4th. The relief was modest: the 30-year fell about eight basis points, the 10-year about five. And Treasury itself calls this a liquidity-support tool, not a change in how much debt it's actually issuing, so the effect may not survive the November refunding.

Why it matters here: that long yield is the discount rate underneath every utility, independent power producer, and transmission project being sized against these inflated gigawatt requests. Overbuilding against demand that's three quarters phantom is survivable when long money costs three percent. Above five, it strands capital. And that cost lands on ratepayers and on IPP equity holders, not on the hyperscaler who filed five duplicate requests and walked away from four of them.

What to watch from here: whether federal regulators act on PJM's filing, and whether any grid operator becomes the first to publish a clean split between requested and actually committed load. That's the number that will finally tell you how big this buildout really is.

That's the briefing. Have a good weekend.
