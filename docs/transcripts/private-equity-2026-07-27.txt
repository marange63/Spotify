Good morning. It's Monday, July 27th, and this is your private equity briefing.

All week the story has been the industry engineering liquidity without engineering actual exits. Today gives us the clearest single data point yet, and it cuts against the narrative the industry wanted: Blackstone's Jersey Mike's IPO isn't pricing as a markup. It's pricing flat to down.

Here's what we know. Jersey Mike's amended its S-1 on July 20, setting a range of $21 to $25 a share across 43.5 million Class A shares. At the midpoint, that implies a post-offering equity value of about $7.3 billion. At the top of the range, roughly $7.9 billion. Blackstone bought its majority stake in November 2024 at an $8 billion valuation. So even the best-case outcome in this range prices the company below what Blackstone paid less than two years ago. And it's a step down from what the market originally expected: earlier reporting had pointed to Jersey Mike's seeking something closer to $10 to $12 billion before this range was set. One commentator put it well: the original ambition has come down, the market hasn't rejected the company, it's asked for a better price.

That's a meaningfully different answer to a question that was open just a few days ago, which was whether this IPO would validate Blackstone's entry mark. It answers that question, and the answer is no, not yet, and maybe not at all. The offering hasn't priced as of this recording, so we don't have a final number. But the range itself is the signal. Public markets are disciplining the ask down toward, or even below, the private valuation a sponsor set at the top of the last cycle.

Why does this matter beyond one sandwich chain? Because it undercuts the template-risk case that looked live a few days ago. If this deal had priced at a premium to the $8 billion entry, other large sponsors sitting on aged, appreciated consumer and franchise assets would have had a green light to run the same play: sell a slice to the public, keep control, bank partial distributions. A flat-to-down print is a weaker advertisement. It suggests public investors are re-rating this vintage of private-equity-marked consumer assets downward relative to the marks sponsors have been carrying them at, not confirming those marks.

There's a structural point underneath this too, separate from the price. However this prices, it is still a partial exit, not a full one. Blackstone and Abu Dhabi Investment Authority are selling secondary shares, and the company itself is selling new primary shares, but Blackstone keeps supervoting control of the business, retaining about 76.5% of the vote after the offering. That's cash back to the fund without giving up the asset or crystallizing a complete mark-to-market realization. It's the same instinct behind the record secondaries volume and the growing NAV-lending market this cycle has leaned on: return some cash, keep the exposure, defer the full reckoning. Jersey Mike's is just the equity-market version of that same move, and now we have a live read on what the market will actually pay for it.

And that matters for the metric limited partners actually use. Distributions-to-paid-in doesn't distinguish clean, whole-asset cash from partial, control-retained monetizations. So when a slice-sale IPO like this gets booked as a distribution, headline DPI ticks up while the fund still owns the business and still carries the mark. The same drift we flagged on the secondaries and NAV-loan side is now visible on the equity side: the number LPs lean on for their re-up decisions is quietly getting noisier, crediting engineered liquidity as if it were a full realization.

So the honest read on this one is: watch the actual print, not the range. If it prices at the bottom, that's confirmation the exit window is reopening only grudgingly, and only at a discount to where private marks currently sit. If underwriters manage to push it toward the top and it still lands under $8 billion, that's still a flat outcome dressed up as a win. Either way, don't let the headline "Blackstone takes Jersey Mike's public" read as proof the exit machine is healthy. The number underneath it says otherwise.

Now, the reason this matters for how you read the rest of the alternative-manager cohort: it's a preview of the test coming next week. Apollo already gave an early, unaudited signal on July 1st, disclosing preliminary Q2 alternative net investment income of about $350 million pre-tax, which it framed as implying a 9% annualized return on its alternative book. Apollo was explicit that this number is unaudited and could change materially once full results close. And keep it in proportion: this is an investment-income read off Apollo's insurance and Athene balance sheet, not a private-equity fund-level realization, so it doesn't speak directly to the cash-to-LPs question. Treat it as a directional appetizer, not a verdict, ahead of the firm's full print on August 4th.

The bigger tests land in the following two weeks. KKR reports on July 30th. Apollo's full Q2 results follow on August 4th. Carlyle reports August 5th, and Carlyle has already disclosed nearly $5 billion of exit transactions it expects to close in coming quarters, alongside roughly 4% portfolio appreciation over the trailing twelve months — though that figure comes from secondary reporting rather than Carlyle's own transcript, so hold it loosely until the firm confirms it directly. Those three prints are the real test of whether the pattern we've been tracking, rising paper marks running well ahead of realized cash to limited partners, is an industry-wide condition or something specific to Blackstone's mix of businesses.

Pull it together and here's where the cycle actually stands. The exit window is reopening, but only at the margin, and overwhelmingly through partial or engineered channels rather than clean, whole-asset cash sales at a premium to entry. Secondaries and GP-led continuation vehicles keep setting records because LPs need liquidity and sponsors would rather sell an asset to a vehicle they still control than sell it outright. NAV lending keeps scaling because it lets funds distribute cash against unrealized marks rather than realized sales. And now the one clean, dated equity-market test we have, Jersey Mike's, is pricing flat to down against Blackstone's own entry valuation, which is the opposite of a triumphant signal.

The binding risk hasn't moved. It's still the gap between what funds are marking their portfolios at and what buyers, public or private, are actually willing to pay in cash today. Every mechanism the industry has built this cycle, secondaries, continuation vehicles, NAV loans, and now partial IPOs with retained control, treats that gap as something to be bridged with structure rather than closed with a real sale. Jersey Mike's pricing is the first hard evidence this month that when a clean market test actually happens, the gap doesn't disappear. It shows up in the price. Keep that frame running into KKR on Thursday, and into Apollo and Carlyle the following week: the question isn't whether these firms can report growing fee income and rising marks, they can. It's whether real cash to limited partners is finally catching up. So far, it isn't.

That's the briefing for today. Have a good one.
