Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Equity & the Deal Economy

Transcript · October 30, 2026

Good morning. It's Friday, October 30th, and this is your private equity briefing.

We lead with a deal that extends the question we asked yesterday: who is still writing checks now that sponsors have largely stopped buying from each other. On Thursday, October 29th, Thrive Holdings agreed to take ADT private. That makes it the second time the home-security company has gone private in a decade. The price is ten dollars a share in cash. That's a 45.6 percent premium to ADT's unaffected close on October 27th, according to the deal announcement ADT filed with the SEC. It values ADT at roughly 7.6 billion dollars of equity and 15.3 billion dollars of enterprise value. The financing commitments come solely from funds managed by affiliates of Apollo Global Management, and the deal carries no financing condition. This is a signed agreement, not a closed one. A thirty-day go-shop runs through November 28th, and the companies expect to close in the first quarter of 2027, subject to shareholder and antitrust approval.

Yesterday we walked through two ways deals are getting done without a sponsor buying from a sponsor. One was an activist insider, Trian, proposing to roll its stake into a Wendy's take-private. The other was a strategic, McKesson, partnering with Clayton Dubilier and Rice on Option Care Health. ADT adds a third template: a non-traditional buyer, with one manager's funds standing behind the entire financing. Three workarounds in a row tell you deal flow is rerouting around the shut sponsor-to-sponsor channel, not reopening it.

Start with the financing, because it tells you more than the headline price. Apollo is not named as the equity sponsor or the owner. Its disclosed role is providing the financing commitments, full stop. The filing doesn't say how much of that is debt versus equity. It doesn't say whether ADT's existing debt gets assumed or refinanced. We went looking for that breakdown, and it isn't public anywhere. So hold two readings. The first is strength. One manager offering certainty of funds beat the friction of a syndicate, and the seller valued that certainty. The second is less flattering. The syndicated market either wouldn't underwrite these terms or simply wasn't needed, because Apollo has capital in its funds that it wants deployed. The record doesn't tell you which is true. What it does tell you is narrower than the easy headline. This is one private balance sheet choosing to deploy, not proof that large-cap buyout financing has broadly reopened.

Now the arithmetic. Equity value is 7.6 billion dollars against 15.3 billion of enterprise value. So roughly half of what Thrive is paying for is debt rather than equity. That makes this a leveraged bet on a recurring home-security billing stream. Who ends up holding that leverage depends on the financing mix nobody has disclosed. But whatever new money the deal needs is coming from one manager's funds, not a bank syndicate spreading the exposure across dozens of balance sheets. That concentrates the risk of a single large credit on the investors in those Apollo vehicles. If you're an allocator in those funds, that's the question to put to your manager.

There's also a round trip worth naming, and the sourcing now supports it. Apollo took ADT private the first time in 2016, in an all-cash deal valued at approximately 6.9 billion dollars, according to the trade outlet Security Info Watch. ADT later returned to public markets through an IPO. Apollo then sold out of its remaining stake, reportedly in May of this year. So the manager that once owned ADT, and exited through the public market, is now financing someone else's purchase of the same company at a steep premium to where it last traded. One reading is informational edge, since few financiers know this cash flow better. The other is circulation. The same asset is passing back through the same manager's hands, inside the private-markets ecosystem. Both can be true at once.

The buyer is the other unusual piece. Thrive Holdings is a permanent-capital company spun out of Josh Kushner's Thrive Capital venture firm, and it's designed for long-term ownership. That's a different kind of owner from a buyout shop with a ten-year fund clock ticking. A permanent-capital holder doesn't need an exit on a schedule. Who supplies Thrive Holdings' own equity for this purchase hasn't been disclosed. So the cleanest takeaway is structural. The buyer that stepped up is one that has opted out of the exit-and-distribution cycle entirely.

A quick contrast with Wendy's. The Trian group's proposal there, still non-binding, offered roughly a 20 percent premium, with no new debt and the insiders rolling their stake. ADT's premium is more than twice that, paid by an outside buyer with dedicated financing behind it. An insider rolling equity at a modest premium is a different animal from an outsider paying a full control premium. But don't over-read the premium itself. It's measured against a single unaffected close. If ADT had already been de-rated, a big premium can flatter the deal without telling you whether the entry multiple is cheap or rich.

The point to hold onto is simple. A take-private is capital going into private markets, not cash coming back out. ADT's deal returns nothing to private equity limited partners, and it does nothing for the distribution drought this show has tracked since Monday. The near-term test is the go-shop. A topping bid before November 28th would show real competitive tension for large take-privates. Silence would say this is a bespoke, relationship-driven transaction that nobody else is positioned to top.

So if deals like ADT are capital going in, how fast is that capital being raised? The freshest read comes from Ares Management, which reported on Thursday, the catalyst we flagged on Monday and again yesterday. Two figures are worth repeating because they're the ones we can source cleanly. Assets under management topped 690 billion dollars, with perpetual capital surging, per Investing.com's read of the company's results deck. And gross capital commitments in the quarter exceeded 31 billion dollars.

Two caveats before you lean on that. First, Ares is credit-weighted, so this is a read on the fundraising machine, not on buyout realizations. Second, gross commitments are capital promised, not capital deployed, and certainly not capital returned. Growth in assets under management isn't performance either. With those caveats, the signal is still clear. Money keeps arriving at the scaled managers, with perpetual vehicles at the center of the growth story, even while cash going back to limited partners stays scarce. Fee-related earnings compound on that capital whether or not any of it comes back as distributions. That's the bifurcation this show keeps flagging. Manager economics are built on inflows, limited-partner experience is built on cash out, and the two curves are not moving together.

There's a second split inside the first. A strong quarter at a scaled multi-strategy platform tells you little about the middle of the buyout market. On Monday we quoted PwC's warning that managers coming to market without credible distributions face an "existential fundraising challenge." Ares's numbers fit that picture rather than refute it. Capital is concentrating in the biggest brands and their perpetual products, while mid-tier buyout managers fight for re-ups. A healthy headline at the top does not mean a healthy middle. That's where the market may be complacent: reading inflows at the giants as proof the whole fundraising machine is fine.

Put the two stories side by side and a pattern emerges. The largest managers are raising capital at scale. They are committing it to single-name take-privates. And in ADT's case, one of them is financing the return to private ownership of a company it already owned once. None of it involves the arm's-length exit that would validate today's marks. Capital is recycling inside the ecosystem faster than it leaves for limited partners' pockets.

So here's where the cycle stands. Deal-making is alive, but in a narrow, relationship-driven form, increasingly financed by the big managers' own funds rather than a broadly reopened market. The exit and distribution logjam remains the binding constraint, and nothing this week has loosened it. Four dates will confirm or refute that read. Wendy's reports November 5th, and the special committee's response to Trian could come around then. KKR reports November 9th, the cleanest private equity realization read we'll get. The ADT go-shop closes November 28th. And Blackstone has promised a fourth-quarter step-up in realizations, a claim that stays unproven until the cash actually shows up.

That's the briefing. Have a good weekend.
