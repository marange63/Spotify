Good morning. It's Thursday, August 6th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

All week this show has tracked a single fear: that subsidized Chinese memory capacity was about to cap global DRAM pricing. It started with CXMT's Shanghai IPO surge on July 27th, ran through a trillion-dollar-plus chip selloff in late July, and picked up a Senate sanctions letter to Apple on the 4th. Today's news cuts against all of it, and it deserves to lead.

Reporting dated August 5th, from the Chinese business outlet 36Kr, says Apple spent months trying to qualify CXMT as a fourth DRAM supplier for iPhones and Macs, alongside Samsung, SK hynix, and Micron. The talks collapsed. Not because CXMT couldn't deliver volume, but because of price. 36Kr quotes CXMT's own position directly: on its quotation to Apple, "its quotation will not be lower than that of South Korean enterprises, and will even be higher." I want to flag the sourcing plainly, because it matters here: this is unnamed-source, translated Chinese trade reporting, not a filing or an on-the-record statement from Apple, CXMT, Samsung, or SK hynix. Treat it as a strong signal, not a confirmed fact. And it's worth remembering CXMT's roughly 7.7 percent global DRAM share, per its own IPO prospectus, sits almost entirely in legacy consumer memory, not the HBM tier that's driving AI infrastructure margins. This is a commodity-DRAM story, not an AI-memory story.

But if it holds up, it's the clearest evidence yet that the CXMT-as-disruptor thesis was wrong-footed. The market priced in a Chinese producer willing to undercut on price to buy share. Instead, even a buyer with Apple's negotiating leverage couldn't extract a discount. The threat CXMT poses looks like it's capacity and geopolitics, not price. So what actually explains a company with eight-plus billion dollars of fresh IPO capital declining to compete on the one lever new entrants usually pull first?

And here's the consequence for anyone hoping the commodity half of the memory cycle was about to soften. If the cheapest credible new entrant won't undercut the incumbents, the feared price floor under conventional DRAM and NAND doesn't drop this year. The memory cost tax the show tracked all week, from HBM down through server DRAM and into the DDR4 revival on old PCs, stays on. Every downstream device maker keeps paying it into 2027, and the memory oligopoly's pricing power looks more durable, not less.

The mechanism is lithography, and that's the natural handoff to ASML. CXMT doesn't have EUV access, so it's stuck running DUV multi-patterning to hit the same feature sizes Samsung and SK hynix reach in fewer, cheaper steps. That raises its cost per bit and takes price competition off the table, whatever its balance sheet allows. Now, I can't verify the specific magnitude of that disadvantage that's circulating in secondary reporting, so I won't cite a number — just note the direction is real and structural.

And Washington is aiming directly at that gap. A bipartisan bill called the MATCH Act, introduced back on April 2nd, would extend U.S. export restrictions to cover DUV immersion lithography systems, the tools Chinese fabs have kept sourcing even under existing EUV controls. It's a bill, not law, and its odds of passage aren't clear. But the intent is unambiguous: keep CXMT's cost disadvantage open rather than let it close.

There's already a read-through in ASML's own numbers. On its July 15th earnings call, ASML said China's share of its total sales fell from 19 percent in the first quarter to 14 percent in the second. That's a data point, not a collapse — ASML still expects China at roughly 20 percent of full-year sales. But it's moving the direction export-control pressure would predict. Meanwhile the rest of ASML's order book is telling a completely different story: management is guiding net system sales growth above 25 percent in logic and above 75 percent in memory for the full year, as advanced-node logic and HBM investment both pull harder on lithography capacity. The equipment side of the AI buildout isn't slowing down. It's just increasingly gated to customers who can still buy the newest tools.

Now zoom out one more layer, because the same capability moat that's boxing in CXMT shows up again at the top of the stack, at TSMC. On its own July 16th call, TSMC raised full-year 2026 revenue growth guidance to slightly above 40 percent and lifted capital spending guidance to a range of 60 to 64 billion dollars, roughly a 10-billion-dollar increase from its prior plan. It also added another 100 billion dollars to its Arizona buildout, bringing total planned Arizona investment to 265 billion dollars. CEO C.C. Wei described what he called a very big gap between customer demand and TSMC's available leading-edge capacity — language from a company sitting on 40-percent growth and record margins, not one hedging for a slowdown. TSMC's advanced packaging is keeping pace on its own terms: CoWoS yield now tops 98 percent on the company's largest reticle configuration, easing what had been one of the tighter chokepoints in the AI-accelerator supply chain.

Put together, that tells you the binding constraint on the AI buildout isn't chip design, and increasingly it isn't packaging either. It's leading-edge wafer capacity itself, concentrated overwhelmingly in Taiwan. Arizona's 265-billion-dollar commitment strengthens the reshoring narrative, but Arizona fabs still lag Taiwan by generations on ramp timing, so that concentration risk persists for years regardless of how large the U.S. investment gets.

So here's how the week resolves. The late-July selloff repriced a multiple — investors questioning whether AI-memory economics could keep re-rating on a single quarter's shipment-timing miss. It did not break the underlying moat. CXMT's IPO windfall doesn't translate into pricing power without EUV access, and the policy apparatus in Washington is explicitly trying to keep it that way. TSMC's capacity gap says the same story is playing out one level up in logic. If both hold, incumbent pricing power in memory and foundry manufacturing looks durable well into 2027, and the corrections investors have been trading this week are sentiment resets, not thesis breaks. The one caveat worth repeating: today's lead rests on a single translated trade report, not a company disclosure, so watch for confirmation or denial in the coming days.
