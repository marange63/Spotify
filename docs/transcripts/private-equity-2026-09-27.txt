Good morning, it's Sunday, September 27th, and this is your private equity briefing.

We're leading with a personnel move that is really a statement about what a buyout franchise is now worth. Joe Baratta, the longtime face of Blackstone's signature buyout fund, is leaving the firm by year-end after twenty-eight years. He led Blackstone's private equity business for more than a decade. A year ago he shifted into a role overseeing strategies across the whole private equity unit, which now manages roughly four hundred fifty billion dollars. The departure is announced, not yet effective. And Blackstone is not naming a successor, by design. In the firm's own words, "given the strength of the leaders for each of our dedicated PE verticals today, we do not intend to replace Joe's role." Sit with that for a second. A four hundred fifty billion dollar business is being told it doesn't need a single person at the top.

This lands less than three weeks after Blackstone's real-estate chief, Nadeem Meghji, also stepped aside. David Levine and Giovanni Cutaia were named co-heads of real estate in his place, and each has been at the firm for at least twelve years. So two of Blackstone's top vertical leaders have moved on inside a month. Nothing in the record ties either exit to performance. The competing reading is ordinary succession: a twenty-eight-year veteran moved into an oversight role last year, and now that role is being retired. My reading goes a step further, and it is my reading, not Blackstone's stated rationale. The firm is retiring the star-dealmaker model in favor of vertical heads reporting into firm-level governance.

This has been Blackstone's week on this show. On Monday it sat in the three-sponsor consortium lining up for GFL Environmental. The same day, we covered it cutting fees to slow redemptions in one of its Property Partners real-estate funds. Now it is redesigning its own leadership structure. Limited partners weighing re-ups into the flagship buyout funds will ask what this means for deal judgment at the top. No key-person terms are public, so that is a question, not a triggered clause. The so-what is simple. The market increasingly prices these managers on durable fee income and capital formation, not on any one dealmaker's reputation. Blackstone just told you it sees itself the same way.

There's a whole market that prices managers exactly that way. Blue Owl's GP Solutions platform, the former Dyal stakes business, had raised ten point six billion dollars toward its latest GP-stakes fund as of July 30th. That's money committed so far, not a final close, and the number is two months old. But the logic mirrors Blackstone's reshuffle. A GP-stakes buyer underwrites the durability of a manager's fee stream across the whole franchise. It is not betting on any single fund's carry, and it is not betting on any single person. That is a claim, not evidence, because it assumes fee streams outlive the exit logjam. When the listed managers report third-quarter results from mid-to-late October, the split between fee-related earnings and realized carry is exactly where that bet gets tested. Fees are what's being paid for right now. Carry is the part nobody can institutionalize, because carry still depends on getting cash back to limited partners.

And getting cash back runs increasingly through continuation vehicles. That brings us to a question this show left open twice this week. On Wednesday we said we had no pricing data on whether these deals clear at, above, or below the prior mark. Yesterday we said continuation-vehicle pricing still wasn't published reliably. Here is a partial answer. An adviser tally of the first half of 2026 puts the average discount to net asset value on single-asset continuation vehicles at just two point nine percent. One sourcing caution first. Different reports attribute that figure to different advisory firms, and the primary document couldn't be confirmed. So treat it as an industry aggregate, directionally useful, not a precisely attributed print. For scale, Evercore's first-half review puts GP-led secondaries at sixty-five billion dollars. That's fifty-four percent of total secondary volume, the highest GP-led share ever recorded.

Here is what the two point nine percent says. The exit channel doing the most work right now clears within about three points of the seller's own mark. There are two honest readings of that. The first is that pricing gets managed toward the mark, because the sponsor sits on both sides of the negotiation. The second is selection. Single-asset continuation vehicles usually hold a sponsor's best asset, which the lead secondary buyer underwrites individually. A tight discount on a trophy asset may reflect quality, not a thumb on the scale. Two more limits apply. The figure is an average, so it says nothing about dispersion or whether any individual mark was right. And the advisers who publish these tallies earn fees on the volume they count. I can't adjudicate between the two readings with what's in hand.

The so-what holds either way, and it lands on limited partners. Continuation vehicles are becoming a bigger share of what counts as a realization. So reported distribution metrics increasingly reflect a mark the seller helped validate. That is not clean, third-party price discovery. And it is arriving just as limited partners lean on those distribution metrics hardest.

That's the exit side. The entry side is bending the same direction, toward less control, not more. Earlier this month, Apollo-managed funds completed a three billion euro capital solution for Bayer's long-acting contraceptives business. Apollo and KKR are investing equity into a new entity holding that business. Bayer keeps a majority stake and full operational control. This is the far end of a pattern that has run through the week. On Monday it was a three-sponsor club lining up for GFL. On Wednesday it was Advent holding just thirty-seven percent of InPost. On Thursday it was a chief-executive-led buyout of Priority Technology, with Searchlight supplying only part of the equity. Each step ceded a little more control to the target or to partners. With Bayer, two of the largest managers in the industry deploy capital without winning a control auction at all. Note what this is not. It is capital going in, not cash coming back to limited partners.

Why now? PitchBook's private-market dry-powder data says more than forty percent of deployable capital has been available for the past two years. That money is aging inside its investment window. Structures like Bayer's let large managers put it to work without paying a full control premium. The cost sits on the sponsor's own economics. On Wednesday we argued that Advent's thirty-seven percent stake diluted its control over how and when cash comes back. A minority position in a company Bayer controls goes further. It earns fees on deployed capital, but it gives up control of the exit clock. And pressure to deploy aging dry powder is exactly the dynamic behind the 2021-vintage overpayment problem. Watch whether this becomes the default entry shape rather than the exception.

So where does the cycle actually stand? Managers are institutionalizing themselves and monetizing durable fee income. The exits that are supposed to return real cash mostly clear at prices the sponsors helped set. And new capital increasingly goes in without control of the exit clock. The exit-and-distribution logjam remains the binding constraint on the whole asset class. Nothing this week resolves it. If anything, measuring it is getting murkier, because fewer realizations carry a true third-party price. Three things would confirm or refute that read. First, the large alternative managers' third-quarter earnings from mid-to-late October. Watch fee-related earnings against realized carry, and any Blackstone commentary on running private equity without a single chief. Second, the secondary sale Blackstone is arranging in its Property Partners fund, and whether it prints near or below net asset value. Third, whether second-half data shows continuation-vehicle discounts widening as more deals price.
