Good morning, it's Saturday, October 31st, and this is your private credit briefing.

The headline this week is a split screen. The liquidity scare in private credit is easing. The credit stress underneath it just printed a record. That isn't a contradiction. The risk is being relocated rather than resolved, first inside loan books through PIK and extensions, and now off bank balance sheets and onto insurers.

Start with Fitch's third-quarter private credit monitor, out Thursday. According to trade-press reprints of Fitch's commentary, the trailing-twelve-month default rate reached a record 6.3 percent. That's up from 6.0 percent in the second quarter. Fitch counted 27 default events in the quarter.

The composition matters more than the level. Fitch's rate blends two portfolios. The privately monitored ratings book, the names Fitch watches most closely, actually improved, falling to 8.0 percent from 9.4. The entire increase came from the broader model-based portfolio, which rose to a record 5.7 percent from 4.9. So stress isn't intensifying among the names everyone already flags. It is spreading into the wider book that gets less scrutiny. That's the first thing to bank.

Now one caveat, because it changes the framing. The same Fitch commentary describes the quarter-end rate as flat from August. Fitch had already printed a record in its August read. So this is a record level that has persisted for months, not a fresh acceleration. The cycle isn't breaking. It is stuck at a bad reading.

And notice how this sits against what we told you yesterday. On Friday, Proskauer's narrower default index eased for a second straight quarter. Today the broadest agency lens sits at a record. That is the whole week in miniature. On Monday, the KBRA measure cited in Apollo's filing nearly doubled once non-paying loans were included. On Wednesday, Moody's took its reported rate from 4.0 percent to 5.8 once PIK amendments and weak-credit extensions were counted. The broader the definition, the worse the number. And the gap between narrow and broad measures is the signal.

So what is actually driving the Fitch number? Fitch's own answer is that most default events are stressed maturity extensions, interest deferrals, and payment-in-kind introductions. In the third quarter, 13 of the 27 events were deferrals or PIK introductions. Another 10 were stressed extensions. By my count, that leaves only four events in the quarter of any other kind. Over the trailing year, deferrals and PIK alone made up half of all default events. These are soft events. A lender agrees to take less cash today, or to wait longer. They are not missed payments or bankruptcies.

Moody's gives you the scale of the pool those events come from. In the middle-market sample we covered Wednesday, 106 companies were paying interest in kind. That's 17 percent of the borrowers. One caution: that is a count of PIK payers in a CLO-held sample, not the share in distress, and not a market-wide figure. But it's the reservoir. Here is my inference. PIK is the mechanism that lets a broad default measure set records while payment-based measures stay calm. A loan that flips to PIK can count as a Fitch default event and still show up as performing everywhere else.

There's a second-order problem for allocators in all of this. With this much dispersion across methodologies, every manager can cite whichever default rate flatters its book. Cross-manager comparison on reported numbers is close to meaningless. The only consistent lens is an agency-style one that counts PIK and extensions as the events they are.

So does any of that show up in the biggest direct lender's own book? Ares Capital reported its third quarter on Tuesday. Core earnings came in at 46 cents a share, against 47 cents the prior quarter. Net asset value ended at $19.31 a share. On the call, management said nonaccruals were unchanged sequentially at 2.4 percent of cost. At fair value, management said the rate remained well below historical levels.

Taken at face value, the bellwether's book looks clean. But nonaccrual is the narrowest lens there is. A PIK-amended loan can stay on accrual the entire time it is quietly deteriorating. And in my read, Ares Capital's book skews toward larger, sponsor-backed credits, which sit furthest from the model-based portfolio where Fitch is seeing the stress. So flat nonaccruals at the top of the market don't refute a record reading further down. They describe a different neighborhood.

If stress isn't showing up as nonaccruals, it isn't showing up in the redemption queue either. Blue Owl said on its call Thursday that redemption requests at its flagship credit income vehicle, OCIC, fell for a second straight quarter. More than 90 percent of its roughly 90,000 shareholders stayed fully invested.

That's the third read in a week pointing the same way. On Monday we covered the gates, and the reported queue at Blue Owl's technology fund. On Thursday, Stanger's data showed tender requests easing across the NAV BDCs. Today, Blue Owl's largest credit vehicle eased again. One flagship easing is not a sector all-clear, and the tech-fund queue was far higher. Still, the direction on liquidity is clear.

But read it correctly. Calmer queues don't mean safer credit. They mean capital is staying locked in at the same moment reported defaults sit at a record. And fewer forced sellers means fewer forced sales. A real liquidation is the test that would check private marks against a price. That test simply isn't happening. So the stress stays inside the marks instead of surfacing in the flows.

Now the other side of that trade. Inside the funds, risk is being deferred. On bank balance sheets, it is being handed off. And this brings Barclays back for a second day, in a different role. Yesterday it was the Bank of England's inquiry into Barclays' private-credit swap book. Today's story is separate.

Bloomberg reported this week that Barclays is in talks to buy credit-risk insurance on AI-related debt. Barclays is targeting cover for more than half the credit risk on certain AI facilities. Insurance Journal reports HSBC is pursuing a similar structure. And Bloomberg says Deutsche Bank has been working a risk transfer tied to about $2 billion of project-finance debt. These are talks, not signed deals. Size, structure and pricing are all undecided.

Here's the constraint. Bloomberg cites a Willis Towers Watson survey of credit-risk insurers. Several providers said they are "approaching sector limits or are already full on data center exposure." That's a survey of some providers, not an industry-wide capacity measure. And it cuts two ways. It may mark a real ceiling on the cheapest channel. Or it may partly be insurers negotiating for a higher price.

Either way, the pattern is now hard to miss. All week we've flagged the lender concentration on the SpaceX GPU lease, where an Apollo-led syndicate sits on one borrower. Now the banks financing the same buildout want someone else holding the tail. My inference on the second-order effect is this. If insurers really are near capacity, the marginal AI loan has two places to go. It stays on bank balance sheets, which caps how much more banks can lend into the buildout. Or it migrates into private credit and insurance-funded direct lending. That would reinforce the bank-to-nonbank shift this market has lived through all year. And moving risk onto insurers doesn't retire it. It parks the exposure on balance sheets that get far less public scrutiny than a bank's capital ratios.

So where does that leave the cycle? It's a grind with widening dispersion, not yet a turn. Narrow, payment-based measures are calm or easing. Broad, event-inclusive measures sit at records. The biggest unresolved risk is that PIK-sustained loans keep getting counted as performing, marked by the managers who hold them. And they are being marked just as fewer forced sellers exist to test those marks. The real test still sits in the 2027 and 2028 maturities we covered Wednesday.

Watch two things. First, the rest of the earnings wave in the first half of November, with Apollo, Blue Owl's listed BDC, and KKR, as we flagged yesterday. Look at PIK share alongside nonaccruals, because that's where the gap between Fitch and Ares Capital would show up. Second, whether any of these bank-to-insurer AI risk transfers actually sign, and at what price. That price will tell you how much capacity is really left in the system.

That's the briefing. Have a good weekend.
