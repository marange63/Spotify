Good morning. It's Tuesday, August 18th, 2026, and this is your strategic power briefing.

Two days ago, this beat argued that Russia's energy export machine was being damaged less by the West's sanctions bills than by Ukraine's drones — the physical channel outpacing the paper one. This week that thesis escalated fast. Ukraine's General Staff says its forces struck the Gazprom Neftekhim Salavat refining and petrochemical complex in Bashkortostan on Thursday, the fourth Russian refinery hit in three days. That followed an August 10th strike on the Nizhnekamsk oil hub that Ukrainian officials say killed 13 people. This is not an isolated spike. Bloomberg-sourced data shows Russian refineries processed an estimated 3.6 million barrels of crude a day in July, the lowest level since May of 2002, against a normal seasonal range of 5.3 to 5.6 million barrels a day over the prior five years. Eighteen refineries were targeted in July alone, a new monthly record. And the export side looks worse: Russian diesel and gasoil exports crashed to just 80,000 barrels a day in the first week of August, down from roughly a million barrels a day a year earlier — a collapse of about 92 percent. Atlantic Basin diesel margins have hit record highs as that supply disappears from the market. None of this is formally attributed to the specific four-refinery wave this week — the newest verified throughput and export numbers predate it by one to two weeks — but they show exactly the trajectory that wave is extending, not reversing.

So what does this mean. Kinetic strikes are functioning as sanctions enforcement that actually enforces. A 24-year low in refining runs and a near-total wipeout of diesel exports raise the Kremlin's political cost of the war independent of any Treasury designation, and they're pulling more American, Indian, and Middle Eastern diesel into Europe and Africa to fill the gap — reshaping tanker flows and adding to European fuel costs heading into autumn.

Now contrast that with the paper channel aimed at the same target. The Senate passed the Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act 86 to 11 on August 8th, authorizing tariffs up to 100 percent on countries importing Russian oil and gas — squarely aimed at China and India. It's a genuinely bigger hammer than anything sanctions policy has tried so far. But the bill now heads to the House, and a vote won't happen until at least early September because of the congressional recess. The EU's own 21st sanctions package, adopted back in July, froze the Russian oil price-cap adjustment mechanism through 2027 and tightened LNG rules. Both are real. Neither is moving faster than a drone. And there's a second-order tail worth naming on the tariff. If it ever becomes law and gets enforced, the likeliest response from Beijing and New Delhi isn't to stop buying Russian barrels — it's to route more of that trade into non-dollar, non-SWIFT channels, which quietly feeds the de-dollarization story rather than reinforcing the sanction. That's a reason to read the bill as negotiating leverage more than an imminent shock. So the pattern worth banking is this: this week, the physical channel did more damage to Russian energy revenue in days than the legislative channel has managed in months of drafting.

Now pivot from Russia's physical supply squeeze to America's physical demand squeeze, because the same discipline — announced versus delivered — applies on the other side of the ledger. TSMC's July revenue came in at 467.58 billion Taiwan dollars, about 14.5 billion US dollars, up 44.7 percent year on year. That alone would be a strong print. What matters more is that TSMC's board raised full-year 2026 capital expenditure guidance to a range of 60 to 64 billion dollars, a mid-year increase on top of guidance that was already elevated. That's not a forecast or a press release — it's capital actually being committed to physical fab capacity, which is about as close as this industry gets to a lie detector on AI demand. If the compute bottleneck constraining hyperscaler ambitions were easing, capex guidance wouldn't be moving up mid-year. It's moving up. So the AI infrastructure buildout is still accelerating, not plateauing, and every additional wafer of leading-edge logic TSMC ships eventually shows up somewhere as a data-center power draw. That's the handoff: chip capex today is grid and gas-turbine demand eighteen months from now.

There are two tails hanging off that capex signal. The first runs downstream: sustained spending at this pace keeps upward pressure on the equipment suppliers underneath TSMC — the ASMLs and Applied Materials of the world — and on the grid interconnection queues and gas-turbine backlogs that leading-edge fabs feed into. The second is strategic. The more the entire compute stack routes through TSMC's leading-edge capacity, the more Taiwan's centrality to the AI supply chain rises, which raises, not lowers, the Taiwan-risk sensitivity baked into every hyperscaler's roadmap — even as Washington pushes fab reshoring to Arizona. Diversification of production and concentration of demand are moving in opposite directions at once.

Which brings us to the supply-side answer, and its limits. Blue Energy and GE Vernova Hitachi Nuclear Energy announced an agreement last Friday to advance engineering design, licensing, and safety analysis for a 2.5-gigawatt hybrid power plant in Victoria, Texas — roughly one gigawatt from two gas turbines targeted online by 2030, and up to 1.5 gigawatts from as many as five small modular reactors targeted from 2032. This is being framed as the template for how gigawatt-scale AI power actually gets built going forward: gas as an immediate bridge, nuclear layered in later once small modular reactors are licensed and constructed, rather than waiting eight to ten years for a reactor alone. If this model gets replicated, the winners stack up on both ends — GE Vernova and the gas-turbine makers on the bridge, and the small modular reactor developers on the back end, paid twice off the same procurement logic. It's a sensible design. It is also, right now, an agreement to advance engineering — not a construction start, not an operating plant, and not yet a final investment decision, which the companies are targeting for 2027. So the honest read is this: even the fastest nuclear-adjacent path to new baseload power still runs four to six years from today. Near-term AI power growth in the United States stays gas-constrained and grid-constrained, not nuclear-led, for the rest of this decade, no matter how promising the hybrid template looks on paper.

Step back and the same pattern runs through every input this week. In Russia, physical strikes on refineries are outrunning the sanctions bill sitting in House recess. In the AI supply chain, TSMC's committed capital is outrunning the grid capacity still stuck at the engineering-agreement stage. Announced capacity and delivered capacity are pulling apart in both directions at once — one because attacks are moving faster than legislation, the other because chip demand is moving faster than power construction. For markets, that argues for staying skeptical of press releases on both sides: Russian sanctions bills that haven't passed the House, and gigawatt power plants that haven't reached final investment decision. The capital and the drones are what's actually moving. Everything else is still paper.
