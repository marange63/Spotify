Good morning. It's October 16th, 2026. Here's the enterprise technology briefing.

The thesis for today: every layer of the enterprise AI stack just published its own unit of economics. A hyperscaler is selling tokens per megawatt. A lab is pitching quarterly revenue. A CRM vendor is counting agent activity. Each unit flatters whoever invented it. And none of this week's headline numbers comes from an actual filing.

Start at the infrastructure layer, with Microsoft. Microsoft disclosed that Maia 200, its in-house AI chip, is now serving production workloads, including Microsoft Copilot. That's production, not internal testing. On Monday we reported Microsoft steering its own staff toward GitHub Copilot over Claude. This week shows the same vertical-integration instinct one layer down, in the silicon itself.

Microsoft also laid out a roadmap for its next chip, Maia 300. It expects Maia 300 to deliver 40 percent more tokens per megawatt, and up to seven times the rack-scale token throughput of Maia 200. Keep the two tiers of evidence separate. Maia 200 in production is a fact today. The 40 percent and sevenfold figures are Microsoft's own projections, for a chip that doesn't begin production deployment until 2027. Nothing has been independently benchmarked. Microsoft didn't say what share of Azure's inference runs on Maia rather than Nvidia or AMD silicon, and it gave no unit cost.

Why lead with tokens per megawatt? Yesterday we said the binding constraint on AI buildouts had migrated from power and permits to funding. More output per megawatt, from capital you already own, is the hyperscaler's answer to both. That's why Microsoft chose the metric. My inference on the second-order effect: if hyperscalers serve their own flagship workloads on in-house silicon, their cost per token can fall faster than that of neo-clouds renting GPUs on expensive debt. That would tilt enterprise compute bargaining power further toward the hyperscalers.

There are two readings of Maia. One is a real fleet shift away from Nvidia. The other is negotiating leverage, since every hyperscaler with custom silicon still buys GPUs in volume. The evidence so far supports leverage, not displacement. So the so-what: the cost of a token is becoming a hyperscaler's own lever to pull, even if most of Microsoft's fleet stays Nvidia for years.

That's the cost side of a token. Now the revenue side, from a lab that sells them. A standing disclosure: Anthropic makes the model that powers this show, so weigh what follows accordingly. Bloomberg reported that Anthropic told prospective investors its third-quarter revenue hit sixteen point eight billion dollars. Anthropic also reportedly told them it reached profitability in the quarter on an adjusted basis. None of this is confirmed. Anthropic is in a quiet period ahead of a reported IPO and hasn't commented publicly.

Follow-up reporting fills in part of what "adjusted" means. According to TipRanks, executives cited an adjusted operating margin of 11 percent, excluding stock-based compensation. For a lab, that exclusion matters. And separate coverage of the same disclosure cautions that these adjusted figures do not necessarily establish profitability under standard accounting measures.

Now the part that is genuinely unresolved, and I want to flag it plainly rather than paper over it. Multiplying the 16.8 billion dollar quarter by four gives roughly 67 billion dollars a year. Other reports put the year-end annualized rate anywhere from under 100 billion to as high as 120 billion. Those figures cannot all describe the same trajectory. They might rest on different conventions, a quarter's actual sales versus a year-end exit rate, but no source says so, and none bridges them. So don't chain them into one growth story.

There's also the basis question we raised Monday, when we said OpenAI books partner sales net and Anthropic books them gross. Coverage of this disclosure confirms the two labs account differently for sales through cloud partners like Amazon Web Services, which makes direct comparison difficult. But no source we found says how this specific 16.8 billion dollar figure was booked. That stays an open question.

For enterprises, the so-what is this. A profitability claim, however loosely defined, changes the risk calculus on multi-year AI contracts. A handful of labs sit on one side of nearly every enterprise AI contract. That makes a lab's financial durability a procurement variable. An eventual IPO filing will be the first real test of which of these numbers holds up.

Labs report revenue. Application vendors are choosing a different unit entirely. Salesforce announced a 15-year agreement worth up to two billion pounds to become the enterprise CRM provider for HMRC, the UK tax authority, deploying Agentforce alongside its other cloud products. That figure is a ceiling over a decade and a half, not committed spend. Even fully drawn, it works out to roughly 130 million pounds a year. Treat it as a reference win with a regulated government buyer, not a revenue inflection. The durable part is the term. A 15-year contract raises switching costs far beyond a typical SaaS renewal cycle, and it gives Salesforce a template for selling into other sovereign and regulated accounts.

Separately, Salesforce disclosed 7 billion cumulative Agentic Work Units across Agentforce and Slack, with 3.2 billion of them in the second quarter alone. That's Salesforce's own unit of account, the application-layer counterpart to Microsoft's tokens per megawatt. It measures activity, not revenue. There's no external standard, so it doesn't convert into dollars or compare across vendors. And the quarterly jump could reflect real acceleration, or simply what gets counted, with Slack included. Contrast that with the roughly 600 million dollars of AI annual recurring revenue that Workday itself reported. That's a revenue metric, not an activity count.

The pattern across this week is seat-based software giving way to metered, vendor-defined units. Whoever defines the unit sets the denominator of value at renewal time. That's good for Salesforce's pricing power, and bad for buyers trying to compare vendors like for like.

The other place this week's narrative outran the evidence is security. Yesterday we asked whether the unnamed OpenAI cybersecurity acquisition would get a name. When that deal was first reported, on October 14th, Palo Alto Networks shares fell about 4 percent and CrowdStrike about 3 percent. Now the target has a name: Trail of Bits, a security research firm. The two companies have worked together since June on an initiative called Patch the Planet, pairing OpenAI's security models with Trail of Bits engineers. The talks are early and non-binding, and they could fall apart. No price or terms have been reported. The reporting traces to The Information, and neither company has confirmed it.

The name changes the read. The existing relationship suggests OpenAI is buying capability to harden its own models and agents for enterprise sale, not storming endpoint or network security. That's my inference, not a reported fact. On that reading, the selloff in CrowdStrike and Palo Alto Networks targeted the wrong category. The counter-reading is fair, though. Capability bought for internal use today can become a product tomorrow. So if labs do threaten security incumbents, the risk lands first in AI-agent security, not in firewalls. The repricing may be misdirected, or merely early.

The one hard, official signal in security this week also cuts against Palo Alto, and it comes from Palo Alto's own code, not a lab. The company disclosed and patched an actively exploited vulnerability in PAN-OS, its firewall operating system, affecting PA-Series and VM-Series firewalls and Panorama management appliances. Exploitation so far is described as very limited and aimed at the management interface. Still, CISA added it to its known-exploited-vulnerabilities catalog, with an October 17th remediation deadline for federal civilian agencies. That's tomorrow.

This is distinct from the GlobalProtect flaw we covered on Tuesday. That one had been patched for about five months before attackers exploited customers who hadn't applied the fix. On Tuesday the problem was customer patch lag. This time the vendor's own code is the live issue, and that's a more immediate pressure point for Palo Alto's customers than any acquisition rumor.

So, three things to watch as filed numbers start arriving. First, whether Microsoft quantifies Maia's actual share of Azure inference on its next earnings call. Second, whether Anthropic's eventual IPO filing defines "adjusted" and states its revenue basis, settling the gap between the 16.8 billion dollar quarter and the very different annualized figures around it. Third, whether tomorrow's CISA deadline passes without broader PAN-OS exploitation, and whether the Trail of Bits talks produce a deal. Every layer of this stack is grading its own homework right now. Filed numbers are coming.

That's the briefing. Have a good day.
