Cautious Optimism Briefings

Digital Money, Crypto, and Market Structure

Transcript · August 19, 2026

Good morning. It's Wednesday, August 19th, and this is your digital money briefing.

Bitcoin is sitting becalmed near $64,000, and the real question this week isn't the price, it's who is actually buying. Look across every institutional channel at once, and the answer, at the margin, is nobody.

Start with the largest corporate holder. Strategy, formerly MicroStrategy, has now gone eight straight weeks without buying a single Bitcoin. We flagged over the weekend that the company sold coins at a loss to fund preferred-share buybacks. This week the tell is what it didn't do. Strategy still holds 840,447 Bitcoin, acquired for $63.36 billion at an average price of $75,385 each. With Bitcoin near $63,500, that stake is worth roughly $53.4 billion, which puts the company about $10 billion underwater against its own cost basis. Instead of buying the dip, Strategy raised $333.7 million this week by selling 3.46 million shares through its at-the-market program. None of that money went to Bitcoin. Fifty-two million dollars went to preferred dividends, $132 million bought back its STRC preferred shares, and $149 million padded the company's dollar reserve to $4.8 billion.

Before you write this off as the flywheel breaking, a deep dive into Strategy's premium closes an important gap. The company's market value still trades at roughly 1.05 times the value of its Bitcoin holdings, a modest premium, not the discount that would make issuing equity to buy Bitcoin actually dilutive. That premium has been volatile this month, swinging between a small discount and this low premium depending on who's measuring it, so treat it as a moving signal, not a settled number. But at face value, a premium above parity means the buy-Bitcoin-with-equity trade is still technically available to Strategy. It's choosing not to use it. The STRC preferred itself has climbed from around $89 in mid-June, roughly 11% below its $100 par value, to $95.39 this past week, its best level in two months. So the redirected cash is working on its stated goal, defending that preferred stock's par value, and that reads less like a forced unwind and more like a deliberate choice to shore up the balance sheet before resuming purchases. Either way, the largest corporate bid in Bitcoin has left the market for now.

The second channel showing the same story is the ETF wrapper. After a strong start to August, US spot Bitcoin ETFs ran five sessions between August 10th and 14th that together netted $385.2 million in outflows. Then on August 17th, the category posted a $137.3 million inflow, and headlines called it a rebound. Look harder and that inflow recouped only 35.6% of the prior losses, leaving a cumulative six-session deficit of $247.9 million. And that inflow wasn't broad-based: Fidelity's FBTC alone supplied $111.9 million of it, 81.5% of the total. One fund, not a wave of institutional buyers. And that concentration cuts both ways. The same single-fund dominance that inflated the rebound means a redemption shock in one product could pull just as hard in the other direction, moving price out of proportion to any real change in demand. The flow data itself can't tell you who bought, and it can't prove the flows moved Bitcoin's price at all. That's a critical caveat, because the market keeps treating ETF flow headlines as a clean read on institutional demand, and this week's numbers say the opposite: thin, concentrated, and reflexive. A supporting data point from the same window: spot Ether ETFs recorded exactly zero net flow on August 14th, the first zero-flow day for that category in 277 days. The wrapper is trading a range right now, not accumulating.

The third channel is the one that usually absorbs supply in a downturn: long-term holders. On-chain data referenced in VanEck's mid-August research shows Bitcoin's long-term holder supply, coins held over a year, fell about 356,000 coins, or 2.9%, over the past 30 days, down to 11.84 million Bitcoin. That's the first meaningful monthly drawdown from this cohort in months, against a roughly flat average over the prior year. I'll flag two caveats. This figure comes from a single source that couldn't be directly verified beyond a search summary, so hold it a little more loosely than the other numbers today. And there's a confounding factor: a hardware-wallet vulnerability disclosed in July and August, involving over 1,800 stolen Bitcoin, may have pushed some holders to migrate wallets defensively, which can look like distribution on-chain without being an actual sale. Still, even with that caveat, this is the third channel in a row pointing the same direction: even the patient money is trimming.

So that's the throughline today: the corporate treasury bid is paused, the ETF wrapper is fragile and concentrated, and long-term holders are distributing for the first time in months. No single one of those is dramatic on its own. Together, at a flat $64,000 price, they describe a market with no obvious source of fresh marginal demand. And the order book tilts with it. When the biggest corporate holder isn't bidding, the ETF wrapper is churning rather than accumulating, and long-term holders are letting coins go, the path of least resistance at this price is down, not up.

Now the capital-markets bridge: where's the dollar liquidity that would normally step in to backstop a wobble like this? It isn't coming from stablecoins. Total stablecoin supply has plateaued near $308 billion in mid-August, and Tether's USDT has actually contracted by about $4 billion over the past 60 days. That's a continuation of a trend we've flagged before, so the new detail worth banking is this: despite USDT's larger total supply, USDC dominates actual settlement activity, having processed $32 trillion in settlement volume versus USDT's $8 trillion. That gap between raw supply and real usage matters, because a flat-to-shrinking float means stablecoin issuers are buying marginally fewer short-dated Treasury bills to back their reserves, a small but real drag on front-end Treasury demand precisely when nobody's stepping up to buy Bitcoin either.

One closing thread on where institutional energy is actually flowing. Twenty-four of the fifty largest US banks are now tracking tokenized-deposit technology, four with live products, and Wells Fargo joined that group on August 4th. JPMorgan, Citigroup, Bank of America and Wells Fargo are building a shared tokenized-deposit network through The Clearing House, targeting the first half of 2027 for instant, 24/7 settlement. It's explicitly a competitive response to stablecoin payment rails. The catch: a rival stablecoin consortium is set to launch shared payment rails roughly a year ahead of the banks' network. So the banks are racing to build their own rails rather than cede the payments business to USDC and USDT, but on the current timeline, stablecoins get there first. That's the real institutionalization story right now, not the coin itself, but the plumbing underneath it.
