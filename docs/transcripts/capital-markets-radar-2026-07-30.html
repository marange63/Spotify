Transcript · July 30, 2026

Good morning, this is your Capital Markets Radar for July 30th, 2026.

All week this show has flagged two live tails: a genuinely two-sided Fed meeting, and an AI-capex ROI debate waiting on Microsoft and Meta to settle it. Yesterday both fired at once, and the market had cushion for neither. Let's start with the Fed.

The FOMC voted 9 to 3 to hold the federal funds rate at 3.5 to 3.75 percent. That hold was fully priced. What wasn't priced is that all three dissenters wanted a hike, not a cut and not more patience. That's the first time since September 2016 that FOMC dissents have unified in the hawkish direction. Chair Kevin Warsh explicitly rejected the "pause" label, calling the decision instead "a rigorous review of the economic situation." Put those two facts together and the market's question flips overnight: from when does the Fed cut, to is the Fed already behind on inflation.

The bond market answered fast. The 10-year Treasury yield, which had already climbed to around 4.64 percent into the decision, jumped roughly 7 basis points to above 4.67 percent afterward. Equities took the read badly: the S&P 500 fell 1.5 percent, the Nasdaq fell 1.7 percent and closed more than 10 percent below its all-time high, and the Dow dropped over 1,150 points, its worst day since April 2025. The damage wasn't even. Energy and consumer defensive names held up, while industrials fell 3.4 percent and tech dropped 2.4 percent, which is exactly the pattern you'd expect when the long end reprices up and duration gets punished. One caveat worth holding onto: we don't know if this hawkish bloc is a durable shift in the Fed's reaction function or a one-meeting reaction to an oil shock and hot prints, because no dot plot or forward rate path came with it. So what: the term premium just moved higher on three votes, not new guidance, and that's a fragile thing to build a narrative on, but markets are trading it as real for now. And the reach of that is wider than equities. A term premium that grinds higher re-anchors mortgage rates and corporate borrowing costs upward even if the Fed never hikes again, which tightens financial conditions through the back door, no policy move required.

That oil shock is the next link in the chain, and it's not backward-looking. On July 28th, Iran's Revolutionary Guard launched ballistic missiles at U.S. forces, ending a ceasefire lull that had let Brent slide below 90 dollars just the day before. President Trump vowed retaliation, telling reporters Iran is "going to get a beating." Brent crude jumped 7.9 percent to close at 90.74 dollars a barrel, and WTI rose 6.6 percent to 84.46. This matters specifically because of its timing: higher energy prices flow straight into breakevens and consumer inflation expectations, on the same day the Fed's dissent bloc is arguing the central bank is already too slow. So what: oil isn't just a geopolitical story this week, it's the transmission mechanism reinforcing the hawkish read, and whether the U.S. has actually carried out retaliatory strikes remains unconfirmed, so treat this as a risk-premium repricing on a threat, not a confirmed supply disruption yet.

Now to the long-duration side of this, and here's why the Fed story and the AI story are really one story. When the long end reprices higher on an inflation-behind-the-curve fear, the discount rate on every long-dated cash flow moves up with it, and nothing is longer-dated than a data-center buildout that won't earn its return for years. So the same three votes that lifted the term premium yesterday also quietly raised the hurdle rate on the largest capital-spending cycle in corporate history. That's the lens the market brought to the clearest test yet of the AI-capex argument, delivered on the same night by Microsoft and Meta.

Microsoft beat on both lines, revenue of 90.01 billion dollars against 87.62 billion expected, and earnings of 4.74 dollars a share against 4.24 expected. Azure growth accelerated to 43 percent, up from 40 the prior quarter, and Microsoft's commercial remaining performance obligation, its contracted backlog, rose 84 percent year over year to 678 billion dollars, with growth still running at 25 percent even excluding OpenAI. That's demand that isn't riding on a single customer. The stock rose 9 percent after hours, even as Microsoft guided fiscal 2027 capex to 255 to 260 billion dollars.

Meta told a different story on the same night. Revenue of 60.8 billion dollars beat estimates, up 28 percent year over year, but adjusted earnings of 6.18 dollars a share missed the roughly 7.22 dollars expected, hit by 3.58 billion dollars in one-time legal and severance charges and a tax rate that jumped from 11 to 16 percent. Meta also raised the low end of its full-year capex guidance to 130 billion dollars, up from 125, still inside a range that tops out at 145 billion. And free cash flow collapsed to just 784 million dollars, down about 91 percent from 8.5 billion dollars a year earlier. Meta shares fell more than 7 percent after hours. Set that 784 million dollars of quarterly free cash flow against 130 to 145 billion dollars of planned capex this year, and the self-funding math stops working; a gap that size gets filled with debt, which is why Meta's data-center financing plans are now the thing to watch.

So what ties the two together: the market isn't discriminating on how much capex a company spends, it's discriminating on quality. Spend tied to visible, monetizing demand like Azure's backlog gets rewarded even as the dollar figure climbs toward 260 billion; spend paired with margin compression and one-time charges gets punished even when revenue beats. That framework is now the lens for Thursday's Apple and Amazon reports, though we can't yet confirm whether Microsoft's gain and Meta's loss held into the regular session, so hold this as a live test, not a settled verdict.

Last, the cushion question, in a few quick data points. Gold rose just 0.27 percent to 4,048.99 dollars on decision day, barely moving through a missile attack and a hawkish surprise at the same time, which tells you real yields and the dollar are still dominating the safe-haven channel more than geopolitics is. Crypto barely reacted either: about 401 million dollars in liquidations hit the market over 24 hours, mostly on long positions, but that was largely pre-positioning, since markets had already priced a 70.6 percent chance of exactly this hold before it happened. There's a subtler read there too: Bitcoin shrugging off a genuinely hawkish surprise is one more sign it's trading on its own ETF and liquidation flows this cycle, not as a clean liquidity proxy for the Fed path. So what: gold, credit, and crypto were all priced for calm, and none of them had room built in for a hawkish surprise landing the same week as an oil shock. The asymmetry from here runs one way. For a book that came into this week long duration and long the AI trade, the message is that both legs now carry a higher discount rate and less protection than they did on Monday.

That's the Capital Markets Radar for July 30th. Trade safe out there.
