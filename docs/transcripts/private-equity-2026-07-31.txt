Good morning. It's Friday, July 31st, and this is your private equity briefing.

Start with the deal that just cleared its last hurdle. Electronic Arts confirmed in a Form 8-K filed Thursday that every regulatory approval needed for its $55 billion take-private is now in hand, with closing expected on or about August 4th. That's the biggest leveraged buyout in history, built by Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund, Silver Lake, and Jared Kushner's Affinity Partners. We flagged this deal stuck at CFIUS back on Sunday, with the outside date not until late September. Instead, the last approval arrived weeks early, and shareholders are set to collect $210 a share, a roughly 25% premium to where EA traded before the deal was announced. Secondary reporting on the deal has pegged the financing at roughly $36 billion of equity against about $20 billion of debt — call it two-thirds equity, one-third debt — arranged by JPMorgan. I'll flag that split as reported, not filing-confirmed; today's deeper check couldn't pin the exact financing terms to the primary document itself, and it couldn't establish whether the CFIUS clearance came clean or with conditions attached. But directionally the equity share tells you something real. Even on the single largest LBO ever attempted, underwriters would not carry a leverage-heavy structure. That tempers any story about financing markets having fully reopened for scale take-privates. And here's the so-what on the deal itself: this is the entry side of the business working. It's a sovereign-anchored consortium putting capital to work, not a single dollar of cash going back to any limited partner. Keep that distinction in mind, because it's the spine of everything else today.

Now the fee machine — because that's working too. KKR reported yesterday morning, and Blackstone's numbers landed last week. Yesterday's briefing had to hedge on KKR's figures because the release wasn't fully indexed yet. Those numbers are confirmed now. KKR's fee-related earnings rose 34% year over year to $1.32 per adjusted share, with a 70% margin this quarter and a streak above 65% for a tenth straight quarter — that's about as close to a printing press as an asset manager gets. KKR also disclosed realized performance income of $848 million, which management called its largest monetization quarter in the firm's history, and the firm has now raised $305 billion since the start of 2024, beating its own three-year fundraising target roughly six months early. Blackstone's fee-related earnings climbed 22% to $1.78 billion, distributable earnings rose 26% to $1.52 a share, and the firm logged $31.8 billion of monetization activity in the quarter — including a data-center stake sale to Digital Realty and a controlling stake in Sabre Industries sold to TPG — on nearly $1.35 trillion of total assets under management. Read that as dispersion, not a cleared cycle. Fee-related earnings ride assets under management and don't care what the exit market is doing. And even the realized carry here leaned on strategic and infrastructure buyers that only the very largest managers can reach. A record quarter at KKR and Blackstone is not proof the carry cycle has turned for the median general partner, and it says nothing about firms further down the league table.

Which brings us to the part of the cycle that's still frozen. This show has already covered the exit collapse in detail — total US PE exit value down 46% quarter over quarter, sponsor-to-sponsor sales at a decade-low deal count — so I won't relitigate the numbers. The one thing worth restating is where the slack went: public listings, which took a much larger share of a much smaller pie, are the only channel picking up. Strategic buyers and other sponsors, the two routes that actually clear inventory in volume, both seized at once. That's exactly why record fee earnings at the top of the industry don't settle the argument. The exit engine that would turn paper marks into cash for limited partners is still not running for anyone outside the top handful of franchises.

So how does the industry paper over that gap. Continuation vehicles. Secondary market volume is tracking toward a third straight record year, and the mainstreaming stat worth banking is this: 83% of the top 100 global buyout sponsors have now completed at least one continuation vehicle. That's no longer a niche liquidity tool for distressed vintages — it's default infrastructure for extending fund life while still handing existing limited partners some cash. The catch is that a continuation-vehicle sale is typically GP-led, single-buyer, and priced by an advisor the general partner itself hires. The same firm sits on both sides of the trade. It lifts the reported distributions-to-paid-in number without a market-tested realization behind it. If your pension board is seeing improved distribution metrics this year, ask what fraction of that came from a real third-party sale versus a general partner effectively selling an asset to itself.

One step downstream, that same liquidity pressure shows up in fundraising. Headline dry powder sits near a record $3.7 trillion globally, but fund close rates fell to 57% in 2025, down from 94% in 2020 — limited partners are re-upping more selectively and it's taking longer to hit targets. The capital keeps concentrating at the top: KKR's $305 billion raised since 2024, Blue Owl's newest GP-stakes flagship targeting roughly $13 billion, now expected to close sometime in 2026. Everyone else is finding the fundraising trail longer and harder. So headline AUM growth and genuine performance are two different stories again, and the gap between them is widening, not narrowing.

There's one more place the money is coming from, and it's the part allocators should watch most closely: the retail channel. A record 123 evergreen and semi-liquid vehicles launched in 2025, with 30 more in just the first two months of this year. Evergreen fund fair-market value reached $113 billion as of September, and 93% of that growth has come since 2021. Nearly nine in ten managers surveyed say they plan to launch a semi-liquid structure within two years. This is the equity analog of private credit's retail push, and it exists for the same reason continuation vehicles do — to answer a distribution drought the traditional exit market isn't solving. But it imports a structural mismatch. These wrappers promise periodic redemptions against genuinely illiquid underlying assets, and that promise is only tested when enough investors ask for their money back at once. As long as inflows run hot, the mismatch stays invisible. It's the kind of risk that only shows up on the way down.

Pull it together and the read hasn't changed, it's sharpened. The private equity cycle is running at two speeds. Entry and the fee machine are firing — the biggest LBO ever closes next week, and the largest managers just posted record fee-related earnings and record realized carry. Exits and distributions stay jammed, and what liquidity limited partners are seeing increasingly comes engineered through continuation vehicles, NAV loans, and retail wrappers rather than earned through a market-tested sale. The binding risk here isn't headline assets under management. It's the exit environment and the growing distance between a paper mark and cash actually returned. Watch for confirmation or complication from Apollo's results on August 4th, Carlyle's on the 5th, and easyJet's formal takeover offer deadline from Apollo on August 7th.

That's the briefing for today. Have a good weekend.
