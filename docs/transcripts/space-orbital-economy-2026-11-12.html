Cautious Optimism Briefings

Space & the Orbital Economy

Transcript · November 12, 2026

Good morning, it's Thursday, November 12th, and this is your orbital economy briefing.

This week, public money for space arrived in three different shapes, and none of them is revenue yet. One is a shared contract ceiling. One is an appropriation that investors are openly discounting. And one is a sovereign funding commitment whose terms are still undisclosed. The thread connecting them is simple. Announced capital keeps running ahead of the task orders and launch slots needed to turn it into cash.

Start with the lead. On November 9th, NASA selected Intuitive Machines for CLPS 2, its next-generation lunar delivery contract. Our figures come from financial-press summaries of the company's release, not from NASA directly. Those summaries describe a combined ceiling of ten billion dollars across all awardees. That could rise to fifteen billion with a five-year extension. The vehicle runs ten years, and its scope is broad. It covers full payload delivery missions, direct lander purchases, orbital transportation, launch rideshare, surface mobility, and operations through the lunar night.

Here is what the headline obscures. That ten billion dollars is not an award to Intuitive Machines. It is the most NASA can spend across every company selected, over a decade. Intuitive Machines' actual revenue depends on the individual task orders it wins over time. Nothing in the reporting we have describes a guaranteed minimum. Last Saturday, this desk called Viasat's four hundred fifty million dollar Golden Dome award a ceiling, not booked revenue. CLPS 2 is the same structure at more than twenty times the size, and it is shared among multiple winners.

So what is a single lunar task order actually worth? NASA's own release gives us one real data point. Under its Moon Base program, NASA named Astrobotic, Firefly Aerospace, and Intuitive Machines to land four missions on the Moon in late 2028. Astrobotic's share is two of those four deliveries, for two hundred ninety-seven point nine million dollars total. Call it roughly a hundred and fifty million dollars per landing. By our arithmetic, filling even the base ceiling at that price would take dozens of deliveries over the decade. One caution on timing. That Moon Base award may predate this week's news, so treat it as a price benchmark, not a fresh order.

Notice, too, where this cycle's firmest lunar dollars land. SatNews reports that around the same announcement, NASA issued one point three billion dollars in commercial launch task orders to SpaceX, Blue Origin, and Relativity Space. Those are task orders, not a ceiling. The money NASA has actually ordered is flowing to rockets first.

There is a fair steelman for CLPS 2. A ten-year, broad-scope vehicle turns lunar delivery from a string of one-off bets into something closer to a recurring procurement channel. That lowers single-mission risk for lander makers like Intuitive Machines. One failed landing no longer has to define the business. But it changes the investable question. It is no longer whether the next landing works. It is the task-order win rate, and the cadence of awards. Which reading does the evidence support today? The demand signal is real. But the only priced orders we can see are a small fraction of the ceiling. A win-rate story is also harder for the market to model, which makes it easier to over-capitalize before the cash shows up. So for Intuitive Machines holders, the number to watch is task orders, not the ten billion.

The same discount shows up on the Pentagon side, and there investors are saying it out loud. SatNews this week quoted Breakwater's Jen Ross. She said investors are not "counting Golden Dome funding as solid" in companies' projected revenues. That is despite, in her words, "a ton of dry powder." It's one investor's view, not a survey. But the discount looks rational. Take a booked award that is not news. Rocket Lab holds an eight hundred sixteen million dollar prime contract from the Space Development Agency, awarded last December, for eighteen Tracking Layer satellites. Even a firm award like that turns into revenue over years, as satellites are built and delivered. So in defense space, the binding constraint isn't whether demand exists. It's when the cash arrives.

Now the one piece of public money this week that looks closer to cash. AST SpaceMobile's third-quarter business update, filed with the SEC, discloses an agreement called J-LEO. The counterparties are Japan's Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications and partner Rakuten. It features roughly one billion dollars of non-dilutive funding for manufacturing and launching satellites. That makes it the most cash-like money in today's set, because it is earmarked funding, not a shared ceiling. But the filing gives no disbursement schedule. It doesn't say whether this is a grant, a prepayment, or a capacity purchase. Nor does it say what AST owes Japan in return. Non-dilutive does not mean free. One conditional inference is worth holding. If other sovereign telecom partners copy this structure, AST's dilution path could diverge from peers that fund buildout through equity and converts. Today, that is one deal, not a pattern.

And money that pays for launch still needs a launch. The same AST filing says BlueBirds 14, 15, and 16 shipped to Cape Canaveral in September. They are still awaiting SpaceX launch date confirmation. Meanwhile, BlueBirds 11 through 13 are unfolded and operating in orbit. That revises yesterday's read. We said AST looked less hostage to Blue Origin's recovery, and that still holds. But AST's next three satellites sit in SpaceX's queue. And SpaceX is the company this desk has tracked since Sunday building its own Starlink Mobile network, aimed at the same customers. To be clear, we have no evidence SpaceX is slow-walking a rival's satellites. A wait of roughly two months could be ordinary manifest friction. The structural point holds either way. AST's own guidance calls for initial, intermittent nationwide coverage in key markets by year-end. That forward-looking target now visibly depends on a competitor's launch schedule. So AST's money is no longer its constraint. Its place in someone else's queue is.

Launch scarcity has a price, and we can now roughly size it. Via Satellite reports that Rocket Lab raised Neutron's base price to seventy million dollars per launch. It adds that the previously reported target was fifty million. On Tuesday, this desk said it couldn't size that increase. Against the reported target, it is about twenty million dollars more per slot, on a rocket that hasn't flown. A reported target is not a contracted price, and the contract terms are still undisclosed. But the direction is the point. A non-SpaceX seat, even one that exists only on paper, now commands a premium.

That sets up tomorrow's doubleheader, and neither outcome is known. SpaceX's fifteenth Starship flight test targets Friday, November 13th, pending regulatory approval that had not cleared as of early November. It plans to deploy twenty-six Starlink V3 satellites. It would also be the first time a V3 booster attempts to return to its launch site. That flight tests the incumbent's next leg of supply. The same day, United Launch Alliance's Vulcan targets LV-01, its first mission for Amazon Leo. Orbital Radar's tracking put Amazon Leo at three hundred ninety-six satellites in orbit as of November 9th. Vulcan is the cleaner test of whether non-SpaceX heavy lift can return on schedule.

Pulling it together. Government and sovereign demand for space services is now genuinely multi-year and multi-channel. It runs from lunar delivery to missile defense to direct-to-cell broadband. That demand is real. What's still a demo is converting it into recognized revenue, and the gate on that conversion is launch access. The open question is who captures the value when the company holding the launch manifest is also the competitor.

For catalysts, watch for the first CLPS 2 task orders, and their size. Intuitive Machines' third-quarter results are reportedly due this morning, the first chance to hear management frame CLPS 2 against its existing business. Watch for a confirmed SpaceX date for those three waiting BlueBirds, and for AST's progress against its year-end coverage guidance. AST's Blacklift rocket unveiling in New York, which this desk flagged for today, may say more about its longer-run launch plan. And then comes Friday's doubleheader.

That's the briefing. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
