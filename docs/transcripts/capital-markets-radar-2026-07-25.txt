Good morning. It's Saturday, July 25th, and this is your Capital Markets Cross-Asset Radar.

All week we've priced a hawkish Fed as an oil story. On Thursday, the labor market handed the hawks a second reason. Initial jobless claims fell 22,000 to a seasonally adjusted 187,000 for the week ended July 18. That's the lowest print since September 1969, a 57-year low. And it lands against a Reuters consensus that expected claims to rise, to 212,000. So this wasn't a marginal beat. The number came in 25,000 below what economists penciled in, and it moved in the strong direction, not the weak one. Continuing claims, the proxy for how easily laid-off workers find new jobs, fell to a six-week low of 1.796 million. Put those together and you get a labor market showing no sign of the slowdown the Fed could use as cover for looking through an inflation scare.

That matters because the inflation hawks on the committee now have a cleaner argument. Governor Lisa Cook said in a July 15th speech she's "prepared to act" if disinflation doesn't resume, and that she now sees inflation risk outweighing employment risk. She named three pressure points: AI investment, tariffs, and the Middle East. Governor Christopher Waller has gone further, saying the committee may need to consider tightening in the near term. Treat Cook's line as standing context feeding into the meeting, not fresh Friday news. But the direction of travel is clear. As of Friday, fed funds futures priced roughly a one-in-three chance of a hike at next week's July 28th-29th meeting, and near 80% odds of one by September. A month ago, the debate was about when the Fed would start cutting.

Here's the skeptical read, though, and it's the one I'd hold onto. This is one weekly print. Jobless claims are heavily seasonally adjusted, and a single 57-year low is not proof that the labor market is re-accelerating. Summer is exactly when that seasonal adjustment does the most work. If this number turns out to be noise rather than signal, the Fed risks tightening into an economy that is actually still cooling. That's a genuine policy-error risk, not a footnote, and it's the reason one data point shouldn't decide a rate path on its own.

Now the other half of this week's hawkish case is pulling the opposite way. The oil shock that started all of this just paused. Brent crude closed Thursday at $100.69 a barrel, its highest close since May 22nd. Then it fell back toward $97 on Friday, its biggest one-day drop since late June. Be careful how you read that pullback. There is no confirmed evidence of an actual diplomatic breakthrough with Iran. This looks like technical exhaustion and positioning unwinding after a fast climb, not a resolved crisis. President Trump has threatened to strike Iranian infrastructure, including sites near Tehran, if attacks on shipping continue. Iran has claimed responsibility for strikes on petrochemical plants in the UAE, Kuwait, and Bahrain. The Pentagon says the Strait of Hormuz remains open, but tanker traffic has reportedly slowed to a crawl.

So keep the pullback in proportion. Oil is still up roughly a quarter over the past month even after Friday's drop. This is a pause off a peak, not a reversal. And the geopolitical risk premium doesn't leave just because the headline price ticks down. As long as Hormuz can be threatened, that premium stays embedded in freight and insurance costs even when crude itself eases. That's the harder problem for the Fed. A demand-driven inflation impulse, the Fed can lean against directly. A supply shock from a shipping chokepoint, it usually prefers to look through, precisely because raising rates does nothing to move a tanker. The upshot is that the two forces behind this week's hawkish repricing are now diverging. Labor is hardening the case for a hike. Oil, the original trigger, is softening it. That divergence is what makes next week's meeting a genuine live decision rather than a foregone hawkish hold.

There's a second channel worth naming before we leave rates. A repricing driven by firm labor data and a firmer dollar tightens financial conditions on more than one front at once. Higher discount rates press hardest on the longest-duration assets. A stronger dollar is a headwind for emerging markets and for commodity importers. And non-yielding assets, from gold to crypto, tend to lose ground when real rates push up. In other words, the hawkish turn doesn't hit one corner of the market. It leans on several at the same time. That's why the equity reaction this week was concentrated exactly where duration is longest.

Which brings us to the AI-capex trade, where the repricing landed hardest. Thursday's selloff was brutal. The Nasdaq fell 2.2%, its worst day in weeks. Tesla fell sharply, and Alphabet's raised capital-spending guidance overshadowed a revenue beat. Friday brought a partial recovery. The S&P 500 closed essentially flat, and the Dow gained under half a percent. But the Nasdaq still slipped another 0.6%, which tells you the capex anxiety didn't fully clear. And here's the honest question about that bounce: we don't yet know whether it reflects real conviction or just short-covering ahead of a binary week. Those are very different signals, and this week's tape doesn't settle which one it is.

What is clear is that the real test is still ahead. Microsoft and Meta report earnings on July 29th, and Amazon on July 30th. Combined 2026 AI capital spending across the four largest hyperscalers is now projected near $725 billion. That's up about 77% from roughly $410 billion last year. So the exact capex-versus-monetization question that spooked investors over Alphabet this week gets asked three more times in five trading days. If Microsoft, Meta, and Amazon each raise spending again without showing where the revenue shows up, one bad week becomes a pattern that's hard to dismiss. If even one of them delivers a credible return story, this week's skepticism could reverse just as fast as it arrived.

So here's where that leaves us heading into next week. The hawkish case now has two legs pulling in opposite directions. A hardening labor market on one side, a softening oil shock on the other. And the market has to net them out live, in real time, at the July 28th-29th meeting. That decision lands in the same five-day window as three hyperscaler earnings reports carrying a combined three-quarters-of-a-trillion-dollar spending bet. Rates and equities aren't taking turns as the story this week. They're colliding, and the same few days will settle both.
