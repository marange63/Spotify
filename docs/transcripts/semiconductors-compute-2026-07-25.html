Good morning. It's Saturday, July 25th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

At an AI summit in San Francisco this week, South Korea's chip giants put a number on how long they expect the memory shortage to last: years, not quarters. Korean companies and US big tech agreed to pursue a combined nine hundred and fifty billion dollars in five-year supply and cooperation deals. That's the headline out of a summit hosted by South Korea's president, with Nvidia's Jensen Huang, Broadcom's Hock Tan, Sam Altman, and Dario Amodei all in the room. The nine-hundred-and-fifty-billion figure is Reuters wire reporting, republished across Korean and US outlets, so hold it as a framework total, not a signed order book.

Two pieces of that framework the companies did put their own names to. SK Group and Nvidia announced their piece directly, in a joint release stating the partnership at, quote, "five-hundred-billion-plus," covering AI factory construction and next-generation memory supply. Separately, Samsung and Broadcom signed a memorandum of understanding the companies size at more than two hundred billion dollars across memory and foundry work through 2030. Both of those figures now trace to primary company releases, not just wire copy.

Worth flagging up front, because it's the biggest number and the softest: wire reports out of Korea have circulated a seven-hundred-and-fifty-billion-dollar figure for the SK Group side. That number does not appear anywhere in SK Group or Nvidia's own language. Their joint release caps it at five-hundred-billion-plus, two hundred and fifty billion dollars lower. And Nvidia's own newsroom post on the memory partnership puts no dollar figure on it at all, calling it simply a multiyear technology partnership. So take the seven-fifty as a secondhand aggregation across SK Group's deals with Nvidia and other, unnamed partners, not a confirmed company figure. Biggest number of the week, thinnest confirmation.

This is an escalation of a story we've been tracking all week. On Wednesday, SK Hynix's HBM4 ramp was the pacing signal for when Nvidia's Rubin chips could actually ship. Now the same duopoly, SK Hynix and Samsung, which together supply the overwhelming majority of the world's high-bandwidth memory, is converting that scarcity into multi-year contracts. By dollar value, this is the largest single batch of forward memory-supply commitments the industry has disclosed to date. The message from Korea's memory makers is that they don't expect the crunch to ease anytime soon, and they're pricing that conviction into five-year paper.

Look closely at the structure, though, and the deal is softer than the headline suggests. Samsung's own release calls it a memorandum of understanding. SK Group and Nvidia call theirs letters of intent, signed, in their words, "to formalize the agreement." Both are explicitly non-binding at this stage. Neither release discloses cancellation terms or volume-flexibility language. What they do describe is a first concrete phase: SK Group and Nvidia point to a two-gigawatt AI Cloud facility targeted for 2027. That's the near-term anchor under a much larger, looser pledge.

The structure is a deliberate contrast with the chips-for-equity financing we've covered this week between Nvidia and OpenAI, or AMD and Anthropic. Those deals tie compute supply to an equity stake, the vendor helping fund its own customer. This is simpler and, in one sense, more honest. Officials describing the deals put it plainly: these are not investments, they're advance orders that say, quote, "if you produce the memory chips, we will purchase them in those expanded volumes." There's no circular self-funding. But that difference also moves where the risk sits. In the equity deals, the vendor is exposed if the customer stumbles. Here, without penalties attached, it's a promise either side could walk back if the demand math changes.

So what does this mean if it holds? Locking up Samsung and SK Hynix capacity for Nvidia and Broadcom years in advance squeezes the open market for everyone else buying memory: AMD, the hyperscalers building custom silicon, and PC and phone makers. In a shortage, forward commitments this size push those buyers to the back of the queue and reinforce the memory-pricing upcycle already running. It's the clearest sign yet that memory, not the GPUs themselves, is where the AI supply chain is tightest. There's a second angle on the Samsung side too. The Broadcom deal isn't only memory, it's foundry and advanced-packaging work. That hands Samsung's long-struggling foundry business another anchor customer to help fund its two-nanometer ramp, right as it tries to close the gap on TSMC. For Samsung, this framework is as much about foundry credibility as memory volume.

And it raises execution risk on the other side. These are pledges sized against a meaningful slice of Korea's GDP, built years before the compute is deployed. If AI capex growth slows in 2027 or 2028, both the Korean chipmakers building out capacity and their US counterparties are exposed to demand they contracted for but that never shows up. That's the flip side of selling scarcity forward: the pledges only pay off if the shortage lasts as long as the contract assumes. Contracting scarcity years ahead cuts both ways.

That raises the obvious question: is the demand underneath this bet actually broad enough to justify it? Texas Instruments gave us a partial answer this week. Its second-quarter revenue came in at five and a half billion dollars, up 23 percent from a year earlier and 13 percent from the prior quarter, with net income just under two billion and earnings of two dollars and fourteen cents a share. The company called the growth broad-based, led by industrial, data-center, and automotive markets. Texas Instruments then guided third-quarter revenue to a range of five-point-six-five to six-point-one-five billion dollars, with earnings of two-twenty-three to two-fifty-seven a share, above its normal seasonal pattern.

That matters because Texas Instruments doesn't sell GPUs or memory. It sells the analog and power-management chips that surround them. A guide like that suggests the AI buildout is pulling demand into a much wider slice of the chip industry than just accelerators and high-bandwidth memory. Keep it honest, though: this is one bellwether, and Texas Instruments doesn't break out a discrete data-center segment, so the read is directional, not proof. But it's a genuine data point on the demand side of the ledger.

Put the two stories together and you get the shape of where this market actually is. Supply is being locked up years in advance, on soft, non-binding terms, while demand shows real signs of broadening across the stack. The next hard evidence lands soon. SK Hynix reports Tuesday, July 29th; Samsung the following day. Those prints will be the first chance to see actual HBM and DRAM pricing test whether this week's pledges are backed by reality, or whether the biggest number of the week stays exactly that, a number.
