Good morning, it's Saturday, September 26th, and this is your AI products briefing.

All week this beat has said the same thing: nobody publishes what an AI agent actually costs to run per task. This week, Microsoft's Copilot relaunch came with the closest thing yet to an answer. What it shows is that the meter is now bigger than the seat.

The headline change is Microsoft's redesign of Copilot into three destinations. Home merges Chat with Cowork. Code turns written instructions into hosted applications, aimed at people who aren't engineers. Autopilot is a persistent agent that, in Microsoft's framing, can watch conversations, pursue updates, handle recurring responsibilities, and resume a project after several days. Autopilot is also not new from scratch. It is the new name for Scout, Microsoft's earlier personal-agent project.

None of this is shipping broadly yet. Microsoft's own announcement says Home and Code will start rolling out through its Frontier early-access program in the coming weeks. Code reaches ordinary Microsoft 365 Premium and Pro subscribers only later this year, and even then in preview. Autopilot moves more slowly still. In Microsoft's words, it is expanding to private preview at the end of the month, with no general-availability date at all. So treat this as a direction Microsoft is committing to, not a product most Copilot users can touch today.

The part that matters more than the feature list is the billing model. According to Fortune, usage-based billing can now apply to Code, to Autopilot, and to access to the newest, most capable models, on top of the enterprise seat. That's the meter this show flagged earlier in the week on Copilot's channel licenses, which switch to usage billing by default on November 2nd. Now it reaches the flagship product itself.

VentureBeat's coverage carried a modeled example of what that looks like. One workload came to $73 a month using Opus 5, or $69 using GPT-5.6 Sol. Of that, $43 or $39 was consumption, not the base subscription. Hold that figure loosely. It comes from press reporting, and Microsoft's own blog post names no dollar figures at all. But directionally it says something real. In that example, more than half the bill is metered. For a heavy agent user, the variable part of the invoice can now outweigh the flat part.

Notice, too, how little the model choice moved the total. Only four dollars separated the Opus 5 option from the GPT-5.6 Sol option, against roughly forty dollars of consumption either way. Volume drove the bill, not which lab's model did the work. Microsoft's own post doesn't sharpen the picture. It refers only to "frontier models like Astra and Fable," names no Opus or GPT version, and gives no per-unit rate. And the example was stale the moment it was quoted. As this show covered earlier in the week, both models were superseded on September 22nd. Opus 5.5 arrived with list prices a fifth below Opus 5 and cache reads sixty percent cheaper, and OpenAI replaced GPT-5.6 Sol with GPT-6 Sol. So Microsoft has partly answered the cost-per-task question, but with a modeled example, not a customer invoice, built on pricing that had already moved.

The business implication reaches well beyond Microsoft. If the biggest enterprise software vendor teaches buyers to expect a seat plus a meter, seat-only pricing starts to look incomplete. SaaS incumbents that charge per user capture none of the task volume an agent generates on their platform. That's an inference, not something any vendor has said. But it's the pricing conversation every seat-based software company should expect at its next renewal.

There's also a second-order risk buried in the pitch. Microsoft describes hosting a Copilot-built app as, quote, "as simple as saving and sharing a Word document." That ease is exactly what becomes a governance headache. Apps built by business users outside IT review can spread the way macro-laden spreadsheets once did. Microsoft's chief executive seems to know it. Satya Nadella framed the redesign around a governance principle: "Every agent has to have an identity. Everything it does needs to be observed." So what Nadella is selling isn't just capability. It's an admission that ungoverned agents are becoming the real bottleneck.

If every agent needs an identity, someone has to count them. That is the gap Dataiku is targeting from outside Microsoft's walls. This week Dataiku announced Agent Management, a product built to inventory every AI agent a company runs, whichever platform built it. The pitch starts from a real problem. According to IBM research that Dataiku cites, fewer than one in five organizations maintain a complete, current inventory of their AI systems. Dataiku says the product automatically identifies each agent's structure, including the tools and models it relies on, then measures value and flags risk. Access is priced per instance annually, with monitoring metered per agent. So governance itself is becoming a metered product, just like the agents it watches.

There's an obvious tension here, though. Nadella is selling identity and observability inside Microsoft's own console. Dataiku is selling a neutral layer across everyone's consoles. Platforms like Microsoft, Salesforce, and AWS have every incentive to keep their agents fully visible only inside their own tools. That makes Dataiku's "no matter where they were built" claim hard to verify from outside. And the product doesn't reach general availability until October, so today it's an announcement, with no named customers. Still, the direction is the story. Governance is becoming its own per-agent line item, arriving right behind the agents themselves.

Now the meter moves further down the stack, from the whole agent to the individual tool call. Perplexity's legacy Sonar API retires tomorrow, September 27th. Anyone who built on it has to move to Perplexity's Agent API, which launched on August 13th. That gave developers roughly a forty-five-day migration window, which is short for a production API. The Agent API reportedly bills third-party models, from OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and others, at provider rates with no markup. Perplexity then adds a fee for every tool invocation. A web search currently runs a quarter of a cent. Fetching a URL costs a fifth of that.

So Perplexity isn't trying to make money on model tokens at all. It's charging for what the agent does. That's the mirror image of Microsoft's approach. Microsoft layers a meter on top of a seat. Perplexity drops the seat and bills each action directly. For a search-heavy agent, that means tool fees, not tokens, could become the cost that matters. That's an inference, not a measured result. One sourcing flag: these figures come from a secondary outlet summarizing Perplexity's launch, not Perplexity's own pricing page. Whether integrations quietly break tomorrow is worth watching, not assuming.

Perplexity's wasn't the only API retirement this week. OpenAI's Sora API reached its scheduled sunset on September 24th, five months after the consumer Sora app closed in April. That ends the Sora line entirely. OpenAI's guidance told users to export their files as soon as possible. It named no successor video API, and recommended only its gpt-image-1 model, which generates images, not video. OpenAI has given no stated rationale for the exit. Compare the two retirements. Perplexity handed builders a migration target. OpenAI handed video developers none. For anyone building on a frontier lab's API, that's the lesson: the platform can retire the ground you're standing on, with or without somewhere else to stand. Portability across model and tool vendors is now a design requirement, not a nice-to-have.

Put together, three of this week's stories share one pattern. The agent is becoming the unit that gets counted and billed. Microsoft meters consumption above the seat. Dataiku meters the inventory of agents itself. Perplexity meters the individual tool call. And the fourth story, Sora, is a reminder that the vendor sets the schedule. What would settle whether any of this is cheaper, rather than just more visible, is a real customer's invoice under Copilot's new consumption pricing, not a modeled example. Nobody has published that invoice yet.
