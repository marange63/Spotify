Good morning, it's Friday, September 25th, and this is your digital money briefing.

Yesterday this desk set an explicit test. The spot bitcoin ETF flow for Wednesday, the day the long end repriced and bitcoin was rejected, wasn't in yet. We called it the cleanest test so far of whether this bid survives a rates shock. That print is now in. According to aggregator data, spot bitcoin ETFs took in $346.9 million on September 23rd. It was positive. The bid survived.

But look at the sequence, not just the sign. Monday brought in $999 million. Tuesday brought in $714.7 million. Wednesday brought in $346.9 million. Together that's roughly two billion dollars in three sessions. Each day, though, was smaller than the last, and Wednesday's print was about half of Tuesday's. Survival at half strength is a weaker result than either "the bid held" or "the bid broke."

Start with the bullish reading, because it's real. Wednesday extended the inflow streak to a fifth straight session. It came on a day when real yields jumped sharply, into a Fed already guiding toward one more hike. That is demand that did not flee. Now the cautious reading. A series that shrinks this fast is also what fading demand looks like just before it flips negative. There's a third possibility, which we raised yesterday. Some of this flow may be a hedged carry trade rather than directional conviction. A carry trade shrinks when funding compresses. It does so for reasons that have nothing to do with belief in bitcoin. The flow series alone can't tell these three stories apart. We don't have a confirmed print past Wednesday, so the next one is what starts to resolve it.

Now to the shock itself, because the kind of rate move matters as much as the flow. The ten-year Treasury yield jumped 15 basis points in a single session, to 5.11 percent. Thirteen of those 15 basis points came from the real yield, which rose from 2.63 to 2.76 percent. So this was not an inflation scare. It was not a debasement story either. It was a growth-and-policy repricing of real rates.

The trigger was a composite PMI of 58.4, the strongest expansion reading since July 2021. That landed on a Fed that had already hiked on September 16th, with most members looking for one more quarter-point move by year-end. A real-yield spike raises the opportunity cost of holding a zero-yield asset directly. Bitcoin fell to an intraday low of $83,500 the same day. The mechanism is clean on paper. But a same-session yield jump and a bitcoin drop are still co-movement, not proven causation.

There's a competing story sitting underneath it. Monday's short squeeze forced out roughly $648 million of bearish bets. Yet open interest climbed that same day, even as those shorts were being closed out. Traders were chasing the move rather than stepping back. Forced buying builds positions that then have to be defended. So the market walked into Wednesday holding a freshly built long book. That book was primed to flush, whatever the trigger turned out to be.

The liquidation data backs that up. In the twelve hours after bitcoin slipped below $84,000, close to $400 million of positions were liquidated, and $360 million of that was longs. Bitcoin futures open interest has since dropped back below 700,000 coins, and shorts now outnumber longs. On Tuesday this desk said leverage had rotated long through the squeeze. Wednesday's flush is that call paying out. So the honest read holds two things at once. The real-yield move was likely the trigger. A crowded long book was the fuel. Neither one alone explains the size of the move.

That brings us to the test the digital-gold case actually has to pass, and it's harder than "bitcoin fell." Gold is sensitive to real yields too. So a bitcoin selloff on a real-yield shock doesn't, by itself, disprove the idea of bitcoin as amplified gold. The fair test is relative magnitude on the same day. Did bitcoin fall like leveraged gold, or like leveraged tech?

This week's gold evidence doesn't settle it. On Tuesday, the session before the PMI shock, gold fell 0.81 percent. Then on Wednesday, the day of bitcoin's low, gold was trading modestly higher by mid-morning. It was up about $52 from the same time a day earlier. That's an intraday snapshot, not a close. It doesn't fit a clean story in which gold held firm while bitcoin cracked. It doesn't cleanly fit the opposite story either.

The correlation backdrop is also out of date. Bitcoin's ninety-day correlation with gold was near a six-year high in late August, before the hike. It hasn't been re-measured since this week's shock. And Glassnode has cautioned that bitcoin's decoupling from equities during bond selloffs tends to be, in their words, "local exhaustion rather than structural regime shifts." So here is the plain takeaway. This week neither proved nor disproved digital gold. Until a clean same-day test says otherwise, don't count on bitcoin as a hedge against rising real yields.

Now the other side of the same real-yield move. A regime that raises bitcoin's hurdle is a tailwind somewhere else in the stack. Stablecoin issuers hold their reserves in Treasury bills, so their reserve income rises with bill yields. But the latest disclosed quarter from Coinbase, the one that ended June 30th, shows that tailwind doesn't reach distributors automatically. The third quarter hasn't reported yet.

Coinbase's stablecoin revenue fell to $292 million, down $17 million from a year earlier. Over the same period, average USDC held in Coinbase products grew 44 percent. Balances went up sharply. Revenue went down. The disclosure doesn't say why. Lower bill yields than a year earlier, the revenue-share terms, and product mix are all candidates. Whatever the split, the lesson holds. Balance growth alone doesn't set a distributor's revenue. Rate levels and deal terms both sit between the float and the income line. So the next time someone frames stablecoin float as an automatic winner from higher rates, ask who holds the contract, not just who holds the balance.

So where does that leave the bigger picture? Bitcoin's marginal buyer this cycle has been the ETF allocator. That buyer kept buying through a real-yield shock, but noticeably less of it each day. If that sensitivity holds, bitcoin increasingly trades like a long-duration, zero-yield asset. In that world, the real-rate calendar matters more than bitcoin-specific headlines. The September inflation prints in mid-October and the October 28th FOMC meeting become bitcoin catalysts. That's a falsifiable claim, and the next few flow prints will start to test it.

One housekeeping note. Today is the quarterly expiry this desk has flagged all week. Its outcome isn't in yet, so nothing in this briefing describes how it went.

Three things to watch. First, the next confirmed ETF flow print. Does the shrinking streak flip to outflows, or steady? A flip on a calm rates day would say something beyond rate sensitivity is draining the bid. Steadiness would favor the bull reading. Second, a clean same-day gold comparison the next time real yields jump, so the digital-gold test can finally be graded. Third, the path of real yields into the October Fed meeting, which is now the backdrop this bid has to clear.

That's the digital money briefing for September 25th. Have a good one.
