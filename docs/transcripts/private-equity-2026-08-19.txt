Good morning. It's Wednesday, August 19th, and here's the honest read on private equity today: the industry's marginal growth this cycle isn't coming from clearing exits. It's coming from financializing itself — buying the businesses that trade the assets, buying stakes in the managers, and buying up cheap public companies. Three separate moves this month all point the same direction, and none of them puts real cash back into the hands of the limited partners who are actually waiting for it.

Start with secondaries, the industry's release valve for a broken exit market. We've walked the headline number on this show before, so treat it as scene-setting: H1 2026 volume hit $121 billion, the strongest first half on record. What's new is who's consolidating around that flow and how it's actually pricing. EQT agreed back in January to buy Coller Capital, one of the largest dedicated secondaries shops, for $3.2 billion upfront plus up to $500 million more tied to performance through March 2029, funded by issuing 81 million new EQT shares. EQT says the deal is mid-single-digit accretive to fee-related earnings, and Coller brings roughly $50 billion in assets, two-thirds of that fee-generating. But be precise about status: this is agreed, not closed. EQT's own guidance points to a third-quarter 2026 close, and a competing claim circulating that the deal already completed traces to an unverified secondary source that conflicts with EQT's own timeline — so treat it as pending. The reason this deal matters is composition. Within that record secondaries volume, GP-led deals rose 35% to $65 billion, while LP-led sales — the diversified portfolio trades that actually return cash across a fund's whole investor base — grew just 4%. And inside GP-led, single-asset continuation vehicles, where a sponsor rolls its single best asset into a new vehicle it still controls, jumped 88% to $34 billion and now make up 53% of all GP-led activity. That's the fastest-growing corner of the market, and it's the most conflicted: the GP is seller and buyer-side sponsor at once.

Here's the sharper point. Follow-up pricing data shows those single-asset deals aren't clearing at a discount that would signal genuine liquidity stress — they're pricing mostly at par, and 14% above net asset value, according to trade reporting on the same H1 dataset. Multi-asset vehicles, by contrast, priced below par 71% of the time. Read plainly, buyers are paying for underwriting certainty on a trophy asset the GP already knows intimately, not for a diversified, independently discovered market price. So when EQT buys Coller, Blackstone's Strategic Partners unit, Ardian and Lexington keep scaling, you're watching the buyer base for this pricing consolidate into a handful of the same scaled owners who benefit from less competitive tension on continuation-vehicle terms. Jefferies is now forecasting the whole secondaries market approaching $300 billion a year within 12 to 24 months. So what does that mean for an allocator. Record secondaries volume is real, but it systematically overstates genuine cash returned to the broad LP base — the fastest-growing piece of it is a sponsor re-underwriting its own asset near full value, not a market clearing distressed sellers at a discount.

That same logic shows up one level up the stack, in who's buying stakes in the managers themselves rather than in their deals. Blue Owl's GP Stakes strategy — buying minority pieces of alternative-asset managers' fee streams and carry — has reached $10.6 billion for its latest flagship fund, and the firm's CFO said on the earnings call it will wrap up fundraising this year. That's notable for what it's short of: the firm had previously guided to a $13 billion target for this vintage. Petershill, Goldman's GP-stakes unit, is out targeting $5 billion for its fifth fund, so the strategy is still crowded with capital. But Blue Owl coming in roughly $2.4 billion light of its original goal, and still not formally closed, is the clearest data point in this whole space that LP appetite is thinning at the margin — even inside a strategy explicitly marketed as a lower-beta, resilient way to ride the alternatives boom. It's worth remembering why GP stakes is attractive in the first place: managers like Apollo are showing funds like Fund X delivering a 21% net internal rate of return against a roughly 14% industry benchmark for that vintage, so owning a slice of the fee machine behind returns like that looks like easy money. Blue Owl's shortfall says some of that easy-money framing is starting to crack, even if it's a crack and not a collapse. So what does that tell you. When even the buy-the-manager, not-the-deal trade is missing its own targets, LP caution is broader than just the exit-starved buyout funds — it's reaching into the strategies sold as the safe way around that problem.

The third form this financialization takes is simpler: if you can't sell your own portfolio companies, go buy someone else's cheap public company instead. Vista Equity Partners and Quinti Capital made an unsolicited approach for the French ad-tech firm Criteo, offering a premium of more than 50% and valuing the company at roughly $3.7 billion on an equity basis. News of the approach sent Criteo's shares up more than 21% in a single session to $23.17, their best day since 2021. But keep this in proportion. It's an approach, not a deal — Criteo's board hasn't responded, financing terms haven't been disclosed, and the news itself is now several weeks old. The one number to hold loosely is the valuation itself: that roughly $3.7 billion equity figure sits awkwardly next to a pre-news market capitalization closer to $1.16 billion, so how big the "premium" really is depends heavily on which reference price you anchor to. So what it tells you is narrower than a full take-private thesis: under-owned, cheap public names in structurally out-of-favor sectors like ad-tech are becoming a hunting ground for sponsors with dry powder and nowhere else obvious to put it. That's deployment into a bargain, not a clean cash exit for anyone's existing fund.

Pull the threads together and the pattern across all three stories is the same. Buying the secondaries business, buying stakes in the managers, and buying cheap public companies all generate deal activity, AUM growth, and fee-related earnings that show up as good news on an earnings call. None of them clear the actual logjam: selling the portfolio companies sponsors already own to third-party buyers for real cash, at prices LPs can trust as independently discovered rather than GP-marked. The near-term catalysts that would actually confirm or refute this read are not another consolidation deal — they're whether single-asset continuation vehicles start showing genuine realized returns to the LPs who roll into or cash out of them, and whether EQT's Coller deal, once it does close, brings pricing discipline back to GP-led deals or simply adds one more large owner to a shrinking, cozier club. Watch for that data, not for more volume headlines.
