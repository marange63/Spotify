Good morning. It's Friday, October 23rd, and this is your digital money briefing.

The shock test this desk has been waiting for arrived on Thursday, and bitcoin failed it as a hedge. Meta's latest results showed its first cash outflow in more than a decade, driven by AI spending. The company also disclosed future spending commitments of roughly 825 billion dollars. Thursday, the market answered. Chipmakers including Intel, AMD, Sandisk and Marvell each fell more than 8 percent. The Nasdaq Composite fell 2.6 percent. And bitcoin fell 3.4 percent over the twenty-four hours CoinDesk measured, to 81,387 dollars.

Here's why that matters to this desk specifically. On Tuesday, we reported Block Scholes data showing bitcoin's correlation with gold climbing while its link to equities faded. We squared that with the CPI-day selloff using a two-part answer. Bitcoin might drift toward gold on slow statistics, while still trading like leveraged risk when a macro shock lands. Thursday supplied the shock, and bitcoin did the second thing. It traded with tech, not with gold. So the evidence now favors the second half of that reconciliation.

One honest limit belongs up front. This is one session of co-movement. The line from Meta's cash flow to bitcoin's price is reporters' interpretation, not a demonstrated mechanism. And bitcoin's move is a twenty-four-hour figure, not a matched close-to-close comparison with the Nasdaq. Still, the transmission channel is worth naming. A megacap AI-capex disclosure moved crypto risk appetite directly. That puts bitcoin inside the same factor book as AI equities. That is exactly where the uncorrelated-diversifier pitch says it shouldn't be.

So why didn't three days of ETF buying hold the line? The buying was real. CoinDesk puts the three-day inflow at 1.08 billion dollars, citing SoSoValue data. But CoinDesk also makes the more useful comparison. Average daily inflows since the start of July have run about 86 million dollars. Past steep rallies ran above 200 million a day. So the current bid runs at less than half the pace that has historically carried price. That means inflows and a falling price aren't a contradiction. A bid that thin cannot offset equity-driven de-risking. The marginal price-setter is still risk appetite and derivatives, not allocators. One gap remains. Trackers disagree on Thursday's own flow, so we won't give you a number for that day.

There's a sharper way to see that thinness. Glassnode has been tracking a cost-basis line for the average US spot ETF holder, near 81,500 dollars. In a post ahead of the selloff, Glassnode said every daily close for three weeks had held above it. It warned that a break below could set off forced selling. CoinDesk's snapshot price sits just under that line. Be careful with what that means. It's a twenty-four-hour snapshot, not a daily close, and comparing it to Glassnode's level is our own arithmetic. No one has reported a confirmed close below the line. And a cost basis is an average, not an order book. Being underwater forces no one to sell, whatever the warning says. But if a daily close confirms the break, the level ETF buyers held for three weeks will have given way to an equity-market AI scare, not to anything crypto-specific.

The leverage side tells the same story, with one twist. On Monday, this desk described the range as rented. Longs were paying about 10.9 percent annualized in funding to sit there. Per TokenPost, funding on bitcoin perpetuals was negative 2.7 percent annualized on Thursday. That's one tracker and one snapshot. It may not be the same series as Monday's reading, so treat it as directional. Here's the twist. The same tracker shows 64.5 percent of accounts long, slightly more than the share we cited Monday. That counts accounts, not size. So the long crowd didn't leave. What changed is that longs stopped paying a premium to stay. The likely reading is that the size pushing funding negative now sits on the short side. The rent on this range has gone from expensive to negative. The owners who would make it price-insensitive still haven't shown up.

Which is why the correlation headlines deserve suspicion. Fidelity Digital Assets has reported that bitcoin's correlation with ten-year real Treasury yields hit a record high. In its words, that flips a historically near-zero or negative relationship. Bulls read that as bitcoin graduating into a macro asset. But per reporting on the study, it compares levels, not returns. Level correlations between two trending series are notoriously easy to overstate. A change-based measure, reported by FXStreet, tells a less flattering story. Bitcoin's 90-day correlation with changes in the nominal ten-year yield sits at negative 0.17. Gold's sits at negative 0.41, more than twice as strong. Both readings date from mid-October, before Thursday's shock. So method decides the answer, and a capital-markets book should discount the headline level number.

The strongest bull case deserves a plain statement. Bitcoin fell by less than half as much as the chipmakers did. That's lower beta to the epicenter of the shock than its history might suggest. But lower beta to semiconductors is not hedge behavior. A hedge rises, or at least holds, when the risk book falls. Bitcoin did neither.

Now, while the asset traded like tech, the rails underneath it kept getting built. Per the aggregator Solana Compass, JPMorgan Asset Management made its MONY and JLTXX tokenized money-market funds available on Solana on Thursday. Both funds launched first on Ethereum. We didn't retrieve a primary JPMorgan release. Still, a top-tier asset manager running one regulated cash product across two chains is treating blockchains as distribution venues, not pilots. That's a modest point in Solana's favor as a venue for regulated products. Now keep the scale in view. No fund size was disclosed for either fund. Access is limited to qualified US investors. And the tokenized Treasury category sits near 15 billion dollars per RWA.xyz, across roughly 86,000 holders as of early October. On our arithmetic, that implies an average position well into six figures. That's a concentrated, institutional base, not broad adoption. The category is also essentially flat against the figure this desk cited on Monday. And on Wednesday we found only about 2 percent of leading products' value working as DeFi collateral. So JPMorgan widened distribution. Usage, the real gap, didn't visibly move.

That same gap between rails and real demand shows up in the dollar layer. A Coin Metrics study, relayed by Cryptonomist, finds that neither USDT nor USDC recorded monthly supply growth above 10 percent at any point this year. That ceiling corrects any stablecoins-are-exploding narrative. The more useful finding is about timing. USDT issuance tends to pick up after crypto rallies, while USDC growth typically eases in those same periods. That's a historical pattern, not a causal law. But it reframes the share debate this desk ran on Tuesday and Wednesday. We concluded then that who's gaining share depends on which window you measure. Coin Metrics suggests why. Where the window sits in the cycle decides the winner. USDT supply behaves like a trading-float pulse. USDC behaves more like a steadier base. So a USDT surge is a signal about crypto risk appetite, not about dollar adoption.

Now apply that to Thursday. If USDT issuance is rally-driven, a high-beta down day should slow it. That's our inference, not a measured outcome. But it would keep the float flat. And a flat float argues against counting stablecoins as a growing source of Treasury-bill demand right now.

One more thread, because it shows what the stablecoin rulebook is really about. The American Bankers Association has objected to the OCC's final GENIUS Act rule, which leaves third-party rewards to case-by-case review. Its CEO said the approach undermines Congress's intent to protect local lending. Take that as a stated position, nothing more. But it confirms the economic stake. Rewards decide whether a stablecoin competes with a bank deposit on return. Deposits fund local lending, so the banks are telling you which dollar they think is at risk.

So here's the thesis in one line. On the first real macro shock since this desk started asking whether bitcoin is becoming digital gold, it traded as high-beta Nasdaq exposure, despite three days of ETF inflows, while the plumbing underneath keeps getting built faster than it gets used.

Watch three things from here, all still pending. First, whether a daily close settles below Glassnode's ETF cost-basis line, rather than just a snapshot. Second, the Fed's October 28th decision, which hasn't happened yet. Third, Coinbase's third-quarter results after the close on October 29th. And beyond the calendar, watch whether more megacap AI-capex disclosures keep feeding directly into crypto risk appetite.

That's digital money for October 23rd. Have a good weekend.
