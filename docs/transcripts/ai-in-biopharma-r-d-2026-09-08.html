Cautious Optimism Briefings

AI in Biopharma R&D

Transcript · September 8, 2026

Good morning. It's Tuesday, September 8th, and this is your briefing on artificial intelligence in biopharma R&D.

Insilico Medicine takes a conference stage in Paris today, at Nature's "Redefining Healthcare in the Age of AI" meeting, with a new readout on rentosertib. That's its TNIK inhibitor for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. The claim is a reversal in predicted biological age. Six proteomic aging clocks were applied to a panel of 2,841 serum proteins. Treated patients showed roughly three to four years of reversal, peaking at week four in the 30-milligram twice-daily arm, with one clock reading as high as six years.

Place that immediately. Same trial. Same 42 patients. Same twelve-week window this briefing worked through on Sunday, when we covered rentosertib's Phase IIa efficacy result — a 98.4-milliliter improvement in forced vital capacity on the 60-milligram once-daily dose, against a 20.3-milliliter decline on placebo. Two days on, the company has returned to that identical cohort and extracted a second, softer readout. That's the takeaway to bank before any of the science. This is a new narrative, not new evidence. No new patients were dosed to produce it.

Now one thing I have to say plainly, because the reflex on this beat all week has been to treat every capability claim as scored by whoever sells it. That reflex doesn't fit here. The aging-clock analysis is a genuine peer-reviewed paper, published in Nature Biotechnology with a resolvable DOI, co-authored with academics from Harvard, Stanford, the Broad Institute, RWTH Aachen, Peking University and Westlake University. That is a materially stronger evidentiary claim than a company blog post timed to a conference slot, and it deserves to be said rather than waved off.

But peer review answers one question and leaves the harder ones open. Start with what was planned in advance. The trial protocol did prospectively include longitudinal proteomic sampling. The aging-clock analysis built on top of that sampling did not. It is framed explicitly as exploratory. So the samples were pre-specified and the endpoint was not, and those are very different things. That distinction carries the argument, because an exploratory analysis is where you go looking for the result you want.

Next, the six clocks. Six models pointing the same direction is not six independent replications. They are six models trained over one overlapping proteomic panel, from one cohort of 42 patients, in one trial. Proteomic aging clocks correlate by construction, because they draw on the same underlying biology. That is one experiment reported six ways. And be precise about what the two rentosertib numbers now in circulation each measure. The forced-vital-capacity change measures the disease. The biological-age delta measures a model's prediction about the patient. Only one of those sits on any path to an approval.

Then the shape of the signal, which is the part that should worry you most. The effect peaks at week four, a third of the way into a twelve-week window. A durable, disease-modifying signal should still be building by the end of that window, or at minimum holding. A peak that early is the shape you would expect from an acute treatment-response artifact.

And here I have to be careful about what I can and cannot tell you. We went looking for the week-twelve figure, and for any p-value or confidence interval attached to these results. None of it is publicly available — not in the company release, not in press coverage, not in the accessible parts of the paper. The full text sits behind a paywall, so it is possible the paper reports a week-twelve number that simply hasn't surfaced. So I'm not going to tell you the signal faded, and I'm not going to tell you it held. What I can tell you is that the number a buyer would most want is the one not in circulation.

Which gets at the commercial function of this readout. Insilico cannot yet show a registrational efficacy result. It can show a novel biomarker story. Biological age is not an endpoint any regulator grades, and there's no path by which this figure appears on a label. But it is a differentiator you can market today. If translational biomarkers become what AI-native platforms sell ahead of efficacy data, the valuation input shifts from clinical probability toward narrative optionality. That widens, rather than narrows, the gap between headline deal values and committed cash this beat has documented all week. There's a tools-layer read too: the demand shows up not at the drug developer but at the proteomics vendors, with Olink-style panels sold as companion analytics on trials never designed to test them. Meanwhile the registrational question — whether rentosertib slows fibrosis over fifty-two weeks in the Phase III now running in China — is untouched by any of it.

Which brings me to who does the checking, and a correction this briefing owes. On September 5th we said on air that Roche now owns both Flatiron Health and TriNetX, the real-world-data networks. The Flatiron half is right. Roche bought that in 2018. The TriNetX half is wrong, and it looks like a straightforward conflation. The evidence against it is dated and concrete. On April 2nd, Regeneron announced it will invest up to 200 million dollars in TriNetX for licensed access to de-identified health records on roughly 300 million individuals, 170 million of them in the United States, to be linked against Regeneron's own genomic and proteomic databases. A company Roche owned outright would not be licensing its network to a Roche competitor for nine figures.

So: correction issued. And it matters beyond the fact itself. Even the ownership map of the least glamorous layer of this stack — the data plumbing — is being reported secondhand, and getting it wrong. It also updates a thread this beat had called dormant for three sessions running. Real-world data moved. It just moved with a correction rather than with a capability.

And the correction cuts against the optimistic read, not for it. Licensing, not acquisition, is the actual structure of this market. Regeneron is paying up to 200 million dollars for a denominator, not a moat. Other pharma companies can buy access to the same 300-million-patient network. That is a weaker competitive position for TriNetX than outright ownership would be. It also prices the data layer richer than anything the modeling layer built on top of it has demonstrated. Truveta's natural-language query layer over more than 130 million de-identified records is the comparison: a real capability, with no published case of it surfacing a new indication or confirming an epidemiological estimate. Nobody has published one for TriNetX either. The binding constraint is still normalizing balkanized, HITECH-era record formats — a curation problem, not an access problem — and a bigger licensed patient count does not touch it. Differentiation gets pushed back onto curation, which is the part nobody is funding.

One short beat on the other side of that coin. Xaira Therapeutics, the best-capitalized closed platform in this field, has reportedly begun courting outside partners to validate its compounds, having concluded internally that technological prowess alone is not enough. No partner, no deal value, no named candidate. This is outreach, reported secondhand, not a signed agreement. Read it only as direction. But note what the direction is: the company with the least commercial reason to admit it needs outside scrutiny is the one going looking for it.

Put it together and none of today's items move the two things that actually matter. Clinical success rates turn on registrational trials, not on a second pass through an existing dataset. The fifty-two-week Phase III that will settle rentosertib costs exactly what it did last week. And R&D spend keeps reallocating toward earlier, cheaper decisions rather than shrinking outright — reporting a Phase IIa a second way is nearly free, which is precisely why it happens. So the test to watch is not another biomarker story from a conference stage. It's a registrational endpoint, adjudicated by someone other than the company that designed the molecule.

That's the briefing. Have a good day.
