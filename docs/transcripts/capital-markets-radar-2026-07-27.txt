Good morning. It's Monday, July 27th, and this is your Capital Markets Radar. One oil-driven macro shock is now running through every asset class at once, and it comes to a head this week with the Fed.

Start with the bond market, because it's the discount rate underneath everything else this week. The 10-year Treasury yield hit 4.71% last Thursday, July 23rd, the highest since January 2025. The 30-year jumped 10 basis points on the week to 5.16%, briefly touching 5.19% intraday — levels that put it 153 basis points above the effective fed funds rate. This is bear steepening: hike risk building at the front end, term premium building at the long end, both at once, which squeezes bank net interest margins in a different way than a classic bear flattener would. Swap markets were pricing roughly a one-in-three chance of a hike at Wednesday's meeting — the CME FedWatch tool showed hike odds tripling from under 11% on July 15th to 35% by July 22nd. Consensus still expects a fifth straight hold, at 3.5 to 3.75%. But here's the update worth flagging: I'd expected the weekend's oil reversal to have already deflated that hawkish pricing by today. It hasn't. Data through July 24th and 25th still shows hike odds sitting at 36 to 38%, and the 10-year has only retraced about 7 basis points off its peak, to roughly 4.64%. So going into Wednesday, this is still a genuinely live, two-sided meeting, not a tail risk that's already faded. New Fed Chair Kevin Warsh has also dropped traditional forward guidance, so the committee's internal hold-versus-hike split is unusually visible right into the decision.

And here's the part that matters even if the Fed sits still: it's real yields doing the damage. The 10-year TIPS auctioned Thursday at a 2.44% real yield. That's the discount rate that actually compresses long-duration growth stocks and speculative assets, and it's climbing before the committee lifts a finger. A hike on top of that would be the first in years — a genuine regime signal for terminal-rate pricing, dollar funding costs, and every carry trade built on a stable front end.

Now the mechanism behind all of that: oil. Brent spiked above $100 a barrel last week for the first time since 2022, settling near $100.68 with WTI at $92.36, on Iran hostilities and Houthi attacks threatening both the Strait of Hormuz and Bab El-Mandeb. Then Sunday, the picture flipped. Brent fell more than 7% intraday, dipping below $90 before settling near $92, after the US paused strikes against Iran — even as the Houthis kept hitting Saudi targets. The dollar tracked the same move, pulling back from a one-month high near 101.5 on the dollar index. So the entire hawkish Fed repricing traces to this one commodity, and given how little the rates market has actually unwound so far, the honest read is that traders aren't fully buying the de-escalation yet. Worth flagging the other direction too: a single geopolitical headline swinging oil ten dollars in two sessions is also a market that may be over-reading any one data point, in either direction. And one thing that may not reverse even if oil does — as long as a Hormuz risk premium lingers, breakevens stay elevated and the case for energy-infrastructure and LNG reallocation holds, regardless of where spot crude settles this week.

That same discount-rate pressure is already showing up in how equities are pricing AI capex. Alphabet beat Q2 estimates badly — EPS of $9.11 against a $2.88 estimate, revenue up 24% year over year, Google Cloud up 82% with operating margin expanding to 35.6%. And the stock still fell about 4.2% after hours, because Alphabet raised full-year capex guidance to $195 to $205 billion, up from $180 to $190 billion. That's the read-through for the other hyperscalers still to report: a beat-and-raise is now getting sold on the raise, because investors are pricing AI infrastructure spending as a margin risk rather than a growth catalyst. There's a second, sharper point here from FactSet. The S&P 500's blended Q2 earnings growth rate jumped to 37.9%, the highest since 2021 — but strip out Alphabet alone, and that growth rate falls to 25.9%, with the earnings-surprise percentage collapsing from 39.3% to 12.6%. And breadth isn't the rescue either: 86% of reporters have beaten, above the five- and ten-year averages near 77%, but they're clearing a bar analysts had already marked down. Most of this week's "earnings boom" headline is one company's statistical artifact, not broad market strength. So if you're an allocator reading index-level earnings momentum as a signal for rotation, that signal is thinner than it looks.

Now the liquidity side of higher-for-longer: private credit. Apollo's Debt Solutions fund saw redemption requests reach 16.8% of shares, about $2.4 billion, up from just over 11% the prior quarter, and capped withdrawals at the standard 5% limit. Gross outflows near $700 million will more than double the $300 million coming in. Offshore investors are driving the rush — 12.5% of offshore capital asked to leave, against 4.3% onshore. Blackstone gated its flagship $79 billion BCRED fund at 5% too, its second straight quarter of capping withdrawals, after requests surged to 10%. And on July 20th, BCRED's co-CEO Jonathan Bock resigned; Blackstone says it's unrelated to fund operations, though that characterization comes from secondary reporting on the filing, not a verified primary document. Rising long-end yields make this worse on their own: the higher a 30-year Treasury pays, the higher the opportunity cost of staying locked in semi-liquid private credit, which compounds redemption pressure independent of any actual credit deterioration. And here's the divergence to watch — public high-yield spreads are still sitting near 269 basis points, historically tight, nowhere near pricing this stress. If that gap closes, if public credit starts widening too, that's the confirmation this is systemic rather than contained to retail-facing vehicles. Right now it isn't confirming.

Close the loop with Bitcoin. Spot Bitcoin ETFs had strung together seven straight days of inflows, roughly $1 billion, before reversing hard on July 24th with $225 million in net outflows — BlackRock's IBIT alone responsible for about 90% of that. And notice it was one fund, IBIT, driving that exit — the same vehicle that led the inflows. The marginal flow-setter for the entire ETF complex now sits in a single product, which is its own kind of fragility. Bitcoin dipped to $64,600 intraday before recovering to $65,403, on the exact same session Treasury yields hit their highest since January 2025. Ether ETFs actually added about $26 million that same session, so read this as Bitcoin-specific rather than a blanket crypto exit — though that Ether figure is provisional. Gold, meanwhile, traded around $4,090 an ounce and kept its bid, grinding higher through the same stretch. That's the tell: Bitcoin is still trading as a high-beta risk asset that turns with rates and oil headlines, not as the uncorrelated hedge its bulls describe.

So heading into Wednesday's decision, the question isn't really whether the Fed hikes — it probably won't. It's whether the oil de-escalation holds. If it does, yields ease, private credit gets some breathing room, and this week's cross-asset wobble stays contained. If it doesn't, everything you just heard tightens together at once.
