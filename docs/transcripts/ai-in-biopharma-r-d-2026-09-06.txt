Good morning, it's September 6th, 2026, and this is your briefing on AI in biopharma R&D.

I owe you a correction. On September 1st, 2nd and 3rd, this briefing closed on the same line: none of this touches Phase II, the expensive end of drug development. I said it three times in three days and treated it as settled. It was wrong. Two AI-originated drugs are in registrational Phase III trials right now, and the evidence for that has been sitting in the public record for months. Here's what that actually means, and, just as important, what it still doesn't.

Start with Insilico Medicine's rentosertib, a TNIK inhibitor for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. Insilico says the target was identified with AI and the chemical structure was designed with generative AI. Its own release describes the Phase III, called GENESIS-IPF, as a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study enrolling 320 patients across 47 centers in China, dosed once daily over 52 weeks. The primary endpoint is the annualized rate of decline in forced vital capacity. That is the company's own language. What the release does not say is when the trial started or when it reads out. Insilico discloses no completion date anywhere in it, whatever dates are circulating secondhand. So the honest framing is years out, with no date attached.

Now compare that to the evidence Insilico already has in hand, the Phase IIa result published in Nature Medicine. At the 60 milligram once-daily dose, patients showed a mean FVC change of positive 98.4 milliliters at 12 weeks, against negative 20.3 milliliters on placebo. That is the best data point the program has. But notice what it isn't. It is a 12-week absolute change, and the Phase III is powered on a 52-week annualized rate of decline. Those are different quantities measuring different things, in a disease that worsens continuously rather than in steps. A favorable 12-week snapshot is a weak predictor of a full-year slope. The Phase IIa was also small, and reported as run entirely in China, so its read-through to a global regulatory context is untested.

So here is the point to land. A company announcing it has started a Phase III is a milestone the sponsor controls and chooses to publicize. A primary endpoint, three or four years from now, is a number the sponsor cannot spin. That gap between milestone and result is exactly why this beat could hold a wrong standing claim for three days running without anyone's read getting updated. Announcements arrive constantly. Evidence arrives almost never.

And it isn't one program. Generate Biomedicines priced a 400 million dollar Nasdaq IPO in February at 16 dollars a share, the first AI-native generative-biology platform of this wave to go public. Its lead asset, GB-0895, is now in global Phase 3. That's an AI-engineered long-acting antibody against TSLP for severe asthma, running as SOLAIRIA-1 and SOLAIRIA-2, with a 52-week primary endpoint on exacerbation reduction. Generate's own framing is that the AI contribution was computational optimization of the antibody's affinity and half-life. That is engineering a dosing profile, not finding a new target, and the longer dosing interval is a design objective rather than a demonstrated clinical property.

Be clear about what exists there. No Phase 3 efficacy data. The human data to date is safety and pharmacokinetics. What two assets rather than one gives you is a state change rather than an anecdote. And because Generate is already public, its clinical outcome will print into a live equity price rather than into a private valuation nobody outside the deal can see.

Here's the pattern underneath both bets, though, and it cuts against the more excitable version of this story. TNIK inhibition in fibrosis and TSLP blockade in severe asthma are not exotic mechanisms. Both sit on biology the industry already understands, which is exactly what a field with zero approvals would choose in order to maximize its first win. So a win in either trial would show that AI-driven design can reach a plausible, differentiated molecule faster. That is a real result. It is not proof that AI is expanding the druggable space into biology nobody could otherwise reach. Those are two different claims, and this field conflates them constantly.

The asymmetry that matters for capital is what happens on failure versus success. If either trial reads out positive, the repricing lands unevenly. The platform that owns the asset gets re-rated on pipeline. A platform that only sells access to a design model gets re-rated on nothing, because somebody else's molecule succeeding does not validate your benchmark.

Failure is the more interesting case. Insilico and Generate are the only two names on this beat whose thesis can be falsified on a calendar date. The two biggest privately held platforms sit in a completely different position. Isomorphic Labs raised two point one billion dollars in May, from its corporate parent Alphabet plus sovereign wealth money at MGX, Temasek and the UK's sovereign AI fund. Chai Discovery raised four hundred million dollars in July at a three point eight billion dollar valuation. Between them those two rounds were most of the money that went into this field all spring. Nobody outside those deals ever set those prices, and no clinical result is owed to anyone in them. So a rival's Phase III miss would move their marks not at all. The sector's public price could move hard on one of these readouts while its private price sits still. That would be the first time this field's valuations move on evidence somebody else produced.

That connects to R&D spend, and I'll hold it to one line because I've made it all week. Whatever AI is saving at the discovery end is being spent right back at the registrational end at full price. Three hundred and twenty patients over 52 weeks, and two global Phase 3 trials over the same 52 weeks, are conventional, expensive, unhurried studies. Nothing here changes the cost curve of the expensive half of drug development. It only changes what gets fed into it earlier. What would change it is a Phase III win, not another funding round.

Which brings me to the software-layer version of the same question: who does the scoring. On August 18th, the FDA's Digital Health Center of Excellence published a discussion paper proposing a purpose-built regulatory framework for generative-AI-enabled medical devices, distinct from the agency's earlier lifecycle guidance for locked AI and machine-learning models. Comments are due October 19th, under docket FDA-2026-N-7874.

Be precise about what this is. It is a discussion paper. Not draft guidance, not a rule, and it imposes no legal obligation on anyone today. The agency is asking questions, and the modal outcome of a CDRH discussion paper is years of drift before anything becomes enforceable. It also sits inside the device center, so it says nothing about how the drug side of the agency treats AI in development tools or regulatory submissions. Resist the urge to read it as regulation finally reaching the discovery platforms that hold the capital. It doesn't. This is the third consecutive regulatory item on this beat to land on the diagnostics and device layer. First the EU AI Act, which bites on diagnostics sold as devices rather than on the discovery models built upstream. Then PathAI, cleared to update its pathology model within pre-agreed limits without refiling with the FDA. And now this docket. The burden keeps arriving at the layer holding the least capital, and investors still price both layers as one bucket.

So here's the honest scoreboard. This field now has two dated tests it cannot grade itself: rentosertib's Phase III endpoint, and GB-0895's Phase 3 endpoint. Everything else, the funding rounds, the platform launches, the discussion papers, is still self-scored, announced on the sponsor's own timeline in the sponsor's own language. That was true last week and it's true this week. What's different is that for the first time there's a clock running that the sponsor doesn't get to stop. Watch for the first disclosed completion date on either trial. That is the moment this sector acquires a deadline instead of a narrative.
