Cautious Optimism Briefings

Digital Money, Crypto, and Market Structure

Transcript · September 7, 2026

Good morning, it's Monday, September 7th, and this is your digital money briefing.

Twice in the past week this show has pointed at September 15th as the date that settles market structure in digital finance. Today the market finally put a price on that date, and the price collapsed.

Start with the mechanics. The Senate returns from recess on September 14th and holds a cloture vote on the Digital Asset Market Clarity Act the next afternoon. Cloture needs sixty votes. And cloture only opens debate. It is not a statute.

Now the price. Polymarket's contract on CLARITY passage has fallen from 82 percent in February to 16 percent as of September 6th. Galaxy Research, working from its own read of the whip count, cut its full-year passage odds from 50 percent to 30 percent. Those two numbers disagree by nearly a factor of two. Neither one is a vote count. They are two different guesses about a Senate that has not voted yet.

The vote math is also more fragile than the headline defection story suggests. Republicans hold fifty-three seats. If they hold at fifty-two, Majority Leader Thune needs eight Democrats or independents to cross. If support slips to fifty-one, he needs nine. That is a moving target, not a fixed number of seven.

And the wobble is not only hard no-votes. Senator Rounds has specific objections to the bill's stablecoin-yield language, but has signaled he will still vote to proceed while pushing for changes. So there are more open reservations inside the yes column than a simple defection count captures. On the Democratic side, the most recent public position is a joint statement from seven Democrats, including Booker, Warner and Warnock, issued July 22nd. They called the draft insufficient on ethics, consumer protection and illicit finance, and they explicitly did not walk away from the bill. Kirsten Gillibrand has separately drawn a harder line, demanding an enforceable ban on presidential crypto profits. No senator, in either party, has gone on record specifically on the September 15th vote since recess started. So what is being priced at 16 percent is a guess layered on a six-week-old guess.

What is actually stalling this bill is not disagreement over custody or exchange registration. It is an ethics fight over an estimated 1.4 billion dollars in President Trump's crypto income, plus the question of DeFi developer liability. Drafting cannot fix a political standoff.

Now, the number the sector keeps quoting for what is at stake needs a correction. Crypto press has been citing 1.35 billion dollars a year as the Coinbase stablecoin revenue that the bill's yield provision would legalize. That figure appears to be Coinbase's own reported full-year 2025 stablecoin revenue, not a forward number for this year. Coinbase's second-quarter 10-Q, filed with the SEC, reports stablecoin revenue of 598 million dollars for the first half of 2026. Annualize that and you get closer to 1.2 billion, meaningfully below the figure in circulation.

More important than the size of the correction is what the filing does not say. Nothing in it ties this revenue to CLARITY's passage, and nothing describes it as currently unlawful. Coinbase is earning and disclosing this money right now, under existing rules. So if cloture fails, that revenue does not disappear. It simply stays unlegalized in the narrow sense the provision addresses, without threatening what is already on the income statement. Failure here is a continuation of ambiguity, not a cliff, which means the sector's read on the direction of the risk is wrong.

There is a second-order consequence of that continuation worth naming. If the yield provision dies with cloture, a dollar token stays a settlement instrument rather than becoming a deposit substitute. That relieves near-term pressure on bank deposit funding costs, and it pushes yield-seeking on-chain dollars toward tokenized Treasury and money-market wrappers instead. It also means the rules keep getting written by the SEC and the CFTC rather than by Congress. That is the faster path, and it is the one a future administration can reverse without ever holding a floor vote.

If the policy catalyst is being priced out, what is the tape doing on its own? Bitcoin is back near 80,000 dollars, up about 24 percent since early August. That comes after a fall to 76,548 on September 2nd, in a liquidation event of 367.7 million dollars that forced out roughly ninety thousand traders.

The composition of the recovery matters more than the level. Shorts made up 78.4 percent of recent liquidations, so the move back through 80,000 has been led by short covering. Futures open interest, meanwhile, actually fell over the week, by about 890 million dollars, to 53.44 billion. Price rising while open interest falls is a deleveraging bid, not fresh accumulation.

That cuts both ways into a binary calendar. Less leverage on the pile means less mechanical fuel for a downside gap around the fifteenth and sixteenth. It also means less short-covering fuel for an upside squeeze. The honest expectation is a smaller move around those two dates than the positioning chatter implies.

Here is an on-air correction this show owes you. Open interest was reported here yesterday as rebuilding to 57.68 billion dollars. Today's dated series says it fell to 53.44 billion over roughly the same window. Those cannot both be true. Rather than adopt whichever number is newest, the honest conclusion is that this aggregated positioning data has now told three different stories in five days, and is not precise enough to carry an analytical thesis. Treat the direction as informative and the decimals as noise.

The ETF flow sequence makes the same point about reactive money. Spot Bitcoin funds bled 236.5 million dollars on September 1st. Two sessions later they took in 731 million, the biggest single inflow day since January. The next day the inflow was just 174.6 million. A flow that decays by three quarters in one session is money following price, not a steady institutional bid. And those are aggregator figures, not issuer filings, so take the shape and leave the decimals.

While Washington cannot decide whether a dollar token may pay yield, the banks stopped waiting. Twenty-one institutions, including Bank of America, Citi, Goldman Sachs, UBS and Wells Fargo, confirmed on September 1st that they will form a joint company in the second half of this year to issue a shared U.S. dollar stablecoin. They are targeting launch in the first half of 2027, with a euro token after that. And it will run on public blockchains rather than a private ledger. That last detail is the irony of the whole project. It validates the settlement infrastructure of the very issuers, Circle and Tether, that this consortium intends to compete with.

Note also where a bank token would compete first. Institutional settlement, where counterparty comfort dominates the decision. That happens to be the segment that generates the least float yield per dollar of volume, so any revenue hit to Circle and Tether would lag the share loss. Tether is the more exposed of the two, because it has no comparable institutional rail today. One caveat before anyone trades this. No primary release from the consortium has surfaced, the roster is not final, and what was announced is a plan to form a company that will later launch a product.

The sizing case for urgency is genuinely unsettled. Citi puts deposit displacement at 182 billion to 908 billion dollars by 2030. Standard Chartered projects roughly 500 billion leaving U.S. banks by the end of 2028. Treasury has flagged the entire 6.6 trillion dollar transactional deposit base as exposed. Those three use different methods and different horizons, so they do not blend into one number. The smallest estimate is 182 billion. The largest is the whole 6.6 trillion dollar deposit base, a factor of thirty-six. That spread tells you nobody has actually sized this threat.

The one measurable proxy tells a quieter story. Tokenized Treasuries, where yield-seeking on-chain dollars should already be landing, sat at roughly 15.1 billion dollars as of August 31st, with BlackRock's BUIDL fund back on top at 2.8 billion. That is a month-end snapshot rather than a live print. It is also essentially flat against the mid-May figure this show cited a day ago. If deposit flight in the hundreds of billions were underway, this segment would not be flat. The banks are building serious infrastructure against a risk the data has not yet confirmed.

One closing contrast, covered here on Saturday. Coinbase's stock jumped 10 percent on September 3rd on unapproved perpetual futures filings, on a sector-wide bid day, even as the market priced out the legislative cover for its stablecoin revenue.

What is checkable from here is narrow. The cloture roll call at 2:15 in the afternoon, Eastern time, on September 15th, and the Fed's decision the following day. Neither has happened yet, so treat everything ahead of them as positioning, not outcome.
