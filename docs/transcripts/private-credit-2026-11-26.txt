Good morning, and happy Thanksgiving. It's Thursday, November 26th, and this is your private credit briefing.

The clearest market test of private-credit marks this desk has seen didn't come from a default print. It came from a buyer that would only take a book at a discount. The deal is two weeks old, and we haven't covered it until now. Let's start there.

On November 11th, BC Partners' business development company, BCP Investment Corporation, agreed to buy Investcorp's BDC. Per Private Equity Wire's reporting, the deal values the Investcorp fund at a 10 percent discount to its net asset value. The same reporting values BCP itself, the acquirer, at 100 percent of its own NAV. The deal is announced and pending, not closed.

Here's why that matters more than another buyback. On Saturday, this desk called a BDC buying back its own shares below NAV one of the few market prices we get on private marks. A sale of the whole fund is the stronger version of that test. A buyback is a fund trading against its own stock price. Here, an outside buyer had to underwrite an entire loan book. And it agreed to pay less than the book claims to be worth.

But resist the easy read that the marks were simply ten percent too high. Look at the structure again. Investcorp is priced at 90 percent of its own NAV. BCP is priced at full NAV. So the discount isn't a price against an outside market. It's a spread between two managers' model marks. And it's paid in BCP's shares, whose value rests on marks set by BCP's own adviser. If BCP's marks run generous, the true gap is wider than the headline discount. We simply can't see that from here.

There are also two competing explanations for why Investcorp would sell at a discount at all. One is that the buyer didn't trust the underlying loan marks. The other is the economics of scale. A subscale BDC carries expense drag, fee leakage, and integration costs. Those can make its NAV worth less than its loans, even if every single mark is accurate. Both readings are plausible, and the reporting doesn't separate them. Either way, a sophisticated buyer would not take this book at its own marks.

One more caution on the lead. Private Equity Wire frames the deal as part of increasing consolidation across what it calls the 1.8 trillion dollar private credit market. That's the reporter's framing, and it rests on one deal. One sale is a data point, not yet a wave.

So why would a manager sell now? The same reporting names the driver directly. In its words, weaker valuations and redemption pressures encourage managers to consider strategic alternatives. That's a squeeze from two sides, the assets and the liabilities. Third-quarter reporting at the biggest platforms shows both.

Start with the assets. Bloomberg reported on November 12th that the largest listed BDCs, run by Ares, Blackstone, and Blue Owl, all made fewer loans in the third quarter than a year earlier. Take that as a direction, not a collapse. It extends the origination slowdown this desk covered on Saturday. And slower isn't stopped. Blue Owl Capital Corp, the manager's flagship listed BDC, still committed over 890 million dollars of new investments in the quarter, with fundings over 700 million. Origination at the top of the market is cooling, not freezing.

Now the liabilities, where the retail channel shows the pressure in hard numbers. Blue Owl runs two non-traded BDCs for individual investors. At Blue Owl Technology Income Corp, shareholders sought to pull 39 percent of shares in the third quarter. That's a slight rise from 38.1 percent the quarter before.

At Blue Owl Credit Income Corp, requests eased, to 16.8 percent of shares from 18.8 percent. Per Financial Advisor magazine's reporting, Blue Owl again limited redemptions at both funds to 5 percent.

So what's the actual risk inside those numbers? It isn't a credit loss. These vehicles are doing what they're designed to do when they prorate requests above the cap. The risk is a liquidity mismatch. Investors want their money faster than an illiquid loan book can pay it out. At Technology Income Corp, requests near 39 percent against a 5 percent cap, two quarters running, imply a backlog that keeps rolling forward.

Then watch how managers work through a queue like that. They can keep prorating, which leaves the stress with the investors stuck in line. They can draw on credit lines, which moves it to the banks. Or they can sell their most liquid loans, which leaves a weaker book for everyone who stays. On Sunday, this desk noted that bank exposure to these vehicles runs through lines against funds, not direct loans. A long redemption queue is exactly how that exposure would get tested. So the binding near-term risk in the retail channel is liquidity, not credit losses.

Now a genuine counterpoint, and it cuts against something this desk argued on Monday and again yesterday. At Blue Owl Capital Corp, payment-in-kind income fell to 9.9 percent of total investment income in the third quarter. That's down from 10.7 percent the quarter before. On its face, that's good news. It pushes back on our running read that PIK and accommodation are quietly flattering this cycle's default numbers.

But the data can't rule out a less flattering explanation. A falling PIK share can also mean PIK loans moved to non-accrual, or were restructured and left the book. That would lower the PIK share without any borrower returning to cash-pay health. And one manager's number isn't the sector's. The honest takeaway is dispersion. A large platform's book looks healthier on this one measure, while a subscale platform just sold at a discount.

Put the lead and the squeeze together, and a second-order effect comes into view. This is the desk's inference. Consolidation can work as a loss-recognition channel that bypasses the default statistics. When a subscale BDC is absorbed into a larger vehicle below its own NAV, the haircut shows up as a deal price. It never registers as a non-accrual or a default. It never touches the headline default rate.

The Investcorp sale also gives peers a reference point. Every other subscale manager's fourth-quarter marks can now be read against a negotiated price of 90 cents on NAV. If more boards run strategic reviews, the market gets more transaction prices on private books. And if consolidation does build, origination concentrates in fewer, larger hands. That would happen just as the largest platforms are themselves making fewer loans. For middle-market borrowers, that would mean fewer lenders competing for each deal, and more pricing power on the lending side.

So where does the cycle stand? Start with the default data. Reporting on Houlihan Lokey's third-quarter data, which this desk covered Monday, showed private-credit defaults nearly doubling, to 1.5 percent from 0.8 percent. So defaults are rising. But today's evidence says the default print isn't the whole story. Stress is also surfacing at the exits, in deal discounts and redemption queues. The public loan and bond markets gave this desk no fresh credit signal into the holiday, so that read rests on private credit alone. The biggest unresolved risk isn't credit quality in isolation. It's model-marked books sitting inside vehicles whose investors want out faster than the vehicles can pay.

Watch four things. First, whether other subscale BDC boards launch strategic reviews or announce sales. Second, the Investcorp deal itself, through to closing. Third, fourth-quarter tender results at the big non-traded funds, and whether Technology Income Corp's requests stay near 39 percent. Fourth, fourth-quarter BDC marks, read against that new 90-cent price.

That's the lending machine for November 26th. Stay cautiously optimistic, and enjoy the holiday.
