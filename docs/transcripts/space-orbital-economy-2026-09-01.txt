Good morning. It's September 1st, and this is your briefing on the space and orbital economy.

Start with Rocket Lab's quarter, because it is the cleanest split this sector has offered in a while between a business that flies and a business that is still a promise. Second-quarter revenue came in at a record 234.1 million dollars, up 62 percent year over year. Gross profit was 84.6 million. The company still posted a GAAP net loss of roughly 49.3 million on that record quarter. So record and profitable are two different claims, and only one of them is true today. The other headline number is the order book. Launch backlog grew past 90 missions, the highest in company history, after more than 437 million dollars of new bookings landed in the quarter and the weeks just after it. Note that the bookings figure blends launch work with space systems, so do not read all of it as rockets sold.

Here is the split that matters. Electron and its suborbital variant HASTE have flown thirteen times this year with full mission success, and that is the business that actually paid for the quarter. Most of the backlog is written against Neutron, the medium-lift vehicle that has never left the ground. What the company has committed to is producing a stage-one tank aligned with delivering Neutron to the launch pad in the fourth quarter. That is a delivery target, not a launch date, and Spaceflight Now reported on August 10th that the window for a 2026 debut is narrowing. A record order book on a rocket that flies every few weeks is a fundamentally different asset from a record order book on a rocket with no flight record at all. The market prices them as though they were the same instrument. The practical consequence is narrower than it sounds. Electron's cadence and gross profit are what fund Neutron internally, so a further slip costs Rocket Lab competitive position against SpaceX, ULA and Blue Origin on medium-lift and national-security work. It does not cost the company its solvency. The schedule risk here is a share-of-market risk, not a balance-sheet risk.

That same pattern, capital and concrete arriving ahead of flights, shows up in starker form at Blue Origin. On August 12th the company announced it is building a second pad at Cape Canaveral, Launch Complex 36B, explicitly to support the larger New Glenn variant and higher-cadence launch operations. That is a primary-source announcement from Blue Origin itself, not a rumour. To see why it lands oddly, go back to what happened to the first pad.

On May 28th, a static fire of a New Glenn booster exploded at the Cape. In our August 29th briefing we covered the diagnosis behind the resulting grounding. Chief executive Dave Limp traced that explosion to the main oxygen valve on a BE-4 engine, and in the same disclosure the Blue Moon Mark 1 lunar lander slipped to the first quarter of 2027. The part that got less attention is what the explosion did to the ground. It damaged Launch Complex 36 itself. Reporting from that night described at least one lightning-protection tower and the transporter-erector as destroyed. That same reporting said that until a full damage assessment was complete, it was impossible to know how long it would take to resume launch operations at the pad. LC-36 is Blue Origin's only orbital launch facility. Its repair timeline therefore gates every New Glenn flight, whatever a second pad eventually adds. Blue Origin has announced no return to flight since, and no primary source confirms one. Treat New Glenn's status as unresolved rather than restored.

Now step back to April, before the explosion, because the vehicle's high-water mark belongs there. The NG-3 mission was a genuine milestone. Blue Origin reused a New Glenn booster and landed it on a droneship, making it the second company after SpaceX to recover and refly an orbital-class booster. That deserves stating plainly before the caveat. The caveat is the upper stage. The second stage hit an off-nominal thermal condition, one of its BE-3U engines did not reach full thrust, and the payload was left in an off-nominal orbit. So put the sequence together. One partial success in April, a booster and a pad lost in May, and a second pad announced in August for a cadence the company has never demonstrated. The charitable read deserves its sentence. Pads have multi-year lead times, and building one during a stand-down is correct sequencing rather than overreach. The uncharitable read is that ground infrastructure is the cheapest and most controllable part of a launch business. Pouring concrete is not the same as solving engine and upper-stage reliability, and reliability is the actual constraint.

Now take the same phenomenon from the customer's side of the transaction. AST SpaceMobile launched three more BlueBird satellites, numbers 11 through 13, on a Falcon 9 on August 5th. The number worth having is not the launch. It is the factory. Production has advanced through satellite 42, against thirteen satellites launched. That is roughly three built for every one flown. Be careful about what that figure is. It is a manufacturing-line position from a single secondary source, not a count of flight-ready, funded, manifested hardware. Read it as inventory accumulating behind a launch queue rather than a triumphant scale-up.

Put the economics on it. Twenty-nine of those satellites have been built and none of them can earn a cent until somebody sells AST a ride. In the August 28th briefing we reported more than 3.7 billion dollars of pro forma cash after July's convertible offering, so raising the money to build was never the constraint. Roughly two thirds of the fleet AST has paid to build is therefore sitting as dead capital on the ground. AST discloses no revenue-per-satellite figure, so I will not put a dollar on it, but the direction is unambiguous. In that same August 28th briefing we framed AST as a fully funded company paced by somebody else's manifest, with its own 45-satellite year-end target sliding into early 2027 on launch availability. That slip does not just move a milestone. It pushes the start of the earning life of most of the constellation out by about a year, and a year of deferred first revenue lengthens the payback period on every satellite already financed. That tells you what the scarce input in direct-to-cell actually is right now. It is not the price of a launch. It is access to one.

Turn now to what happens when a system forces the flight rate instead of waiting on it. China's Long March 7A exploded roughly 85 seconds after liftoff from Wenchang on August 10th, taking the Zhongxing-4B communications satellite with it. That was the vehicle's first failure since its maiden flight malfunctioned in March 2020. Six years of clean flights ended in the middle of the most aggressive national launch manifest China has ever attempted.

The tempting read is that the megaconstellation buildout is straining quality control. Resist it. Long March 7A is not the vehicle that lifts Guowang or Qianfan satellites. It is a workhorse for geostationary payloads. So any read-through to constellation deployment pace is inference, not evidence. The root cause has not been publicly determined, the investigation is ongoing, and one failure in six years is not a trend on its own. What it does legitimately raise is a question. What does a state-directed flight rate cost in reliability? And if this triggers a broader stand-down across the Long March family, then the binding constraint on China's orbital land grab is not spectrum filings or slot claims. It is flights.

Close on the one clean counterexample of the past few days. NASA's Roman Space Telescope launched on a Falcon Heavy on August 30th, separated on schedule 31 minutes after liftoff, and is on its way to the Sun-Earth L2 point. No anomaly, no drama, on time. Be honest about what that is. It is a science mission on a mature vehicle, and it says nothing about the marginal cost per kilogram that would change anyone's business case. But it cuts one way that matters. Starship, the vehicle SpaceX's public valuation is increasingly written against, still has not put a payload into operational service. The asset compounding value for the company right now is the rocket it built years ago and simply keeps flying.

That is the thread through all of it. On August 29th we argued the binding constraint had moved from launch capacity to schedule credibility. Today's evidence moves it one step further along. The money, the factories and the concrete are all arriving faster than the flights are. What accumulates is backlog, inventory and ground infrastructure, not deployed capacity. The honest counter-case is that all three have long lead times, so building them during a stand-down is rational sequencing, and the whole correlation would resolve the moment one vehicle starts flying reliably. So watch two things from here. Starship's next flight, no earlier than mid-September. And whether Neutron's stage one actually reaches the pad before the fourth quarter runs out.
