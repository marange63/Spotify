Good morning, it's Saturday, November 14th, 2026, and this is your private credit briefing.

All week this desk has said private credit's back book is still waiting for its first external price. We pointed to ING's data-center loan auction, with bids due November 19th, as the next live price test. A price arrived first, and from a different direction. On Wednesday, November 11th, BC Partners' BCP Investment Corporation agreed to buy Investcorp's business development company, ticker ICMB, in an all-stock deal. It's the second BDC takeout in about two weeks, after Ares agreed to buy MidCap Financial. And it puts a transaction price on exactly the kind of book this desk said had none.

Start with the terms, because two different numbers are circulating. The deal itself is struck on net asset value. ICMB holders get new BCP shares worth 90% of ICMB's NAV at closing. That NAV is adjusted for transaction expenses, debt repayment and tax items. So 90% of adjusted NAV is not 90% of the last reported figure. The second number is a market-price calculation. Using BCP's share price on November 9th, the consideration worked out to about a dollar thirty-eight a share. That was a 126% premium to ICMB's 61-cent close. One yardstick is NAV, the other is market price, and they shouldn't be read as the same thing.

The gap between those two yardsticks is the story. Before the deal, the public market valued ICMB far below its own stated NAV. A buyer then agreed to pay close to that NAV. But that buyer is a strategic acquirer with its own motives, not a neutral market. And the sale resolves a strategic review, so the terms are the news, not the fact of a sale. Investcorp's CEO, Suhail Shaikh, was blunt: "ICMB's size and scale have been challenging to navigate."

Then came the market's verdict. ICMB's stock rose about 71% by Thursday's close. BCP's shares fell about 15.5%. ICMB's jump falls well short of the 126% headline premium. Part of that shortfall is simply BCP's own stock falling, which shrinks the value of what ICMB holders receive. Part of it is the risk that the deal doesn't close. So the gap isn't purely skepticism about the credit.

The easy read on BCP's drop is that it overpaid for stale marks. There are two more mundane explanations. In all-stock deals, merger-arbitrage desks routinely short the acquirer and buy the target. And this deal is paid in newly issued BCP shares, which can weigh on existing holders if BCP trades below its own NAV. Today's evidence can't separate those three stories, so don't award a clean verdict. What the deal does establish is that a sub-scale BDC book now has a real transaction price attached to it.

That price also works as a benchmark. Here's this desk's inference, not a forecast. Every other listed BDC trading below NAV now has an observable reference point. Boards, auditors and the plaintiffs in the BDC adviser cases this desk covered yesterday can all point to it. A quarterly mark gets harder to defend once a comparable book has actually changed hands.

The non-accrual figures released alongside the deal show why the buyer matters too. They belong to BCP, the acquirer, per its own third-quarter earnings release, not to ICMB. At fair value, BCP's non-accruals fell to 2.8% of the portfolio, from 3.1% in June. At amortized cost, they rose to 6.0%, from 5.7%. The two ratios use different denominators, so don't read a recovery rate off the gap. Stress is rising on a cost basis while the fair-value ratio looks calmer. In other words, the marks are absorbing more of the problem than the headline ratio shows. And this time that pattern showed up in the buyer, not just the target.

The same gap shows up at the sector's biggest buyer. Just over two weeks before the ICMB deal, Ares Capital agreed to buy Apollo-affiliated MidCap Financial, ticker MFIC. It's a stock-and-cash deal with an enterprise value of about 2.6 billion dollars. The price was a 30.1% premium to MFIC's prior close. That's a premium to market price, not to NAV. It's the same yardstick trap as the ICMB headline.

Ares Capital enters this deal under its own pressure. This is the bellwether whose slow NAV erosion this desk flagged yesterday. Its third-quarter core earnings came in at 46 cents a share, against a 48-cent consensus. A two-cent miss in one quarter isn't a trend on its own.

The credit line is more telling. Ares' non-accruals rose to 2.4% of investments at amortized cost and 1.4% at fair value. At the end of 2025 they stood at 1.8% and 1.2%. That's still low by any historical standard, and management calls it historically low. But the rise is wider at cost than at fair value, the same shape as BCP's book. So both buyers in this consolidation are absorbing other lenders' books while their own credit metrics soften.

Why is scale suddenly existential for the smaller players? Blue Owl's third quarter offers a mechanism, though it's one manager describing its own flow. Management said, "In direct lending, third quarter activity was driven heavily by existing borrowers." It echoes the Fullscript incumbency point this desk made on Thursday. And it comes as Blue Owl raised 49 billion dollars over the trailing twelve months. So capital is abundant. But the deal flow it chases is concentrated with lenders who already hold the borrower relationship. A sub-scale BDC with a thin borrower roster gets starved of that flow. Its earnings compress, and a sale to a bigger platform becomes the exit.

That makes scale partly defensive, not pure opportunism. It also makes consolidation a release valve. Losses on weak books are being realized as takeout discounts and falling acquirer share prices. Neither registers as a default. That's how a sector can keep reporting a benign default rate while real value is written down at the level of the vehicle. It's the soft-restructuring pattern this desk has tracked, one level up.

And this is no longer just two deals. Per Private Equity Wire, relaying Bloomberg, BlackRock's TCP Capital and WhiteHorse Finance "have said they are reviewing strategic options that could include a sale." Add ICMB and MidCap, and that's four BDCs in some stage of a sale process.

Fewer, bigger lenders raise a different question. The chair of the Financial Stability Board asked it directly yesterday. Bank of England governor Andrew Bailey spoke in that role, addressing private credit and stablecoins together. He said, "It may not move the system alone, but it could be one domino in a chain reaction." He added, "We should not wait for a risk to become large before examining its potential to generate contagion." And he framed the core question this way: "How large, or how connected, must private credit become before it poses a financial stability risk?"

Hold that carefully. These are speech remarks, not a policy action, and nothing binding follows from them. Bailey also wasn't singling out private credit. Still, the frame has moved from size to connection. And it lands days after the Dallas and New York Fed opened their pilot survey of direct lenders, citing limited visibility into new lending. That's two official voices in one week treating private credit as a visibility and connection problem.

Here's this desk's inference, not Bailey's claim. Consolidation cuts the number of independent vehicles. It pulls their loan books into fewer, larger balance sheets. That means fewer distinct marking policies. It also means bigger single counterparties for the banks and insurers that fund these lenders. That's the interconnection channel Bailey's remarks point toward. The counter-case is real. Bigger, more diversified platforms can be sturdier than a scatter of thin BDCs. Which effect dominates is an open question, not a settled one.

So where does the cycle stand? This is not a default-rate turn. Stress is being priced at the level of whole vehicles, through takeouts at a discount to NAV like ICMB's, while headline default numbers stay flattered. The biggest unresolved risk sits with the acquirers. They're absorbing weaker books under similar marking conventions. The question is whether those combined books hold up now that there's a transaction benchmark to measure every discounted BDC against.

Watch three things. First, ING's data-center loan bids, due November 19th, the next live price test. Second, whether TCP Capital or WhiteHorse actually transact, and at what fraction of NAV. Third, the first post-close quarterly prints from Ares and BCP. Read the gap between cost and fair-value non-accruals as MidCap and ICMB are folded in.

That's the lending machine for November 14th. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you next time.
