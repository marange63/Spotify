Good morning, it's September 28th, 2026, and this is your robotics and physical AI briefing.

For a week this desk has been asking one question about humanoid robots: who is actually paying for them. This week, the public market answered that question for Unitree, in real numbers.

A quick callback first. Two days ago we relayed an unverified report that Beijing was slowing humanoid IPOs over state-linked data-collection revenue. That report named no regulator, no company, and no number. Reuters and The Robot Report have now supplied the companies and the numbers. The regulator, as you'll hear, is still not on the record.

Start with the scale of the move. Unitree, the Chinese quadruped and humanoid maker, listed in Shanghai last month. Its shares soared 460% on the first day of trading. At the peak, the company was worth roughly 66 billion dollars, more than 250 times its 2025 revenue. Reuters now has the stock down 55% from that peak.

Here's the honest framing, though, because this is not a failed IPO. By this desk's arithmetic, Unitree still trades at about 3.4 times its IPO price, so anyone who got an allocation is deeply in profit. The same arithmetic puts today's implied valuation at around 120 times trailing 2025 revenue. What broke was a speculative peak above 250 times revenue, not the listing itself. So the market has cut the multiple hard, but it has not yet priced Unitree like an ordinary hardware company.

So what actually triggered the re-rating? Revenue quality. Unitree's own filing response to the Shanghai exchange breaks out its humanoid revenue by customer type for the first nine months of 2025. Research and education customers took 73.6% of it. Industrial applications took just 9%. That's a flagship product line bought mostly by universities and labs, not by factories putting robots to paid work.

Now add the funding side. Part of the sector's robot revenue comes from data-collection centres buying robots. Reuters reports that in some projects, local-government joint ventures fund most of the initial investment. That describes the sector's structure, not Unitree specifically. But it explains why an analyst quoted by Reuters estimates that valuations at some robot companies could fall 60 to 70% if revenue tied to data-collection centres were stripped out. That's one analyst's estimate, not a disclosed revenue split. The point is that a meaningful slice of reported demand may rest on public money rather than on paying end customers.

Two caveats matter here. First, the regulatory mechanism is reported, not confirmed. Reuters describes it as informal "window guidance." The China Securities Regulatory Commission did not respond to a request for comment. And the three pipeline companies Reuters named, AGIBOT, Deep Robotics, and X Square Robot, did not respond on whether regulators slowed their plans. So treat the "freeze" as reported and unconfirmed, and note that sources disagree on how hard it bites. Second, there's a competing explanation worth naming plainly. A stock that rises 460% on its first day is going to mean-revert hard with or without a regulatory story. The evidence can't cleanly separate ordinary post-IPO froth unwinding from genuine revenue-quality scrutiny. Both are probably at work.

The per-unit math sharpens the picture. Unitree booked 868 million yuan of humanoid revenue in full-year 2025, across more than 5,500 humanoids shipped. By this desk's arithmetic, that's roughly 158,000 yuan per robot, or about 23,000 dollars. It's a blended average across small research units and larger models, not a list price. But a 23,000-dollar average is a low-ticket, lab-weighted book, and it fits the nine-month customer mix.

Zoom out, and the second-order effect is capital access. Reuters counts at least six more humanoid companies preparing to go public. If that window has narrowed, their capital gets pushed back toward private rounds or state-directed money. And Reuters says some private-market projects have already taken valuation cuts of 30 to 50%. Here is this desk's read on two further consequences. First, Unitree's re-rating becomes the first public comparable that private Western humanoid rounds get marked against. Second, the shipment counts that dominate the humanoid bull case may overstate paying demand. If a large share of Chinese volume is research and data-collection purchasing, then China's scale lead in units is partly a subsidized-demand lead. So what: units without a paying customer are now being marked down in real time.

One of those pipeline companies answered the scrutiny with a deployment that same week. AGIBOT, last year's global humanoid shipment leader, is one of the IPO candidates Reuters named. On September 24th it delivered its 20,000th production robot to Chimelong, as part of a launch of more than 300 robots at Chimelong Spaceship Park. That 20,000 is cumulative production across AGIBOT's whole line.

The robots cover seven guest-facing roles. They include martial-arts and tai chi performances, education, visitor guidance, retail service, companionship, hotel service, and sports activities. They run on a dedicated 5G-A network built with China Mobile, under a centralized multi-robot coordination system.

Test this against the standard the Unitree story just set. Is this paid, productive work, or a showcase? The announcement discloses no contract value and no per-robot cost. It doesn't say whether the robots run autonomously or with teleoperation. That's the announcement's silence, not proof the deployment is unpaid. What we can observe is centralized coordination over a dedicated network, plus choreographed performances like tai chi. That is consistent with scripted routines or remote assistance. It is not evidence of onboard general autonomy. And because AGIBOT is itself waiting on a listing, a high-visibility deployment also serves its IPO case.

The hypothesis worth holding, though, is that leisure and hospitality are forgiving environments. Failures are cheap, and a performance counts as success. That could make them a faster route to real revenue than a factory floor. So what: Chimelong is the kind of evidence the market now wants, but until AGIBOT attaches a price to it, it's a showcase not yet proven paid.

The same disclosure question applies on the other side of the Pacific. Tesla's Optimus ramp, from dozens of units a week to hundreds, was covered in depth two days ago. Two things are new. Tesla is reportedly aiming for a production line capable of more than 1,000 Optimus units a week by year-end. And reporting indicates Tesla plans to lease Optimus rather than sell it, to keep its data feedback loop. Both points come from secondary reports and are unconfirmed by Tesla.

But note what leasing does. It hides unit price and margin from public investors. That is exactly the disclosure the Chinese market just started demanding of Unitree. And remember, line capacity is not output, and output is not paid demand.

Pulling it together. This week's news was about capital and disclosure, not capability. Nothing at the model layer this week moved the picture. Where embodied AI is genuinely growing is in units built and robots deployed, especially among the Chinese humanoid makers. Where it is still unproven is the paying customer behind those units, and the market has started pricing that gap. Unitree is the first case where a humanoid valuation met a public float and got tested against real disclosure. The open questions are whether any Chinese regulator ever puts its name to this test, and whether AGIBOT ever says if Chimelong is a paid contract. On the American side, watch whether Tesla puts leasing terms or real Optimus output into a primary filing rather than a secondary report. And watch Unitree's first post-listing periodic report for the next hard data point.
