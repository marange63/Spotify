Cautious Optimism Briefings

Digital Money, Crypto, and Market Structure

Transcript · September 19, 2026

Good morning, and welcome to Cautious Optimism Briefings for Saturday, September 19th.

Three sessions ago, this show said the crypto sector had lost its irreversibility when the CLARITY Act died in the Senate. We also said the idea that agencies would fill the gap was industry expectation, not evidence. This week supplied the evidence. The SEC and the CFTC redrew the perimeter inside forty-eight hours. They redrew it in pencil, faster, and the market treated that as a victory.

Start with the vote, but only as setup. Cloture failed forty-nine to fifty, with zero Democratic support. Reporting ties the breakdown to ethics and conflict-of-interest provisions covering public officials' crypto holdings. Coinbase's Brian Armstrong said, quote, "I would just at this point assume it's dead." He added that, quote, "there's another path, luckily, with the regulators, the SEC, and the CFTC." That is the industry conceding that statute is off the table.

The agencies moved on Thursday. The SEC issued what it calls an Innovation Exemption for tokenized stock. The order grants a five-year conditional exemption from the definition of exchange. It covers venues that use automated market makers and liquidity pools. A second five-year exemption covers liquidity providers from the definition of dealer. Both expire five years after publication.

The conditions are where the economics live, and the SEC's own fact sheet lets us read some of them. Tokenized stock on these venues is subject to limits on the number of symbols and the volume traded, and the fact sheet publishes no numbers. So the size of that cap is the single most important unknown in the whole trade, and the market bid the sector up without it.

Other conditions cut both ways. Venues must stop trading a tokenized stock when the underlying halts on its primary listing exchange. That ties the tokenized book to the incumbent book. And where an unaffiliated third party tokenizes a stock, the venue must give the issuer written notice and a chance to object. That hands incumbent issuers a lever over crypto-native venues. Some coverage read a thirty-day clock in the fine print as that issuer objection window. It is not. The thirty days is the venue's own website notice before it starts operating.

Now the CFTC, where the headline overstates the stage. The agency sent a rulemaking called Regulation of Crypto Asset Transactions and Crypto Asset Markets to the White House review office on Thursday, September 17th. Per an explainer citing the federal regulatory database, it is classified as a prerule with no legal deadlines. That is a step before a proposal. The same explainer says two comment periods and two reviews come before a binding rule, in late 2027. That is secondary reporting, not CFTC guidance. If the route holds, it would let crypto exchanges be designated as a type of derivatives market and offer leveraged trading.

So what is the takeaway? Speed is being mistaken for durability. A five-year conditional exemption and a prerule are thin foundations for institutions asked to rebuild plumbing. A future SEC or CFTC can withdraw both without a floor vote.

Here is the first thing built on that permission. On Friday, Coinbase filed to list more than fifty single-stock perpetual futures on its US exchange. Nvidia, Microsoft and Tesla are named. The product offers, quote, "24/5 exposure to individual stocks," with no expiry. Coinbase calls it the first US product of its kind.

Compare that with Coinbase's earlier filing on September 1st. We called that one a filing, not a product, with no named underlyings. Three weeks later the vagueness has resolved in one direction. Still, this is Coinbase's own announcement. The approving regulator and the timeline are not stated, and nothing is approved.

The tape paid up anyway. According to The Defiant, the tokens that ran hardest were the ones tied to trading securities onchain. Crypto equities also jumped. Aggregators disagree on the size, from the teens to about thirty percent depending on the name, so read direction, not precision. That is a relief trade on headline permission, before anyone has seen the caps.

Two skeptical points. First, tokenized stock on an automated market maker is only as liquid as the market makers arbitraging against the primary exchange book. On a macro-gap day, that arbitrage leg is exactly where liquidity withdraws. The tokenized venue inherits national market system volatility without national market system depth. Second, twenty-four-hour equity perps mostly relocate leverage that already exists offshore. If the CFTC route survives, that migration onshore validates Coinbase's model. It also competes with it, and squeezes take rates.

The same relief trade showed up in bitcoin, and the mechanics there are uglier. Bitcoin touched eighty-one thousand dollars on Friday, its best level since September 4th. The Defiant had it at eighty thousand eight hundred and twenty-nine dollars late Friday morning, up five point eight percent over twenty-four hours. Prints vary by timestamp, so treat eighty-one thousand as the round number.

Consider what it defied. The Fed raised the target range by twenty-five basis points on Wednesday, to three and three-quarters to four percent. The ten-year was back near five percent, on a CNBC headline; the exact level is unverified. Chair Warsh told reporters, quote, "I'm not in the forward guidance business."

On Thursday's show we argued that a five percent ten-year raised the hurdle rate against a zero-yield asset. Bitcoin rallied anyway. Grading that call honestly, rates did not stop mattering. They were simply not the marginal driver this week. Macro set the backdrop. Positioning set the price.

So what was the driver? CoinDesk, citing CoinGlass, reported roughly four hundred and seventy million dollars of short crypto derivatives liquidated in twenty-four hours. Daily Hodl put total liquidations at five hundred and forty-seven million, with shorts at four hundred and sixty-nine million of that. Those are aggregator estimates, not venue disclosures. But both point the same way. The move was mostly shorts being forced out.

Implied volatility fits that story. Deribit had bitcoin implied vol between thirty-eight and thirty-nine percent across tenors, with put-call skew close to neutral. That reading is dated September 16th, so it describes positioning into the events, not the squeeze itself.

So what? This was mechanics, not repricing. A short-covering rally with flat implied vol has no second engine unless spot demand shows up. Which brings us to the two hardest measures of demand.

Start with the ETF wrapper. US spot bitcoin ETFs took in one hundred fifty-nine and a half million dollars on Thursday, their first daily inflow since Monday. But for the week through Thursday, the net was an outflow of four hundred and twenty-seven million. On the same aggregator data, Thursday's inflow came entirely from BlackRock's IBIT, while Fidelity and VanEck saw redemptions. That is one issuer carrying the headline. Ether ETFs saw outflows of about thirty-nine million, a third consecutive day. Friday's flows had not published at the research cutoff.

Now the bull case in its strongest form. August took in roughly three and a half billion dollars, including a nine-day inflow streak into August 27th. The wrapper can switch back on fast. So one negative week is noise by itself. What makes it more than noise is the concentration, and the second measure.

That second measure is Strategy. Its September 14th filing shows holdings unchanged at roughly eight hundred and forty-five thousand bitcoin. That is the second straight week without a purchase. The average cost is about seventy-five thousand four hundred dollars a coin, so spot is barely above it.

Instead of buying bitcoin, Strategy spent about one hundred and thirty-nine million dollars repurchasing its own STRC preferred. The premium that financed four years of accumulation has essentially closed. By the company's own account, enterprise mNAV is one point one. Our caution on the sixteenth that one buyback is not a regime change has been overtaken. Two weeks of zero purchases is a regime change.

There is a rate link too. With the Fed hiking into a ten-year near five percent, Treasuries compete directly with Strategy's preferred paper. That raises its cost of capital just as the equity premium disappears. The marginal corporate bid is fading while ETF flows sit negative.

Pull it together. The sector got its rules back from agencies instead of Congress, and bid up everything that trades on permission. Meanwhile the two things that require someone to write a check both went the other way. Two tests come next. Does the ETF wrapper confirm next week? And do the exemption's caps leave room for real volume?

That is the briefing for today. Thanks for listening, and have a good weekend.
