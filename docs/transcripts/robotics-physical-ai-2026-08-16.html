Robotics & Physical AI

Good morning. It's Sunday, August 16th, and this is your robotics and physical AI briefing.

All week this show has been tracking access gates swinging open for the hardware makers — Waymo's county expansions, Zoox's first fares, Unitree's oversubscribed order book. Today the freshest signal sits one layer down. It's about who supplies the brain underneath all of them. Increasingly, the answer is Nvidia.

Start with the lead. On August 4th, Nvidia open-weighted Alpamayo 2 Super, a frontier reasoning model for autonomous driving, and put it on Hugging Face under a permissive commercial license called OpenMDW-1.1. That license matters as much as the model: anyone can fine-tune it, build derivatives, and redistribute them without asking Nvidia for permission. Nvidia's own newsroom settles a parameter count that had been reported inconsistently elsewhere: this is a 34-billion-parameter vision-language-action model, built from a 32-billion-parameter reasoning component paired with a 2-billion-parameter action expert. On the industry's LingoQA benchmark, scored by its Lingo-Judge truthfulness classifier, Alpamayo 2 Super topped the leaderboard — beating Qwen2.5-VL, a model more than twice its size, by 17 points, and edging out Gemini 2.5 Pro and GPT-4o as well. The model family has now passed 500,000 downloads on Hugging Face.

Here's the skeptical turn, and it comes straight from Nvidia's own documentation. The company describes Alpamayo 2 Super as a cloud-side teacher, evaluator, and data-labeling engine — not an onboard driver — and it explicitly calls for system-level validation before anyone deploys it. So downloads are not deployments, and a benchmark win is not a safety result. On that last point, dig into what LingoQA actually measures. It's a vision-language question-answering benchmark: the model watches driving footage and answers questions about what it's doing and why, and Lingo-Judge scores whether that answer matches what a human would say, calibrated to a 0.95 correlation with human judgment. That's a test of whether the model's stated reasoning sounds right. It is not a closed-loop test of whether the vehicle avoids a collision, and it says nothing about disengagement rates. Topping that leaderboard is a genuine capability signal, but don't let it stand in for a fielded safety record.

The adoption question does have a real answer now, though. Mercedes-Benz has named Nvidia Alpamayo as core to its planned S-Class robotaxi platform, a program built alongside Nvidia and Uber. Nvidia's own blog post on that partnership says Alpamayo "enables vehicles to drive smoothly and naturally like a human driver while reasoning step by step through complex situations to choose the safest possible action." That's a named automaker committing to the Alpamayo family for an actual robotaxi program — worth noting the commitment is to the family broadly, not confirmed specifically as the 2 Super checkpoint released this month. Still, it's the clearest evidence yet that this isn't just a weights dump nobody uses.

Put those two facts together and you get the real story: Nvidia is running the same playbook it used with GR00T in humanoid robotics, now extended into autonomous driving. Open the model, let the ecosystem build on it, and collect the platform rent regardless of which chassis or which robotaxi operator ends up winning. If that holds, it puts real pressure on vertically integrated stacks like Waymo's and Tesla's, whose moat has partly rested on having trained their own perception and planning models from scratch. A frontier base model available under a commercial license lets smaller AV developers, and Chinese entrants like Baidu Apollo, Pony AI, and WeRide, fine-tune rather than build from zero. That's a directional risk to those integrated moats, not a proven outcome yet — but it's the trend to watch.

Now the same bet, showing up on the factory floor. On August 13th, LG and Nvidia signed an agreement at Nvidia's Santa Clara headquarters — LG's chairman and Jensen Huang both in attendance — committing LG to build a bipedal humanoid robot on Nvidia's Isaac GR00T foundation model, running on Jetson Thor compute onboard, with Nvidia's Halos safety system layered in. That's another large industrial conglomerate, following Hyundai and Boston Dynamics, choosing to build on Nvidia's shared brain rather than develop its own. The public unveiling is targeted for the first quarter of 2027, and as of today Nvidia and LG have disclosed no degrees of freedom, no payload figures, and no cost target. This is a roadmap, not a product.

What is shipping sooner is far less glamorous. LG will validate its existing wheeled CLOiD robots on its own washing-machine manufacturing line in Tennessee sometime within 2026 — ahead of any humanoid, and ahead of any broader rollout. That gap is the honest takeaway here: the bipedal humanoid gets the press release and the eighteen-month timeline, while a wheeled, task-specific robot is the thing actually going into a real US production line this year. That's the pattern across this sector right now — narrow, wheeled, purpose-built automation reaching the factory floor well before general-purpose humanoids do.

Finally, the counter-model to Nvidia's open-platform bet, and it's sitting in Shanghai. Unitree priced its STAR Market IPO at 150.80 yuan a share, raising roughly 6.1 billion yuan, about 905 million dollars, for around 10 percent of its enlarged share capital. The retail tranche was oversubscribed more than 8,000 times over, a record for a tech startup on that exchange. Unitree is already profitable and is the world's largest humanoid maker by unit sales — the first humanoid robot maker to list on a mainland Chinese exchange at all. Where Nvidia is selling an open, shared brain that commoditizes the hardware underneath it, Unitree is the opposite bet: vertically integrated, mass-manufactured, profitable hardware, built in a country that holds an estimated 85 percent of the global humanoid robot market. That dominance is exactly why the FCC added foreign-made humanoid and quadruped robots to its covered list on July 29th, banning new equipment authorizations in a move that targets China directly — though that ban is forward-looking and doesn't touch units already authorized or in the field. Trading in Unitree's shares has not begun as of this recording; watch for a debut sometime in the window of August 17th through 21st. Treat any triple-or-more valuation pop you've seen floated as pre-listing speculation from illiquid private-share and derivatives platforms, not a real print.

Pulling the thread together: the headlines this month have been about access — counties opened to robotaxis, fares turned on, an IPO book overfilled eight-thousand-fold. But the durable value question is being answered quietly, one layer down, in whether the model and compute substrate underneath all of this consolidates around an open standard like Nvidia's, or stays walled inside vertically integrated hardware bets like Unitree's. Watch three things from here: whether any other named automaker follows Mercedes onto the Alpamayo family, what Unitree's actual debut price says about public-market appetite for humanoid unit economics, and whether an open frontier driving model genuinely erodes Waymo's and Tesla's integrated advantage — or turns out to be a tool they use too.

That's the briefing. Talk to you next time.
