Cautious Optimism Briefings

AI in Biopharma R&D

Transcript · September 13, 2026

Good morning, it's September 13th, 2026, and this is your briefing on AI in biopharma R&D.

This beat has spent the whole week on platforms selling optionality on unproven science. Today, look instead at where pharma is actually spending cash on AI, right now, before any clinical result exists.

The lead is Owkin. On September 2nd, Owkin announced a license agreement with Boehringer Ingelheim covering K Pro, its so-called AI Scientist for biopharma research, together with access to Owkin's multimodal patient data, aimed at oncology and immunology discovery. That announcement follows an initial pilot the two companies ran in 2025.

What makes it worth leading on is that it is the third such license in four months. AstraZeneca signed a three-year K Pro license in May, aimed at competitive-intelligence and decision-support workflows. Sanofi signed a five-year K Pro license in June, to co-develop biopharma agents. Three separate large-cap pharma R&D organizations, three multi-year commitments, inside four months. That is a pattern rather than an anecdote — though the primary trade reporting on it was inaccessible during research, so the Boehringer characterization comes through secondary aggregation rather than a release we could read directly.

Here is the limit on that pattern. No financial terms are disclosed for any of the three licenses. Follow-up research this week went to the primary sources to check, and the answer came back closed. Owkin's own release on the AstraZeneca deal describes the license as a three-year licensing of K Pro and says nothing about what AstraZeneca pays for it. There is no upfront, no annual fee, and no Owkin revenue figure anywhere in the public record. So the logo count is the signal, and the economics are unknown. Don't let anyone size this for you.

Now, what are these licenses actually for? Owkin's AstraZeneca release says K Pro autonomously orchestrates a suite of AI skills and tools to decode complex biology, that it is built on Owkin's multimodal patient data network, and that the agents are meant to help AstraZeneca's decision-making teams with complex competitive-intelligence questions. Sanofi's license is for co-developing biopharma agents. None of that is molecule design. It is competitive intelligence, decision support, and agent-assisted knowledge work.

So draw the conclusion the buyers are implying. Pharma is purchasing language-model workhorse capability for research operations, not outsourcing discovery itself. Discovery is being disaggregated into components and bought piecemeal rather than contracted out whole.

That distinction matters commercially, because it is a different asset class than the one this sector is usually valued on. A milestone-and-royalty pipeline bet pays only if a molecule survives the clinic. A multi-year enterprise license pays Owkin whether or not any drug ever works. It is the first item this beat has covered all week where the money moves before any clinical result exists. And a buyer with a budget who renews is a more informative test of a platform than any scientific committee with nothing at stake.

Follow that disaggregation one step down the supply chain, because it lands on somebody. If the buyer is assembling the AI stack in-house from licensed components, the addressable market for full-service discovery collaborations shrinks, and so does the market for CRO informatics offerings that were sold as the assembled version of the same thing. Pharma is renting the pieces, not the finished system. That is a quieter competitive story than any molecule, and it has no readout date attached.

If that knowledge-work layer is the one getting paid, the next question is who else is selling into it. The frontier labs are. OpenAI's GPT-Rosalind, a reasoning model for biology and translational medicine, is in research preview through a trusted-access program, with OpenAI working with biopharma and research organizations including Amgen, Moderna, the Allen Institute and Thermo Fisher Scientific. Anthropic launched a life-sciences product line, Claude Science, and Bristol Myers Squibb's own release states it will deploy Claude broadly as a shared intelligence platform, empowering more than thirty thousand employees. That thirty-thousand figure is the only hard scale number in this whole layer, and note what it measures: how many people can open the tool, not what BMS pays or what work actually moves through it.

These are the same accounts and substantially the same tasks Owkin licenses K Pro for. And there is no benchmark data anywhere comparing GPT-Rosalind or Claude Science against a specialized biology model, or against Owkin's own agents. Availability in a trusted-access program is a distribution fact, not a capability fact, and these launches are backdrop from earlier this year rather than fresh news. So the honest read is that the vertical vendor's defensible asset is not the agent layer, which a frontier lab can replicate. It is the multimodal patient data underneath it, which the frontier labs do not have.

It is worth being concrete about why that asset is hard to copy. Multimodal patient data means records for the same individual patients linked across modalities — clinical course, images, molecular profiles — rather than text scraped from the open web. That data does not exist in a crawlable corpus. It sits inside hospital and health-system systems, in the balkanized electronic-record formats the post-HITECH build-out left behind, and it moves only under consent and privacy terms negotiated institution by institution. That is a permissions-and-plumbing moat rather than a compute moat, which is exactly the kind a frontier lab cannot buy its way past on a training run. But hold the skepticism here too: Owkin calls its network unrivalled, and the public record gives no patient count, no institution count, and no measured performance result from it. The moat is asserted by the vendor and not yet measured by anyone else.

That also answers the standing question on this beat about which models do which work. The lane the frontier labs are contesting is reasoning over text and structured records: literature synthesis, competitive intelligence, decision support, agents for operations. The specialized architectures still own the other lane — geometric and diffusion models for structure and molecule design, perturbation-prediction models for cell biology, vision models for pathology and imaging. Nothing in today's record shows a general reasoning model displacing those. What today's record does show is that the contested lane is the one currently attracting the cheques.

Now notice what none of this touches. A 2026 regulatory-focused industry survey of digital and AI-enabled models in pharmaceutical development and manufacturing found that fewer than fifteen percent of the AI tools it counted had ever been included in an actual regulatory submission, and described CMC and manufacturing as still in digital infancy relative to discovery. The summary available does not publish the respondent base or the tool count behind that percentage, so take it as directional rather than precise. But the boundary it draws is the organizing line of this briefing. Competitive intelligence, decision support, agent orchestration — everything in today's licenses sits on the side of that line where no regulator ever reads the output.

Be careful with the fifteen percent both ways. It might mean the tools don't hold up under scrutiny. Or it might mean sponsors are using AI extensively in submission-adjacent work while declining to formally name a model as a submission element, under a framework that is only eight months old — an accurate count of disclosure and an undercount of use. Either way, the boundary is real, and it is exactly where today's spending stops.

One closing fact anchors it. No AI-discovered or AI-designed drug has received full FDA approval, even as AI-associated candidates have moved further into clinical development than at any prior point. That includes the AI-discovered fibrosis candidate rentosertib, which started its Phase III trial this week. The FDA and EMA jointly released ten guiding principles for AI in drug development on January 14th of this year, but those are high-level principles, not a review-time commitment, so resist calling the agency a bottleneck. There is no queue-length or backlog figure to support the word. The more defensible read is that the constraint is evidentiary rather than computational. Sponsors are not yet submitting AI-dependent packages in volume, which the submissions survey backs up directly.

So here is where that leaves the sector. The money in AI-for-pharma is accumulating on the unregulated side of the line — the knowledge-work layer that does not set drug economics and does not move clinical success rates. That reallocates R&D spend toward the cheap end of the funnel without necessarily shrinking it.

It also means AI-platform equities are being priced as one story when they are really two: cash-generative enterprise software with visible renewals, and binary clinical-pipeline bets that only get marked on a readout. Those do not deserve a shared valuation framework. Watch the first of these license renewals. That is when frontier-lab competition first tests a vertical vendor's pricing power, and it is the moment the undisclosed economics finally become observable — the sector's first real price signal for this layer.

That's the briefing. Talk tomorrow.
