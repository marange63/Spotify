Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Credit & the Corporate Credit Cycle

Transcript · November 16, 2026

Good morning, it's Monday, November 16th, 2026, and this is your private credit briefing.

All week this desk has tracked one direction of traffic: risk moving from banks to non-banks. A failed syndication pushed Fullscript's buyout financing toward private lenders. ING put data-center loans up for sale to private credit and insurers. Morgan Stanley estimated private credit could supply over half of the AI data-center build. Every one of those pointed the same way. Bloomberg reported on Thursday that traffic is also running the other way. That's today's lead.

Per Bloomberg, the largest publicly traded BDCs run by Ares, Blackstone, and Blue Owl all made fewer loans in the third quarter than a year earlier. Bloomberg gives two reasons. Private equity dealmaking stayed lackluster. And Wall Street banks lured away a record of nearly twelve billion dollars of deals with cheaper prices. That figure comes from a single Bloomberg piece. No filing or second outlet corroborates it, so treat it as reported, not confirmed. Be precise about the other half too. "Fewer loans" is a gross-origination claim. It doesn't say these BDCs' portfolios shrank, because repayments drive net growth separately.

Separate those two causes, because only one matters for the thesis this desk has been building. Soft sponsor dealmaking is cyclical. It says nothing about who wins the next deal when volume returns. Banks undercutting private lenders on price is competitive. That's the one that compresses the illiquidity premium direct lenders have charged on sponsor loans. Bloomberg also reports that managers say they're lending more prudently. That follows the investor rebuke over the industry's exposure to software borrowers seen as vulnerable to AI. Hold that as the managers' own description, not proof of tighter terms. But the shape fits. In effect, the largest lenders ceded volume rather than match the banks on price. That's the pattern this desk named on Sunday. In this cycle, stress shows up in quantity, not in spread.

Here's the inference worth naming out loud, and it's this desk's, not Bloomberg's. If banks are taking back the cleanest, most syndicatable credits, private lenders keep more of what banks don't want. Think smaller names, Fullscript-type deals the loan market passed on, and long-dated construction risk like the data-center loans ING is still trying to sell. Aggregate origination and loss numbers won't show that mix shift. A book can get riskier at the margin and still report the same blended average until something breaks. The benign counter-case deserves equal weight. This may simply be a strong bank and syndicated-loan bid, which eases refinancing risk for borrowers generally. That kind of bid can vanish as fast as it appeared. Either way, the so-what is the same. The one-way migration story this desk told all week just got a counterweight. What matters now is what private books are left holding.

There's a capital side to this too. On Friday this desk flagged direct-lending fundraising running well ahead of last year's pace. Set shrinking origination at the largest BDCs against that money, and you get one of two outcomes. Either cash sits idle and drags on yields, or the pressure to deploy loosens terms.

Not every Blue Owl number tells the shrinking story. Investing.com reports that Blue Owl's direct-lending originations jumped roughly forty-five percent year over year, to five-point-two billion dollars in the third quarter. On its face, that clashes with Bloomberg's claim about Blue Owl's listed BDC. The BDC's own filing mostly resolves the tension. Blue Owl Capital Corporation's third-quarter release, filed with the SEC, shows new investment commitments of eight hundred ninety-one million dollars. Per the filing's tables, that's down from roughly one-point-three-four billion a year earlier. So Bloomberg's fewer-loans claim holds at the vehicle level.

The forty-five percent figure appears nowhere in that BDC's filing, or in a recap of its earnings call. Five-point-two billion is roughly six times the BDC's own commitments. That strongly suggests a platform-wide number, spanning Blue Owl's other vehicles and strategies. Treat that as an inference from the gap, since this desk hasn't checked the parent company's own materials. Either way, platform growth and single-vehicle contraction aren't contradictory. They're different entities sharing one name.

There's a subtler point inside the BDC's quarter. Per that earnings-call recap, more than half of fundings went to existing borrowers. That's the incumbent add-on pattern this desk flagged on Saturday. Add-ons lift origination activity without winning new market share. So a manager can show commitments rebounding sharply from the second quarter and still be losing the new, competitively bid mandates to banks.

The same Investing.com report puts Blue Owl's annualized realized credit losses at just twelve basis points. That's the asset class's favorite marketing number, and it lags. Realized losses are the last line to move in a cycle where stress gets deferred through PIK toggles and maturity extensions. That's the disguised-default gap this desk laid out last Wednesday. Twelve basis points tells you what's been written off. It tells you nothing about income quietly accruing as PIK that may never arrive as cash. So bank this. A clean realized-loss line can coexist for years with loans being amended, extended, and marked to model rather than to a buyer's price.

If banks are winning these deals back, somebody has to buy the paper once it's syndicated. That's where the picture gets shakier. Egan-Jones reports October CLO issuance of ninety deals worth thirty-six-point-seven billion dollars. That's down nearly twenty-eight percent by volume from a year earlier. CLOs are the largest structural buyer of syndicated loans. So the bank bid reclaiming these deals isn't resting on a surging CLO bid underneath it. Two caveats apply. A year-on-year drop depends on how strong last October's base was. And the Egan-Jones summary doesn't say whether those ninety deals are new issues or include resets, which changes the arbitrage read.

Here's why that matters. Banks look like they're winning the competitive fight today. But they need downstream buyers, and those buyers are showing up in smaller numbers. If the syndicated bid weakens, deals swing back to private lenders. They'd return on lender-friendly terms, but also as the riskier tail. That makes the bank re-entry more fragile than the origination headlines suggest.

And when the lending slows, the fee doesn't have to. This is a new detail from the BDC adviser litigation this desk covered on Friday. Per Mayer Brown's November update, one amended excessive-fee complaint alleges advisory fees rose forty-seven percent between 2021 and 2025. Over the same stretch, gross assets grew only about thirty-five percent. The plaintiffs' framing is blunt. The same adviser that is paid based on asset values also decides what those values are. These are allegations, not findings, and the source doesn't name the defendant. Fees outgrowing assets could also partly reflect incentive fees earned in a higher-rate environment.

Still, the incentive question stands regardless of how the case resolves. Here's this desk's inference. When banks take the new lending and books stop growing through origination, an asset-based fee leans harder on holding assets and on their marks. It doesn't lean on cash recoveries. Put that next to Blue Owl's twelve basis points from a moment ago. A fee that rewards asset values, paired with a loss metric that only counts write-offs, gives a manager little reason to surface trouble early.

So where does the cycle actually stand? This looks like a competitive turn at the top of the market, not a default turn. Banks and private lenders are fighting over the cleanest credits. That isn't evidence that credit quality itself has cracked. The biggest unresolved risk is what private books keep once that fight settles. It's a quality mix marked to model, and no downturn has tested it yet.

Watch three things. First, Thursday, November 19th, when bids are due on ING's roughly five-hundred-million-dollar data-center loan portfolio, including the Stargate-linked slice. It's still a live test of what bank AI-exposure paper actually clears at. Second, whether fourth-quarter BDC prints show origination recovering or the bank takeback persisting. Third, November's CLO tally, for whether that buyer base comes back.

That's the lending machine for November 16th. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
