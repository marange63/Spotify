Good morning, this is The Throughline for Thursday, November 5th, 2026.

Today's headline: voters sided with restriction in nearly every data-center contest on Tuesday's ballot, and a day later AI-infrastructure stocks fell even as the Dow had its best day since April of last year.

Ten briefings shipped today. We'll start with the macro backdrop, move through the AI and compute stack, and close on the applied frontier.

Strategic Power has the detail: across ten states, led by Ohio, voters backed restrictions or rejected pro-development measures everywhere but North Dakota. The range runs from an outright ban in Trenton, passed eighty to twenty, to Ashville, where residents voted to block a data-center campus already under construction. The desk's read is that the voter is now a third gatekeeper on AI power, after the state and the utility, and self-generation that dodges the first two walks straight into the third.

From the ballot box to the tape, Capital Markets Radar has CoreWeave and other neocloud names falling even as the Dow surged more than three percent, hitting a group whose Goldman AI basket was already more than twenty-four percent off its peak. The desk can't separate political risk from profit-taking in a crowded trade, but notes that any federal AI bill still needs a Republican president's signature. Meanwhile Brent shed about a fifth from last week's peak, yet December hike odds still sit at eighty-two percent, so conditions eased everywhere except the policy rate.

Digital Money asks who paid for bitcoin's bounce from an election-night low near seventy-five thousand six hundred dollars back above eighty thousand. The desk's answer is mostly forced short covering, not fresh buying, while ETF flows stayed thin and split, with BlackRock's fund still selling as the rest of the complex bought. The floor sits right at recent buyers' average cost and held through two flushes in a week, a real positioning level but not yet proof of demand.

That's the macro backdrop. Now the AI and compute stack, where the fight over who builds and who finances each layer keeps spreading.

Semiconductors and Compute Infrastructure has trade press reporting Samsung's new Texas fab fully booked for HBM base dies, reportedly by AMD, while Nvidia is said to be steering its base dies to TSMC. Both claims are unconfirmed and four years from mass production, so the desk reads a reservation, not a market-share shift. The nearer clock is commodity DRAM, where TrendForce sees another ten to fifteen percent contract-price rise this quarter, and one module maker sees the squeeze staying tightest through mid-2027.

A standing disclosure for the next two desks: Anthropic makes the model behind this show. Frontier AI Lab Competition has a TeraWulf filing confirming that Google backstops Anthropic's data-center lease, with a path to equity if things go wrong, making Google its chip supplier, sales channel, credit backstop, and rival at once. Per Dealroom's summary of the amended S-1, Anthropic's third-quarter revenue rose nearly half to just under seventeen billion dollars, alongside an operating loss of more than half a billion. Add AMD's billion-dollar charge for warrants tied to OpenAI and Meta, and lab compute is increasingly financed on someone else's balance sheet.

AI Products and Applications has Meta buying Conductor, which orchestrates coding agents from rival labs, and winding down its hosted product about two weeks after the announcement. DeepSeek went the other way, open-sourcing a proactive coding agent while warning it can consume a team's attention. With OpenAI also opening its always-on dots agents to healthcare and education workspaces, the desk's thread is that agents are becoming standing infrastructure before anyone has shown they're reliable at that scale.

Enterprise Technology finds the money outrunning the controls: per Menlo Ventures, enterprises spent about two and a half times their planned AI budgets, yet Okta finds agent rollouts missing their own schedules eighty percent of the time. The concrete gap is identity, since fewer than a quarter of agent builders give each agent its own, so a misbehaving agent can't be cleanly revoked. CrowdStrike is chasing that gap with two acquisitions reported near half a billion dollars and a new AI kill switch, a narrative its December print will have to back with numbers.

That's the compute stack. Now the applied frontier, where the gap between announced and proven capability shows up again.

Robotics and Physical AI has Xiaomi claiming a humanoid ran a full eight-hour factory shift unsupervised, while publishing none of the success-rate or cycle-time data it gave for smaller trials earlier this year. The desk calls that silence, not the eight hours, the real signal. Underneath, even Nvidia's reference humanoids lean on China-rooted suppliers for the dexterous hand, whose magnets are already under Chinese licensing, with the broader rare-earth suspension still legally due to lapse November 10th.

Space and the Orbital Economy has China's Qianfan constellation flying the largest batch yet on a Chinese commercial launcher, though both it and Guowang remain well short of their annual targets. America's would-be SpaceX challengers are still on the ground, with Neutron yet to debut and New Glenn grounded since a May anomaly, their dates now set by analysts and trade press rather than their operators. Every quarter they stay grounded extends SpaceX's hold on US medium and heavy lift.

AI in Biopharma R&D closes the tour with two buyers pricing the same technology on opposite evidence. Isomorphic Labs is reportedly in talks at a valuation near fifty billion dollars with nothing yet in human trials, while Vertex's executive chairman calls the industry's grandest cure claims complete BS, and no AI-designed drug has FDA clearance. GSK's answer was to test Chai Discovery's models on its own bench before signing, which the desk reads as the template for how pharma will buy.
