Cautious Optimism Briefings

Strategic Power, Energy, and Institutional Change

Transcript · September 30, 2026

Good morning. It's Wednesday, September 30th, 2026, and this is your strategic power briefing.

The theme today: the chokepoints are reopening on volume, but not on control. Gulf oil is flowing again at close to pre-war scale, and Nvidia chips are about to move into China. In both cases, the flow is moving on terms set by whoever holds the gate. Meanwhile Beijing is closing the one channel the West has used to route around it. That channel is people: the engineers who actually know how to refine rare earths and build batteries.

Start with a correction to our own frame. On Sunday, this beat described the Strait of Hormuz as effectively closed since March. We also said nobody had produced a tracked transit figure against the pre-war baseline. That figure now exists. Kpler, the marine tracking service, put oil and petroleum product flows through the Strait at 13.1 million barrels a day last week, according to CNN. That's just under 80 percent of the 17.1 million barrels a day that moved through before the war. And that number is a floor, not a ceiling. According to The National, Kpler's figures exclude tankers crossing with their transponders switched off. Widen the lens and the picture is fuller still. JPMorgan, as cited by CNN, puts total Middle East crude flows, through Hormuz and around it, back at 98 percent of prewar levels. So the physical shock is much smaller than "closed" implied, and we should say so plainly.

Now the mechanism behind that recovery, and it starts on the other side of the Arabian peninsula. A drone attack hit Saudi Arabia's East-West Pipeline on September 10th. That halted crude loadings at the Red Sea port of Yanbu from September 11th. Saudi Arabia has since restarted the pipeline and resumed loadings, but flows remain below pre-attack levels, per The National. So Riyadh pushed its crude back through Hormuz. Saudi shipments through the Strait jumped to 2.58 million barrels a day in September, per preliminary Kpler data. That's more than double the roughly one million of August. The same report says this gives the kingdom 43 percent of the Strait's oil traffic. That share doesn't reconcile with Kpler's 13.1 million total, where 2.58 million is closer to a fifth, and no source defines a different base. So bank the Saudi volume, and treat the share as unreliable.

Iraq points the same way, a month earlier. OPEC's August figures, relayed through secondary coverage of its monthly report, show the group's crude output up 346,000 barrels a day from July. Iraq alone added 664,000, the largest gain in the group. Iraq's southern exports have no route out except the Gulf. So more volume was already leaning on the Strait before the Saudi pivot.

Here's the skeptical turn, and it matters. Recovered volume is not a restored market. Saudi crude flooding back into Hormuz isn't a sign the Strait is safe. It's a sign the Red Sea bypass is still impaired. That route was fragile even before the pipeline was hit. After a missile strike on the cargo ship Tihamah in August, Saudi flows through Bab al-Mandeb fell to 0.45 million barrels a day. So more of the kingdom's exports now sit in the single chokepoint that any renewed Iranian escalation could threaten. And look at how the flow came back. CNN describes military-escorted shuttles making surreptitious "dark" transits of the Strait. Kpler's Matt Smith told CNN that with such strong volume, it is clear Iran is losing its influence over the Strait. Maybe. But volume that depends on escorts and switched-off transponders is a different asset than peacetime flow. Remove the escort, and the volume question reopens overnight. So the oil risk premium doesn't vanish as the barrels return. It shifts from a volume story to a tail-risk story, and that argues against pricing the Strait as solved.

The same pattern, flow resuming on the gatekeeper's terms, showed up in chips this week. This time the gatekeeper is Beijing.

The backdrop in one line: Washington licensed H200 sales to roughly ten Chinese firms back in December 2025, yet not one has shipped, because Beijing won't let its own companies take delivery. What's new is one tier down. China's Ministry of Industry and Information Technology has asked ByteDance, Alibaba and Tencent to disclose their purchase plans for Nvidia's new RTX PRO 5500. That's a workstation-tier card, well below frontier training capability. According to Asia Times, ByteDance alone is considering about one million units. Nvidia plans to start shipping to China in late December, at roughly 500,000 units a quarter. So ByteDance's interest by itself equals two full quarters of Nvidia's planned China supply.

Notice what this actually is. It's a disclosure request, not a purchase order, and nothing has shipped. A ministry that asks to see every buyer's plans can also cap those plans, or steer them. My read, and it is analysis: Beijing is choosing which tier of American silicon it lets in. It accepts workstation and inference hardware that doesn't threaten its push for domestic accelerators like Huawei's Ascend. It keeps refusing the H200 that would. The competing read is simpler. Beijing just wants volume inference compute fast, and is being pragmatic. Both readings share one fact: the gate now sits in Beijing, not Washington. US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer told Bloomberg that semiconductor controls weren't even on the bilateral agenda at last week's summit. For Nvidia, that means a modest China volume path from late December, while the frontier-compute gap stays in place. The takeaway: China now controls both what it sells the world in rare earths and what it lets in from Nvidia.

Beijing is gating what comes in. As of September 15th, it's also gating who gets out.

China's new exit-entry rules took effect that day. Per Asia Times, they target experienced engineers in industries subject to export controls. The named targets are engineers with know-how in electrolyte formulas, N-type solar cell technology, and rare-earth separation. That closes a path foreign companies used in the past. They poached Chinese engineers with higher pay, green cards and equity, to build identical factories in Vietnam, India or Mexico. Asia Times says flatly that the path is now closed.

This is a structural move, not a symbolic one. It targets the actual mechanism by which the West has tried to route around Chinese refining dominance. It also rhymes with something this beat flagged on Sunday about Taiwan. There, a shortage of experienced construction workers, not capital, was pacing how fast TSMC's commitments become working fabs. Add this to that pattern. Increasingly, the binding constraint on rebuilding strategic industrial capacity outside China isn't money. It's people, and Beijing is now restricting their movement directly. The rule lands on an industry Beijing was already squeezing. Back in June, it put ten US companies, including MP Materials and USA Rare Earth, on its own export-control list.

The caveat is real. The reporting describes the rule's intent and its targets, not a single blocked case, and enforcement looks discretionary. Know-how also leaks through equipment vendors, documentation, and engineers already abroad. And allied projects can license or re-engineer processes over time. So my read is that this adds years and cost to allied rare-earth and battery buildouts, rather than making them impossible. That favors slower, duplicated, higher-cost supply chains outside China. The shortcut around Chinese process know-how just got longer.

Every one of those workarounds has to be financed: the escorts, the second-source fabs, the allied refineries. And the price of money has moved against them. As of September 24th, CME FedWatch pricing pointed to a 4.75 to 5 percent funds range by June 2027. That's roughly four quarter-point hikes from today's 3.75 to 4 percent range. It's a sharp reversal from the cutting-cycle narrative, and it lands under Fed Chair Kevin Warsh. To be clear, that's market pricing, not a Fed decision. The next FOMC decision isn't until October 28th, and the reporting doesn't establish what drove the repricing. But it fits our read yesterday that gold and Bitcoin were probably answering to rate expectations or the dollar, not to the oil shock. The fiscal side adds its own clock. The House passed a stopgap funding bill, 370 to 48, that runs only to December 11th. That pushes the real budget fight past the November 3rd midterms. So the capital cost of every chokepoint workaround in this briefing is rising, just as those workarounds become more necessary.

What to watch: whether Kpler's Hormuz numbers keep climbing or stall near 80 percent, and whether Yanbu gets back to pre-attack loadings. Then the October 18th window tied to the Russia secondary-tariff trigger, the October 28th Fed decision, Nvidia's first RTX PRO 5500 shipments in late December, the December 11th funding cliff, and January 10th, when the US-China tariff truce is set to expire.

That's the briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
