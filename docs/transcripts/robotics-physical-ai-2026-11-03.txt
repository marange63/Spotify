Good morning. It's Tuesday, November 3rd, and this is your robotics and physical AI briefing. The spine today is a pricing exercise. American capital is treating the robot brain as the scarce asset, and the robot body as something you can buy, increasingly from China. Two stories this week test both halves of that bet. A third shows what financing the body side actually looks like. And a fourth puts a price on the magnets every body depends on, whoever builds it.

Start with the body. Meta has built a humanoid prototype, internally codenamed Max, with help from China's Fourier Intelligence. Reporting from The Information, relayed by Interesting Engineering, describes Max as customized hardware based on Fourier's existing robotic platforms. It draws on hardware and engineering experience from three generations of Fourier robots. So this is not a from-scratch Meta design, and it is not an off-the-shelf Fourier product either. The build took about three months, and a second version is already underway.

Keep the status straight. Meta has said it has no plans to sell the robots developed through the collaboration. Max is a research prototype, not a product launch. And the reporting describes Fourier's role only up to a point. Fourier supplied the platform and the engineering experience behind it. Nothing in the reporting puts Fourier on a production line manufacturing Meta's robot.

The sourcing choice is still the story. On Friday, October 30th, this desk covered Boston Dynamics and its parent, Hyundai, preparing a Korean supplier base for the Atlas humanoid. Per KED Global, the stated selection criteria included "reduced reliance on China." Meta went the opposite way, straight to a Chinese body maker for a research platform. Localizing the body and outsourcing it to China are now both live strategies among large, US-allied players. Our inference is that at least one major platform is treating the humanoid body as a buyable input, not a moat. There is a competing reading. Fourier may simply have been the fastest available partner for a three-month build, which says much less about commoditization. The current reporting cannot separate the two. Either way, the so-what holds: for Meta, the body was something to source, and the differentiation is meant to live somewhere else.

If the body is the part you can buy, the money should go to the brain, and it is. On Monday, this desk reported Chinese capital rotating into robot-brain labs. Today the American version shows up. Physical Intelligence is reportedly in talks to raise at least one billion dollars at a valuation near twenty billion. Bloomberg reported the talks on October 27th, and nothing was finalized as of that date.

The comparison point matters more than it first looks. The last round Physical Intelligence actually announced valued it at five point six billion dollars. So twenty would be more than a tripling of its own confirmed number. A higher mark of roughly eleven billion appears only in corporate filings, for a round the company never announced. That is why this desk won't call the new figure a clean doubling.

There's a skeptical counterweight, and it comes from the chip vendor. Nvidia's current humanoid model is GR00T N1.7. It is already out, so treat it as context, not this week's news. Nvidia bills it as an open model. By Nvidia's own account, it was trained on more than twenty thousand hours of human egocentric video. Note what kind of number that is. It is a data-scale claim, not a fielded reliability figure. Still, it is an attack on the same data layer a standalone brain lab wants to own. And it comes from the company selling the chips under everyone's robots. Our inference is that, for Nvidia, an openly distributed robot model is a way to sell compute. A lab charging for intelligence has to outrun that. There's a second-order effect if the round does close. A twenty-billion-dollar mark would push hardware makers toward a build-versus-license decision on the brain, with Nvidia's open model sitting at the other end of that choice. So what: the scarcity premium on the robot brain is being priced before it has been proven.

Now the other side of that trade: the company actually trying to sell bodies. We've flagged Agility Robotics' SPAC listing with Churchill Capital Corp the Eleventh as a catalyst all week. Now there's a number on it. On October 30th, Agility and Churchill said they expect gross capital of about four hundred sixty-five million dollars before transaction expenses, as reported by The Robot Report. Set that beside Physical Intelligence's private talks, raise to raise. The body maker is financing itself at less than half the scale of the brain lab's at-least-one-billion-dollar ask.

And four sixty-five million is gross, not net. We've seen no redemption figure from the Churchill trust, so the cash Agility actually banks could come in lower. Underneath the raise sits a real but thin commercial base. On October 20th, Agility said it signed a commercial agreement with AGCO. And GXO Logistics is expanding Digit deployments to its sites in North Carolina and Tennessee. None of the reporting attaches a unit count, a price, or an uptime figure to either deal. So the unit economics an investor would need are simply not public yet. That is exactly what a listing changes. Agility would become the first US humanoid maker filing quarterly reports that outside analysts can check, instead of the periodic press releases its private peers rely on. Our inference is that Agility then becomes the public benchmark for private brain-layer marks. Suppose a public body maker with disclosed unit economics draws a modest multiple, while a private brain lab draws twenty billion on talks alone. That gap is itself a data point about where investors think the durable value in this stack sits.

And whoever builds the body, its actuators depend on materials China controls. The next test of that exposure lands a week from today. China's one-year suspension of its expanded rare-earth export controls ends November 10th. But a separate layer, the April 2025 licensing rules covering some magnet materials, stays in force whatever happens on the 10th. The only public signal toward an extension is a May 21st statement from China's commerce ministry that Beijing and Washington would work toward one. No extension has been confirmed in the reporting since.

The exposure is real, and Elon Musk has said so about his own humanoid. "Optimus was affected," he said, because "the Optimus actuators in the arm do use permanent magnets." Electric humanoids like Atlas need dozens of high-torque actuators with sintered neodymium magnets. Those magnets contain dysprosium and terbium, materials China overwhelmingly controls. So the same class of exposure sits under Tesla's Optimus, under Hyundai's Atlas, under Agility's Digit, and under the Fourier-based body in Meta's lab.

The outcome on November 10th is unknown, and this briefing won't forecast it. But even a clean extension would only buy time. It would not touch the April 2025 licensing layer underneath. And this is where the Hyundai strategy meets a limit. Our inference is that localizing Atlas suppliers in Korea does not, by itself, remove the magnet dependency inside the robot. The body is buyable, but no humanoid body in this story is free of China risk, wherever it is assembled. So what: the magnet exposure under the entire body layer outlasts next week's deadline, whichever way it breaks.

Pull it together. What's genuinely inflecting is capital formation around the robot brain, on both sides of the Pacific, and the normalization of the humanoid body as an outsourceable, financeable input. That body still sits on a magnet supply chain China controls. What's still a demo is Max itself, a research prototype with no product. So is a Physical Intelligence round that is talks, not cash in the bank. The biggest open question is whether a standalone brain lab's moat survives a chip vendor distributing an open model of its own. Watch four things. First, whether the Physical Intelligence round closes, and at what mark. Second, what Agility's first public filings show on units, pricing, and uptime once it lists. Third, what changes, if anything, on the rare-earth suspension on November 10th. And fourth, what Meta's second version reveals about where Fourier's role ends.

That's the briefing. Stay cautiously optimistic.
