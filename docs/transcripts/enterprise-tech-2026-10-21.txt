Good morning. It's Wednesday, October 21st, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

The theme today is concentration. Enterprises keep moving more of their critical operations, and more of their workflow automation, onto fewer platforms. The last few days tested both sides of that bet. One test was what it costs when a platform breaks. The other was what it actually returns when it works.

Start with what broke. On Tuesday morning, a power event hit Google Cloud's London region, europe-west2. According to TechRadar's reporting, Google named a single zone, europe-west2-b, as the cause. Google's own incident report for this event is not on the record yet. So treat the cause and the scope as press-sourced, not as Google's confirmed account. The fallout is clearer. Revolut, the digital bank that runs on Google Cloud, lost app access, card payments, transfers, and cash withdrawals. One outlet, Tech Insider, puts the affected customers in Ireland alone at 3.4 million. That is a single lower-tier source, and it covers Ireland only, so don't stretch it into a European total.

Revolut's own words are thin. Its first post said it was, quote, "experiencing a service disruption affecting multiple services." A follow-up said customers' money and data, quote, "are safe and remained safe throughout." Notice what's missing from both. There's no cause, no duration, and no mention of Google Cloud by name. The Google link comes from press reporting, not from a Revolut technical statement.

So which story is this: cloud concentration, or customer architecture? The first reading says one provider's power event took down a multi-country bank, so concentration is the risk. The second reading says a single-zone failure should never have reached Revolut's customers at all. Spreading workloads across zones inside one region is standard design, and enterprises already pay for it. On the current record, the evidence leans toward the second reading. Every press report names only one zone. If that holds, Revolut had a single-zone dependency somewhere in its stack. It might be a database primary, a payments service, or something else. That says more about Revolut's failover design than about Google's reliability. The alternative is that the outage cascaded into the wider region or the control plane. That would swing the blame back toward Google. But nothing on the record supports that wider reading yet. Until Google publishes its own report, the scope question stays formally open.

Here's the second-order effect for regulated buyers, and it's my inference. If one zone can take a bank offline, the procurement question shifts from "which cloud" to "prove your failover." That favors spending on multi-zone design and on resilience testing, run before an incident rather than after. It does much less for the multi-cloud switching story. There's a competitive angle for Google Cloud, too. A visible reliability failure in banking lands just before Microsoft and Amazon report, expected around October 28th. That hands AWS and Azure sales teams a talking point. In my read, a single outage rarely moves share on its own, so treat it as a sales-cycle irritant, not a forecast. The so-what: the bill for Tuesday sits with the architecture at least as much as with the cloud.

Some regulatory machinery for this already exists. According to legal commentary on HM Treasury's first designations, Google Cloud's EMEA entity has been a designated critical third party since July. That puts it under joint oversight by the Bank of England, the PRA, and the FCA. The designation predates Tuesday by about three months, so it isn't a reaction to the outage. What it does mean is that the live test is whether regulators use powers they already hold, not whether they invent new ones.

Regulators have been working on cloud dependency from another angle too, but on a different problem. Britain's Competition and Markets Authority published an update on its cloud remedies on October 16th, four days before the outage. It says, quote, "Amazon and Microsoft now allow UK customers to move data out for free when switching providers." That's a real remedy. But it solves portability, not resilience. Free egress lowers the cost of leaving a provider over months. It does nothing for an enterprise mid-incident. In that moment, the problem isn't the cost of switching. It's staying up until service comes back. To be clear, the CMA's update was not a response to the outage, and the connection is my own reading. Britain has made leaving a cloud cheaper. It has not made staying on one safer. That is exactly the gap Tuesday's outage fell into.

The concentration story isn't confined to infrastructure. It's moving up a layer, into the agents enterprises now run on top of it. Salesforce published its 2026 Agentic Enterprise Index this week. Regular listeners will recognize the pattern. Last Friday it was Agentic Work Units. This week it's activated agents and chat volume. Each time, Salesforce supplies a new unit of its own design. And each time, there's no new revenue figure since its last quarterly print. One caveat up front. These are Salesforce's own index numbers, drawn from its own customers. They are not audited, and they are not SEC disclosures.

Here's what the index claims. Organizations, quote, "increased activated agents by nearly 3x by the end of this fiscal year." And agents handled, quote, "170 times more customer service chats than previous years." There's also a wording gap inside the release itself. The headline says deployments "more than double," while the body says "nearly 3x." It's a small inconsistency, but it's Salesforce contradicting Salesforce.

More important, these are activity metrics. None of them measures revenue, seats, or resolution quality. The 170-times figure is a ratio with no disclosed base. A huge multiple off a small starting point tells you growth happened, not that it's material. That's the usage-versus-revenue line enterprise buyers should be drawing. And more activated agents isn't automatically value. It could just as easily be agent sprawl. That's the overprivileged-agent problem Microsoft's own security blog warned about on Monday. More agents means a bigger governance bill, and Microsoft wants to be the one collecting it.

The counter-reading deserves its due. The index also says average agent creation time fell by 53 percent, which more than halves it. That's the genuinely useful number in the release. It suggests deployment friction is falling. It matters because of what Salesforce showed us on Monday in Madrid. Salesforce paired its Spain investment with a forward-deployed engineering team for AI implementation. That implied a services-heavy cost of sale for agents. Faster creation cuts against that concern. If agents get easier to build, the embedded-engineer bill should shrink over time. But faster creation also means more agents, sooner, and that feeds the sprawl problem. The so-what: Salesforce is offering activity data in place of a return-on-investment answer, and nobody has supplied that answer yet.

Put the two halves together. More workloads and more agents are landing on fewer platforms. That raises the blast radius of any single failure. It might be a power event in one zone. It might be one overprivileged agent with too much access. Either way, the evidence that these platforms are resilient is lagging the concentration. So is the evidence that the agents pay for themselves.

Here's what to watch. First, whether Google publishes its own incident report, and whether it confirms a single-zone scope. Second, whether Revolut says anything specific about cause or duration. Third, whether UK regulators use the oversight powers they already hold. And fourth, whether Salesforce or its peers attach a revenue or resolution-quality number to agent activity. The late-October and November earnings cluster is where that should show up. None of it has happened yet, so don't treat any of it as settled.
