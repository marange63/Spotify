Good morning, it's Wednesday, August 5th, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

This week's earnings cycle already settled one question: enterprise AI demand is real and it converts to revenue. The hyperscalers all printed accelerating cloud growth, and the platform vendors kept turning AI interest into paid seats and production deployments. So the debate has moved. The marginal question now isn't whether enterprises want AI. It's whether they can source and afford the compute to run it. Two fresh signals this week say the binding constraint is cost and supply, and they come from opposite ends of the enterprise stack.

Start with IBM, because it's the clearest casualty yet of the memory supercycle this show has tracked climbing the stack all week. On July 14th, IBM warned that its second quarter would miss expectations, and shares closed that day at $217.05, down 25.21 percent — the worst single session in the company's 115-year history. When IBM confirmed results on July 22nd, revenue came in at $17.2 billion, still up 1 percent year over year but short of what the Street had modeled. Software revenue actually grew 5 percent. Consulting was flat. The damage was concentrated in Infrastructure, down 7 percent. And here's the tell that this was timing rather than deterioration: operating earnings per share still rose 5 percent, to $2.93. The profit engine held. What broke was the top line, and only in one quarter.

Here's the important part: this is IBM's own explanation, not a reporter's gloss. The company's Q2 materials state that in the last few weeks of June, clients shifted their quarterly capex toward servers, storage, and memory purchases to secure supply-constrained infrastructure ahead of expected price increases — and that this dynamic drove the majority of the quarter's shortfall, because numerous large deals simply failed to close on the timelines IBM expected. CEO Arvind Krishna put it plainly to investors: server and memory purchases are now priorities for customers. That's a company, on the record, telling you its own clients diverted budget away from it to go buy hardware before prices go up.

This is the enterprise-software instance of a pattern this show has now traced across an entire stack in a single week. AWS raised its 2026 capex guidance to roughly $220 billion, citing higher memory costs. We've watched that same scarcity climb from high-bandwidth memory into phones, into PCs. Now it's reached software and mainframe budgets directly, through capex substitution. The caveat worth holding onto: this is a mix and timing shock, not collapsing demand for IBM's software. Software still grew. What moved is when and how clients spend, not whether they spend. The so-what is this: as long as memory stays scarce and prices keep rising, that same substitution risk sits over any vendor whose revenue depends on discretionary capex cycles rather than recurring cloud consumption. That's a relative tailwind for the hyperscalers' OpEx model over on-premises infrastructure sellers like IBM. And it's likely good news for the systems integrators who get paid to manage the resulting reallocation — the same data-grounding and integration plumbing this show flagged as the near-term money in enterprise AI just two days ago.

If memory scarcity is squeezing enterprise budgets from the supply side, the other half of this week's story is buyers attacking the same cost problem from the demand side. That brings us to Cisco.

Cisco is rolling out a personalized AI agent to all roughly 90,000 of its employees starting this month, one of the largest enterprise-wide agentic deployments announced to date. At 90,000 seats, this isn't a lighthouse pilot in one department; it's the entire company at once, which is what makes the cost architecture the story rather than the rollout itself. Cisco isn't defaulting every request to the most capable frontier model. CFO Mark Patterson said it directly: "We're not going to burn a whole bunch of tokens with frontier models." The company is routing requests to cheaper, more suitable models depending on the task, and keeping much of the infrastructure on-premises for cost and data control. Inside Cisco's own finance function, AI now generates most of the first draft of its management discussion and analysis filings — the narrative section of its own earnings reports.

A few caveats belong here, because everything about this rollout is Cisco's own framing. There's no disclosed implementation cost, no independent productivity measurement, and no confirmation yet that the August start has actually gone live in production. Take it as an announced intent with real specificity behind it, not an audited result.

But the intent itself is the news. This is the first concrete, named, at-scale data point this earnings season on an enterprise engineering AI cost down rather than proving AI revenue up. The whole week has been about vendors — the platform incumbents and the hyperscalers — showing how they capture value from AI. Cisco is a buyer showing how it avoids paying full frontier-model prices for it. So what does that mean going forward? If large enterprises build out this kind of routing infrastructure at scale, frontier-lab API revenue from their biggest customers likely grows more slowly than revenue from mid-market buyers who lack the engineering resources to build equivalent cost controls. It also puts pressure on the SaaS finance and reporting tools that don't yet offer comparable in-house automation, since Cisco is already using AI to draft the very filings those tools were built to support.

Put IBM and Cisco together and you get the shape of where enterprise AI competition is actually heading next. The demand side of this argument is closed, and the hyperscaler prints already covered on this show are the backdrop, not today's news. Azure grew 43 percent last quarter. Google Cloud grew 82 percent to $24.8 billion, with cloud operating income more than tripling to $8.8 billion. AWS accelerated to 36.7 percent, its fastest growth in eighteen quarters, on its way to a $169 billion annualized run rate, with its AI revenue line alone now above $25 billion annualized and a custom-silicon business at a matching twenty-five-billion-dollar run rate, both compounding at triple-digit rates. Adoption is broad, too: Microsoft's Copilot has passed thirty million paid seats, and nearly ninety percent of the Fortune 100 now touch Google's Gemini Enterprise. What ties all of that to IBM and Cisco is one number: Amazon's own capex guidance increase, up to roughly $220 billion, explicitly cited higher memory costs. That's the same supply-side scarcity showing up as a demand drag on IBM's software business, and the same scarcity Cisco is trying to engineer around with model routing.

So the story this week isn't "is enterprise AI real." It's "who controls the cost of running it." Vendors that sell recurring cloud consumption look better positioned than vendors selling capital-intensive infrastructure cycles. And enterprises that can build their own cost-routing infrastructure look better positioned than those stuck paying frontier-model list prices. Watch two tells through the rest of August: whether other infrastructure-heavy vendors echo IBM's timing miss when they report, and whether any buyer besides Cisco puts hard numbers on what model-routing actually saved. That's the competitive line to watch through the rest of this earnings season.
