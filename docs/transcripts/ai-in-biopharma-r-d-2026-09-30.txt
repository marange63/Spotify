Good morning. It's September 30th, and this is your briefing on AI across the biopharma value chain.

It's a thin week for data, so here's the thesis up front. Pharma's AI money is getting more specific about what it buys. The strongest signals this fortnight are a deal that names the hard problem it wants AI to solve, and a lab that won't let anyone touch its models. The weakest signal is the one with the biggest headline.

Start with that weak signal, and keep it brief. On September 24th, Eli Lilly signed a discovery deal with InnoCare Pharma, the Beijing-based biotech. Per InnoCare's own release, it is eligible for up to one hundred million dollars in upfront and near-term payments. Against that sit roughly three and a quarter billion dollars in milestones, plus single-digit royalties. By our own arithmetic, that's about three cents committed per headline dollar. And not all of those three cents are cash at signing. The scope is up to five undisclosed targets, with no therapeutic area and no modality named.

On the 24th, and again on the 28th, this show counted three large-pharma AI deals in about a week with no cash disclosed. InnoCare is the first deal in this late-September wave to put a number down. That number confirms the pattern. Lilly is buying options, not making a capital commitment.

But check the label before you file this as an AI deal. Neither InnoCare's release nor Fierce Biotech's coverage uses the words AI, artificial intelligence, or computational. InnoCare describes only its, quote, proprietary drug discovery platform and extensive research experience. That doesn't prove there's no AI inside. It does mean the AI reading has no primary-source backing. A plainer reading fits the same facts: Lilly buying conventional medicinal-chemistry capacity from a Chinese partner. So for investors, discount headline biobucks and track upfront cash and milestones actually paid. And treat the AI label as unverified until a company says the word itself.

If a generic five-target deal tells you little about AI, the better signal is a deal that names its problem. That's Novo Nordisk's agreement with Orbis Medicines, announced September 17th. BioPharma Dive reports Orbis could receive up to one point four billion dollars in upfront and downstream payouts, plus sales royalties. We've mentioned this deal before, but only as a data point on deal structure. Today's point is what Novo is actually buying.

Orbis uses a platform it calls nGen to model and design molecules. In the company's words, those molecules are meant to be both orally bioavailable and capable of modulating challenging intra- and extracellular targets. Translate that. Novo is paying for a macrocycle that works as a pill against targets that are hard to drug. BioPharma Dive frames macrocycles as one way Novo could overcome the limitations of standard oral peptide medicines. So the commercial logic is legible even without a named target.

The caveats are real. The one point four billion is a ceiling, and the upfront isn't disclosed. There are no named candidates and no preclinical data. Designing a molecule to be orally bioavailable is an in-silico intent. It is not a measured exposure in an animal, let alone a patient. No generative model has yet been shown to solve oral bioavailability for peptide-class drugs in humans.

Still, the contrast with InnoCare holds. Capital that names the hard problem it's paying AI to solve is a stronger signal than capital paying for a platform. The second-order edge falls on AI-native platform companies. If pharma pays for specific design problems, general-purpose discovery platforms face pressure to pick a modality or a disease wedge. Otherwise a platform pitch gets priced as an option, the way InnoCare's was.

Paying for a narrow problem is one way to define a moat. Isomorphic Labs, the Google-backed drug-discovery lab, is taking another: keeping the models themselves out of reach. At the Semafor Future of Health Forum on September 15th, Isomorphic's head of drug discovery, Chris Butler, was blunt. Quote, all of our AI models are locked down and locked down in-house. It's a recent on-record statement we haven't used before. And it answers a question this show has been building for ten days: who owns the model, and who owns the molecule?

Put it on a spectrum. At one end is Boltz, a company that builds biomolecular foundation models. On September 20th we described its January deal with Pfizer. That's an open base model, fine-tuned exclusively on Pfizer's historical data, with Pfizer keeping full ownership of the compounds. In the middle, as we covered on the 24th, sit two platforms that kept tiered royalties on what they produce. One is Orbis. The other is Iambic Therapeutics, the small-molecule design company AbbVie signed on September 21st. Isomorphic is the far pole. It keeps full control of the weights, with nothing shared outward.

Butler added that Isomorphic is, quote, able to still progress regardless of what decisions are made elsewhere. That was his answer to calls from frontier labs to slow AI development. He also said the landscape is shifting toward more in silico data guiding development programs. Be clear that this is Isomorphic's own read of regulators, not a regulatory action.

Now the skeptical read. Semafor reports Butler describing the company as on track toward first human trials, with no date attached. This show has tracked Isomorphic's end-of-2026 clock across several briefings, and the silence on a first-in-human date continues as that window shrinks. No new preclinical or benchmark data was disclosed either. Locked weights protect the moat, but they cut both ways. Nobody outside can benchmark the platform, so its claims can't be falsified from outside. The IND, whenever it lands, is the only external test Isomorphic will face.

Step back from the deals to the stack, because they show where the capital is concentrating. It's going to specialized design models. That means nGen for macrocycles, Isomorphic's in-house models, and Boltz's structure-and-affinity models. None of them is a general-purpose language model. And that layer is where the evidence is thinnest. Every design claim in this fortnight's news is still upstream of a patient.

So what does proof look like in that layer? The best example in our research is older. In March, Manifold Bio, working with NVIDIA, reported testing one million AI-designed protein binders against 127 targets in a single multiplexed experiment. That's prospective wet-lab validation at scale, not a benchmark score. But the release as we found it gives no hit rate. And the hit rate is the number that would tell you whether the design actually works. The design layer is producing throughput, not yet success rates.

The workhorse layer looks the opposite. Large language models do the literature synthesis, regulatory documents, protocol drafting, and code. As we argued on the 28th, that's where value is most real today, showing up as labor productivity inside sponsors. It's also where public numbers are missing, and nothing this fortnight quantifies it. Nor did we find a new frontier model release or biology benchmark in this window, so there's no fresh basis for ranking models by task. The takeaway is uncomfortable. Capital is concentrated where proof is furthest away, while the proven gains sit in unglamorous work nobody prices.

That points to where the easier wins are. This show's view, argued on the 25th, is that the low-hanging fruit sits outside discovery, in real-world data. That means tightening eligibility criteria, sizing trials, and choosing indications, where enrollment time rather than model capability is the constraint. Nothing this fortnight tests that view, so treat it as judgment, not finding. Our standing read is that HIPAA is mostly not the binding constraint, since de-identified records already move under license. The harder problem is linking records across the fragmented EHR formats the HITECH era left behind. And nobody has yet attached a measured trial saving to any of it.

One honest note before the close. Nothing in this window moves clinical evidence or regulation. There's no trial readout, no model release, and no FDA action. This week is about deal structure and strategy, not data.

So, the open questions for next week. Does Orbis or Iambic disclose an upfront number, or does InnoCare's three cents on the dollar become the template? Does Isomorphic attach a date to on track? And does the AI label on deals like InnoCare's ever get primary-source backing?

On clinical success rates, nothing this week moves the needle. Novo's bet on oral macrocycles is the kind of wedge that could eventually show up in the odds. But that only happens once a designed molecule has human exposure data. On R&D spend, the pattern holds. Contingent structures keep committed cash low while headline totals climb. That flatters the appearance of pharma's AI commitment without matching it in dollars.

That's the briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
