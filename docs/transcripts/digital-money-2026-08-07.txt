Good morning. It's Friday, August 7th, 2026, and this is your digital money briefing.

Let's start with a puzzle in Bitcoin, because it's the cleanest evidence this week that the market's favorite narrative about who moves price is breaking down. U.S. spot Bitcoin ETFs just posted their strongest week since April, pulling in $754.69 million, according to CoinDesk. That follows a genuinely bad July, which closed with a $265.4 million single-day outflow on the 31st. The turn happened fast: over the first three trading days of August alone, ETFs took in $626 million, with BlackRock's IBIT accounting for $479 million of that and pushing its cumulative inflows toward $61 billion. Layer on top of that on-chain buying. Santiment's wallet-cohort data, cited in reporting this week, shows wallets holding between 10 and 10,000 bitcoin — the whale and shark band — added roughly 19,610 coins since July 29, worth on the order of $1.2 billion. And Bitcoin's long-term-holder supply, coins that haven't moved in over 155 days, sits at a fresh all-time high: 16.64 million bitcoin, about 83% of everything in circulation.

So you have three independent signals of real accumulation — ETF creations, whale wallets, and long-dated holder supply — all pointing the same direction. Here's the puzzle. Bitcoin was trading at $64,382, down slightly, and it is still struggling to clear $65,000. Strong, measurable demand, and no price follow-through. That matters because ETFs have been priced by this market as the marginal buyer that sets the tape. If they can absorb three-quarters of a billion dollars in a week and price still can't break resistance, that assumption needs re-examining.

Who's on the other side of this trade? The honest answer is we don't fully know. The best evidence available points partly toward retail. That same Santiment data shows the smallest wallet cohort — holders under 0.01 bitcoin — shed about 0.55% of their holdings over the same window, which is directional evidence that smaller holders are selling into the whale and ETF bid. But that's not the same population as short-term holders defined by coin age, and no dated, sourced figure on short-term-holder distribution or exchange balances for this week could be confirmed. So treat "retail is quietly distributing into institutional strength" as the leading hypothesis, not a settled conclusion. On the leverage side, the most recent dated reading — from July 22, so before this week's window, and worth flagging as such — showed funding rates near the 39th percentile of the past 798 days and CME futures open interest at its lowest since 2023. That's a soft positioning backdrop, not a stretched one, which argues somewhat against a forced deleveraging or basis-trade unwind as the explanation, though it's not current enough to close the question. Net: demand is real, supply is locking up, and price still won't move. That gap is this week's central open question.

Now the same pattern shows up a layer down, in the dollar plumbing itself. Aggregate stablecoin supply has fallen about $15 billion since a mid-May peak of $322.121 billion, down to $307.561 billion by August 2nd — the sharpest contraction since Terra's collapse in 2022. June alone accounted for $11.41 billion of that decline, the steepest single month since Terra. Tether's USDT fell roughly $5.8 billion to $183.216 billion, and Circle's USDC fell about $7.9 billion from its March peak to $72.069 billion. This isn't digital-dollar demand evaporating. It's the GENIUS Act doing exactly what it was designed to do: the law bars licensed stablecoin issuers from paying yield, so capital that wants a return is leaving the stablecoin wrapper for tokenized Treasury products instead. Same story as Bitcoin, different asset — measurable demand, just not landing where the old model expected it. The so-what here is for issuer economics: Tether and Circle's profitability now hinges more on where short rates sit than on how many users they add, since the float itself is shrinking under a regulatory constraint they can't route around.

Which brings us to the issuer most exposed to that shift. Tether's BDO-verified second-quarter attestation, published July 31st, shows $187.75 billion in assets against $183.64 billion in liabilities as of June 30th — a $4.11 billion excess-reserve cushion. Three months earlier that cushion stood at $8.23 billion. It's roughly halved. Tether still made money — $1.5 billion in operating profit, mostly Treasury bill and repo income — and USDT issuance actually grew about $446 million during the quarter. So this isn't a solvency story in the making. It's a concentration-risk story. The buffer shrank because Tether holds part of its reserves in bitcoin and gold rather than pure Treasuries — it added 14 metric tons of gold, to 146.2 tons, and about 1,796 bitcoin, to 98,933 coins — and both assets lost dollar value during the same weak quarter we just described. Tie that back to the lead: the safety margin behind the world's largest stablecoin is now more correlated to the same range-bound bitcoin price that won't clear $65,000. That's the kind of plumbing risk that stays invisible until it isn't, and it's worth watching precisely because it's measurable today, not hypothetical.

Last, the one catalyst that might have reset any of this just slipped. Senate Majority Leader John Thune confirmed the chamber will not vote on the Digital Asset Market Clarity Act before its August recess. The earliest floor action now is September 14th, when the Senate returns. Thune himself put it plainly: Democrats are insistent on no Clarity vote before the break, and he's queuing the bill up, in his words, "first thing when we come back." An ethics provision tied to President Trump's disclosed crypto income remains an unresolved sticking point. Market-structure clarity — custody rules, exchange registration, clear SEC and CFTC lanes — has been priced as a medium-term institutional catalyst. Pushing it past summer removes that trigger for at least six weeks, and reopens the door to it stalling again in September if the underlying political dispute doesn't resolve.

Put it together and the thesis for this week is consistent across three layers of the stack. Bitcoin demand is real but isn't moving price. Dollar demand is real but is leaving the stablecoin wrapper. Issuer profit is real but isn't rebuilding the safety cushion. None of that is a collapse story — supply is locking up, not fleeing, and the float that's leaving stablecoins is landing in tokenized Treasuries, not cash. Call it repricing and maturation rather than retreat. But it does mean the easy bull case — that institutional flow alone reliably lifts price and strengthens the plumbing — isn't showing up in this week's data, and the catalyst that might have forced the question just moved to September.

That's the briefing for today. Have a good weekend.
