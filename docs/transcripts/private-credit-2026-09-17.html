Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Credit & the Corporate Credit Cycle

Transcript · September 17, 2026

Good morning. It's Thursday, September 17th, and this is your private credit briefing.

All week this show has been asking a version of the same question. Are private credit's own numbers honest? We've walked through a default statistic that turns out to be elective, non-accruals at a post-2017 high, and a record default rate built on maturity extensions rather than actual payment failure. Every one of those was an argument about whether the industry's own reporting describes the industry's own book. Today that question changes hands. It's no longer analysts and rating agencies asking it. It's federal prosecutors.

Phil Tseng resigned on August 31st as chief executive of BlackRock TCP Capital, the publicly traded BDC known as TCPC. He leaves BlackRock entirely on October 1st. That departure lands in the middle of active investigations by the SEC and by the US Attorney's Office into how TCPC valued its assets. New reporting sharpens what the inquiry actually looks like. The Manhattan US Attorney's Office has, in recent months, sought information from the fund, and executives have been questioned as part of it.

Be precise about what that is. It is a fact-gathering posture, document requests and interviews. It is not a charge, and it is not a finding. There are no public findings from either investigation. And the company's own explanation for Tseng's exit is the standard no-disagreement language you would get regardless of what sits underneath it. That wording is not evidence in either direction.

Here is the one hard number we do have. TCPC disclosed an unusual off-cycle update back in January, outside its normal quarterly reporting cadence. It warned that it expected to cut the value of its portfolio by about 19 percent. That is a quotable markdown, and it is worth saying what it isn't. It isn't the endpoint. Multiple outlets have since pointed to further deterioration in net asset value and a share price well off its highs. I can't hand you a precise, sourced figure for either, so I'm not going to manufacture one. Take the magnitude as directionally severe and specifically unquantified past that first cut.

Notice the shape of that January disclosure, because it is the whole thesis in miniature. A 19 percent write-down large enough to require an announcement outside the quarterly calendar is, by construction, information the fund did not have in its published marks the week before. Anyone who transacted before that update priced a materially different portfolio than anyone who transacted after it.

So what does a valuation probe at a listed BDC actually threaten? Not, in the near term, a wave of realized losses. That transmission runs through the underlying loans, not through a legal inquiry into how they were marked. What it threatens is process. If either investigation produces findings, the industry response won't be write-downs. It will be auditor conservatism, boards leaning harder on their valuation committees, and pressure toward mandatory third-party valuation agents. That arrives at exactly the moment managers are counting on discretion to run a stress cycle through amend-and-extend rather than recognition. And BlackRock is scaling a private credit platform through HPS to compete with Apollo, Blackstone and Blue Owl, while a listed fund carrying its name carries a federal inquiry. The cost there isn't credit loss. It's fundraising friction in the wealth channel the whole industry is chasing.

Now, from a process under investigation to what that process looks like from the inside. Blue Owl's business development company, OBDC, gave us the endpoints on this a few days ago. Its first-lien Loparex loan is marked at 22 cents, its second lien at 5. What's new today is the path between those points.

That second-lien position was marked around 88 cents at year-end. It was marked at roughly 63 cents at the end of the first quarter. It is about 5 cents now. The first lien was marked near par at year-end and now sits at 22. That is not a single bad print. It is a quarter-by-quarter decay curve. Moody's has since deemed Loparex in default and flagged a possible Chapter 11, though no filing has actually occurred.

Set that trajectory against OBDC's reported non-accrual rate, which remains a low 0.8 percent of fair value. The tempting read is that the December marks were simply wrong. The more disciplined read is narrower. Fair value on a private loan is a judgment about enterprise value, and enterprise value can genuinely collapse in a quarter without anyone having lied about it in December. What the trajectory does establish is this. The marks moved only after external events forced them — a forbearance lapsing, a rating agency default determination — rather than leading those events. That is an information-lag problem, not necessarily an integrity problem, and it's the version of the claim the evidence actually supports. One limit worth stating plainly: this is a single credit, and we can't verify Loparex's size relative to OBDC's total book, so nothing here establishes a portfolio-wide problem at Blue Owl.

But information lag has teeth of its own. Anyone tendering shares at NAV in a non-traded vehicle before an external trigger transacts at a materially different price than someone who tenders after it. That is the same redemption-queue mechanic this show has flagged all week, seen from the valuation side rather than the liquidity side. The queue and the lagged mark are not two separate problems. They interact.

If you don't trust the manager's own mark, the only outside price you have left is what a real buyer will pay. PennantPark Investment Advisers has closed a 745 million dollar continuation fund led by Pantheon. It holds roughly a hundred mostly first-lien loans pulled out of seven legacy funds, spanning business services, healthcare, IT and industrial services. Pantheon also led Bridgepoint's oversubscribed 1.2 billion euro continuation vehicle, which ran on this show Monday. Both deals were announced the same week.

Hold that evidence at its actual weight. One buyer showing up twice on two continents inside a week is doing a great deal of work in any argument that outside capital validates current marks. A continuation vehicle is negotiated, not competitively bid, and neither deal disclosed terms or a discount to NAV. It is evidence that a price exists. It is not evidence that the price is right. The honest companion data point is the one we covered on the 13th: Blue Owl cleared a third of one non-traded fund's book to four outside pension and insurance buyers at 99.7 percent of par. Outside money is transacting rather than backing away. That cuts against the claim that these marks are fiction, and it sits alongside a credit in the same manager's complex that went from near par to a nickel.

So where does the cycle actually stand? The deterioration is real and it is concentrated, not systemic. One governance event at one BDC and one blown credit at another do not add up to a sector-wide marking failure, and this briefing isn't going to pretend they do. What those facts do establish is a pattern. Private credit's stress keeps getting recognized late rather than denied outright. The binding risk right now is the lag between an external trigger and a mark catching up to it, not the level of headline spreads.

Watch four things from here. Whether TCPC's or BlackRock's own filings disclose more about the scope of the SEC and US Attorney inquiries. Whether Loparex actually files for Chapter 11. The next monthly Fitch private credit print, for whether the extension-driven default rate keeps climbing. And third-quarter BDC non-accrual and payment-in-kind disclosures when they land in November.

That's the briefing. I'm out — talk to you next time.
