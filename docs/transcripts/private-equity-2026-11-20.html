Good morning. It's Friday, November 20th, 2026, and this is your Cautious Optimism Briefing on private equity and the deal economy.

On Wednesday we told you to watch whether TPG's reported run at BILL Holdings turned into a signed deal, and at what premium. It signed Thursday. The shape of that deal points to a conclusion that two fresh data reads back up. Concentration at the top now shows up at every stage of this cycle: what gets bought, what gets sold, and who gets cash back.

Start with the terms. TPG is taking BILL private through TPG Capital, its U.S. and European private equity platform. It's an all-cash deal valued at approximately six billion dollars. The offer is sixty-three dollars and twenty-five cents a share. The deal still needs shareholder and regulatory approval, and the target close is the first half of 2027.

Now the premium, because the headline flatters it. The offer is pitched at a 31 percent premium to BILL's 60-day volume-weighted average price. Per the deal coverage, that window ends November 16th, the day before the Financial Times reported the talks.

Against the last trade, the picture flips. Working back from the after-hours move, BILL closed Thursday at around sixty-four dollars. That's our arithmetic, not a reported figure. It puts the last close above the sixty-three twenty-five offer. So against the last close, the premium is roughly zero. After the announcement, the stock slipped to about sixty-two dollars, just under the offer. Anyone who bought the leak hoping for a bidding war got a mild disappointment. And a spread that thin, on a deal targeted to close in the first half of next year, says the market is pricing completion at the offer, not a topping bid.

Now the structural piece, and this is the part that changes the read. Corpay, the payments company, is not a passive co-investor. Corpay is putting in one point one billion dollars for roughly a one-third equity stake. It also holds an option to buy the remaining stake in 2030. No pricing mechanism for that option has been disclosed, so we won't guess at the economics. But the mechanism itself matters. A typical sponsor-only take-private leaves the exit speculative until someone goes looking for a buyer years later. Here, a strategic buyer has a dated, contractual right to take the whole company, roughly three years after the expected close. TPG's likely exit route is wired in at entry. It bypasses both the IPO window and the sponsor-to-sponsor market. One more confirmed term is that there's no financing condition. Nothing has surfaced on the debt package, the lenders, or the termination fee. So treat this as a confirmed mechanism, not yet a priced one.

Put BILL next to the rest of the recent take-private tape. Last week, GFL Environmental's board turned down a buyout consortium on price. Clayton, Dubilier and Rice and GTCR walked away from Global Payments. BILL is the deal that cleared. It's a de-rated software name, pushed toward a sale by activists, with a strategic partner sharing the equity cheque. So the lesson is plain. In this market, a take-private clears when the public tape has already done the repricing and someone besides the sponsor wants the asset.

There's a marking point here too. A signed BILL deal puts a hard price on a de-rated public software name. Limited partners holding 2021-vintage private software, marked on a manager's model, now have a fresh public benchmark to hold those marks against. We don't have a revenue multiple for BILL, so keep it to the principle. The tape has repriced this kind of asset. Private marks haven't been forced to.

BILL is also one data point in a bigger pattern on the deployment side. That comes from EY's third-quarter US Private Equity Pulse. EY's exit headline, a doubling in quarterly exit value, has had most of the attention. The buying side has had far less. EY says significant deal value, meaning new buyouts, rose 74 percent from the prior quarter, to 123.9 billion dollars. Yet the number of deals fell, to just 39.

So where did the value go? Seven megadeals, each of five billion dollars or more, made up 91 billion of that total. That's most of the quarter's deal value in seven transactions. BILL, at six billion, would sit in exactly that tier. So dry powder isn't broadly reactivating. It's converting into a handful of very large cheques.

The exit side tells the same story. EY's doubling in exit value was itself concentrated. Three exits above 10 billion dollars each made up roughly 30 percent of the quarter's exit value. So nearly a third of the rebound came from three transactions.

Over a longer window, that quarter barely moved the year. Through the third quarter, EY puts year-to-date exit value at 308 billion dollars. Over the same stretch of 2025, it was 319.9 billion. And it took more exits to reach the smaller total, not fewer. So the average exit across the year actually got smaller, even as a few third-quarter exits ballooned.

One flag on that. On Monday we cited CohnReznick's data, built on PitchBook, showing year-to-date exit value rising. EY shows it roughly flat. The methodology and geography differ, so don't stitch them into one series. They simply disagree on direction. That disagreement is itself a reason to hold any recovery read loosely.

So who's actually getting cash out the door? That brings us to KKR's third-quarter call on November 9th. KKR said it has already returned more capital to clients in 2026 than in any year in its history. It expects the full-year figure to reach over 40 billion dollars. That would beat KKR's previous annual record by more than 50 percent. Then came the line that matters most. KKR called its pace, quote, "in sharp contrast to the muted monetization activity that we are seeing across our industry."

That number is doing a lot of work, so here are the caveats. It's a full-year expectation, not yet realized. It's firm-wide, so it may include credit, real assets and insurance books, not just buyout funds. KKR gave no netting against new capital calls, and it isn't a DPI figure for any fund vintage. And it's KKR comparing itself to its own history. So part of this is diagnosis, and part of it is marketing. Notice, too, that the fee engine doesn't depend on any of it. KKR posted record fee-related earnings of 1.3 billion dollars for the quarter.

There are two ways to read KKR's edge. One is that KKR simply underwrites exits better. The other is structural. Scale managers can tap strategic buyers, IPO windows and secondaries buyers at a size the mid-market can't reach. That would mean the biggest firms get liquidity first, without the broader cycle having turned. Either way, and this is our inference, limited partners' experience is splitting by manager size.

That sets up the second-order point. Distributions are becoming the weapon for the 2027 fundraising season. A mega-manager is now explicitly marketing its distributions against everyone else's. Allocators should ask two questions of any record. First, where did the cash come from? A dividend recap, for instance, can manufacture DPI with no buyer ever testing the mark. Second, is the figure firm-wide, or their own fund's? There's a feedback loop here too. If scale managers keep capturing the available exit liquidity, re-ups concentrate further at the top. And that can happen without the median fund's DPI moving at all.

Put it together. Deployment is top-heavy, with seven megadeals carrying most of the quarter's new buyout value. Exits are top-heavy too, led by those three exits above 10 billion dollars. And distributions are top-heavy, with the largest managers pulling ahead of an industry they themselves call muted. For mid-market sponsors, that's a double squeeze. They face a thinner buyer pool for their exits, and a slower cash recovery for their LPs.

So where does the cycle actually stand? It's moving, but only at the top. The exit-and-distribution logjam for the median asset, below the megadeal tier, is still the biggest unresolved risk. The complacent read treats KKR's record and BILL's signing as proof the cycle has broadly reopened. The over-discounting read treats a signed six-billion-dollar deal and a tight merger spread as no signal at all. Neither holds up.

Watch four things from here. First, BILL's shareholder vote, and whether any activist pushes for a better price. Second, whether Blackstone delivers the significant step-up in fourth-quarter realizations it guided to, when it next reports. Third, whether EY's next Pulse shows deal and exit value broadening past the megadeal tier.

And fourth, today's re-vote at Partners Group. Holders of Global Value, the firm's flagship evergreen private-equity fund for wealthy individuals, are voting on whether to split it in two. Redemption requests there have been hitting the fund's quarterly cap. So the vote is a live test of the liquidity mismatch in semi-liquid private equity. We don't have a result yet.
