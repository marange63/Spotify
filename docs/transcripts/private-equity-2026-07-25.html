Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Equity & the Deal Economy

Transcript · July 25, 2026

Good morning, it's Saturday, July 25th, and this is your Private Equity and the Deal Economy briefing.

After two years of a distribution logjam, the exit machine is finally restarting. But restarting is not the same as clearing, and this week's evidence shows both at once. Blackstone cracked the IPO window open. The secondaries market set another record engineering exits the industry can't yet make the ordinary way. The largest buyout ever struck got stuck on a regulator's clock. And limited partners kept their checkbooks closed for an eighth straight quarter. One spine runs through all of it: paper marks are not yet cash, and everything this week is a fight over how fast that converts.

Start with Blackstone, which reported second-quarter results on Wednesday and put the strongest hard number yet behind the reopening thesis. President Jon Gray told analysts that US IPO activity rose sixfold year-over-year in the first half of 2026, with global issuance up more than three and a half times. That's a big multiple, but it's a big multiple off a nearly frozen 2025 base. So treat it as evidence the freeze is thawing, not proof of a normal issuance market. Blackstone put real deals behind the claim. It has executed three IPOs since May: a US mobile-advertising business, an Indian office REIT, and its own stabilized data-center REIT, BXDC. And the pipeline keeps building. The firm launched another significant US listing the week of the call, and says it has eight IPOs on file globally across a range of sectors and geographies. For the bellwether of the industry, that is the clearest sign in two years that public markets are willing to reabsorb sponsor-backed companies.

Now separate the appreciation from the cash, because that distinction is the whole story. Blackstone's corporate private-equity funds were marked up 3.7% in the quarter and 14% over the trailing year. That's paper. The number that decides limited-partner behavior is cash actually returned, and the reopening is a leading indicator of that, not proof it has arrived. Blackstone's own filing shows why. Total realizations, the value of assets actually sold or monetized, ran $31.8 billion in the quarter. But the growth in Blackstone's earnings isn't coming from realized carry yet. Fee-related earnings hit $1.78 billion, ahead of the $1.59 billion analysts expected, and distributable earnings rose 26% to $2.0 billion. That's management fees on a record $1.35 trillion of assets doing the work, not a flood of harvested gains. The net realized performance line, the compensation-adjusted carry that actually flows through, was just $414 million. So the window is cracking open, but the carry hasn't fully arrived. And keep the source in mind: "the year of the IPO" is the house call of the firm that benefits most from it, not a neutral forecast.

If the window does hold through year-end, expect a wave of sponsor-backed listings fast-tracked into the fourth quarter and early next year. That's the moment to watch whether public markets keep their pricing discipline or slide back toward 2021-style overvaluation, because the quality of these exits, not just the count, is what turns marks into durable distributions.

While that front door reopens, the industry is still leaning hard on a side door. Evercore's half-year data, reported this week, shows the global secondaries market hit more than $120 billion in volume through June, a fresh record, on pace to challenge 2025's full-year record of $226 billion. GP-led deals made up $65 billion of that. And single-asset continuation vehicles, where a sponsor moves a prized asset into a new vehicle instead of selling it outright, now account for more than half of that GP-led volume. This is engineered liquidity. It lets a general partner keep collecting fees on a trophy asset while handing some limited partners an exit and asking others to roll forward. But look at who sets the price. In a continuation vehicle, the sponsor sits on both sides: seller of the asset and manager of the buyer. So the liquidity can be priced by the same party that benefits from a high mark, which is exactly the conflict regulators and limited-partner groups keep flagging. The so-what is uncomfortable: record secondaries activity can coexist with, and even mask, weak portfolio-wide realized returns, because the assets generating the headline volume are precisely the ones sponsors are choosing not to actually sell.

Not every exit is engineered. The cleanest kind still runs through strategic buyers, and this month gave one example. On July 6th, Advent's Cobham Ultra agreed to sell its Ultra Maritime undersea-warfare unit to Lockheed Martin for $3.45 billion, an outright trade sale onto a defense prime's balance sheet. That's the exit sponsors most want and can least count on: a strategic acquirer paying cash, no continuation vehicle, no IPO roadshow. It's a reminder that defense budgets are one of the few places where buyers are actively competing with sponsors for assets, and it points to where the healthiest exits are clearing right now, while the public window is still only cracking open.

Even at the very top of the market, though, closing a deal has stopped being purely a money problem. Electronic Arts' $55 billion take-private, led by Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund alongside Silver Lake and Jared Kushner's Affinity Partners, is the largest leveraged buyout ever struck, topping the old TXU record from 2007. Financing is fully committed, roughly $36 billion in equity and $20 billion in debt arranged by JPMorgan. Shareholders and antitrust regulators have already signed off. And it still missed its June 30th closing deadline, because the CFIUS national-security review of the Saudi fund's role is still running. Rather than let the deal lapse, the parties invoked a built-in extension and pushed the outside date to September 28th. As reported, if that date slips again, they face renegotiation or a $1 billion break fee. The lesson for anyone underwriting the next sovereign-backed megadeal is genuinely new: capital committed on day one no longer guarantees a closing date, because the binding constraint has migrated from financing to a regulatory clock nobody at the table controls. And the second-order risk cuts the other way too. A further delay or a collapse would chill sovereign-wealth participation in US take-privates right as fundraising is already soft, narrowing one of the deepest pools of equity the megadeal market has left.

That migration matters because of who's waiting on the other end. Private-equity fundraising fell for an eighth straight quarter through the first quarter of this year, even with dry powder sitting at $3.7 trillion, roughly double its 2019 level. The median gap between a firm's successive fund closings has stretched past two and a half years, the longest since 2014. That's the demand-side mirror of everything above it. Limited partners aren't committing fresh capital at the old pace because they haven't been getting cash back. And the pain isn't evenly shared. Elongated fundraising favors the largest managers, the Blackstones and KKRs that can self-fund the bridge between flagship funds, and squeezes mid-market sponsors toward selling stakes or merging. Which means the IPO reopening isn't just a Blackstone earnings story. It's the single variable that decides whether the next fundraising cycle actually bottoms out, or whether the industry keeps sitting on record dry powder with nowhere fresh to put it.

The near-term test comes fast. KKR reports on July 30th, with Apollo and Carlyle close behind, and whether they corroborate Blackstone's reopening call or undercut it is the catalyst worth watching. For now, the honest read is a machine that has restarted but not yet cleared.

That's the Private Equity and the Deal Economy briefing for July 25th. Have a great weekend.
