Good morning, and welcome to Cautious Optimism Briefings. It's Sunday, November 8th, 2026.

This week's open question in enterprise software has been whether usage-based pricing is running into budget caps, or whether the market simply grades the forward guide. Datadog's print is the last test of that idea this week. Palantir adds a filed backlog number. And OpenAI puts a growth rate, but no dollar base, on its enterprise business. The common thread is one question. How much of the AI forward story can anyone actually verify?

Start with Datadog, which reported third-quarter results on Wednesday. Per its earnings release, revenue grew 35 percent year over year to 1.20 billion dollars. That beat consensus of roughly 1.14 to 1.15 billion, and earnings per share beat as well. The market did not treat it as a beat. Shares fell more than 14 percent that same day, per GuruFocus. That wasn't the tape dragging it down. Fortune's account describes Wednesday as a rally day for the broader market, and the broad software selloff didn't come until Thursday. So this was a reaction to Datadog's own print.

Here's the mechanism, and it's narrower than the stock move suggests. On the earnings call, Datadog's finance chief said its single largest customer reduced usage in the third quarter. That usage stayed above the customer's contractual minimum. But the fourth-quarter guide assumes its revenue falls to that minimum. That helps explain a fourth-quarter revenue guide of 1.19 to 1.20 billion dollars. By our arithmetic, that's essentially flat on the quarter just reported. Strip that one customer out, and management says implied fourth-quarter growth is in the low-to-mid-30s percent range. That's roughly where the business already runs. So this isn't a broad demand warning. It's a beat, with one fourth-quarter line pulled down by one customer stepping to its floor.

Two qualifiers keep that honest. First, 35 percent growth is a marginal deceleration from 36 percent last quarter, per Datadog's own releases. Second, management said AI customers' growth continues to significantly outpace the rest of the business. It also said non-AI customer growth accelerated for a fifth straight quarter. But it gave no revenue share for the AI cohort. So we can say the mix is shifting, not by how much.

So what does Datadog settle on the pricing question? It confirms the read this show reached yesterday, with a twist. Cloudflare, which also prices on usage, rallied on its fourth-quarter guide. Datadog, on the same pricing model, fell hard. Same model, opposite reactions, so the split sits in the forward guide, not the pricing model. The twist is what drove Datadog's guide, and this part is my inference. Consumption pricing cuts both ways. Revenue compounds when customers use more. It can also fall without any churn, when one big customer optimizes down to its contracted minimum. Here, the floor that protected Datadog was the minimum commitment, not the usage model. Nor does this print show usage-priced growth hitting budget caps. Management tied the step-down to one customer, and outside that customer, growth is holding. So what: at a usage-priced vendor, one concentrated account can override whatever protection consumption pricing is supposed to offer.

A guide is one forward signal. Contracted backlog is another, and Palantir just filed its own. A quick re-ground first. Palantir's third-quarter revenue came in around 2.31 billion dollars, up 96 percent year over year. The new number comes from Palantir's 10-Q. Remaining performance obligations, the GAAP backlog measure, sit at 4.9 billion dollars. That's up 88 percent year over year, but flat from the prior quarter.

Put that next to the debate around Palantir's bookings. On Tuesday, this show flagged that Palantir's revenue was outrunning its pipeline. That rested on remaining deal value, a metric Palantir defines itself. Chief executive Alex Karp's explanation, on the earnings call, was duration compression. Commercial customers, he said, are signing shorter contracts that deploy faster and expand later. Now a GAAP number points the same way. The trouble is that a flat backlog can't tell Karp's story from the less comfortable one. Shorter contracts shrink backlog mechanically, even when demand is fine. So does a failure to refill the pipeline. Both predict exactly what the 10-Q shows. So what: Palantir's forward book has stopped growing, even on a GAAP measure. Only next quarter's remaining performance obligations can tell the two readings apart. If they reaccelerate, that points to duration compression. If they stay flat, it points somewhere less comfortable.

If forward expectations are what got sold, look at which names had the most of them priced in. Thursday's selloff was mostly beta, but it left one split worth banking. Per Yahoo Finance, Palantir and CrowdStrike each fell nearly 10 percent, while Salesforce and Adobe lost 2 percent or less. My read is that this is about where expectations were crowded, not about who is losing to AI. If anything, it runs against the disintermediation narrative hanging over Salesforce and Adobe. The names with the most priced-in forward growth had the most room to fall. The names the market already treats as potential AI casualties had less left to give up. That's one session and an inference, not a durable floor under the incumbents.

And if the market already prices seat-based incumbents as the likely losers, somebody is claiming the budget they're feared to lose. That brings us to OpenAI. The AI Insider reported on president Greg Brockman's remarks at the WSJ Tech Conference this past week. Brockman said the enterprise business doubled in the third quarter. He also said annualized revenue grew 70 percent quarter on quarter. That's a real upgrade. Earlier this week, OpenAI's enterprise push looked like a string of partnerships with no disclosed economics. Now there's a growth rate. There still isn't a base.

Look closely at the two numbers. The account doesn't say whether the 70 percent covers enterprise revenue or the whole company. It also doesn't say whether the doubling is sequential or year over year. If both figures describe the same quantity over the same quarter, they can't both be true. And neither comes with a dollar base, so neither can be checked. There's also a plain alternative reading. Fast growth from a small base would fit both numbers, and still leave the business small next to incumbent software. These are spoken claims at a conference, not audited disclosure. So what: Brockman moves OpenAI one rung up the evidence ladder, from announced partnerships to a stated growth rate. It doesn't get any further than that. And nothing here shows budget actually moving from Salesforce or Adobe to OpenAI. That link remains inference.

Pull the thread together. This week handed the market three kinds of forward signal, and they differ in how checkable they are. Datadog's guide is a specific, quoted number, and its selloff traces to a nameable, mechanical cause. Palantir's backlog now sits in a filed GAAP measure, even if it can't yet settle the debate it was meant to settle. OpenAI's claim is the least verifiable of the three. It's a rate with no base, from the company making the boldest claim. Here's my inference about where that leads. Verification itself is becoming a valuation input. Disclose a checkable figure, and you get graded on it, sometimes harshly, as Datadog just found. Claim a rate with no base, and the discount comes later. For a private company like OpenAI, it waits for the day it has to file. That raises the pressure on any lab or AI-native vendor heading toward public markets to disclose a base.

Three things to watch from here. First, whether Palantir's remaining performance obligations reaccelerate or stay flat next quarter. Second, whether OpenAI ever puts a dollar base under its enterprise growth claims. Third, whether Datadog's next guide repeats the largest-customer caveat, or whether this quarter's step-down to the minimum was a one-time reset.
