Digital Money, Crypto, and Market Structure

Transcript · November 24, 2026

Good morning. It's Tuesday, November 24th, and this is your digital money briefing. Today's thesis is simple: the market's buyers have stopped offsetting its sellers.

Start with the tape. Bitcoin printed a cycle low of roughly fifty-five thousand seven hundred dollars over the weekend. It started November near eighty thousand dollars. Trackers now put it back around the low sixty-thousands, though the prints vary by source. The slide capped a brutal week for equities, with the Nasdaq Composite down about ten percent and the S&P 500 down eight point six percent. On Sunday we argued that this co-movement undercuts the digital-gold pitch, and we won't re-run that today. The question that matters now is narrower. With the wrapper selling, who is still buying?

That brings us to the lead. On Sunday, this desk flagged a specific catalyst: would Strategy's next filing show buying, selling, or buybacks? Strategy's Form 8-K, filed Monday and covering November 16th through 22nd, answers it. The company is still buying bitcoin, but only just. The filing's table shows four hundred fifty-seven bitcoin bought for twenty-nine point six million dollars. That's an average price of sixty-four thousand eight hundred ninety-eight dollars. Holdings now stand at eight hundred fifty-one thousand three hundred five bitcoin. The average cost across the stack is seventy-five thousand four hundred forty-six dollars, well above this week's purchase price. That gap is an accounting mark, not a solvency event. The purchase was funded entirely by net proceeds from sales of MSTR common stock.

The same filing discloses something new. Strategy repurchased one hundred forty-seven million dollars of its STRC preferred stock. That was funded from the same pool of stock-sale proceeds. Against twenty-nine point six million dollars of bitcoin, that's roughly five dollars of buyback for every dollar of coin. Last week this desk flagged a ratio of about six to one. So the pattern holds. Strategy is selling common stock mainly to retire its own preferred, and bitcoin gets the smaller share of the proceeds.

Now the open question, and we should be honest about what we can and can't resolve. Suppose Strategy sold common stock below the value of its bitcoin, below one times mNAV. Then buying bitcoin with those proceeds shrinks existing holders' bitcoin per share rather than growing it. There's evidence pointing that way. Bitcoin critic Peter Schiff posted on Thursday, November 19th, that Strategy's mNAV was, quote, "now below one." That falls inside this week's purchase window. It also fits CryptoTicker's reading of point nine seven for Friday, which this desk cited on Sunday. But one is a commentator's claim, and the other is one tracker's snapshot. Neither is a disclosed company metric. And we don't have MSTR's price and mNAV paired for the days these shares were actually sold. So treat the dilution case as live, not settled.

One more line from the filing. The board raised the preferred repurchase authorization, and one point one two billion dollars remained available as of November 22nd. That's capacity to keep buying back preferred stock. It is not a disclosed purchase beyond the one hundred forty-seven million already counted. So here's where Strategy lands. The treasury company that was supposed to be the permanent marginal bid for bitcoin is still buying, but in tickets too small to matter against the other side of the trade.

Too small relative to what? To the ETF complex. U.S. spot bitcoin ETFs lost one point seven nine billion dollars in the week to Friday, November 20th. That's the largest weekly outflow since the week ended June 26th. BlackRock's IBIT accounted for about seventy-six percent of it. That included back-to-back daily records for the fund, five hundred forty-one million dollars on Thursday and five hundred forty-five million on Friday. That Friday IBIT figure matches the SoSoValue version this desk cited yesterday. Call it partial corroboration, not reconciliation, because the weekly source doesn't name its data provider. Now put Strategy's week against it. Twenty-nine point six million dollars of corporate buying is about one point seven percent of the wrapper's weekly outflow. The largest steady corporate bid in this market is now a rounding error against the exit.

But don't take that exit at face value either. November's cumulative outflow reached four point one four billion dollars through Friday. That's still short of June's record month of four point five one billion, and Monday's data isn't in yet. Meanwhile, combined net assets fell from about one hundred ten billion dollars in mid-September to seventy-one point three five billion on Friday. That's a decline of roughly a third, not half. Here's our arithmetic. November's redemptions are only about a ninth of that roughly thirty-nine billion dollar drop. The rest is price, plus earlier flows this data doesn't break out. So the bigger story in the asset number is holders sitting through a mark-to-market loss, not money walking out the door.

There's a business read-through too, and it's our inference. The issuers' fee-earning base is down about a third since September. And the largest franchise, IBIT, is carrying most of the redemptions. That's a direct hit to the asset managers whose products anchored the institutionalization story. Nor is it only bitcoin. Spot ether ETFs lost another three hundred fourteen million dollars over the week, their eighth straight week of outflows. So the exit is real, and broader than one fund. But it's narrower than the headline asset number implies.

The same question runs through the on-chain dollar, and there yesterday's read didn't hold up. Per Stablecoin Insider, total stablecoin supply fell four point four three billion dollars in the week to November 23rd. That leaves two hundred eighty-four point four four billion outstanding. Circle's USDC accounted for six hundred seven million dollars of the decline. Do the arithmetic. USDC supplied only about fourteen percent of the drop, against a twenty-four percent share of supply. On Monday we flagged USDC shrinking about twice as fast as Tether in the prior week, as a possible regulated-channel exit. This week, USDC was no longer leading the contraction. Most of the drop came from outside Circle, and this snapshot can't tell us which issuers drove it. One week reversing one week isn't a trend either way. But it weakens our Monday inference that the regulated onshore channels are the fastest way out, at least for stablecoins. The contraction is broadening, and it isn't uniquely a Circle story.

So where does that leave the bull and the skeptic? Start with the bull case, built only from what's confirmed. ETF holders have sat through roughly a third of the complex's value disappearing while redeeming only a fraction of it. Strategy is still buying, even in a brutal week. And the stablecoin contraction looks orderly and no longer concentrated in one issuer. What's missing is a buyer of size, and a single inflow session to suggest the floor is in.

Now the skeptical case. ETF redemptions just hit their heaviest weekly pace since June, concentrated in the single largest fund. The only steady corporate buyer is funding a shrinking purchase with common stock. And it's doing so six days before a forced index sale of that same stock, when MSCI deletes Strategy after the November 30th close. Nobody in this data can tell you who's on the other side absorbing the supply.

Three things to watch this week. First, Monday and Tuesday's ETF prints, measured against June's four point five one billion dollar record. Second, Strategy's next filing, and MSTR's share price through the November 30th MSCI deletion. Third, whether next week's stablecoin snapshot shows this broadening continue, or the skew snap back toward USDC.

Bottom line: the marginal buyer in this market still hasn't shown up, and the largest corporate bid is now too small to matter.

That's it for today. Stay cautious, stay optimistic, and we'll see you tomorrow.
