Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Credit & the Corporate Credit Cycle

Transcript · September 30, 2026

Good morning, it's Wednesday, September 30th. Here's what's moving underneath the credit markets.

The thesis today is simple. The unconditional bid for private credit is ending. The regulator, the syndicated loan market, and retail money are all starting to discriminate. That shifts the risk away from a run on the asset class and toward something slower: adverse selection of whatever is left inside it.

Start with the SEC. Yesterday, Tuesday, September 29th, the Commission's chief accountant, Kurt Hohl, and the director of its Division of Investment Management, Brian Daly, issued a joint staff statement on fair-value measurement for private assets. It is staff guidance, not a rule. The statement says so itself. It has "no legal force or effect." It "does not alter or amend applicable law." And it "creates no new or additional obligations for any person." So this is not a crackdown, and you shouldn't hear it as one. Nor is it evidence that the staff sees mispricing today. The better read is a paper trail. The SEC is writing down what it expects boards, valuation designees, and auditors to be doing with these marks, before it ever has to enforce anything.

And the statement singles out private credit. It describes these assets as "illiquid, individually negotiated loans that do not trade on established secondary markets." Valuing them, it says, "frequently requires the use of significant unobservable inputs." Translation: these are model marks, not market marks. Then the statement gets concrete, in three steps. First, calibrate the valuation model so that at initial recognition it matches the actual transaction price. Second, periodically check model outputs against market evidence, such as comparable transactions, secondary indications, and credit indices. Third, disclose the significant inputs, like discount rates and credit spreads, and how much fair value would move if those inputs changed. That is a concrete list auditors can point to going into year-end. The scope is explicit too. It names registered closed-end funds, interval funds, tender-offer funds, and registered private funds. And it names business development companies. So BDCs are squarely inside it.

Why now? Scale is part of the answer. The statement cites private credit holdings inside registered fund portfolios growing from 170 billion dollars in December 2020 to 270 billion in December 2025. That's nearly 60 percent growth in five years. Keep that number in its own box. It measures registered funds, not BDC assets, so don't chain it to BDC sizing figures you've heard elsewhere. The point is timing. This is a fast-growing, retail-adjacent corner of the market, and wealth-channel and 401k access are being widened at the same moment. The credibility of its pricing is now on the SEC's record, not just a talking point for critics.

Recall Sunday's briefing. We walked through a second-lien loan to Loparex, held in Blue Owl's flagship BDC, whose mark went from near 88 cents to around 5 cents in six months. As we said then, a private mark reflects the last test event, not continuous price discovery. Periodic checks against market evidence are an attempt to create those test events before a missed coupon does it for you.

That regulatory push lands against a backdrop where the hard numbers are already moving. KBRA reviewed the rated business development companies. It found the median non-accrual rate at non-perpetual-life BDCs rose to 2.75 percent of total investments at cost in the second quarter. That's up from 1.81 percent in the first quarter, close to a full point in a single quarter. KBRA calls this "selective credit issues," concentrated in a limited number of borrowers and managers rather than broad deterioration. Take that framing with some skepticism. A median can be pushed by a handful of names, and a rating agency has little incentive to sound alarmed. The metric also says nothing about payment-in-kind income propping up reported earnings, and this data doesn't let us check that. It's also second-quarter data, and a separate series from the Morningstar figure we cited Sunday, so don't splice the two. Still, the direction is what matters. Realized stress is climbing in exactly the vehicles whose self-reported marks the SEC just flagged.

Now there's a quieter reason those marks may drift, one no non-accrual report will show yet. It's about who is leaving the book. PitchBook LCD data show a record 12 billion dollars of direct-lending deals refinanced into the syndicated loan market in the third quarter. That's the highest quarterly total since LCD started tracking this flow in early 2022.

The broader volume picture points the same way. By PitchBook's count, sponsor-backed volume in the broadly syndicated market climbed to 65 billion dollars over the latest three months, nearly back to its first-quarter level. Direct lending's sponsor-backed volume was 28 billion, still well short of its own first-quarter mark. So the banks have recovered most of their lost ground, and the direct lenders have not. Meanwhile, direct-lending spreads on new-issue leveraged buyouts averaged 502 basis points in the three months through August. That's up 27 basis points from the first quarter.

Put that together and here's the read. When banks can underwrite competitively, borrowers who can reach cheaper syndicated paper are refinancing out. If those are the cleanest credits, what's left in direct lenders' portfolios skews toward borrowers who couldn't get bank terms. That is an inference, not a measured fact. The data says nothing about the credit quality of who left. There's a benign reading too. These could simply be sponsors harvesting cheaper spreads on performing credits, which signals healthy capital-market access rather than a private-credit problem. The spread widening cuts both ways as well. It could be fair pay for a riskier mix, or just pricing power at the lower end, where bank competition is thin. But if the adverse-selection reading is right, it's the kind of quality drift that doesn't show up in a non-accrual rate until it has already happened. It's also exactly what a model calibrated at origination will miss. That's why the SEC's timing matters.

It also leaves direct lenders with a choice. They can compress spreads to hold on to deals, or accept a riskier mix at wider spreads. So far, the move to 502 basis points suggests they're choosing the second. That's a live test of whether private credit's premium over syndicated loans is structural, paid for speed, flexibility, and certainty of execution. The alternative is that it was a leftover from the post-2022 bank retreat, and the banks are now arbitraging it away. Either way, private credit's premium is being tested from the top of the credit spectrum down.

Retail investors are running a version of the same sorting exercise, on managers instead of borrowers. Over the past ten days we've tracked the redemption queues at the big non-traded funds, with Apollo's and HPS's flagship vehicles still capped but easing. Goldman Sachs' 18.2 billion dollar GS Credit fund just reported third-quarter tender requests of only 2 percent of shares, down from 3.2 percent last quarter. It has stayed under its customary 5 percent cap since inception. By contrast, the biggest non-traded private credit funds saw third-quarter requests ranging from 10 percent to over 16 percent of shares. The reporting doesn't name which funds sit in that range, so don't pin it on any one manager. This isn't one number moving for the whole asset class. It's dispersion between funds.

Be fair about what drives it. It could reflect genuinely stronger underwriting at GS Credit. It could equally reflect a younger vintage, or a private-bank investor base with lower turnover. The reporting doesn't settle which. The second-order effect doesn't depend on the answer, though. Advisors are likely to steer new money toward funds with low request rates and clean tender histories. That would widen the fundraising gap between winners and laggards in the wealth channel. So the wealth channel isn't fleeing private credit wholesale. It's choosing managers, and that discrimination is itself new.

So where does this leave the cycle? There's no break today. But the bid for private credit is becoming selective at every layer. The regulator is questioning marks. The loan market is picking borrowers. Retail investors are picking managers. And selection tends to work against whatever is left behind. The biggest unresolved risk is that the assets sitting in adversely selected books are carried at marks nobody has stress-tested through a real downturn.

Watch three things. First, Blue Owl Credit Income Corp's tender, which closes today, with no results out yet. Second, third-quarter BDC filings in November, which will show whether non-accruals kept climbing after the second quarter's jump. Third, how auditors treat this SEC statement in year-end filings. If they push harder on calibration and market checks, the first sign of stress may show up as NAV markdowns before it ever shows up as a default.
