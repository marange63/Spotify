Good morning. It's Tuesday, September 15th, and here's what's moving across chips and compute.

Start with a reversal. For a decade, Samsung has been the leading-edge foundry that wins business by cutting price. This week it did the opposite. TrendForce reports that Samsung is refusing to lower its price on a two-nanometer manufacturing deal with Qualcomm, and that the disagreement may push that deal into 2027. Take the delay as a reported possibility, not a decision. Neither company has said anything on the record. What is clear is that this is not a technology problem. Samsung's two-nanometer gate-all-around yield has climbed above 70 percent, up from below 50 percent at the start of this year. That figure is TrendForce's characterization, not a Samsung disclosure. Samsung does not publish node yields. But the process is working well enough that price, not capability, is what the two sides are arguing about.

That matters because of a test this show set on Friday. We said the real measure of a credible second foundry source was whether TSMC's pricing power at two nanometers and below softened at all. The second source showed up this week and did the opposite. Samsung is not undercutting the leader. It is holding its price and letting the largest available mobile customer walk. Competition arrived at the leading edge and made it more expensive, not less.

Here's why Samsung can afford that. It has already secured Tesla and Broadcom as anchor two-nanometer customers at its Taylor, Texas fab, where it has invested more than 37 billion dollars. The Tesla order alone, covering the AI5 and AI6 chips, is worth roughly 14.5 billion dollars and targets full production in 2027. That is contracted future volume, not shipped silicon. But it is enough booked capacity that Samsung doesn't need Qualcomm's business badly enough to discount for it. Samsung also raised prices on its older four-nanometer line in July, by 10 to 15 percent for some customers. So what reads as a technology turnaround is really a capacity-allocation story. The pricing discipline is derivative, not structural. It holds only as long as Tesla and Broadcom keep that fab full.

There's a second risk hiding inside the flattering read. A 70 percent yield is a manufacturing achievement, and it tells you nothing about who ends up with Qualcomm's volume. The likelier outcome is that Qualcomm goes to TSMC rather than pay Samsung's price. In that case Samsung's technical position improves at the same moment its commercial position doesn't, and TSMC's leading-edge share strengthens precisely when its only credible competitor becomes credible. Capability convergence and share divergence can happen at once, and they should be scored separately. There's a quieter shift here too. Tesla arriving as a two-nanometer anchor widens the leading-edge buyer base beyond mobile, hyperscaler ASICs, and merchant GPUs for the first time. In 2027, an automaker is bidding for the same wafers as an AI accelerator program.

So if even the challenger can charge a premium, the next question is whether the capacity that would break that premium is on a calendar. On wafer counts, the honest answer is that we don't know with any precision. There is reporting that TSMC's two-nanometer output climbs significantly into next year. But the specific figures conflict badly across sources. Two different secondary aggregators describe ramp trajectories that cannot both be right, and neither traces to a TSMC earnings call, filing, or investor release. So I'm not putting a wafer number on it today. The one capacity figure I'll carry is the shape rather than the level. That same reporting has TSMC's two-nanometer and A16 capacity growing at roughly a 70 percent compound annual rate from 2026 through 2028. Directionally useful, sourcing-discounted.

Packaging is where the evidence is better. TSMC's advanced-packaging process, CoWoS, is what stacks memory alongside AI logic, and it has been the binding constraint on whether Nvidia and AMD ship product or merely announce it. Institutional-investor estimates relayed by Economic Daily News, via TrendForce, put the CoWoS supply-demand gap narrowing from around 20 percent now to about 10 percent by the end of this year. The same estimate has TSMC's monthly CoWoS capacity reaching 120,000 to 140,000 wafers, approaching 200,000 once outside packaging partners are counted. Say the discount out loud: that is analyst estimation relayed through the trade press, not a TSMC disclosure.

The yield evidence is firmer. TrendForce, reporting from TSMC's own technology symposium material, says the 5.5-reticle CoWoS process is yielding consistently above 98 percent across multiple AI customer products. The same material puts advanced-packaging capacity on a growth path above 80 percent a year from 2022 through 2027. A process at 98 percent yield is not being rationed by defects. It is being rationed by tools and floorspace, and tools and floorspace have delivery dates.

Notice what TSMC itself points at next. In that same symposium material, a TSMC speaker names memory and ABF substrate supply, not packaging, as the key bottlenecks in the AI supply chain going forward. That's a handoff from the company best positioned to know where its own constraint sits. It is also an unnamed speaker paraphrased by a trade outlet, which is the fourth time in this briefing I've had to say a version of that. Friday's other forward test asked for a packaging-equipment vendor to disclose a real change to its 2027 order book. Nothing like that has landed. The packaging question is now half-answered, and still not by a primary source.

Which brings us to memory, where the same scarcity pricing shows up most extremely. Micron's adjusted gross margin hit 84.9 percent last quarter. That's against 74.9 percent the quarter before, and 39 percent a year ago. Cloud and core data-center memory sales were 25.29 billion dollars, about 61 percent of total revenue. And Micron's own characterization of why is that supply, not demand, is setting the pace.

That phrasing is the bearish read hiding inside the bullish number. A margin driven by absent supply is not a durable business model. It is a shortage rent, and shortage rents have a mechanical expiry date. Management has guided the current quarter to roughly 50 billion dollars in revenue at an 86 percent margin. But that print hasn't happened. Treat it as a forecast from the party benefiting from it, not a result.

Put the three beats together and you get the tension worth sitting with. Foundry pricing, packaging pricing, and memory pricing are all behaving as though scarcity is permanent, in the same weeks that the capacity which ends that scarcity acquires published dates. Both cannot extrapolate cleanly. Either the pricing is ahead of the physical reality, or the capacity schedules are more optimistic than they'll prove. Either way, the effect on hyperscalers is identical. The bill of materials for an AI server is rising through the foundry and memory lines while the GPU die itself gets no cheaper. The marginal cost pressure on AI economics has moved off the accelerator and onto the inputs. That lengthens payback without ever appearing as a GPU price increase.

One closing item, because it marks where the next physical limit sits. SK hynix has published a paper in Nature Electronics on an optical interconnect claiming over 100 terabits per second of bandwidth per node, at under one picojoule per bit and under 10 nanoseconds of latency. The notable part is that it extends the optical connection to the memory interface itself, not just between compute dies. This is a paper, not a product, with no production timeline disclosed, and it says nothing about HBM4 or any near-term generation. But a memory maker publishing on interconnect rather than density tells you where it thinks the constraint is moving.

So, two things to watch. First, whether Qualcomm's two-nanometer volume is confirmed at Samsung or lands at TSMC instead. Second, whether TSMC itself puts a real number behind the packaging ramp, on an earnings call or an investor disclosure, rather than leaving it to analyst estimates. Until one of those lands, the honest state of play is that pricing behavior and capacity schedules across the leading edge are pointing in opposite directions, and something in that gap has to give.
