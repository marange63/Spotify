Cautious Optimism Briefings

Robotics & Physical AI

Transcript · October 17, 2026

Good morning. It's Saturday, October 17th, and this is your robotics and physical AI briefing.

Today's numbers all point the same way. Physical AI's scale is finally showing up as hard figures: a fleet count, a revenue run-rate, a shipment share. But almost every one of them is self-reported. And where an outside check exists, it doesn't line up cleanly with the headline. That gap, between what's disclosed and what's verified, is the thing worth pricing today.

Start with the robotaxi race. Tesla's Austin Cybercab fleet jumped roughly eightfold in about a month, to more than three hundred vehicles, as reported by the Motley Fool. That is Tesla's own framing, and eightfold growth to three hundred means the base a month ago was only a few dozen cars. Texas registration data tells a related but more precise story. Registry tallies reported by tech-insider show Tesla added a hundred and fifty Cybercabs in a single batch in early October. That pushed its registered Cybercab count from 169 to 319. Add the roughly 420 Model Y robotaxis Tesla also runs in Texas, and its combined autonomous-vehicle registration comes to about 739. Waymo's registry-based total in the state is roughly 1,185, still well ahead. Those are secondary tallies, one step removed from the registry itself, so treat them as credible rather than official.

So Tesla's growth rate is real, but it's a growth rate off a small base, measured in registered vehicles, not unsupervised miles. And vehicle count is the cheap part for an automaker. What nobody has confirmed this month is what share of that fleet actually runs without an in-vehicle safety monitor. Not Tesla, and not any regulator. The last concrete tracking on that question is months old and covered a small fraction of today's claimed fleet. So treat "three hundred plus Cybercabs" as a registration number, not a driverless-operation number, until Tesla or Texas says otherwise.

The operator Tesla is chasing moved the other way this week: slower, and across a border. Waymo announced on October 16th that it has been approved to join Ontario's automated-vehicle pilot program. TechCrunch's headline says Waymo's robotaxis are headed to Canada. But the same report is explicit that Waymo isn't launching a robotaxi service just yet. The first phase is manually driven mapping of Toronto's streets to build high-definition maps. The next phase is testing on public roads with a human safety operator on board. Paid, driverless rides are a later step, and this approval doesn't grant them.

On Thursday, this desk argued that with Waymo's five-billion-dollar debt financing done, its binding constraint had shifted to regulators and edge cases, market by market. Ontario is that pattern in action. Waymo scales jurisdiction by jurisdiction, with regulators setting the pace. Tesla scales vehicle count, while the harder step, proving unsupervised operation and winning approval for it, is still ahead. Those are two different binding constraints, and only one of them is actually the moat.

A fleet count is one kind of self-reported scale. The model layer just produced another, and this one is revenue. Skild AI, the robot foundation-model startup, says it has reached a hundred-million-dollar recurring revenue run-rate just ten months after beginning commercial deployments. It says its general-purpose robot brain is used by more than sixty companies and deployed across hundreds of robots.

That cuts against the close of Thursday's briefing. This desk said then that nobody in the West had shown a diversified order book, or a utilization number, to match the capital flowing into physical AI. Skild is the first scaled counterexample on breadth, and notably it sits at the model layer, not the humanoid-hardware layer. It also answers Thursday's point about Nvidia's free, open GR00T release. We said that release pressures any robot-brain startup whose edge is architecture rather than deployment data. Skild's reply is customers and robots in the field, which is exactly the kind of edge an open model can't give away.

But do the arithmetic before getting excited. A hundred million dollars across more than sixty companies is at most about one and a half million dollars per customer. That's plausible for enterprise integration work, but it says nothing about margin. Skild hasn't disclosed how much is model licensing versus services and deployment labor. That split decides whether this is a software business or a systems integrator wearing a software multiple. And hundreds of robots across sixty-plus customers is only a handful of robots per customer. That looks more like well-funded pilots than fleets. So Skild is the strongest evidence yet that a general-purpose robot brain can be sold across many bodies and many customers. It just isn't audited, and the mix question is unresolved.

If Western revenue is showing up at the brain, the bodies are being built somewhere else. Chinese vendors accounted for more than ninety-five percent of all humanoid robots shipped globally in the first half of this year, according to IDC data reported by Forbes. That needs a correction on the record. On Thursday, this desk cited IDC data, via TechNode, putting China's share at 77.9 percent for the same half-year. Two numbers, the same nominal source, and they don't reconcile. Neither report spells out its definitions, so the gap could come from product scope, units versus value, or the vendor base. Don't read it as China gaining share. Read it as a sign that humanoid shipment tracking is too loose to compare period to period. What both numbers agree on is direction: China owns the volume.

Morgan Stanley's forecasting path points the same way. The bank started this year predicting fourteen thousand Chinese humanoid shipments. By early spring, it had doubled that to twenty-eight thousand. That's an escalating sentiment signal as much as a production number, but it's moving one way. And shipments still aren't deployments. None of these figures comes with a utilization number.

The export-control exposure is concrete at Unitree, the Chinese humanoid maker that surged four hundred sixty percent on its Shanghai debut after a nine-hundred-four-million-dollar IPO in August. Its prospectus says U.S. sales were 13.3 percent of its 2025 revenue. So a U.S. restriction on Chinese humanoids would hit a material slice of revenue at one of China's leading listed humanoid makers. That pressure pushes toward separate China-aligned and U.S.-aligned robot supply chains.

Put those threads together, and the desk's inference is that the humanoid value chain is splitting. Western capital and revenue are clustering at the model layer, while manufacturing volume concentrates in China. The EV-battery and solar industries followed a similar path. If that precedent holds, margin migrates to whoever controls the model and the customer, and to whoever has volume manufacturing. Western humanoid hardware makers sit in the squeeze between them.

One more correction belongs inside that China story. On Thursday, this desk flagged November 10th as a rare-earth export-control date touching the magnets in robot-joint actuators. One report now says that truce has been extended into January. But no primary notice from China's commerce ministry confirms a new date, so treat the extension as reported, not established. The same reporting says the underlying licensing rules for those rare earths were never suspended. If so, the input-cost exposure for humanoid makers everywhere doesn't go away just because a deadline moves.

To close, here's the line between what's inflecting and what's still a claim. Robotaxi fleet build-out is real and accelerating, but driverless operation at Tesla's claimed scale is not verified. Robot-brain revenue is real enough to have a run-rate, but it isn't audited, and its margin mix is unknown. Chinese humanoid volume is dominant by any tracker's count, but the deployment and utilization behind those units remain undisclosed.

Watch three catalysts. Intuitive Surgical reports earnings on October 20th. It's the one embodied-AI business with FDA-regulated revenue at real scale, and the print hasn't happened yet. Tesla holds its third-quarter call on October 21st. The test there is whether Tesla discloses driverless miles or utilization, not just another vehicle count. And Agility's shareholder vote and SPAC close, expected in November, will give the market its first daily-marked humanoid comparable.

That's the briefing. Have a good weekend.
