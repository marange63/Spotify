Good morning. It's Wednesday, November 11th, and here's the orbital economy briefing.

This week the money showed up. The satellites and the rockets did not keep pace. AST SpaceMobile's third-quarter report is the cleanest test of that gap, so let's start there.

Two questions hung over AST's report all week. First, would its November 2nd demo with AT&T, which handed a connection from AT&T's ground network over to AST's satellites, get a dated path to commercial service? Second, what does the cash pile actually buy? The filings answer the first more bluntly than the headline does, and the second only in part.

Start with revenue. Per AST's earnings release, third-quarter revenue was $42.3 million, "driven by gateway deliveries and U.S. Government milestones met." That is hardware and program revenue. It is not subscribers paying for direct-to-cell service. The distinction matters. Gateway shipments and milestone payments are lumpy, and they arrive when contracts say so. A subscription business compounds. So the headline number tells you very little yet about direct-to-cell as a service business.

Now the full year. AST reaffirmed guidance of $150 to $200 million, with "upside based on recent contract signatures." That phrase leans on contracted work, not on recognized service revenue. The quarterly filing shows about $88.6 million booked through nine months. Run the arithmetic, and the fourth quarter alone has to deliver roughly $61 million just to reach the low end. That's about half again above this quarter's $42.3 million. So the real test of the guide isn't in this print. The quarter that decides it closes in about seven weeks.

Next, the constellation. On the call, the CEO said BlueBirds 11, 12 and 13 "have been flown successfully and are in operation in space." He and the CFO both held to a target of approximately 45 BlueBirds in orbit by the middle of 2027. In the call material we reviewed, neither said whether that target had moved. Service readiness, in the release's own words, means "initial, intermittent nationwide coverage expected in key markets by year-end 2026." That answers the question about the AT&T demo. It got a date for intermittent coverage. It did not get a date for commercial service. Intermittent coverage is a regulatory-and-marketing milestone, not a continuous product.

One wrinkle on launch. Last Friday, this desk noted that AST had tied its 2027 satellite target to Blue Origin's New Glenn, which has been grounded since a launchpad explosion in May. On the call, the CEO cast the next New Glenn launch as a hedge, not the backbone of the plan. His words: "That is a risk mitigation as they return to flight." Our read is that most of the 45 satellites will ride on other vehicles. If that's right, AST is less hostage to Blue Origin's recovery than it looked on Friday.

That launch dependence is the backdrop to AST's other big move. Blacklift, announced last week, is AST's own reusable rocket, built around engines it is buying from Firefly Aerospace. Firefly put the engine deal at more than $1.3 billion in cash and stock over three years. That includes a license for AST to manufacture the engines itself. AST's filing calls this a long-held strategy of vertical integration. The practical logic is control. If third-party rockets keep slipping, AST's deployment clock slips with them. The call put a price on the next leg. Management said it expects "several hundred million dollars of incremental CapEx in 2027 associated with Blacklift." The Firefly payments are spread out too, described as "a mix of proceeds in cash and stock and further payments over time." And on the company's own target, Blacklift doesn't fly before 2028. So the open question is whether this is a cadence fix or a capital sink. The first look comes tomorrow, when AST unveils the rocket in New York.

Which brings us to the cash. AST reports approximately $3.2 billion in cash, cash equivalents and restricted cash as of September 30th. Set Blacklift's 2027 bill against that, and the rocket is affordable. The larger draw is the build toward about 45 satellites, and I can't size that today. The cash-flow figures we'd need weren't available in verified form, so I won't hand you a runway number. What the balance sheet does say is that money is not AST's binding constraint. Deployment is.

The fair counter-read deserves its due. For a pre-service operator, that cash plus milestone-driven government revenue is exactly what a credible runway should look like. The market may be right to look through one quarter. For AST holders, though, the equity is now a bet on when service starts. The year-end intermittent-coverage target is the next binary test. A slip would reopen the gap between demo and service, just as SpaceX plans its own Starlink Mobile ground network. So the plain verdict is this. AST is financed. It is not yet a service.

If the problem were only a Western challenger short of rockets, you'd expect China's state-backed constellations to be pulling away. Yesterday, this desk framed it roughly that way: China flies while the West signs. The math is less flattering than that framing allowed.

The tracker Orbital Radar, updated November 4th, counts 231 Guowang and 248 Qianfan satellites on orbit. Those are the same counts we cited last Friday. The new piece comes from a second tracker, KeepTrack. It reports that Qianfan didn't launch at all between July 5th and August 26th, a gap of more than seven weeks. On its observed pace, KeepTrack projects Qianfan's 648-satellite first phase finishing around late 2028. That is close to three years behind plan.

Treat that carefully. It is one tracker's extrapolation, not an official Chinese schedule. Recent batches have been accelerating, and a step-change in cadence could pull the date forward. A plan three years behind may also say as much about an over-ambitious original plan as about slow execution. But the honest update is this. China's programs fly, but years later than advertised. State backing buys capital and ambition. It does not buy launch cadence.

The second-order effect runs to SpaceX. Spectrum coordination and orbital-slot priority reward deployed hardware. A Qianfan running years behind widens the window in which Starlink can entrench that lead. It's a window, not a verdict, because accelerating Chinese cadence could close it.

The same pattern shows up in Washington's own money. The Defense Department's Office of Strategic Capital has announced a $150 million conditional loan commitment to Rocket Lab. As we reported yesterday, Rocket Lab's next rocket, Neutron, still hasn't flown and still has no firm date. A conditional commitment is not a disbursement. The announcement as we have it gives no terms, and it doesn't say what the money funds. Read generously, Washington is treating non-SpaceX launch capacity as strategic infrastructure worth backing with credit. That puts a public-policy floor under the challenger bench, even as its schedules slip. Read skeptically, $150 million is only a bit over half of the $268 million in revenue Rocket Lab booked in the third quarter alone. That makes it more signal than balance-sheet change. Either way, Washington is now extending credit to a launch challenger whose next rocket has not reached orbit, though nothing announced ties this loan to Neutron.

Which leaves the one operator without a cadence problem, and it tests its next vehicle on Friday. SpacePolicyOnline, relaying SpaceX, says Starship's fifteenth flight is targeted for "as early as November 13, 2026 pending regulatory approval." The plan is the first attempt to catch the ship stage at the tower. The booster has been caught three times, on flights five, seven and eight. The ship never has.

The stakes are asymmetric. A successful catch would clear the last major recovery milestone before rapid full reuse. That's the lever behind both Starlink's economics and NASA's lunar lander architecture. It would also widen the gap between SpaceX and every capital-rich, cadence-poor challenger named today. A failure would be a reminder that even the incumbent's next leg of cadence isn't guaranteed. Either way, nothing has flown yet. On the same day, ULA's Vulcan is targeted to carry Amazon Leo satellites. That's a reminder that constellation operators are still spreading launch across vehicles to manage exactly this scarcity.

So where does this leave the orbital economy? What's genuinely inflecting is capital access for challengers: AST's $3.2 billion, Rocket Lab's government loan commitment, and China's state backing. What's still a demo is the deployment rate. AST's coverage is intermittent and targeted, not delivered. Qianfan is, on one tracker's math, years behind its own plan. Neutron hasn't flown once. The open question is simple. Can anyone besides SpaceX turn money into a sustained deployment rate? For catalysts, watch AST's Blacklift unveiling in New York tomorrow, for any sign of cost per launch or a firmer first-flight date. Then Friday brings Starship Flight 15 and the Vulcan Leo flight. And by year-end, watch whether AST's intermittent coverage actually arrives, and whether its fourth quarter carries the guide.

That's the briefing. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
