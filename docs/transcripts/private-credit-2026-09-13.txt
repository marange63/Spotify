Good morning, it's September 13th, and this is Cautious Optimism Briefings.

On Wednesday this briefing set itself a test. We said we could not tell which of two things the spread widening meant. Either banks were offering cheap enough refinancing to force direct lenders to compress spreads and fight for the business, or lenders were pricing in discipline on a book everyone knew was getting riskier. We held both open and named the next quarterly direct-lending pricing wrap as the tiebreaker. That wrap has now landed, and it came back against the idea that bank competition would force direct lenders to cut their spreads and fight for the business.

Sponsor-backed direct-lending spreads widened to 502 basis points in the three months to August 31st, up from 475 in the first quarter, per a secondary read of PitchBook LCD data. That is a three-month average rather than a single print, so hold it loosely. The gap versus comparable syndicated bank loans widened too, by 39 basis points, to 162.

The composition underneath that average moved harder than the average did. Deals priced in the 500-to-549 basis point range now make up 52 percent of sponsor-backed direct lending, against roughly a quarter of deals in the first quarter. So the whole distribution slid wider. This is not a few expensive outliers dragging a mean.

Put it plainly. Direct lending is charging more while winning less. We covered the borrower-side version of that on Wednesday, when Catalent refinanced out of a private term loan into a syndicated bank loan and said the swap would cut its annual interest expense by about a hundred million dollars. What is new today is the pricing data showing that pattern generalizing. Charging more while losing share is not what happens when lenders compete for volume. It is what happens when they reprice a shrinking, residual book they know is worse than the one that just walked out the door to the banks. One honest limit on that: new-issue spreads only describe deals that actually priced. They say nothing about the deals a direct lender never got shown.

So why are direct lenders not fighting harder to keep the business? Follow the money to the funding side. Apollo's non-traded BDC, Apollo Debt Solutions, took redemption requests in its first-quarter tender equal to 11.2 percent of net assets, more than double its 5 percent quarterly repurchase cap, and paid out roughly 45 percent of what investors asked for. Ares Strategic Income Fund took requests equal to 11.6 percent and fulfilled about 43 percent of them.

Blackstone's BCRED went the other way and honored every request in full, and still saw net assets fall 7.9 percent purely from the withdrawals. Those are first-quarter figures, per the trade press rather than the underlying tender filings. They set a baseline rather than break news, and the baseline is the point. A meaningful slice of the wealth-channel capital that funded the direct-lending boom wants out faster than these vehicles were built to let it. A lender managing a queue like that has less capacity, and less appetite, to underbid a bank.

Now the piece that genuinely advances the argument. Blue Owl suspended tender offers entirely at one of its funds, Capital Corporation Two, and wound the position down through outright asset sales instead. On Thursday we named the open question precisely: what happens when a manager has to sell loans rather than gate around a cap. We have now read the primary filing, and the answer is more constructive than the skeptics, this briefing included, expected. The sale was part of a 1.4 billion dollar multi-fund transaction, with Capital Corporation Two contributing 600 million, about a third of its total investment commitments. The buyers were four named North American public pension and insurance investors, not Blue Owl affiliates. And the price, 99.7 percent of par, was stated as fair value as of February 12th, rather than a discount off a stale mark.

Be clear about what that filing proves. A third of a non-traded BDC's book cleared to four outside pension and insurance buyers at 99.7 percent of par, at a price the seller itself called fair value. That is real secondary evidence, and it cuts against the reflexive case that non-traded BDC marks are fiction. The mark is dated, though, since the transaction was announced back in February. Two further caveats. We still cannot state the fund's total net assets in dollars, so we cannot say how representative that third was. And none of it relieves the funding constraint. Trade-press summaries of second-quarter tenders point to redemption demand rising further at Apollo and Ares rather than easing, and because we have not verified those against the filings we are not going to put numbers to them. One manager clearing assets near par, and the queue behind it getting longer, are not mutually exclusive. Both look true.

That takes us from whether the marks are honest to whether the credits underneath them are. Here the aggregate data, for the first time, agrees with what single-name blowups had only implied all week. Across the twenty largest business development companies, the median non-accrual ratio jumped to 2.8 percent of loans in the second quarter, from 2.0 percent in the first. That is the highest reading since 2017, per Morningstar's read of the second-quarter filings.

There is a more conservative construction of the same data at the ten largest BDCs, which counts every tranche owed by any borrower with even one impaired position. On that basis, exposure reaches 5.95 percent of debt at cost. Treat that as a deliberate worst-case perimeter, not a default rate, and do not quote it as one.

Underneath both numbers sits the mechanism. Payment-in-kind loans are now 12.8 percent of BDC loan assets, and roughly a fifth of loans carry PIK optionality. PIK income mechanically inflates reported yield and net asset value even as the cash actually arriving deteriorates, and that is the gap between the headline yield the retail wrapper sells and the cash-on-cash economics behind it. Cut it the other way honestly, though. PIK is often written in at origination for a fast-growing borrower rather than forced on a distressed one, and a 2.8 percent median non-accrual rate is still a low absolute number.

The direction is what matters: eight tenths of a point higher in a single quarter, to a post-2017 high. That is the credit-quality evidence justifying the spread widening we opened with. Lenders are not only capital-constrained by their redemption queues. They are also looking at a book that is genuinely getting worse and pricing it accordingly. The spread data alone cannot separate those two readings, and both are probably running at once.

One thread closes the loop. On Wednesday we told you to watch whether Loparex, the specialty-materials borrower, would file for Chapter 11 once its forbearance window lapsed. Moody's has now declared Loparex in default and said bankruptcy is the likely path, though no filing has happened yet. Blue Owl's publicly traded BDC had already marked its first-lien Loparex loan at 22 cents and its second lien at 5. So inside one manager's book: a portfolio clearing near par to independent buyers, and a single credit trading like a coin flip. That is not a contradiction. That is dispersion, and it is the honest read on where private credit stands right now.

So where does the cycle sit. Direct lending is losing its best borrowers to banks and its stickiest capital to redemptions at the same time, and rather than competing harder for what is left, it is charging more for it. The non-accrual and PIK data say that repricing is justified rather than paranoid. The unresolved risk is the loop connecting all of it. Rising non-accruals force NAV markdowns, markdowns lengthen the redemption queue, and a longer queue tightens the funding constraint that is already showing up in spreads. Watch third-quarter tender data across the evergreen complex, November's third-quarter non-accrual and payment-in-kind disclosures, and whether Loparex actually files. Those will tell us whether Blue Owl's near-par sale was the start of orderly price discovery, or the exception that proves the rule.

That's the briefing for September 13th. Talk to you tomorrow.
