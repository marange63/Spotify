Good morning. It's Thursday, August sixth. This is your robotics and physical AI briefing.

The profit test that embodied AI keeps failing just got its first clean pass, and it didn't come from a humanoid.

Symbotic reported third-quarter fiscal twenty-twenty-six results after the close yesterday, and this was the print we flagged on Monday as the real test: not whether adjusted EBITDA looks good, but whether a scaled warehouse-automation deployment can actually convert into audited profit. On Monday the honest counterweight was Symbotic's own prior quarter, where GAAP earnings came in at a penny a share against a consensus near eleven cents — a miss of more than ninety percent, with the audited bottom line essentially flat even as revenue and adjusted numbers climbed. This quarter closed that gap. Revenue came in at seven hundred twenty-one million dollars, up twenty-two percent year over year. Adjusted EBITDA more than doubled, to ninety-five million dollars from forty-five million a year earlier, at an adjusted gross margin of twenty-five percent. But the number that matters is net income: fifty-five million dollars, positive, against a twenty-one million dollar net loss in the same quarter last year. That's a genuine swing across the GAAP line, not a rounding improvement in a non-GAAP metric you have to squint at. Symbotic now has seventy-seven systems in deployment, up from roughly forty-six the year before, and it guided next quarter to seven hundred sixty to seven hundred eighty million dollars in revenue with EBITDA around one hundred to one hundred five million. It ended the quarter with one point seven billion dollars in cash and added Steve Pagliuca, the former Bain Capital co-chair, to its board — a signal the company expects to keep pressing on scale and capital-markets activity from here.

Here's the caveat, and it's an important one. Symbotic still does not disclose per-system revenue, a payback period, or a backlog figure current to this quarter. We checked the release directly. The most recent hard backlog number on record is twenty-two point seven billion dollars in remaining performance obligations, filed back in the first-quarter 10-Q, mostly undelivered systems under the Walmart and Exol contracts — and the filing expects only about fourteen percent of that to convert to revenue over the next sixty months. But that figure is now one to two quarters stale and shouldn't be read as today's pipeline. So the seventy-seven-system count tells you footprint is growing and the company is now profitable running it. It does not yet tell you the unit economics of any single system. Still, the so-what stands: of everything in physical AI right now, warehouse robotics is the vector actually clearing a profit, on real deployed hardware, not a roadmap. And that has a second-order pull. If structured warehouse automation is where physical-AI capex clears a return first, incremental industrial-automation capital rotates toward the deployed, profitable vector before it funds the humanoid roadmap — the proven line gets the next dollar ahead of the promised one.

Now turn to the humanoid side, where the same profit question is being asked and hasn't gotten a clean answer yet — but this week, for the first time, it got a public one. Unitree's Shanghai listing moved into its final stretch, with price inquiry underway and subscriptions opening within days, targeting four point two billion yuan, about six hundred twenty million dollars, at an implied valuation near forty-two billion yuan — cleared through a record one-hundred-four-day approval, the fastest in STAR Market history. What matters more than the raise is what the prospectus discloses: sixty percent gross margins, five hundred ninety-one million yuan of core net profit, and humanoids now making up fifty-two percent of total revenue on roughly one point seven billion yuan of sales, up from a hundred fifty-nine million yuan in twenty twenty-three. That is a company growing at triple-digit rates and turning a profit while doing it. It is also the sector's first public humanoid unit-economics read, full stop. Every other humanoid maker you hear about — Figure, Agility, the rest — carries a private mark with no market-clearing price behind it. Unitree now has one, and it's profitable.

Scale the two humanoid cohorts against each other and the gap is stark: Unitree shipped more than fifty-five hundred humanoids in twenty twenty-five, versus roughly one hundred fifty each for the leading American makers — something like thirty-five to one, and treat that as directional rather than precise, since Unitree's own shipment figure is reported anywhere from fifty-five hundred to over six thousand. And the US market just moved to formally exclude new Chinese hardware. Late in July the FCC added foreign-made humanoids and quadrupeds to its Covered List, citing surveillance and remote-commandeering risk — and, in secondary reporting the primary order text hasn't yet been pulled to confirm, a Bluetooth exploit dubbed UniPwn said to give root-level access to Unitree machines already in the field. Existing Unitree units, including at several US universities, are grandfathered and stay legal. New models aren't, which shuts a North American launch of fresh Unitree hardware that had been penciled in for August twelfth. So you get a bifurcated market: China fast-tracking its dominant, now-public, profitable humanoid maker onto its own exchange, while the US closes the door to anything new from that maker crossing the border. The so-what: Unitree's tape gives the market a real multiple to check every private humanoid valuation against, and it's arriving at exactly the moment US regulators are walling off the competitor that just proved profitability first.

Set against both of those, Tesla's Optimus is the clean counter-example — the widest gap in the sector between capital spent and hardware shipped. On the second-quarter call, Musk called Optimus potentially the biggest product Tesla's ever made, then in the same breath called it the hardest thing the company has ever tried to manufacture, because essentially everything on the robot is new. He described the early production ramp as flat and long, and said the pace is, in his words, literally impossible to predict, citing roughly ten thousand unique parts and no established humanoid supply chain to draw on. Production is only just beginning at Fremont, on the old Model S and X line torn down to make room, and the first units are earmarked for Tesla's own training-data collection, not customer delivery. Tesla's stated target for this year is fifty to a hundred thousand units, on a line it says is eventually designed for up to a million a year. Capital expenditure more than doubled in the quarter, driven by robotaxi, Optimus, and AI infrastructure. There is still no verified count of units actually produced. So you have capex scaling hard against a founder publicly conceding he can't forecast output, and a fifty-to-a-hundred-thousand target sitting on top of an impossible-to-predict ramp — the opposite of what Symbotic just delivered.

Pull the thread across all three and the pattern for this sector is now legible. The physical-AI capex story keeps getting funded on roadmaps and private marks, but the only names actually clearing a profit or a market price this week are the non-humanoid one and the newly public one — not the story everyone's been pricing off private valuations. Watch two things from here: how Unitree actually trades once its shares clear, since that sets the first real comparable for every humanoid valuation still marked privately, and whether any other humanoid maker ever posts a Symbotic-style swing to positive net income, rather than just a bigger adjusted number.
