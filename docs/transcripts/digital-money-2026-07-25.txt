Good morning, it's Saturday, July 25th, 2026, and this is your Digital Money briefing.

For the better part of a week, this show has been asking one question. Was the bounce in Bitcoin ETF flows a genuine turn, or just a pause before more selling? On Thursday, July 23rd, we got an answer, and it leans toward pause. US spot Bitcoin ETFs posted their first net outflow in seven trading days, roughly two hundred and twenty-five million dollars. That snapped a streak which had pulled in nearly a billion dollars of fresh capital over the prior seven sessions. BlackRock's IBIT accounted for almost all of the reversal on its own, shedding two hundred and two million dollars. Bitcoin itself has gone nowhere, rangebound between sixty-four thousand and sixty-six thousand eight hundred dollars, and it failed again to convincingly clear the sixty-six thousand dollar resistance level. That still leaves the token up thirteen percent from its July 1st low of fifty-seven thousand seven hundred and fifty dollars, so the month is not a washout. But the marginal buyer just stepped back.

What matters here is why it stepped back, because this was not a crypto-native shock. US crude rose almost two percent that day, to eighty-eight dollars a barrel, after a tanker strike near Saudi Arabia and fresh US threats against Iran. Brent cleared a hundred and one dollars. On the same day, the Magnificent Seven tech names fell four point eight percent, erasing about seven hundred and ninety-seven billion dollars in market value on doubts about the scale of AI capital spending. Crypto liquidations over that stretch ran to two hundred and eighty-two million dollars, and they were skewed roughly two to one toward longs. That is a market of leveraged bulls getting caught by a macro shock. It is not a market reacting to bad crypto news.

The so-what matters most for anyone still pricing Bitcoin as a monetary hedge. When the asset moves in lockstep with an oil spike and an AI-capex selloff, it is trading as a levered proxy for the risk tape. It is not behaving as an inflation hedge or a diversifier. If that coupling holds, it undercuts the exact thesis allocators use to justify a strategic allocation in the first place.

Two caveats before anyone extrapolates. First, even with Thursday's outflow, the week still closed with about two hundred and seventy-four million dollars of net inflows into the Bitcoin ETF complex. This is one reversal day inside a net-positive week, not confirmed distribution. Second, spot Ethereum ETFs moved the other way on the very same day, taking in twenty-six million dollars, led by Fidelity's fund. That reads more like marginal institutional dollars rotating from Bitcoin exposure into Ethereum exposure than a uniform risk-off signal.

One supply-side footnote from the same week. Poolin Technology, once the largest Bitcoin mining pool in the world, filed for Chapter 11 with liabilities somewhere between a hundred million and five hundred million dollars. One bankruptcy is not a trend. But if hashprice stays compressed, miner balance-sheet stress is the mechanism that turns into forced selling, and that is a supply overhang worth tracking.

So if ETF flows are the fickle read on institutional conviction, where is the patient money going? Into infrastructure, not spot exposure. Citadel Securities, one of the most sophisticated market makers in traditional finance, has put four hundred million dollars into Crypto.com, valuing the exchange at twenty billion dollars. That is the exchange's first institutional funding round in the decade since it was founded. Crypto.com says the capital accelerates its expansion into tokenized securities, derivatives and other asset classes, and in the chief executive's words, it is a bet that crypto, quote, increasingly becomes the rails for finance.

This is the second such stake in under a year. Citadel Securities also participated in Kraken's round last November, reportedly at a similar valuation, though that comparison rests on secondary reporting I have not independently verified. Take the pattern rather than the precise math as the signal. A top-tier market maker putting real equity into two retail exchanges is a bet that exchange and market-making economics, not spot Bitcoin exposure, is the durable profit pool in this industry. It also sharpens the competitive setup against Coinbase, which reports earnings on July 30th. And it imports a familiar wrinkle from equities market structure. Market-maker ownership of a trading venue can tighten spreads and deepen liquidity. It also raises the conflict-of-interest questions that payment-for-order-flow critics have been making for years.

Now, why does that patient capital stay cautious about deploying further? Because the rulebook it needs still is not written. The CLARITY Act is the market-structure bill that would finally draw the jurisdictional line between the SEC and the CFTC for spot crypto trading and custody. It is now racing the August recess clock, which lands around August 10th. A fresh reconciled Republican draft, combining Senate Banking and Agriculture Committee work, emerged on July 22nd. It still needs sixty votes to clear the Senate, and Democratic senators Alsobrooks and Gallego are conditioning their support on stronger ethics guardrails. The committee only advanced the bill in May with two Democrats behind it, so the coalition math remains thin. There is no scheduled floor vote. This is a deadline race, not a calendar event.

Layered onto that fight is a newer one. Banks versus stablecoins. A coalition of banking trade groups led by the Bank Policy Institute issued a statement on that same July 22nd draft. They objected directly to what they called, quote, interest-like payments for holding stablecoins, warning that such payments will siphon away the bank deposits that fund small business, mortgage and farm lending. In reporting on the same draft, those trade groups said it still puts at risk the local lending that drives economic activity in the United States.

Let me be precise about what is actually contested, because it is narrower than the headlines suggest. This is not a fight over whether the bill bans stablecoin yield outright. It is reported to retain some form of that prohibition. The dispute is over a carve-out that would permit rewards tied to account activity rather than to idle balances, and the banks argue that carve-out is wide enough to function as yield in practice. I cannot verify that reading against the bill's actual text, so treat it as the banks' contested claim rather than a settled fact. The so-what is that this is the opening skirmish of the deposit-competition battle the GENIUS Act framework will force once it takes effect. It is also exactly why the largest US banks are separately building their own tokenized-deposit rails through The Clearing House instead of ceding that ground.

Let's close on the stablecoin scoreboard, where the two leading issuers are diverging in an underappreciated way. On raw circulating supply, Tether's USDT still dominates at roughly a hundred and eighty-four billion dollars, against about seventy-three billion for Circle's USDC. But on transaction volume, the dollars actually moving, USDC has pulled well ahead, taking about seventy percent of adjusted stablecoin volume in the first half of this year against roughly twenty-five percent for USDT. June alone set a record at one point seven nine trillion dollars of adjusted volume, up sixty-three percent from May. Note that adjusted volume is a methodology choice, and this is June data, not a read from this week. The point stands anyway. Stock and flow are telling different stories, and reserve income, the actual profit pool for an issuer, tracks stock. So do not mistake USDC's volume lead for an economic one, at least not yet.

Put it together. The money that looks decisive, the ETF flows, proved fickle inside a single week. The money that looks quiet, exchange equity and stablecoin plumbing, is the more durable signal. Watch two things from here. Whether Thursday's outflow extends into next week, and whether CLARITY reaches the Senate floor before the recess.
