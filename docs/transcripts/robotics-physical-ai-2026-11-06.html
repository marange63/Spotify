Cautious Optimism Briefings

Robotics & Physical AI

Transcript · November 6, 2026

Good morning. It's Friday, November 6th, and this is your briefing on robotics and physical AI. This week, model capability wasn't what decided who scales in embodied intelligence. Regulatory permission and public disclosure were.

Start with the lead. China has blocked SoftBank's $5.4 billion purchase of ABB's robotics division. The deal covers the whole unit. That business booked about $2.3 billion of revenue in 2024, roughly 7 percent of ABB's group total, and it employs about 7,000 people. The European Commission cleared the deal without conditions back in March. Beijing's approval was the one still outstanding. The grounds for the decision were not disclosed, and no Chinese ruling has been published.

Even which Chinese body made the call is contested. The Financial Times, as relayed by Investing.com, names the National Development and Reform Commission, China's top economic planning agency. The Robot Report instead points to the office that runs China's foreign-investment security review. The two accounts don't reconcile. But notice what they share. Neither body is a competition authority. A planner points toward industrial policy, shielding domestic arm makers. A security review points toward a control concern about a foreign buyer taking over a business with deep Chinese operations. A third reading is a bargaining chip inside the broader US-China trade arrangement. The reporting can't separate the three. What it does suggest is that this was not a conventional antitrust call. That's my inference, but it's the one that matters.

ABB's own figures show where the leverage comes from. About 90 percent of the machines ABB builds for Chinese customers are made locally in China, employing thousands of people there. ABB also describes China as a major portion of its revenue, though it gives no percentage. That footprint is what gives Beijing a seat at the table. The veto follows where an incumbent manufactures, not where it's headquartered or who's buying it.

And the deal isn't dead yet. ABB says it and SoftBank are, quote, "in close dialogue and are working constructively on the transaction." So treat this as a live negotiation, not a kill. Neither company has disclosed a termination date or a break fee. The uncertainty is itself the story. A deal Brussels waved through unconditionally now waits on a process nobody outside Beijing can see.

Now price what was blocked. At $5.4 billion against about $2.3 billion of 2024 revenue, SoftBank was paying roughly 2.3 times sales. That's my arithmetic, not a reported multiple. It's a modest multiple for one of the largest Western arm-and-cobot incumbents. Set it against Agility Robotics, the humanoid maker that just listed at a $2.5 billion pre-money valuation. An incumbent with billions in revenue was priced at barely twice a humanoid company whose recognized revenue, as we covered Wednesday, is still tiny. So the market is paying up for the humanoid option, and pricing conventional automation like the mature industrial business it is.

For SoftBank, the block hits Masayoshi Son's push to build an integrated AI-to-robotics business. SoftBank shares fell about 5 percent in Tokyo on the news. But that same session carried weak Arm guidance and a broader AI-stock selloff after the midterms. So don't pin the drop on ABB alone.

The wider consequence is deal risk. Any Western or Japanese buyer of an automation asset with Chinese operations now has to price a Chinese veto point. Unconditional clearance in Brussels didn't remove it. That doesn't make every such deal blockable. It does raise the bar on all of them.

This escalates the arc this desk has tracked all week: China moving up the stack. On October 30th, Hyundai was localizing Atlas suppliers to cut its reliance on China. On November 3rd, Meta's Max prototype turned out to be built on a Fourier platform. Yesterday, it was the China-rooted hands inside Nvidia's reference humanoid. Today the leverage moved from supply to ownership.

But the wall is selective. The new detail on Meta's Max is that it reportedly runs on Nvidia's Jetson Thor chips. That's an American platform, a Chinese body, and American silicon. Meta hasn't confirmed the project, so this rests on reporting by The Information. The so-what is that components stay interleaved, even as Beijing raises a wall at the ownership layer.

Now, while Beijing was gatekeeping ownership of a Western incumbent, America's first listed humanoid pure-play started trading. Agility Robotics, maker of the Digit humanoid, closed its merger with Churchill Capital Corp the Eleventh on Wednesday. It then began trading on Nasdaq as AGLT, which Bloomberg called a first for the US. As we covered Wednesday, redemptions cut the cash. Gross proceeds came in around $465 million, against more than $620 million originally projected.

The fresher material is what Agility brought to market. It cites more than $300 million in multi-year orders for Digit version five. And its Salem, Oregon plant is designed to produce up to 10,000 units a year. Hold those two numbers apart, because neither means what the headline implies.

Take the orders first. Multi-year orders are backlog, not revenue. The disclosure gives no conversion schedule, no cancellation terms, and no unit count behind the dollars. Set against a revenue line that is still tiny, the order book is a claim about the future, not a measure of the present.

Then the capacity. Ten thousand units a year is design capacity. It's what Salem was engineered to make at full utilization, not what it ships today. Foxconn led the PIPE, which keeps a contract manufacturer close to the build. As for the debut itself, we don't yet have a reliable read on the trade. Even when we do, a thin float makes first-week price action noise, not validation.

The real test is Agility's first 10-Q. That filing converts both the order book and the capacity number into checkable figures. It also makes AGLT a continuously priced benchmark. Figure, 1X, Apptronik, and the brain-layer labs we covered Tuesday will be measured against it on their next raises. Until that filing lands, Agility's unit economics are assertion, not disclosure.

So line the two stories up. Beijing just showed it can veto who owns a Western robotics incumbent. Meanwhile, America's first public humanoid company is anchored by a Taiwanese contract manufacturer, and it now has to prove its numbers in public, quarter by quarter. Permission and disclosure moved this story this week. The model and data layer was quiet, with no fresh primary update from Nvidia's GR00T team, Physical Intelligence, or Skild AI.

One more lever, briefly. China's one-year suspension of its expanded rare-earth export controls ends November 10th, four days from today. The April 2025 licensing rules on the terbium- and dysprosium-bearing magnets in robot actuators stay in force regardless. The outcome on the 10th is unknown, and I won't forecast it. But notice the sequence. Beijing pulled the merger-review lever this week, and the export-licensing lever comes up in four days. Whether the two are coordinated is my inference, not reporting. Still, it's a coincidence worth watching rather than dismissing.

Pulling the threads together. What's genuinely inflecting is that conventional industrial robotics is now a priced, contested strategic asset. And a public market for humanoid equity now exists. What's still unproven is whether Agility's order book turns into revenue, and whether the ABB deal survives Beijing at all. The biggest open question is whether a Chinese veto becomes standard for any automation deal with Chinese operations. Watch three catalysts: the rare-earth decision on November 10th, whatever SoftBank and ABB do next, and Agility's first 10-Q as the first hard test of its numbers.

That's the briefing. Have a good Friday.
