Private Equity & the Deal Economy

Transcript · September 20, 2026

Welcome to Cautious Optimism Briefings. This is the private equity briefing for September 20th, 2026.

The argument on this show all week has been that nothing is coming back to limited partners. Today I want to revise that. The better read is that private equity has an inventory problem, and that the inventory is very unevenly distributed.

Start with the stock. Bain's midyear report, per its press release, says the industry is coming off a four-year stretch of record-low distributions as a percentage of net asset value. Bain puts the inventory at around 33,000 unsold portfolio companies. And it says the implied capital cycle and holding periods for private equity assets now run to approximately seven years, well beyond historical norms.

Yesterday we covered PitchBook's second-quarter exit numbers. That was the flow version of this problem. Bain is describing the stock. A stock of that size does not clear in one good quarter, or in two.

Now, Bain's own explanation deserves some skepticism. It blames a triple shock: AI disruption of software, redemption stress in private credit, and an oil spike tied to the Iran conflict. That is Bain's narrative. I would treat it as a hypothesis, not a finding.

There is a subtler point about the headline metric. Distributions as a share of NAV has the manager's own mark in the denominator. So the ratio can fall because cash is not coming back. It can also fall because marks have held up. Blackstone said its corporate private equity funds appreciated 3.7 percent in the second quarter and 14 percent over twelve months, according to a summary of the call. Marks that hold while cash stalls push the ratio lower, without any single number telling you which side is wrong.

The reverse also works. If marks are cut, the ratio improves mechanically, with no extra cash paid out. That matters because of software. According to InvestmentNews's summary of the Bain report, software valuations in private equity portfolios fell roughly 8 percent in the first quarter. Software is where much of the aged buyout inventory sits. So the marks most likely to move are attached to the assets that have been held longest.

The takeaway is that the logjam is a matter of inventory and age, and neither heals quickly.

If that is the stock, allocators are starting to act on it. Buyouts Insider reported this week that Hawaii's employees' retirement system, a 26 billion dollar fund, is reassessing a private-markets tilt of roughly 50 percent. The reported view of its consultant, Meketa, is that the exposure cost the system returns during the prolonged bull market. That article is paywalled, so I would attribute the sentiment loosely. Still, the direction is clear.

Earlier this week we flagged a large pension reporting a headline private equity return with no distribution figure beside it. That looked like complacency about cash. Hawaii looks like the first crack in it. Buyouts also points to other public plans pivoting toward smaller managers.

Think about what that does to fundraising. An allocator that cuts its tilt shrinks the re-up base first for managers who cannot show distributions. So the distribution gap becomes a fundraising gap. And concentration tightens toward the firms that can point to cash.

Now, the counter-evidence, and it cuts against our own running claim. The largest managers are returning cash. Carlyle reported that it distributed nearly 7 billion dollars in the second quarter and 37 billion over the past year. It also posted its highest distributable earnings in nearly four years. KKR called the second quarter the largest monetization quarter in its history, with realized performance income of 848 million dollars.

Those are July and August prints. They are the last hard realization data before the third-quarter numbers arrive in late October.

Now the discipline. Carlyle's 37 billion dollars is firm-wide across every strategy. It is not buyout distributions to paid-in capital. A distribution figure without the capital-called denominator is not a return. KKR's monetization record was set while its remaining unrealized gains kept growing, to roughly 18 billion dollars. The backlog got bigger even as the firm sold more.

Blackstone shows the same tension. Its net accrued performance revenue reached 7.5 billion dollars, the highest in four years. That is carry that has been earned on paper but not paid. In the same quarter, management acknowledged that geopolitical volatility has pushed out exit pipelines. Those lines come from a summary of the call, not the transcript. Even so, a four-year high in accruals next to pushed-out pipelines measures the backlog. It says little about manager health.

KKR's sale of USI Insurance Services to Aon, which this show covered on Friday, is the shape of a good exit. The new detail is the return. KKR puts it at about six times on its 2017 investment, and 3.4 times on capital across the full life of the position. That is the top of the league table. KKR's own management said, on that call, that it does not recall a period where external perception was so disconnected from operating fundamentals.

I think that is half right. The fundamentals at the top are strong. The perception problem is that the top is not the industry.

So the honest statement is dispersion, not a freeze. For an allocator, the question becomes which manager they are with, and whether that manager can get through the inventory sitting behind the winners.

That leads to the harder problem. Those without a clean exit are engineering liquidity, and the main tool is the continuation vehicle. So the question is who sets the price when the general partner sits on both sides of the trade.

Jefferies has GP-led volume at 62 billion dollars in the first half, against 56 billion dollars LP-led, out of a record 118 billion total. On Jefferies' count, the sponsor-initiated side is now the majority of the secondaries market. Hold that crossover lightly. Lazard's own first-half tally, which this show ran on Friday, had GP-led and LP-led nearly even. Whether sponsors have taken the majority depends on whose book you read. What is not in dispute is that continuation vehicles are a primary exit channel now, not an exception.

That is the context for ILPA's draft guidance on continuation vehicles. The draft would have sponsors run a targeted competitive bidding process supported by independent third-party price validation. It would require them to demonstrate no overall increase in management fees or carried interest for rolling LPs. And it says to avoid conditioning roll elections on minimum commitment sizes or stapled financing.

Each of those provisions is aimed at the same weakness. The GP arranges the bid for its own asset, sets the terms, and offers the rolling investor a choice that is hard to refuse. This is the first institutional constraint on a pattern we have tracked all week.

Two cautions. First, guidance is not a rule. ILPA has no enforcement power, the draft is not final, and final guidance is expected later this year at best. The strongest franchises are the ones least likely to need it. Second, the advisers who publish these half-year volume totals earn fees on the volume they count, and none of them publishes a methodology. So treat the volume as directional.

There is a second-order effect. If ILPA's process requirements are adopted, the channel now carrying something close to half of all secondaries volume gets slower and more expensive to run. Either clearing discounts widen, or GP-led deal count falls. Either outcome puts the inventory back where it started.

Now one live example of a partial exit. Blackstone agreed this month to buy Flow Control Holdings, a maker of data-center liquid-cooling components, from Audax Private Equity. Audax retains a minority equity stake. Terms were not disclosed, so there is no multiple and no way to compute what Audax made. Bloomberg reports that UBS is arranging a first-lien loan for the deal. The size is not public.

A price nobody can see, and a seller who did not fully leave. That is a mark and partial cash, not a full realization. It looks closer in kind to a continuation vehicle than to a sale.

So where does the cycle stand? The exit window is open for a narrow set of managers and assets, and shut for the inventory behind them. The unresolved risk is the stock, roughly 33,000 unsold companies on a seven-year clock, not any one quarter.

Three catalysts would confirm or refute that read. The third-quarter manager prints in late October will show whether Carlyle's and KKR's realization pace was a thaw or a one-off. ILPA's final continuation-vehicle guidance will show whether anyone constrains the GP as price-setter. And the last is whether another limited partner follows Hawaii in cutting its private-markets tilt rather than pacing down.

That is the briefing for September 20th. Thanks for listening, and see you tomorrow.
