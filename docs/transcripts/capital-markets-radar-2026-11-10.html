Capital Markets Cross-Asset Radar

Transcript · November 10, 2026

Good morning, it's Tuesday, November 10th, and this is your Capital Markets Radar.

Here's the thesis up front. Monday was the S&P 500's best day since April, and it was a relief rally the AI complex did not lead. It ran on oil and on positioning. The more durable signal sits underneath it, in breadth and in what AI issuers now pay for capital.

Start with the contrast. The S&P 500 jumped two point three five percent on Monday, to close at 7,549.94. That's its best single session since April. And yet Nvidia, the stock that has anchored this entire AI cycle, fell about four percent the same day. A benchmark posting its best day in seven months while its most important constituent drops is unusual. It's a tell about what actually drove the move.

What drove it was oil, not AI. Brent crude fell about five percent on Monday, to 93.44 dollars a barrel, on reports of progress toward reopening the Strait of Hormuz. Those reports are market commentary, not an official diplomatic statement. The dollar index slipped about six tenths of a percent, to near 101.67. That gives back the strength the dollar showed on Friday's jobs report.

The oil war premium has now swung three times in about a week. It drained after the midterm vote. It refilled late last week on tanker reports out of Hormuz. And it drained again Monday, putting Brent back near its post-midterm low. A premium that reverses on headlines twice in a week isn't setting a floor. So what: oil did much of the lifting in Monday's rally, and it's also the least durable driver.

There's a second-order point in the dollar. A softer dollar eases global dollar funding and emerging-market carry, even as the U.S. front end prices hike risk. That decoupling is unusual, and it's worth watching if it persists.

Now the Fed angle, where the equity tape may be over-reading the oil move. Start with what the front end is pricing. The two-year Treasury yield sat at 4.51 percent after Friday's jobs report. That's about half a point above the top of the Fed's 3.75 to 4 percent target range. A two-year sitting that far above the policy rate is a curve leaning toward tightening, not cuts. In the words of 24/7 Wall St, traders are "paying up for December hike risk rather than cuts." Cheaper oil works on headline inflation first and on core later. But the December hike case rests on labor and on core prices, not on energy. And Monday's oil drop can't touch this morning's October print at all, because October's prices are already in the books. So oil relief may move December pricing less than Monday's rally implies. That's our read going into the print.

Next, what rallied inside the tape tells you what kind of rally it was. AMD rose thirteen point three percent on Monday, fully recovering the eleven percent it lost the prior week. That supports the case that positioning, not a reassessment of AI demand, set the size of last week's chip selloff. It also means the feared mechanical next leg, with systematic CTA strategies flipping to short signals, did not show up Monday. One session doesn't make a bottom. But so far, this looks like a crowded book being cleared, not a thesis being repriced.

Nvidia is the exception to that bounce, and it's worth sitting with why. Its board has opened an independent investigation into CEO Jensen Huang's personal relationship with a woman reported to be an alleged Chinese spy. The board says it has, quote, "no reason to believe" that national security or company secrets were compromised. It says Huang fully supports the review and has committed to cooperating. No findings have been published, and the intelligence link is reported, not confirmed.

Monday's drop completes an arc we've tracked across three shows. In last Thursday's rout, Nvidia fell only about half as much as its chip peers. By Sunday, its credit default swaps were widening at the top of the AI stack. And on Monday it lagged a broad rally. Nvidia has moved from the complex's anchor to its idiosyncratic risk. Still, don't read a four percent drop on a governance headline as a fundamental verdict. It may simply be de-risking ahead of Nvidia's November 17th earnings. Or the market may be under-reading a China and Washington risk. Both readings are live. So what: Nvidia goes into its print carrying name-specific risk, while the rest of the chip complex just traded as a positioning story.

Zoom out, and the structural story is breadth, not any single name. Goldman Sachs runs an index that strips AI-linked stocks out of the S&P 500. That ex-AI gauge has now caught up with the S&P 500's performance this year, after trailing it for most of 2026. At the index level, the AI leadership premium that defined this year has essentially evaporated. That has two readings. It could be healthy broadening, with more stocks finally participating. Or it could be profit-taking rotation out of the one trade that worked. Either way, index concentration risk is lower, but so is the engine of this year's returns. Leadership now has to come from somewhere other than the AI complex.

The same question, who keeps funding the AI trade, shows up in what hyperscalers pay to borrow. Reporting that cites Bank of America's credit strategists puts hyperscaler credit spreads about fifty basis points wider year to date. One caution. Without a clean comparison to non-AI issuers, we can't say how much of that is an AI-specific premium rather than broader credit widening. Volume is the other half. By a tally attributed to Goldman Sachs, hyperscalers have issued 296 billion dollars of bonds so far in 2026. That's about eighteen percent above Goldman's own full-year forecast. So what: the AI buildout is borrowing more than even Goldman expected, and paying more to do it.

That's the backdrop for Meta's funding choice. Two weeks ago, Meta announced a plan to raise up to 95 billion dollars in equity. It comes in three pieces. The first is about 12.5 billion dollars of mandatory convertible preferred. According to an SEC filing, that piece closed on October 29th, so for that slice this is an executed transaction, not an intention. The second piece is Meta's ordinary Class A common stock, sold through a forward. Meta says it won't initially receive any proceeds from that sale. The cash arrives only when the forward settles. The third piece, the bulk of the plan, is a separate program that waits on a brand-new class of non-voting shares. Those aren't expected to begin trading until early 2027.

The motive is visible in Meta's own numbers. Third-quarter free cash flow was negative 2.12 billion dollars, against a capex guide of 135 to 145 billion for 2026. Equity is becoming the release valve as debt gets dearer. That trade-off cuts both ways. Shareholders absorb the dilution, but systemic credit risk falls. You can read the choice as funding discipline as easily as strain. So what: Meta is testing whether AI capex can migrate from the bond market to the equity market.

The sharpest second-order point is who wants to buy that equity. Nvidia, AMD and Broadcom have indicated non-binding interest in buying up to 5 billion dollars of the Class A common stock in the current offering. That's the forward-sale piece, not the future non-voting shares. It's vendor-recycled demand: chip suppliers putting cash back into a customer's equity, not just taking its orders. If they follow through, a fall in Meta's valuation would travel back into its suppliers' balance sheets, not just their backlogs. And note one name on that buyer list. Nvidia sits on it in the same week its own CEO came under a board probe.

One counter-signal before the close. Bitcoin didn't get the relief bid. It slipped to about 74,200 dollars on Monday, roughly eight percent below its recent high near 81,000. The flows are the cleaner signal. On the best equity day since April, U.S. spot bitcoin ETFs still lost 201 million dollars, per SoSoValue data. BlackRock's IBIT alone lost more than that total, so at least one other fund took in money. Ethereum ETFs lost another 184.6 million the same day. In last week's rout, bitcoin traded with the same risk budget as semiconductors. Monday broke that pattern. Chips ripped, and the ETF money kept leaving. Our read is that ETF holders are trading bitcoin off the hike repricing, not off the oil relief that lifted equities. So what: ETF demand for bitcoin is behaving differently from the rest of the risk complex right now.

Three things to watch. First, October CPI lands at 8:30 Eastern this morning, and it had not been released as of this taping. Watch core, not headline, because that's where the hike case lives. Second, Nvidia reports on November 17th with its board investigation still open. Third, watch whether the rest of Meta's offering, and the next hyperscaler bond deal behind it, clear without further spread widening. That's where the AI capex story gets tested, not in the stock price.
