Start with the deadline we've been counting down since Tuesday. China's suspension of its broader rare-earth export controls lapses under Chinese law on November 10th. Two days out, there's genuine news, but it comes from the wrong capital. Beijing still has not published a formal notice extending that suspension. What has moved is on the American side. The Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security is again extending its suspension of what's called the Affiliates Rule, this time out to January 10th, 2027. Commerce released that rule on Thursday, and it's due in the Federal Register tomorrow, Monday the 9th.

Now an update to our own read. On Tuesday we told you September's truce extension never named rare earths. The text still says that. What's new is that Treasury Secretary Bessent is reported, in Motley Fool coverage, as saying the extension covers China's broader rare-earth controls. No direct quotation of his words has surfaced, only that paraphrase. And Bessent is a negotiating party with every interest in projecting stability. So treat this as Washington's characterization, not a Chinese confirmation.

Here's the actual shape of the asymmetry. Washington has now written its own concession down for a second time. Beijing's September statement, on one close textual analysis, named no rare-earth control and issued no replacement notice. As of last night, Beijing had published nothing amending its rare-earth suspension. So the narrow legal pause is still set to expire Tuesday, while the American pause runs into January. Even Washington's paper is conditional. Per trade-compliance reporting, the Affiliates Rule could go into effect in January if the two sides can't reach a permanent agreement. Still, this is not a reciprocal, same-day expiry story. One government has a document. The other has a word.

Don't read a lapse as an automatic snap-back, though. Beijing can let the legal suspension end without reinstating the broadest controls overnight. It can simply run licensing on a discretionary, case-by-case basis. That preserves its leverage without producing an escalation headline. That's an inference on our part, not a fact in the record. But it's a more likely middle path than a clean reversal, and it's why the outcome isn't binary.

The market is already positioning around that uncertainty. In one session this week, shares of MP Materials rose nearly five percent. USA Rare Earth rose almost eleven percent, roughly double MP's move. Read that as optionality being bought on non-Chinese supply, not as a forecast that controls are snapping back. A one-session move in small-cap names that trade on headlines is speculative positioning, not a repriced fundamental.

One more date sits in the same window, on its own clock. China's separate suspension covering gallium, germanium, antimony and superhard materials runs until November 27th. Nothing in the reporting ties that instrument to the January truce extension, so don't assume it moves with the rare-earth story. Now the conditional second-order effects. If Beijing lets the rare-earth suspension lapse and runs discretionary licensing, the exposure sits with magnet-dependent buyers. If Bessent's reading holds instead, the urgency premium in MP and USA Rare Earth fades. Neither branch is resolved, and the chokepoint is being held open by a word rather than a document.

If minerals are the chokepoint Beijing controls, power is the one America has been told it lacks. And that shortage just got re-measured too. This week's major story is out of PJM, the mid-Atlantic grid operator. FERC found that the cost allocation, transmission-owner exit rules and collateral provisions in PJM's original data-center backstop plan "may have been unjust and unreasonable." PJM filed a revised version on October 29th. The process now starts on March 1st, about seven months later than planned.

Here's the number that matters more. PPL Electric, FirstEnergy and AES Ohio have cut their near-term large-load forecasts by a combined six and a half gigawatts. Jefferies analysts now estimate the procurement could be as little as roughly six hundred thirty megawatts. The original basis was a six-point-eight gigawatt shortfall in PJM's 2028-29 capacity auction. That's roughly a tenfold reduction. It echoes our read on Texas last Wednesday, when we argued much of ERCOT's enormous interconnection queue was speculative requests holding a place in line. PJM's own utilities appear to have just run the same scrub on their own numbers, in a second grid.

Don't swing too far the other way, though. Six hundred thirty megawatts is a Jefferies estimate, not a PJM-published figure. And FERC hasn't yet ruled on the revised filing. Inside PJM's own planning process, a Charles River Associates analysis presented to its Load Analysis Subcommittee still projects a shortfall of roughly eleven and a half gigawatts by 2030. Those two numbers aren't contradictory. They measure different horizons. The near-term gap looks overstated, and the 2030 gap is still a live argument.

Why did the forecasts fall? There are at least three explanations. One is genuine cancellations. Another is load migrating to grids that interconnect faster. The third is utilities being conservative ahead of a cost-allocation fight that FERC has already flagged as potentially unjust. Nothing in the record lets us pick one. Two consequences follow, both inference. First, a near-term shortfall measured in hundreds of megawatts, not gigawatts, weakens the scarcity-rent case for merchant generators and independent power producers in PJM specifically. That's a demand-side hit on top of the political risk we covered on Friday. Second, a smaller, later backstop pushes cost and risk back toward load-serving entities and state rate cases. That's exactly the axis FERC's own language pointed to. So the near-term shortage was partly paper, and the 2030 one is still an argument.

Demand can be scrubbed down on paper. Permission is moving the other way. In Europe, public opposition has already hit roughly forty-two billion dollars of data-center investment through delays and cancellations, according to STL Partners research cited by CNBC. The comparable US figure is roughly seventy-seven billion. More than seventy European projects were rejected or restricted between January and April alone, per the European Data Center Monitor. STL's methodology isn't disclosed, so treat the dollar comparison as directional. But it shows the consent constraint we've tracked all week isn't an American peculiarity. Our inference is that capital routes toward jurisdictions where power and permission are both already in hand. That's a regional repricing of AI power, not a verdict on AI demand. The harder limit on AI infrastructure increasingly isn't megawatts of demand. It's permission to build.
