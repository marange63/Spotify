Good morning, it's Thursday, October 8th, and this is your digital money briefing. The short version: Bitcoin failed the test we set yesterday, and while the price layer de-risked, the plumbing layer kept getting built underneath it.

Start with that test. Yesterday we said a daily close above $86,000 would show whether the long-term-holder and ETF breakeven band was acting as a floor or a ceiling. Bitcoin answered the other way. Shortly after midnight UTC on October 7th, it dropped below $84,000. Glassnode had put the ETF complex's breakeven at the top of that band. So the cohort that was nearly back to flat is underwater again. That matters for what comes next. Holders who were close to getting whole are natural sellers on the next approach. So the band now looks like overhead supply rather than support, and that raises the bar for the next leg higher.

Now the cause, because the reported story is too tidy. Cryptonomist, which tracked the move, ties the drop to Iranian tanker attacks in the Strait of Hormuz that pushed Brent crude above $101 a barrel. Treat that as the reported trigger, not a proven cause. Our Strategic Power desk had Brent trading higher than that on Monday morning, per Fortune. Bitcoin was rallying toward $86,000 that same day. Higher oil coincided with a rally, so oil makes a weak sole explanation. Rates are the obvious alternative, but they don't fit cleanly either. Our Capital Markets Radar desk reported the ten-year touching its highest level since 2002 intraday on Monday. Then it closed lower on Tuesday, hours before Bitcoin broke. We tried to pin down settlement-level oil and yield data for this window, and couldn't get figures we can verify. So hold both macro stories as unproven. Our read is that a third candidate sits in the chart itself: holders near breakeven taking the exit. The so-what is narrower than the headlines. Bitcoin sold off alongside a geopolitical shock rather than hedging it. But this desk can't yet say which lever pulled it.

The positioning data tells you what kind of selloff this was. Liquidations across crypto jumped 235 percent day over day, to $547 million. Yet open interest slipped 1 percent, even as futures volume rose 16 percent. Funding rates on Bitcoin and ether dipped slightly below zero, so shorts were paying longs. Put together, that's positions being closed, forced and voluntary, not fresh bearish bets being added.

In coin terms, Bitcoin futures open interest sits at 660,000 BTC. That's up from an 11-month low of 626,000 on September 30th, but well short of this year's record of 800,000. Leverage is rebuilding slowly, if at all. That also grades our own calls. On October 2nd we warned of a crowded long book at risk of a forced unwind. On Tuesday the 6th we downgraded that to deleveraging into strength. A liquidation day did arrive, but it was contained, not a cascade. Here's the bull case, stated plainly. The leverage was flushed rather than added, which leaves a cleaner base if macro calms. The bear case is the overhead supply we just described. Both can be true for a while.

On ETF flows, be careful with what's circulating. Trackers suggest spot Bitcoin ETFs saw net outflows on October 7th. But the estimates disagree with each other, and none comes with a source we can verify. So we're not airing a number, and we're not calling it a confirmed outflow day. What we can say is that the pattern would be familiar. On September 30th, a nine-day, $3.1 billion inflow streak ended on a single $148.7 million outflow day. And on Tuesday we showed that this year's ETF demand is marginal. If October 7th confirms as an outflow, the read is that the ETF bid follows price rather than anchoring it. So don't treat ETF demand as a standing floor under this market.

The price layer de-risked. The plumbing layer kept getting built, and that's clearest at Coinbase. On September 28th, the CFTC registered Coinbase Clearing LLC as a derivatives clearing organization. That gives Coinbase its own regulated clearinghouse, which The Block describes as completing its derivatives stack. In Coinbase's own words, quote, "For the first time, we can create and settle fully collateralized contracts directly." Coinbase calls the clearinghouse USDC-native. That's the second-order point for Circle. USDC becomes derivatives collateral, not just a trading pair. That's a source of float that doesn't depend on trading volume, and it feeds the float-times-yield framing we've applied to Circle since October 2nd.

But keep the scope honest. The clearinghouse can clear only fully collateralized futures, options on futures, and swaps. Coinbase says it will keep using existing partners for its margined derivatives business. The same goes for its upcoming single-stock perpetuals, which are still a filing, not a live product. So the leveraged business, the kind that liquidated across the market on October 7th, sits outside the new clearinghouse. Fully collateralized clearing carries little counterparty risk, which is partly why it was approvable. The worry about exchange, broker and clearer under one roof only becomes real if leveraged clearing follows. For now, Coinbase has built its regulated perimeter around its lowest-risk activity.

That build-out is filling a gap Congress left open. After the CLARITY Act failed cloture 49 to 50 on September 15th, the SEC and CFTC began writing as much of a crypto rulebook as existing authority allows. On October 5th, CFTC Chairman Michael Selig gave that effort names. Regulation CTX covers crypto asset transactions, and Regulation CAM covers crypto asset markets. On Tuesday we could only say a CFTC rule sat at White House review. Now we have names, but still no text. And as we noted then, agency rules are easier to rewrite than a statute. So Coinbase's most important new license sits inside a rulebook whose contents nobody has seen.

USDC as collateral is one half of on-chain cash. Tokenized Treasuries are the other, and there's fresh evidence there too. The platform DigiFT has launched tokenized access to a U.S. Treasury money market fund managed by Fidelity Investments. It's open only to institutional and accredited investors who meet DigiFT's eligibility bar. DigiFT says the fund's holdings meet the GENIUS Act's reserve-asset criteria for stablecoin issuers. That's DigiFT's own claim, not a regulator's finding. And the announcement doesn't name the underlying fund or the chain it settles on. That's an odd gap for a product sold on transparency.

Now the scale check, from BlackRock's BUIDL. Per RWA.xyz data relayed by a secondary outlet, BUIDL held $2.25 billion as of October 6th, with just 106 holders. On our arithmetic, that's roughly $21 million per holder. Solana is now its largest network by value, a tell on which chain is winning institutional tokenization share. Yesterday we noted that Circle's USYC and Ondo's USDY had overtaken BUIDL in this category. So the benchmark fund is both losing rank and tightly held. This is not retail adoption. It's a small set of institutions, plausibly including crypto-native firms parking reserves, which would make some of this liquidity circular.

The second-order point follows from DigiFT's own framing. Suppose stablecoin issuers come to hold their reserves through tokenized fund shares rather than bills directly. Then "fully backed" gains an extra layer: dependency on a fund manager. That's an implication, not a flow we can observe yet. The fair bull read is that Fidelity and BlackRock distributing real Treasury exposure on-chain is genuine infrastructure, whatever today's scale. So the takeaway: the collateral layer is real, but for now it's a club, not a market.

Pulling it together. Bitcoin failed its breakeven test, and the positioning says that was a flush, not a new bearish bet. The cause is less settled than the oil headline suggests. Underneath, the plumbing kept getting built, with USDC as clearinghouse collateral and tokenized Treasuries pitched as stablecoin reserves. Watch three things. First, whether Bitcoin can reclaim $86,000 on a closing basis. Second, a confirmed ETF flow print for October 7th, and whether any outflow extends. Third, whether the CFTC publishes actual text for Regulation CTX and CAM.

That's digital money for October 8th. Talk to you tomorrow.
