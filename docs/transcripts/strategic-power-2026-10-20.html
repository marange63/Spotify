Good morning. It's Tuesday, October 20th, 2026, and this is your strategic power briefing.

Today's thesis is simple. In rare earths and on the American grid alike, the deal everyone is talking about is not yet written in the instrument that actually binds. Two stories, one pattern. Washington has announced a rare earth date that Beijing hasn't written into law. And PJM has a backstop for the grid that regulators have put on ice. In both, the paperwork is lagging the narrative.

Start with a correction, because this desk owes you one. Yesterday we told you to treat January 10th, 2027, as the operative date for the US-China rare earth truce. That was too clean. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said in September that the truce had been pushed from its original November 10th deadline out to January 10th, 2027. That truce includes the suspension of China's October 2025 rare earth export controls. But according to reporting by technology.org, a search of China's Commerce Ministry announcements through October 16th found no notice extending or replacing the underlying legal order, known as Announcement Number 70. Washington has stated a date. Beijing has not published the text that would make it binding. So on the legal record, the suspension of that October 2025 control set still lapses on November 10th unless Beijing acts in the next three weeks.

Two caveats on that finding. It comes from secondary reporting, not a document we fetched from the ministry directly. And the search only runs through October 16th, so a notice could already be in the pipeline.

Now layer in a second gate that the truce never touched at all. China's April 2025 licensing regime on seven heavy rare earths was never part of any pause. It still requires a license for every single export. So even a clean extension of the newer truce leaves an older, separate chokepoint fully in place. The flow data fits that metering story. China's rare earth exports fell 9.6 percent year over year from January through September. September alone rose 6.7 percent. One month's rebound against a nine-month decline is not evidence of a reopening.

Here's the skeptical turn, because the absence of a notice is not evidence of intent. Beijing could publish an extension at any point before November 10th. Or it could let the ambiguity sit as leverage heading into other negotiations. Our inference is narrower: November 10th is a live date three weeks out that gets far less attention than the January date Washington quotes. And the pattern is familiar. Earlier this month, Washington announced an energy ceasefire between Russia and Ukraine that Moscow never confirmed. This is another arrangement stated in Washington that the other side hasn't written down.

The second-order read is about direction. If the suspension does lapse on November 10th, the pressure lands first on downstream buyers that still depend on Chinese material: magnet users, defense contractors, and EV assemblers. The relative winners would be refiners and magnet makers outside China, because a lapse would sharpen the case for building that capacity at home and among allies. So the truce is real as a political statement. But the legal instrument that actually governs exports hasn't been extended yet.

The same gap between the headline deal and the binding instrument shows up on the American grid.

PJM's latest capacity auction shows supply that didn't show up, even at the price cap. That auction, for the 2028-2029 delivery year, cleared at the cap of roughly 325 dollars per megawatt-day. Yet according to trade press reporting on PJM's filing, it drew only 525 megawatts of new supply, including uprates. That left a shortfall of nearly seven gigawatts against PJM's reliability requirement. The hole is about thirteen times the new supply that did show up.

To fill that hole, PJM proposed a one-time reliability backstop procurement for long-dated contracts on new resources. In September, trade press reported that the backstop's bid window would run from September 30th to tomorrow, October 21st. That window never opened. PJM's own newsroom says it will not proceed with the procurement on September 30th as originally planned. FERC's September 29th order accepted the design but suspended it for five months, to an effective date of February 28th, 2027. FERC also sent cost allocation to a paper hearing. PJM's statement on the new schedule is blunt. In its words, actual timing is to be determined. So there is no bid deadline tomorrow. The mechanism meant to answer the shortfall is stalled.

The design itself is on the record. Only new supply resources qualify, not existing cleared capacity. Winners get a fixed term of up to fifteen years, running no later than the 2042-2043 delivery year. And every new plant must reach commercial operation by June 1st, 2032.

Hold two readings of the shortfall at once. The first says the cap muzzled the signal. PJM's filing put the uncapped clearing price at 555 dollars, roughly 70 percent above the cap. At that price, more developers might have bid, so this is suppressed pricing rather than market failure. The second reading says even a full price can't compress interconnection queues and turbine lead times inside a 2028-2029 delivery year. On that view, the binding constraint is physical, not financial. The evidence doesn't settle which reading dominates, and it's probably some of both.

Now add the demand side, because it creates a real tension. PJM's independent market monitor has told regulators that about four gigawatts of forecast data-center load had been canceled or delayed. That's roughly 42 percent of the data-center growth PJM had forecast over its window through 2029. So the backstop would lock in up to fifteen years of new capacity against a forecast its own monitor says is partly soft. We found no statement from PJM, the monitor, or FERC saying whether the backstop's target of nearly seven gigawatts has been revised for that. Whoever ends up holding the cost allocation is underwriting that forecast risk. That's the fight the paper hearing will decide.

So what: the grid's administrative answer to the data center crunch just slipped into next year, and the demand it was sized for is itself in question.

When the mechanism that's supposed to conjure megawatts stalls, the buyers with the deepest pockets go around it.

Google and Constellation Energy illustrate that bypass. The two companies signed a fifteen-year deal for 2,700 megawatts in the PJM market. Alongside it sits a separate twenty-year agreement to boost output at eleven existing Constellation reactors in Illinois, Pennsylvania, and New Jersey. Constellation plans to invest more than 4.3 billion dollars in those plants. The deal was reported around October 11th, by Breitbart with Bloomberg corroboration, so treat it as context for a pattern rather than breaking news.

The analytical distinction is what matters here. The twenty-year agreement to raise reactor output adds real incremental supply, though it lands on a multi-year lag. The fifteen-year supply contract most likely does something different. If it draws on existing plants, it reallocates firm capacity from the general PJM pool to one buyer. That's not new power. It's power moving to whoever can sign the longest, deepest contract.

Here's our inference from the pattern. If the auction can't produce enough new megawatts even at the cap, more of the remaining firm supply gets locked up bilaterally. Constellation and similar incumbents capture today's scarcity pricing for fifteen to twenty years. And the residual pool other buyers rely on gets thinner. There's an irony in that. The same soft-demand risk that hangs over the backstop gives an incumbent every reason to sign a long contract with Google now, while scarcity pricing lasts.

So restate the thesis. Rare earths and PJM power are the same story told twice: the headline deal is ahead of the instrument that actually binds it. Watch two things over the next few weeks. First, whether Beijing publishes any notice on Announcement Number 70 before November 10th. Second, whether PJM submits the revised backstop filing FERC encouraged, and what new bid dates it proposes. Until those documents exist, treat both deals as narrative, not structure.

That's the briefing. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
