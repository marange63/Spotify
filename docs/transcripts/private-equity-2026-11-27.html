Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Equity & the Deal Economy

Transcript · November 27, 2026

Good morning. It's Friday, November 27th, and this is your private equity briefing.

Since mid-November, every one of these briefings has told you to watch one catalyst: Blackstone's promised fourth-quarter realization step-up. On the third-quarter call, Blackstone's CFO said the firm expects, quote, a significant step-up in net realizations in the fourth quarter, including the sales of multiple companies. He added that contracted carry alone should surpass the firm's total net realizations from the third quarter. That guidance came after a third quarter in which net realizations fell 20 percent from the prior quarter. Today we have a fourth-quarter exit we haven't covered yet, and it comes with a price tag. One caution up front. Blackstone hasn't said which company sales it meant, so linking this deal to that guidance is our inference.

The deal is Medline, the medical-supply company and the cycle's flagship private-equity exit. On November 17th, its sponsors priced their third sell-down of 2026. Blackstone, Carlyle and Hellman & Friedman sold 60 million Class A shares at $35.50 apiece.

Now look at the price ladder. The March sell-down priced at $41.00, per Medline's own quarterly filing. The May tranche came in at a reported $37.00. November cleared at $35.50. That's three sell-downs this year, each one priced below the last.

Each of those tranches is real cash, not a mark. The March sale alone returned about three and a half billion dollars of gross proceeds to the selling stockholders, again per Medline's filing. That is what distributions-to-paid-in actually look like. Paper value from the IPO gets converted into money, one slice at a time.

Before you read the falling ladder as the market straining to absorb sponsor supply, consider the other side. Medline's last close before the November deal priced was $37.68. By our arithmetic, that puts the offer at roughly a six percent discount to the prior trade. That's within the normal range for a large registered follow-on. It is not evidence of a buyers' strike. The fairer counter-reading is that the sponsors are choosing cash over price heading into next year's fundraising season. That's a rational trade, not a forced one. It also fits the point we made last Friday, that distributions are becoming the weapon for the 2027 fundraising season.

There's a genuinely new wrinkle in the November deal. Medline is concurrently buying back $200 million of stock and units directly from the selling stockholders. The price is about thirty-five dollars and six cents per equity interest, a hair below the public offering price. That repurchase counts toward Medline's existing buyback authorization of up to $1 billion. So part of this liquidity event isn't the public market absorbing sponsor stock at all. It's the portfolio company's own balance sheet quietly taking some of it back.

And the harvest isn't finished. After this deal, the sponsor group still controls about 51.5 percent of Medline's combined voting power, per the prospectus. Voting power isn't the same as economic ownership. The filing also doesn't split stakes or proceeds among Blackstone, Carlyle and Hellman & Friedman, so we won't attribute dollars to any one of them. But a majority-controlling holder group still in place tells you more supply is likely still to come.

That matters for every sponsor-backed company queued behind Medline. If even the flagship exit converts to cash in tranches, each at a discount to the last trade, the IPO route becomes a long, priced drip of distributions. That makes continuation vehicles and strategic sales relatively more attractive. That step is our inference. One gap to name honestly: we have no figure for these prices against the sponsors' original buyout entry, so we can't tell you the realized multiple. So the takeaway on Medline is this. The sell-downs are real distributions, but they measure what sponsors will concede on price, not a reopened exit window.

If the trophy asset can only be sold in slices, consider everything that can't be sold at all. That's where the continuation-vehicle boom comes in. In its latest count, through early November, Mergermarket tracked 50 announced continuation vehicles from North American GPs this year. That's up 52 percent from a year earlier. It counts only announced deals, so true volume is likely higher.

Now size the queue behind that activity. Warburg Pincus's Andrew DiGeronimo, quoted in the same Mergermarket piece, estimates around 35,000 unsold private-equity-backed companies, worth roughly four to five trillion dollars. Treat that as one sponsor executive's informed estimate, not a hard count. Separately, Mergermarket's own data puts the average holding period at exit at six and a quarter years.

Directionally, the point still holds. Continuation vehicles are scaling fast. But against an inventory measured in trillions, they're a release valve, not a drain. That's our inference from the sizes, not Mergermarket's conclusion.

There's also a structural catch worth naming plainly. A continuation vehicle is a GP selling an asset to a vehicle the same GP manages, at a price the GP helps set. Our inference from that structure is simple. A continuation vehicle can lift a fund's reported distributions without any independent buyer testing the full mark. So when distribution numbers rise this quarter, allocators weighing 2027 commitments should ask where each dollar came from. Was it an arm's-length sale, a discounted public sell-down, or a GP effectively trading with itself? Those are three very different qualities of cash.

That's how capital gets out of the system. Now consider how it gets in at the very top, because the same handful of firms keep showing up. On Monday we said the unsolved piece of GTCR's possible take-private of Global Payments was financing. We told you to watch what package, if any, got assembled behind it. This week brought the first partial answer. According to a report relayed by PYMNTS, citing unnamed sources, Blackstone, Warburg Pincus and Apollo are in talks to help GTCR finance the deal. The report puts the potential take-private at about 50 billion dollars. That's an estimate, not a disclosed price.

Hold the caveats firmly here. GTCR, Global Payments' largest shareholder, has said in its own SEC filing only that it is evaluating the move, with no definitive decision. The financing report says no agreement has been finalized. There is no confirmed per-share price, premium or structure.

Be careful, too, about what "help finance" means, because it isn't specified. The two readings point in different directions. If these firms come in as equity co-investors, three large balance sheets are sharing equity risk at a scale one sponsor apparently couldn't clear alone. If instead it's their credit arms lending into the deal, then private credit, not private equity, ends up underwriting the megadeal. Those are very different stories about who's actually on the hook. The reporting doesn't yet tell us which one this is. So the takeaway: the megadeal bar is being approached, not cleared, and being in talks is not committed capital.

One more observation, and it's our inference. Blackstone shows up this week as both a seller, in Medline, and a possible financier, in Global Payments. The two aren't linked transactions. But it extends the pattern we called top-heavy last Friday. Harvesting and deployment at scale keep pooling in the same small set of firms.

So where does the cycle actually stand? Liquidity is flowing again, but it's being bought, not won back. Sponsors are selling their best exit in tranches at successively lower prices, with the company's own buyback helping. More of the unsold inventory is being recycled into vehicles the same GPs control. And megadeal risk at the top may be getting shared across rival balance sheets rather than carried by one firm. The biggest unresolved risk is still the exit-and-distribution logjam for the median asset, the one that has no flagship IPO and no club of mega-managers behind it.

The complacent read is that a fourth-quarter realization wave plus a fifty-billion-dollar take-private proves the exit window has reopened. The over-discounting read is that a falling price ladder and GP-to-self continuation vehicles are just distress wearing a liquidity costume. The fairer read sits between them. Liquidity is available, but at a price, and the price is the real story. On the one flagship asset where we can see it, that price has dropped three times this year.

Watch four things to settle it. First, whether Blackstone's guided realization surge actually shows up when it reports early next year, and at what prices. Second, whether GTCR turns its evaluation into a formal proposal, and whether the financing turns out to be equity or debt. Third, whether Medline's sponsors come back for a fourth sell-down, and what price it clears at. And fourth, the year-end continuation-vehicle tallies, as a read on how much inventory is going to GPs' own vehicles instead of outside buyers.

That's the briefing. Have a good one, and I'll talk to you next time.
