Good morning, it's November 9th, and this is your briefing on artificial intelligence in biopharma R&D.

Today's thread is a change of seats at the table. For weeks, this show has tracked big pharma buying AI-discovery capability as cheap options: small upfronts, contingent milestones, and often no disclosed economics at all. Now one of the platforms has moved to the other side of that trade. Recursion Pharmaceuticals is shopping two of its own programs to partners rather than funding them itself. The AI-designed drug this show has been arguing about turns out to be in a pivotal trial after all. Then we'll step back to where AI is actually earning its keep. Start with Recursion.

Recursion reported third-quarter results last week. Revenue came in around 18.6 million dollars, up roughly 258 percent year over year, and it beat estimates. Now look at the composition. A single milestone payment from Sanofi, worth 10 million dollars, drove the growth. By our arithmetic, not the company's, that one payment is more than half the quarter's revenue.

What triggered the milestone matters. Per Recursion's business update, Sanofi optioned the first development candidate to come out of the two companies' multi-program research collaboration. That is a real platform output. A large-pharma partner judged a candidate worth taking forward. But it is one event, on Sanofi's clock, and it says nothing yet about whether that candidate works in people. So bank it as a beat, not a run-rate.

The funding side is less flattering. Recursion posted a net loss of 119 million dollars for the quarter. Cash stood at about 561 million dollars at quarter-end, a figure the company itself labels preliminary and unaudited. Management says that cash, plus roughly 200 million dollars in gross proceeds from a stock offering last month, funds operations into 2029. So the runway rests on equity and partner milestones, not product revenue.

Now the real news, which is the capital-allocation call. Recursion's business update lists the programs it will keep focusing resources on. First on that list is REC-4881, the familial adenomatous polyposis program whose early human data we covered last week. Two programs are conspicuously absent. REC-617, a CDK7 inhibitor, and REC-3565, a MALT1 inhibitor, will instead be advanced through partnership opportunities, in the update's words, rather than on Recursion's own balance sheet.

In the same update, Recursion cut its cash operating expense guidance for this year, from 390 million dollars to under 375 million. That's real discipline, but a modest trim next to the quarter's loss. The equity raise is what actually buys the runway.

Management's rationale on the call was generic. Recursion says it is concentrating investment "where we see the clearest path to value." Both shopped programs came from Exscientia before the two companies combined, per trade press. Nothing disclosed says how much AI design went into either one, so don't read this as a verdict on AI-designed chemistry.

Recursion is keeping its named priorities funded, including the one program with human data, and putting two legacy programs up for partnering. There are two honest ways to read that. One is capital discipline. A platform that can't carry every asset through late-stage trials chooses to monetize some of them rather than dilute shareholders further. The other is a quieter signal that management ranks these two programs below the rest of the pipeline. The disclosures don't settle it. Either way, Recursion is now behaving like a seller of options, not just a platform that pharma buys options on.

That shift cuts both ways, and what follows is our inference. For pharma business development, a platform shopping several programs at once moves leverage to the buyer. That likely means small upfronts, like the 40 million dollars AstraZeneca paid insitro in October for target-selection work, only now with Recursion on the receiving end. For AI-discovery equities, if this becomes the template, investors value these companies on partner cash and option sales, not owned pipelines. Valuations then hinge on milestone cadence, which is exactly the variable the companies don't control. Recursion's own quarter just showed how lumpy that cadence is.

Recursion isn't the only platform living on partner milestones. Trade press reported on October 29th that Insilico Medicine reached an early drug discovery milestone in its oncology collaboration with Servier. The coverage cites "the successful fulfilment of predefined programme criteria." The payment amount wasn't disclosed, and neither were the criteria. That is the same economics as the Sanofi payment, seen from a private company. And an early discovery milestone is about the cheapest event a partner can pay for. So file it under the opacity pattern in AI-discovery monetization, not under validation.

Insilico also owns the molecule this show has been arguing about. Insilico's own release says it has dosed the first patient in GENESIS-IPF-3, which it calls a Phase 3 trial of rentosertib, its AI-discovered TNIK inhibitor for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. The company describes a "randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled" study of once-daily dosing over 52 weeks. That release dates from the trial's start, not from this week. Our research simply found it this cycle, which means yesterday's withdrawal of the Phase 3 description was wrong. That status rests on Insilico's own release, not a registry check.

What it means. The design thesis for AI-generated small molecules now has a genuine pivotal test, run to a placebo-controlled standard. But a started pivotal trial is not a successful one. Pulmonary fibrosis has a long record of late-stage disappointments. And the mid-stage trial this program rests on was small and safety-primary. Reaching a pivotal trial shows the molecule cleared the bar to be tested. It isn't evidence that AI design improved its odds. With a year-long treatment period, our inference is that the proof clock on AI-designed molecules runs in years, not quarters.

If the clinical proof clock runs in years, the near-term value has to come from somewhere else in the chain. What follows is our standing assessment, not news, because this cycle brought little fresh on most of the stack. Capital is concentrated at the design layer, in protein and molecule design platforms whose clinical proof is furthest away. Isomorphic Labs, as we noted last week, says its own programs are still preclinical. Chai Discovery added Roche as a partner on November 6th, again with no financial terms disclosed. That is pharma buying design access as an option, not making a conviction bet.

The measurable value is landing in two less glamorous places. The first is specialized prediction models trained on curated experimental data. In a blind benchmark we covered on the 7th, a small specialist called Inductive beat more than 270 entrants at predicting CYP inhibition across 750 compounds. Recursion, the platform we led with, placed fourth. On narrow, data-bound tasks like that, curation beats scale. That is the low-hanging fruit: fewer wasted make-and-test cycles before the clinic.

The second is general-purpose language models doing operational work. Literature synthesis, regulatory documents, protocol drafting and agents for operations are where large language models are the workhorse. Sanofi's multi-year agent collaboration with Owkin, which we covered yesterday, is the clearest recent commitment there. Specialized architectures still own structure, pathology imaging and omics. No biopharma-specific frontier or open-weight model release surfaced this cycle, so we have no fresh read on which model leads which task.

On in-house models, the trend is custom models trained on a sponsor's own data. Pfizer's deal with Chai was described as a custom model built on Pfizer's proprietary data, and Lilly's as a model for Lilly's exclusive use. The moat is the data, and sponsors are writing contracts to keep it.

Real-world data follows the same logic. The binding constraint is linking fragmented records, not modeling them. HIPAA is less a wall than a de-identification standard that every licensed dataset has to clear. Data holders are already being paid. Tempus's data-licensing segment grew faster than its testing business last quarter, as we covered on the 7th. What's still missing is proof that any of this data improves trial odds.

Pulling the threads together. On capital, the option trade now runs in both directions. Pharma still buys AI capability cheaply, and platforms are starting to sell their own programs into the same market. On clinical success rates, nothing in this cycle's news moves them, and rentosertib's pivotal verdict is years out. Our realistic view is that the first lift, if it comes, comes from choosing better targets and better patients, not from designing better molecules. That is where the recent human evidence has clustered. On R&D spend, AI looks set to reallocate the budget toward data, prediction and operations rather than shrink it. Three things to watch. Who partners Recursion's two shopped programs, and for what upfront? Does Recursion's next quarter lean on another single milestone? And does Insilico disclose the pivotal trial's primary endpoint and expected readout timing?

That's the briefing for November 9th. Stay cautiously optimistic.
