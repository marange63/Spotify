Private Credit & the Corporate Credit Cycle

Transcript · October 20, 2026

Good morning, it's Tuesday, October 20th, 2026, and this is your private credit briefing. Last week the bank-to-private-credit channel was a policy paper. This week it has a name and a price.

Start with the name. On October 15th we told you the IMF wanted sectoral capital and concentration requirements on banks' exposures to private credit. We argued that instrument would bite on the bank funding layer first. The IMF's blog also made a point we didn't air: some direct lenders have committed to fund borrowers on demand, so in a tightening those draws could hit the funds just when they face their own liquidity pressure. That is why supervisors care where this risk sits. Four days later, the funding layer has a test case.

The Financial Times reported Monday, relayed by several outlets, that the Bank of England's Prudential Regulation Authority is examining a complaint lodged earlier this year. The complaint concerns Barclays' allocation of total return swaps referencing illiquid private credit loans. It alleges those swaps went into the trading book rather than the banking book. That matters because trading-book treatment would potentially lower the capital held against them. So the booking choice is the capital choice. And the capital choice decides how much private credit risk the banking system is visibly carrying.

Now Barclays' side, stated fairly. Its traders argue the exposure could be treated as a traded position because each swap was hedged through a matching, back-to-back swap with another counterparty. That is a standard rationale for trading-book treatment. Barclays also says the vast majority of its roughly fifteen billion pounds of private credit exposure sits in the banking book. And it says its treatment is consistent with accounting and capital rules. No regulator has made a finding. The Bank of England declined to comment. The FT did not disclose the size of the disputed positions, so nobody can yet say how much of that fifteen billion is in question. One more thing not to conflate. An earlier PRA letter told banks generally that some had taken an imprudent approach to certain financing portfolios. That letter predates this complaint and is not about Barclays.

The market reaction was measured. Barclays shares fell roughly two to three and a half percent on the report. That reads as a capital-and-disclosure question, not a solvency one. And if the hedge defence holds, the sharper question moves elsewhere. A genuinely hedged position passes the private-loan risk to whoever sits on the other side of that back-to-back swap. That counterparty is where the economic exposure actually lives.

Here is the second-order effect, and it's our inference, not anyone's finding. If the PRA were to insist on banking-book treatment, every bank financing private credit through swaps would face the same question. That would raise the cost of bank leverage to direct lenders, which is exactly the funding-layer squeeze the IMF's proposal implied. Barclays reports third-quarter results this Thursday, October 22nd. Listen for any disclosure language on this before taking a side. The takeaway: the question is no longer whether banks hold private credit risk, but how it gets counted.

If the question is how that risk gets counted, the market is already pricing the AI-linked slice of it. CoreWeave carries about fifty billion dollars of debt, per LSEG data. Reuters reported the cost of insuring that debt rose to about twelve hundred basis points in the week of October 12th through 16th. Some of its bonds traded at yields of fifteen to sixteen percent that week.

By Monday the 19th, things had stopped getting worse. The five-year CDS spread was roughly ten-eighty-two basis points, about a hundred and twenty below the prior week. The yield on the 9.625 percent notes due 2032 was 14.9 percent, slightly down from above fifteen. CNBC framed it as stock investors moving on from the AI rout while credit investors are not. That's a credit-versus-equity divergence, not contagion. But a plateau at distressed levels is still distressed.

Here's why that matters for the Barclays story. The public bond and CDS prints price CoreWeave's unsecured layer. Asset-backed tranches with contracted cash flows may deserve tighter levels, so don't read fifteen percent across to every GPU-backed loan. Still, private and bank holders of AI-linked paper mark to models, not to thousand-basis-point CDS. That gap between the public price and private marks is our inference, not a reported number. It is also the transmission risk. Stress reaches bank capital and fund NAVs when those marks are forced, not when the bonds move.

CoreWeave is one name. The sector tape shows where the existing damage already sits. JPMorgan strategists reported on October 6th that leveraged loans priced at or below eighty cents on the dollar total 139.8 billion dollars. That is nearly ninety percent higher than a year ago, and only about four billion short of the May 2020 pandemic peak. Technology is thirty-nine percent of that distressed volume, or 54.4 billion dollars.

That matters because software is the precedent behind last week's AI warnings. Carlyle's argument was that private credit should not repeat its software concentration in AI. JPMorgan's tally shows the software bet is already producing real price stress. And the refinancing test is coming. Software companies face more than a hundred billion dollars of approaching maturities. JPMorgan projects leveraged-loan defaults rising to four and a half percent next year, roughly double the 2.25 percent it projects for 2026.

Two cautions. The eighty-cent bucket is a price signal, and in a spread-widening tape price distress can run ahead of actual defaults. And these are broadly syndicated loans, not direct loans. The read-across to private software books is our inference. It is a strong one, though, because the same sponsors and often the same sector sit in both markets. So the concentration risk lenders are warning about in AI has already played out once, in software.

A short coda on where exits and opacity are concentrating. Blue Owl's tech-focused non-traded fund, OTIC, saw third-quarter redemption requests rise to about thirty-nine percent of shares, roughly 1.1 billion dollars. That happened even as requests at its larger flagship eased. Exit demand is hardening exactly where the loan tape is weakest.

And in Australia, reported in early October, Metrics Credit Partners froze redemptions in some unlisted funds. Metrics manages about forty billion Australian dollars. The freeze came after KPMG declined to sign off on accounts for three listed vehicles. We don't know why the auditor balked, and one refusal in Australia says nothing directly about how US BDCs mark their books. But it predates, and rhymes with, the Goldman and UBS funding pullback from Australian managers we covered on October 16th. It is valuation-opacity risk made concrete rather than hypothetical.

Pull it together. The credit cycle is still a grind concentrated at the bottom of the stack, not a turn. The evidence today is a bank capital question, a distressed AI credit that has plateaued rather than broken, and price distress clustered in software. The biggest unresolved risk isn't the headline default rate. It's how much private-loan risk sits synthetically inside banks, and inside vehicles marking to models rather than markets. Watch Barclays' results Thursday and any PRA follow-up. Watch Apollo's asset-level marks on October 30th, and the rest of the alternative managers' third-quarter calls. Then watch the first BDC non-accrual and payment-in-kind disclosures in early November. Those prints will tell you whether this stays a grind or starts to turn.
