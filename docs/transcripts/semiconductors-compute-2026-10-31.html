Semiconductors & Compute Infrastructure

Good morning. It's Saturday, October 31st, and this is your semiconductor and compute briefing.

Two threads this desk has been pulling on all week came together within forty-eight hours. They turn out to be the same thread. On Tuesday we argued that PC and device buyers, unlike compute-constrained accelerator buyers, would have to eat the cost of the HBM boom. On Thursday we named Samsung's divisional breakdown as the test of whether memory pricing power belonged to SK hynix alone or to the whole industry. Both answers have now landed. They are two sides of the same invoice.

Start with the side that paid. Intel beat third-quarter consensus on Thursday night. Adjusted earnings came in at 52 cents a share, against expectations of 40 cents, on revenue of 16.5 billion dollars. The stock fell anyway. It closed down 28.4 percent on Friday, at $65.92, because the fourth-quarter outlook disappointed.

Keep that outlook in proportion. Intel's earnings release guides total fourth-quarter revenue to 15 to 16 billion dollars. That's only modestly below the third quarter, so this is not a company-wide air pocket. The sharp number sits one layer down. On the call, CFO David Zinsner said he expects the client computing segment, the PC chips, to decline more than 20 percent sequentially. That's a segment guide, not a company guide, and the distinction matters for judging how hard the memory shock actually hit.

Now, what's driving it. The popular version is that memory costs crushed Intel's PC business. Management's own language is looser than that. Both executives said they don't expect a sustained recovery "until memory availability improves and pricing begins to normalize." That blends scarcity and price. It is not a clean cost shock. CEO Lip-Bu Tan added that system endpoint prices have increased significantly, putting more pressure on already limited IT budgets. That is the economics-constrained buyer, described by the company that sells into it.

There's a second caveat. Intel's own explanation for the lower fourth-quarter gross margin was generic: lower revenue against a fixed cost base, plus product mix. It did not name memory as a line item. And executives said Panther Lake margins are still not at the corporate average. So the memory story is real and management-stated, but it isn't cleanly separated from the drag of a product ramp.

The selloff wasn't only about Intel, either. Benzinga estimates the chip sector lost roughly a trillion dollars that day. That's an aggregator's tally, not exchange data. Nvidia, AMD, TSMC and Broadcom accounted for much of the decline. Amazon's soft guidance landed the same day and compounded the mood. The takeaway: the memory supercycle has now shown up in a bellwether's guidance, but it arrived tangled up with hyperscaler cost anxiety, not as a clean verdict.

One more Intel point before we move on, because it splits two competitive dimensions cleanly. Foundry revenue was 6.7 billion dollars. External customer revenue was only 411 million. Management said that came primarily from a non-recurring contract, so it isn't a run-rate. The foundry unit still lost 1.9 billion dollars at the operating line. On the manufacturing side, management said 18A costs are down roughly 40 percent since the first quarter, on yield and cycle-time gains. That's a company cost figure, not a disclosed yield. Put the two together. Intel is getting better at making chips faster than it is finding anyone to make them for.

Now the other side of that invoice. Samsung's chip division reportedly brought in 147.1 trillion won of revenue and 106.5 trillion won of operating profit in the third quarter. Those divisional figures come from secondary reporting. Samsung's own release gives only consolidated totals. Take them at face value, and by our arithmetic the division's margin works out to about 72 percent. That answers Thursday's question. The pricing power behind SK hynix's roughly 80 percent margin isn't a company-specific anomaly. It's industry-wide.

The mix tells you which product is doing the work. Samsung's HBM4 revenue more than tripled quarter over quarter and is now 60 percent of its HBM revenue. So the newest memory generation, not legacy HBM3E, is already carrying the load. And the HBM4 transition is not an SK hynix-only story.

Here's the sharper inference. Samsung's consolidated operating profit was 107.4 trillion won. Set the reported chip-division figure against it, and that implies under a trillion won for everything else at Samsung, phones included. It's arithmetic across two sources, and it ignores intersegment eliminations, so treat it as an implication rather than a reported result. But the direction is hard to miss. One conglomerate is effectively paying the memory tax to itself, and its device business is on the losing end.

SK hynix added one genuinely new element. Management says HBM4 has already exceeded its planned yield and should overtake HBM3E in both shipments and revenue this quarter. It still expects supply constraints to persist through 2030. Worth flagging, not resolving: that yield claim sits awkwardly next to the reports this desk covered Tuesday, that suppliers struggled to hit Nvidia's original HBM4 speed bins. Yield and speed bin are different measurements. The tension is unsettled, and it bears on whether Rubin's bandwidth cut was a memory problem.

So why not call this a clean rotation, from PC losers to memory winners? Because the winners fell too. Micron dropped 5 percent and Western Digital 7 percent in the same selloff that hit Intel. If investors were cleanly buying the memory winners and selling the memory payers, Micron wouldn't be falling. What it looks like instead is broad de-risking of the whole AI-exposed chip complex.

Now set Amazon beside that. On the same day, Amazon said it still expects about 220 billion dollars of capital expenditure this year. That's the compute-constrained buyer, still paying up for scarce capacity. So the market wasn't doubting the scale of AI demand on Friday. It was pricing the cost of the buildout, and treating the memory boom and the AI capex boom as one correlated bet. That's the extrapolation risk worth watching. Cyclical memory pricing and structural AI demand are trading as a single position, and they may not resolve together.

The next place the scarcity rent is headed is the accelerator itself. TrendForce reports that foundries and packaging houses are preparing price increases of 10 to 20 percent on CoWoS advanced packaging, with TSMC named in its headline. TSMC hasn't confirmed it. For context, TSMC already posted a 66.4 percent gross margin last quarter. Stack a packaging increase on top of HBM scarcity, and an accelerator's bill of materials gets squeezed from two directions at once. There's a skeptical reading too. A price rise on a sold-out product can be rationing ahead of a capacity ramp. If CoWoS capacity scales, that rent is cyclical, not permanent.

Follow the windfall one more step and it lands at the toolmakers. Lam Research's preliminary view puts 2027 wafer fab equipment spending above 200 billion dollars, up from the mid-150 billion range it sees for this year. That's a vendor's preliminary figure, as reported by 24/7 Wall St. And equipment stocks rallied on October 28th specifically on SK hynix's shortage commentary. So equipment demand is now trading as a derivative of memory-maker confidence. That makes it pro-cyclical, and fragile if memory pricing ever turns.

Two things to watch. First, AMD reports third-quarter results on November 3rd. It's the first accelerator vendor to report since this week's HBM and packaging price signals. Its gross margin against the roughly 56 percent guide, and whatever it guides next, will show whether the squeeze has reached an accelerator vendor's income statement. Second, two China dates arrive back to back. China's suspension of its rare-earth export measures lapses on November 10th. And the comment period on Beijing's draft AI and chip export-control catalog closes November 15th. That catalog is still a draft, not law.

What ties it together: the AI memory shortage has stopped behaving like a rising tide that lifts the whole chip industry. It's now a transfer. It runs from the economics-constrained buyers who absorb scarcity pricing toward the capacity owners who set it. That's our judgment, not a measured outcome, but this week supplied evidence from both ends. Watch where the transfer lands next, because the accelerator makers look like the next stop.

That's the briefing for October 31st. Thanks for listening, and I'll catch you next time.
