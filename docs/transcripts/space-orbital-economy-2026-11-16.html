Cautious Optimism Briefings

Space & the Orbital Economy

Transcript · November 16, 2026

Good morning. It's Monday, November 16th, and this is your space and orbital economy briefing.

All week this desk has asked one question. Who, besides SpaceX, can turn money into a sustained launch cadence? This weekend produced two answers. One is a clean yes, at small scale. The other was the heavy-lift test, and it now looks like a no.

Start with the yes. Rocket Lab flew its 100th Electron mission from New Zealand this weekend. According to 247 Wall Street, no other commercially developed rocket has reached 100 launches faster. That is a cadence milestone, not a one-off demo. And it is the first clean yes to this week's question. Recent challengers have brought money or contracts, but not flights.

Then the forward claim. Founder and chief executive Peter Beck says the next 100 Electron launches are already booked. Treat that carefully. It reaches us from Beck through secondary reporting. It is not a filed contract count, and no dollar value is attached. Booked is also not recognized revenue. Still, this order book sits on a rocket that already flies. Its conversion risk is execution: can Rocket Lab keep building and flying at this pace? That is a very different risk from a backlog sitting on paper.

Which is exactly what Rocket Lab's other order book looks like. Neutron, the medium-lift rocket meant to compete with Falcon 9, has sixteen missions in backlog and has never flown. The company found voids in a Stage 1 tank dome during production. Neutron's debut is now no earlier than 2027, and management has not committed to a month. So inside one company you have two order books with two different kinds of risk. Electron's carries execution risk. Neutron's carries existence risk, because the vehicle has not yet shown it can fly at all. This weekend strengthened the Electron half of Rocket Lab's story. It did nothing for the Neutron half. A hundred small-rocket flights say nothing about whether the tank-dome fix will hold.

There is a second-order effect for customers, and it is an inference, not a reported finding. Suppose Electron's next hundred flights really are spoken for, and Neutron cannot fly before 2027. Then a new customer looking for non-SpaceX small or medium lift has very little to buy in the near term. That pushes marginal demand back toward Falcon 9. Scarcity at the challenger ends up reinforcing the incumbent's pricing power.

So Rocket Lab has proven that a company other than SpaceX can sell and fly cadence. But it has proven that only at small-lift scale. Electron cannot deploy a megaconstellation. That test belongs to heavy lift, and it came up this weekend too.

Which brings us to Amazon Leo, the constellation formerly known as Project Kuiper. A ULA Vulcan rocket carrying Amazon Leo satellites lifted off from Cape Canaveral on November 15th. This desk has flagged that flight as a catalyst all week. As recently as Saturday, we reported it was still waiting to fly.

At first, the outcome was unclear. Photo captions implied a clean flight. A crowd-edited launch tracker listed it as a failure. The balance has since tipped. Two separate outlets, GeekWire and Macau Business, ran headlines stating that the launch failed and that forty Amazon Leo satellites were lost. GeekWire's headline reads, quote, "Amazon loses 40 Leo satellites as ULA Vulcan launch failure deals setback to tech giant's Starlink rival."

Now the limits. Those are headlines from secondary outlets. We have no statement from ULA, Amazon, or the FAA, and no cause has been given. We also can't yet separate the three questions that matter. Did the vehicle reach orbit? Did any satellites separate? Did any make contact? So this is not an official mishap finding, and we'll report the official account when one arrives. But two outlets converging on the same count is a stronger signal than an ambiguous photo caption, and it points to failure.

If that holds, here is the size of the setback. Amazon Leo had just under four hundred satellites in orbit as of November 9th, according to Orbital Radar's tracking. So this one flight would have grown the constellation by about a tenth. No cost or insurance figure has been reported for the satellites or the launch. The dollar loss can't be sized yet, but the schedule loss can.

Amazon's near-term calendar shows how hard that gap is to close. The next scheduled Leo launch isn't another Vulcan. It's an Ariane 6, Arianespace's tenth flight of the rocket, carrying thirty-six Amazon Leo satellites. That is four fewer than this weekend's reported loss. So even a clean Ariane flight would not make up for what Vulcan was supposed to deliver. Arianespace's own announcement targets no earlier than November 23rd, from Kourou. After that comes Blue Origin's New Glenn, whose next flight is expected to carry Leo spacecraft. That flight is now targeted for January 2027, after a static-fire failure in May damaged the Florida pad. Treat January as reported planning, not a locked date. Blue Origin's chief executive, Dave Limp, has publicly held to a 2026 return, even as an internal memo points to January. Weather could push it later still.

So look at what Amazon's deployment clock runs on right now. There is a Vulcan flight that appears to have failed. There is an Ariane 6 flight that hasn't happened yet. And there is a New Glenn flight that has already slipped from an earlier November target. AST SpaceMobile, the direct-to-cell operator, faces the same trap at smaller scale. Its BlueBirds 17 through 19 are due to ship by the end of November for a Blue Origin launch. Spreading a constellation across providers only helps if those providers can actually fly.

Now the market read, and this part is inference. For Amazon, the loss is small against its balance sheet but large against its schedule. For ULA, this was its first Amazon Leo mission, and losing it hits Vulcan's commercial credibility against Falcon 9. Every constellation operator shopping for a non-SpaceX heavy lifter just watched a leading alternative stumble. That tightens the same market in which Rocket Lab has already raised Neutron's price on a rocket that has never flown. And the clearest winner is SpaceX. Starlink's lead over its best-funded rival widened this weekend, and SpaceX did not have to do anything to widen it.

There is one more cost behind all of this. Every flight that succeeds adds hardware to a crowded commons. ESA statistics updated November 2nd count about 68,450 debris objects larger than ten centimeters. By our inference, that bill arrives first as collision-avoidance burden, then as insurance and replacement costs. So the price of winning the cadence race rises with the cadence itself.

Pulling the threads together. Non-SpaceX small-lift cadence genuinely inflected this weekend. Rocket Lab proved it, with a hundred Electron flights in the books and, by its chief executive's account, the next hundred booked. Everything above that scale is still a demo or a setback. Neutron hasn't flown. New Glenn is likely grounded into the new year. And Vulcan, on its first Amazon Leo mission, now looks likely to have failed. The biggest open question is whether anyone besides SpaceX can deliver constellation-scale cadence. On this weekend's evidence, the honest answer is not yet.

For catalysts, watch for a primary statement or mishap finding from ULA, Amazon, or the FAA on the Vulcan flight. Watch the November 23rd Ariane 6 launch, now the next real test of Amazon's deployment math. Watch whether AST's BlueBirds 17 through 19 ship by month-end for their Blue Origin ride. And watch whether Rocket Lab finally commits to a month for Neutron.

That's the briefing for November 16th. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
