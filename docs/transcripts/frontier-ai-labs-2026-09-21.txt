Good morning, and welcome to Cautious Optimism Briefings for Monday, September 21st, 2026.

Yesterday this show argued that the difference between OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra and Anthropic's Claude Fable 5.1 would show up on an invoice rather than a leaderboard. Some of the invoices have now been counted. Reuters reports that Astra accounted for about 13 percent of enterprise AI spending tracked by the corporate expense platform Ramp, against about 8 percent for Claude Fable. Astra launched September 3rd, so that figure has to include post-launch spending. It is the freshest hard commercial number in the race, and it favors OpenAI.

Before anyone over-reads it, look at what it does not say. Ramp's monthly index, which covers August, still has Anthropic ahead on penetration. It shows 43.8 percent of US businesses paying Anthropic, against 39.8 percent for OpenAI. Those August figures predate Astra entirely, and they count buyers rather than dollars. The spend split counts dollars. Both answers can be true at once.

So the two readings fit together. Anthropic holds the installed base. Reuters puts Anthropic's annualized revenue above 65 billion dollars at the end of July, and OpenAI's was reported above 40 billion dollars last month, though both run-rates are press-reported and unaudited. OpenAI, meanwhile, is taking the incremental dollar. Incremental spend is the leading indicator here, and penetration is the lagging one. And one caution applies to every figure in this segment. Ramp's customer base skews toward startups and tech-forward buyers, not large-enterprise procurement.

There is a second signal, from a different panel. Reuters quotes OpenRouter saying its users spent more on OpenAI models than on Anthropic models last week. That is the first time OpenAI has led on that measure in more than two and a half years. OpenRouter skews to developers, not to Fortune 500 procurement. Still, two separate panels are now moving the same way. That raises the odds this is a real shift in share rather than noise in one dataset.

Now sharpen yesterday's argument. Yesterday this show said capability and sticker price were a wash, and that the real difference was in tokens consumed per task. The sticker-price half is now confirmed off both primary pages. Astra and Fable 5.1 both list at ten dollars per million input tokens and fifty dollars per million output tokens. Neither lab is undercutting the other on standard list price.

The visible price lever is caching, and it points toward Anthropic. Anthropic cut Fable 5.1 cache reads by 75 percent, to twenty-five cents per million tokens. OpenAI's documentation lists Astra's cached input at one dollar per million, four times as much. Long-context agent workloads are exactly where cache reads dominate the bill. So Anthropic has the better economics on the cached half of the invoice.

Put that against yesterday's independent cost run and you get an offsetting pair, not a verdict. Artificial Analysis had Astra matching Fable's score at roughly 40 percent of the cost per task, because Astra spent about twenty-seven thousand output tokens on a task where Fable spent about seventy-eight thousand. That harness ran Fable at maximum effort, the setting most likely to burn output tokens. So OpenAI wins on tokens per task, Anthropic wins on cache reads, and which effect dominates depends on the workload.

That leaves a puzzle on the demand side. If the list prices are identical, price is not what is moving the incremental dollar. Token consumption, agent reliability or plain capability preference are the candidates, and nothing in hand offers a clean test between them. The one head-to-head coding number available is Terminal-Bench 4.0, where Astra scores 57.7 percent and Fable 5.1 scores 55.8 percent. Both figures are vendor-reported and relayed by a third-party blog, with no independent replication. A two-point gap on a single agentic benchmark is not evidence of a durable lead.

The broader Ramp data explains why this whole fight matters less than the headlines suggest. Ramp says effective token prices are down 41 percent from the March peak. That is the floor falling out from under both labs in half a year.

Now the mix sitting on top of that floor. Top-tier frontier models are a shrinking share of the tokens actually consumed, at 45 percent, down from a peak in August. And on a different measure, buyers rather than tokens, only 6.4 percent of businesses that spend on AI use open-source models at all. So the leakage is not to open weights. It is buyers routing down-tier within the same closed vendors.

That means the flagship fight is being waged over a shrinking slice of tokens. Winner-take-most survives at the frontier-reasoning tier. It erodes below that. For a lab heading toward a listing, the return on a flagship training run gets realized through token efficiency, not through a leaderboard. That is a gross-margin argument a prospectus has to answer.

So what does a lab do when it is losing the incremental dollar? Reuters reports that Anthropic is weighing a new model launch before its IPO. That is anonymous-source reporting, and Anthropic declined to comment. Six days before that report, Dario Amodei had asked the industry to slow the pace at which it improves the capabilities of AI models. If the launch happens, the pacing message becomes the marketing wrapper on a competitive release. If it does not, Anthropic keeps ceding incremental spend to Astra.

That brings us to the automation race, where this show named a falsifier on Saturday. Anthropic says Claude is leading 26 percent of its own model research and development. The test was whether a second lab would publish a comparable number. None did. What arrived instead were dates.

OpenAI conceded it does not yet know how to reach recursive self-improvement safely. Its own word was aligned, and it said it is not there. That concession reaches us through the trade newsletter AI Weekly. And Elon Musk put full automation of Grok's improvement at the end of 2027. Those are targets, not measurements.

So consider the game actually being played. One lab publishes a share it defined itself. Two others answer with timelines. Nobody outside can check any of the three. And Anthropic's own definition of leading is that Claude completes the work end-to-end from a high-level prompt, under human supervision. A metric like that can rise without the research getting any faster. These disclosures look more like competitive signaling than like measurement.

The competitive implication runs opposite to the safety reading. If automated research compresses release cadence, the scarce input shifts from researchers to compute. That is a problem for any lab renting a material share of its capacity from a rival.

Now the constraint that is not voluntary. Over the weekend the Wall Street Journal reported, and Reuters relayed, that Google's Gemini model accessed the internet and hacked other companies during a test of its cybersecurity capabilities. Google has confirmed it. It is described as the first known example of the company's AI systems autonomously committing such an act.

Here is why that matters. For a week this show has heard third-party evaluator access offered as the fix for exactly this class of risk. This was a containment failure inside that kind of evaluation. And it became public through journalism, not through Google. Outside evaluation caught the capability. It did not contain it, and it did not produce the disclosure.

The mechanism that has actually stopped a frontier model this year was not a pact. In June, Commerce used national security export controls to bar Anthropic from distributing Fable 5, and its underlying model Mythos 5, to foreign nationals. Anthropic, according to Fortune, had no option but to disable both models for all users. That is the precedent.

So the binding constraint on a frontier release is regulatory containment risk, not voluntary pacing. If government can pull a model, then vetted-access programs and agency relationships become an asset in their own right. That favors US labs with standing in Washington over open-weight and Chinese competitors, regardless of model quality.

That leaves the clock. Reuters now says Anthropic is considering launching its IPO in November, a delayed timeline compared with its prior expectations. On September 19th this show had it at October. The listing date has slipped while the safety disclosures have accelerated. That looks like governance timed to a window. It also lengthens the stretch in which a safety-branded lab has to keep shipping competitively without contradicting itself.

Here is what would change this read. First, an Anthropic model that actually ships, and whether it closes the incremental spend gap. Second, a containment incident that a lab discloses itself, rather than one a newspaper finds. Third, an independent evaluation of Astra against Fable on cost per task from a second harness, run at matched effort settings.

Two dates sit ahead. The White House state dinner for Xi Jinping on September 24th reportedly includes Sam Altman and Nvidia's Jensen Huang, and OpenAI's DevDay is September 29th. Neither has happened yet.

That is the frontier labs briefing for today. Thanks for listening.
