Good morning, it's Monday, November 23rd, and here's the Capital Markets Radar.

Futures bounced hard overnight after Friday's rout. The question for the week is simple. Is that a bottom, or a reflex?

Start with Friday, banked quickly. Per UPI, the S&P 500 fell 3.7% to 6,910.39. That was its seventh straight losing session, and it left the index in correction. Hold that close in mind, because it's the yardstick for everything that happened overnight.

The composition matters more than the headline. The Nasdaq Composite erased its entire 2026 gain. The Nasdaq-100 briefly traded more than 20% below its high, touching bear-market territory intraday. The Dow, for comparison, fell a milder 1.93%. The Dow still closed in correction, so the de-risking is broad. But the marginal selling sat squarely in AI. Friday's index-level damage confirmed what Saturday's briefing argued: an AI-specific shock landing on a market that was already de-grossing.

Then Sunday night, the reverse. As Asia opened, S&P futures were up around 2.2%, per investingLive. That's enough to put the index back above seven thousand if it holds into the cash open. Nasdaq futures did better, up about 2.8%. So the AI-heavy index led the rebound, just as it led the rout. These are pre-cash-open snapshots. Treat them as a read on appetite, not a verdict.

The driver wasn't new information about chip demand or earnings. It was policy. China cut margin requirements and trading fees. South Korea was reported to be readying a market-stabilization fund. When regulators go after margin and leverage directly, they're treating this as a forced-selling problem, not a fundamentals problem. That's the same mechanism that amplified Friday's selling, now running in reverse.

Here's how that connects to Saturday's read. Saturday's briefing inferred that the fast money had largely left the AI trade before this week. So Friday's selling hit the remaining long-only, passive, and systematic holders. Those holders sell on mandate and volatility, not on a view. A sharp bounce with no new catalyst is exactly what that thesis predicts in reverse. But there's a catch, and it's our inference rather than a sourced finding. Volatility-targeting and trend-following books re-lever only as realized volatility comes down. One green night doesn't deliver that. So the same flow that sold Friday can buy Sunday night, and sell again on the next shock.

The overnight tape wasn't uniformly relieved, either. Per the same investingLive report, the Kospi fell nearly 4% in the same window that US futures rallied. A genuinely global all-clear doesn't produce a drop that size in Seoul alongside a policy-fueled bid in New York. The so-what: the relief was concentrated, and concentrated relief is fragile.

Gold sent a more interesting signal. Saturday's briefing noted that gold fell nearly 3% on Friday, on a day Treasury yields were falling. That tilted the drop toward margin selling rather than real rates. Overnight, spot gold rose about 2.3%, to roughly $3,620 an ounce, alongside the equity futures. A liquidity casualty should recover once forced selling stops, so the bounce fits Saturday's read. Don't lean on it too hard, though. A returning haven bid predicts the same bounce, and one session can't separate the two. The so-what: gold is consistent with a deleveraging story, not proof of one.

So a one-night, policy-driven pop resolves none of the three things that would actually make this a bottom. Call them the three tests.

Test one is the stock at the center of it. Nvidia has carried headline risk for more than a week. Jensen Huang resigned as chief executive after a board investigation, and the board has said it does not intend to reinstate him. Then, on Friday, came reporting that the SEC is examining the company's accounting. Neither overhang moved over the weekend. There has been no company or regulator statement clarifying the accounting review. The original Reuters report itself said it could not determine when the inquiry opened, or exactly what it covers.

Huang, for his part, has said publicly he'd return if the board decided it was in shareholders' interest. He also acknowledged that he, quote, "created problems for the Board that I deeply regret." That's an offer, not board movement. Senior administration figures have reportedly signaled openness to his return, but nothing on the record commits to anything. That's a new risk vector in its own right: political voices weighing in on succession at one of the heaviest names in the index. And even a returning CEO wouldn't answer the accounting question. The so-what: Nvidia's headline risk did not clear over the weekend. With this much index weight in one name, that caps how far a bounce can run on flows alone.

Test two is the one actually moving, and it's the pattern worth flagging. December hike odds climbed all month. CME FedWatch had them at 67% on November 5th, per the Motley Fool, and at effective certainty a week later. The driver was a stronger-than-expected October jobs report, with 165,000 payrolls added. Fed Governor Waller even floated a half-point move.

Now the direction has turned. Secondary aggregators report the odds of a quarter-point hike slightly lower. That's the first pullback after weeks of climbing in this desk's reads. Their snapshots disagree on the exact level, so take the direction, not the decimal. The cleaner signal is the tail. Secondary readings of FedWatch now put the half-point option at essentially zero. That's the first sign all month that financial-conditions stress is feeding back into policy expectations.

Pair that with what Saturday's briefing flagged. On Friday, Treasuries acted like a hedge for the first time all week. A softer hike path, plus a long end that rallies on stress, is the one channel that could turn a reflex into something durable. It's also the only one of the three tests where the direction actually flipped. But don't over-read it. A December hike is still the base case. This is a softer path, not a returning Fed put. And for discount rates, the relief only reaches long-duration multiples if Nvidia's own risk premium stops rising.

Test three is the one that actually decides it, and it's still open. Saturday's briefing had CoreWeave's credit default swaps widening again on Friday. It called CoreWeave the canary, and the next broad spread print the verdict. That broad print still hasn't arrived. There is no high-yield spread data covering Friday's rout onward, so the credit verdict is pending.

What we do have is a CNBC report that hyperscalers collectively posted negative free cash flow in the third quarter. That means the AI buildout is increasingly running on borrowed money at the margin, not internally generated cash. It makes the cost of credit, not a night of futures, the real test of the capex thesis. Here's the feedback loop, and it's our inference. Wider spreads raise the cost of the buildout itself. That can feed into capex guidance, and from there back into the equity story. To be fair to the other side, negative free cash flow at a capex peak can be a deliberate choice to invest, not a sign of distress. That distinction only gets settled by watching spreads, and nobody has that print yet.

So here's the week. Watch the Monday cash session for whether the bounce holds through a full day of trading. Watch for any word from Nvidia or the SEC on the accounting review. And watch the next broad high-yield spread print for the credit verdict. Then the calendar: October PCE on Wednesday, November 25th, payrolls on December 4th, the Fed decision on December 9th, and November CPI the day after. None of those has printed, and nothing about them should be inferred yet. Fed pricing just blinked for the first time this month. The question to carry is whether that blink survives two macro prints before the meeting, not whether futures were green for one night.

That's the Capital Markets Radar for November 23rd. Stay cautious, stay optimistic.
