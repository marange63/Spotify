Good morning. It's Saturday, August 1st, and this is your digital money briefing.

Start with the Bitcoin tape, because it tells you where institutional money is not going. Spot Bitcoin ETFs posted $265.4 million in net outflows on July 31st, led by BlackRock's IBIT at $122.7 million, ending a two-day inflow streak. We flagged yesterday that one strong ETF day was doing a lot of narrative work for what had otherwise been a record-weak month, and this outflow is that call getting vindicated within twenty-four hours. Bitcoin itself is sitting around $63,000, roughly half of its October 2025 all-time high of $126,198. That's not consolidation near a peak. That's a coin still digesting a real drawdown nine months later. Long-term holders have edged back into net accumulation, but only barely. On-chain estimates put that pace at 50,000 to 100,000 bitcoin, a fraction of the roughly 400,000 seen in prior upswings, and remember these are behavioral heuristics, not confirmed intent. Wallets holding 10,000 bitcoin or more remain neutral. So the accumulation is happening at the retail and mid-size level, not among the whales, and it's happening while the ETF channel, the cleanest proxy for institutional flow, is still net selling. The marginal institutional buyer of spot Bitcoin has stalled.

Which raises the obvious question: if that money isn't buying Bitcoin, where is it going? Increasingly, into the regulated dollar rails sitting underneath the whole asset class, and the freshest read on those rails just landed, with a real tension inside it.

Tether reported $1.5 billion in Q2 operating profit, driven by Treasury and repo income, a strong headline number. But the company's excess reserve buffer, the cash cushion sitting above what's needed to back USDT one-for-one, fell from $8.23 billion to $4.11 billion in a single quarter. The attestation put total assets at $187.75 billion against $183.64 billion in liabilities as of June 30th, and that $4.11 billion gap is the entire margin of safety. Stated as a share of tokens outstanding, that cushion went from 4.5 percent to 2.2 percent, according to a deep-dive follow-up on the attestation. That ratio is the sharper way to say it. The safety margin roughly halved relative to a liability base that kept growing. And Tether didn't just let the buffer shrink passively. It simultaneously grew its gold holdings to about 146 metric tons and its Bitcoin holdings to nearly 99,000 coins, the Bitcoin alone worth about $5.8 billion. That's a deliberate shift of reserve composition toward volatile, non-cash assets even as the cash cushion thins. One more detail matters for how much weight to put on any of this. These figures come from a BDO attestation, not a completed independent audit. Tether's own release says a Big Four audit process is underway, but gives no completion date, the same posture the company has held for years. So the unfinished audit is itself a data point. The market should not assume that attestation gap closes any time soon. So-what: the largest stablecoin issuer is becoming both more profitable and less cushioned at the same time, and the market is largely pricing the profit, not the cushion.

Set that against Circle, which is moving the opposite direction on transparency. Circle received final OCC approval on July 10th to open a national trust bank, a charter that restricts it to trust and custody activity. It still can't take retail deposits or make loans, but it formalizes Circle as regulated federal infrastructure rather than an offshore-style construct. Circle's USDC reserves stood at $73.15 billion against $72.95 billion in circulation, with 84 percent parked directly in Treasury bills and overnight repo through the BlackRock-managed Circle Reserve Fund. That's about as transparent as reserve backing gets in this industry. So you now have a real split inside the stablecoin complex: the chartered, disclosed issuer on one side, and the largest, least-audited issuer thinning its own margin of safety on the other, both growing.

And that growth has consequences for traditional capital markets, because stablecoin issuers are becoming genuine buyers of short-dated government paper. Circle alone holds over $60 billion in bills and repo. Add Tether's Treasury book and you have a meaningful, fast-growing bid competing with money market funds for the same short end of the curve. Banks are already warning about the other side of that coin. Standard Chartered estimates stablecoins could pull roughly $500 billion out of US bank deposits by the end of 2028, and the Federal Reserve has floated a much wider range, $65 billion to $1.26 trillion in potential lending-capacity reduction, depending on adoption. Both are model projections, not observed flows, so treat them as scenario bands, not forecasts you'd trade on. But the direction of the argument is coherent: every dollar that moves from a bank deposit into a stablecoin is a dollar that stops funding bank lending and starts funding Treasury bills instead. That's a structural reallocation of dollar liquidity, not a rounding error.

Last beat, briefly, on the regulatory clock that governs how fast all of this gets formalized. The Digital Asset Market Clarity Act has cleared the House and a Senate committee, but Majority Leader Thune has said he doesn't expect a floor vote before the recess beginning around August 7th. That pushes any legislative resolution to at least September. What's new is the fallback: the SEC and CFTC are visibly building a rulemaking alternative, branded Project Crypto, alongside a fresh CFTC proposal on conflicts of interest among derivatives-market affiliates. That's regulators signaling they no longer expect Congress to move quickly. It's worth being skeptical of how far that substitutes for statute, because the load-bearing question, whether a given token is a security or a commodity, is a legal classification that agency rulemaking alone can't settle. Legislative limbo just gets managed a little more smoothly, it doesn't get resolved.

Pull the threads together and the story of this cycle isn't really about Bitcoin's price anymore. It's about the dollar plumbing underneath it. Stablecoin issuers are becoming systemically weighty buyers of Treasury paper and, in Circle's case, regulated banking infrastructure. But the largest issuer in that complex just told you, in its own attested numbers, that it's carrying less of a cushion than it was three months ago, even as it leans harder into gold and Bitcoin. Institutionalization is arriving as both greater systemic weight and greater hidden fragility, in the same balance sheet, at the same time.
