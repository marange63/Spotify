Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Equity & the Deal Economy

Transcript · October 11, 2026

Good morning. It's Sunday, October 11th, and this is your private equity briefing. The sponsor is no longer just negotiating the price of liquidity. It's negotiating its own share of the upside, too.

Over the past week, this show has tracked a pattern of insider-priced liquidity. We've seen pre-sold exits, co-sponsor buyouts, and continuation-vehicle pricing that lands suspiciously close to the sponsor's own mark. This week's reporting adds a new wrinkle. It's not just the price. It's the carry.

Start with the lead. Bloomberg reported on October 7th that continuation vehicles are increasingly carrying what the industry calls "super carry." That's any carried-interest claim above the standard 20% of deal profit. Citing Evercore, the reporting puts numbers on the shift. By dollar volume, about 35% of continuation funds that closed in the first half of 2026 had premium economics. Compare that with 15% in Evercore's full-year 2025 survey. One caveat on that comparison: it sets a half-year figure against a full-year survey, so treat it as a direction, not a precise rate. Even so, the share has more than doubled.

PJT Partners, counting a different way, sees the same direction. It found super carry in almost a third of new single-asset continuation vehicles launched in the first half. That's nearly three times the proportion of a year earlier. The two firms measure different things, so don't splice the numbers together. But two independent counts pointing the same way is harder to dismiss.

Here's the mechanism. Three-quarters of continuation funds now use tiered carry, keyed to how well the asset performs. The reporting gives an illustrative example, not a specific deal. A 2x return might earn the sponsor a 20% profit share, and a 3x return might earn it 30%. That's a ceiling Morgan Lewis also found in a 2025 study. So the better the asset does, the bigger the slice the sponsor keeps. Put plainly, if the recovery delivers on these assets, a growing share of the gain goes to the general partner, not to the LPs who roll or the secondaries buyers who fund the vehicle.

Two named deals show how this plays out at the negotiating table. Parthenon Capital raised more than $1.7 billion for a continuation vehicle extending its ownership of Kroll Bond Rating Agency. Other investors balked at the premium terms. HarbourVest stepped in and backed the richer economics anyway. On the other side, Lightspeed Venture Partners proposed a 25% carry on a planned $600 million multi-asset continuation fund. Coller EQT, the lead investor, rejected it. One buyer bent. One didn't.

That split is the whole story, and there are two honest ways to read it. The bullish read is pay-for-performance. A sponsor that only earns the extra carry if the asset doubles is signaling real conviction. A buyer who accepts that structure is pricing that conviction, not getting rolled. The bearish read is about leverage over a captive LP base. Selling limited partners need the distribution now. Rolling LPs are negotiating with a sponsor who controls both the asset and the valuation used to price the deal. In that reading, premium economics measure bargaining power more than asset quality.

What would separate those two stories? Whether premium-carry deals clear at a different discount to net asset value than standard 20%-carry deals. Nobody has published that comparison. It's an open question, not a settled one. For now, buyer discipline is the only visible check. The pattern to watch is buyers saying no on weaker assets, the way Coller EQT did. That's what would separate vehicles that extend a winner from vehicles that hide a laggard.

There's also a composition effect to flag before anyone calls this a trend across the board. Single-asset continuation vehicles now make up more than half of all GP-led secondary volume, and they are the fastest-growing slice. Premium terms cluster in exactly that deal type. So part of the 15%-to-35% jump is mix. But PJT's count, which looks only inside single-asset vehicles, suggests terms are getting richer deal for deal as well. So mix explains some of the move, not all of it.

Who pays? Rolling LPs keep the paper upside, minus a higher carry. Selling LPs get cash now and forgo the upside. Either way, more of the eventual gain lands on the sponsor's carry line. The so-what: if you want to know who's capturing this recovery, watch net-of-carry distributions to paid-in capital, not headline continuation-vehicle volume.

The same question of who sets the terms shows up on the way in, not just the way out. Bloomberg reported Friday, in a story picked up by Reuters, that GTCR and the activist fund Elliott Investment Management are in advanced talks to take CCC Intelligent Solutions private. Nothing is signed, and no financial terms were disclosed. The report said a deal could come as soon as next week. Elliott had accumulated a large stake in CCC. The company hired Morgan Stanley to run a sale process and reached out to buyers, including private equity firms. Now the activist that built that stake is reportedly on the buying side. CCC shares rose about 7% on the report, closing at $7.02. That gives the company a market value of around $4.1 billion. That's a market value, not a deal price, and with no offer on the table there's no premium to calculate.

The obvious read is that take-private appetite is back. Resist it. This is one mid-cap deal, and it's unsigned. The sharper read, and this is our analysis, is structural. A buyer that already owns a large stake has an information and blocking edge that other bidders lack. That can discourage competing bids. It could hold the eventual premium down rather than up. That cuts against the "sponsors paying up again" narrative.

There's a callback here too. On Friday we flagged that Elliott and Siris were reportedly exploring a sale of Gigamon, nearly a decade after Elliott took it private. So in a single week, Elliott shows up on both sides of the private equity cycle. It's selling an aged holding and buying into a fresh one. The so-what: activist-seeded deals are becoming a real sourcing edge for sponsors in a thin market. But that edge can come at the expense of public minority holders, who'd benefit from a cleaner auction.

Step back to why sponsors are pushing so hard on terms in the first place. PitchBook data, reported by Fortune in late August, sizes the backlog. There's roughly $860 billion of net asset value in U.S. private equity funds that are more than seven years old. That's background, not this week's news. But it's the pressure behind every flow story this week. Continuation vehicles and super carry are clearing the best assets first, on better terms for the sponsor. The long tail of aged, harder-to-sell holdings isn't moving at all.

One more implication runs through the managers' own earnings. Carry on continuation vehicles, super carry included, is cyclical performance income. It is not fee-related earnings. Richer carry tiers make manager earnings more geared to the recovery. They don't make those earnings more durable.

So where does the cycle actually stand? Liquidity through secondaries is real, but it's selective. The economics on it are shifting toward the sponsor and away from both the LPs and the secondaries buyers who fund the vehicles. The unresolved risk is how much of this liquidity is realized, and how much is engineered.

Watch three things from here. First, whether the CCC deal gets signed, and at what premium. Second, whether anyone publishes data on how premium-carry continuation vehicles price against standard ones. Third, alternative-manager earnings in the coming weeks. Read them for realized carry against fee-related earnings, and for any sign that continuation-vehicle carry is doing the lifting.

That's the briefing. Have a great Sunday.
