Here's the thread for today. Washington's chip enforcement has finally named a Southeast Asian diversion network. But the formal listing arrived long after informal government pressure had already done the work, through Nvidia's own compliance team. And the counter-levers and hedges on both sides of this fight are still mostly paper.

Start with the chips. On Monday, the Commerce Department added Megaspeed and six affiliates to its Entity List, over suspected diversion of Nvidia AI chips to China. The action covers seven entities across Singapore, Malaysia and Thailand. Licenses to these entities will now be reviewed under a presumption of denial. In practice, that shuts future licensed sales through those names.

This isn't only press reporting. It's a dated, numbered Federal Register final rule, filed November 23rd and scheduled for publication on the 25th. One sourcing note, though. We haven't been able to read the rule's own text. So its stated basis, including which Chinese end users it cites, reaches us only through press accounts.

Where it lands matters. Megaspeed's entities sit in exactly the kind of third-country jurisdictions that Commerce's new remote-access rule targets. That rule pushes compliance onto data-center operators outside the United States. Operators already running restricted chips get 90 days to secure their licenses. On our arithmetic, counting from the rule's November 19th publication, that clock runs out around mid-February. So the diversion channel the rule was written for now has a name and a rule number attached.

Now the timeline, because it shows how the pressure actually worked. In May of 2025, Commerce asked Nvidia for its Megaspeed sales records. The next month, Nvidia put Megaspeed on its internal "no sell" list. The formal Entity List action came this week. On our arithmetic, that's about seventeen months after Nvidia's own cutoff.

So the state did move first, but informally. A records request from Commerce worked through Nvidia's compliance team long before the formal rulebook caught up. That sharpens the argument we made on Monday. The binding constraint on China's chip access runs through a vendor's legal exposure, not just through the formal rules. Megaspeed is the cleanest evidence for it so far.

It also fits Treasury's posture. Last week, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said the proper chip restrictions are in place. So this isn't a new restriction. It's enforcement of the existing ones. The action has moved from writing rules to naming names.

Now the skeptical turn, because an Entity List designation is a lagging indicator, not a cure. It stops future licensed sales to seven named entities. It recovers nothing that already moved through them. And in nothing we've seen is there a figure for how many chips, or how many dollars, passed through the Megaspeed network. The listing's strategic value depends on whether replacement shells appear in the same jurisdictions. That's the obvious next move for anyone still diverting chips, and we can't observe it yet.

The legal side is murkier still. Reporting is split on which agency leads the criminal side of this case, Justice or Commerce. No charges have been announced. So treat a criminal case against Megaspeed as unconfirmed.

Then there's the personal angle swirling around this story. The connection is narrower than the headlines suggest. CNBC reports that US investigators are probing Alice Huang, whom it describes as, quote, "a key figure in Megaspeed International." CNBC also reports that Jensen Huang's November 12th resignation as Nvidia's chief executive came, quote, "in part because of his relationship with Alice Huang." That's a personal link. Nothing we've seen ties Megaspeed's export conduct to the Nvidia board investigation that preceded Huang's resignation. Megaspeed, for its part, says it conducts, in its words, "legitimate and lawful business." Keep those threads separate.

Two second-order effects follow from the listing itself. First, Nvidia and every other chip vendor now carry entity-level enforcement risk on sales through third-country partners, on top of rule risk. Once a reseller sits under a presumption of denial, the channel through it is effectively closed. Second, the compliance cost moves onto Southeast Asian cloud and data-center operators, and onto the lenders behind them. On Sunday, our private-credit desk reported a Malaysian data-center loan that stalled mid-syndication. Nothing links that loan to Megaspeed. But it's consistent with lenders starting to price exactly this kind of risk. So here's the takeaway. Enforcement is real, but the formal step trails what informal pressure and corporate compliance had already done. And its cost is landing on the region's would-be AI hubs.

Washington's lever just moved. Beijing's is the reply. China's suspension of last year's rare-earth export controls expires in January. We've cited January 10th as that date in several briefings. That needs a correction: sources now disagree. Some point to January 10th, others to January 27th. And we haven't verified the primary Chinese notice either way.

One caution. Any link between the Megaspeed listing and Beijing's rare-earth decision is our inference about leverage. Neither government has stated it. Still, the timing is awkward for Washington. An action against Chinese chip access lands just as Beijing decides whether to keep its own materials lever suspended.

And there's an earlier test. Beijing's suspension of its gallium, germanium and antimony export ban to the United States runs out tomorrow, November 27th. As of yesterday's briefing, we had found no official notice on what happens then. Either way, these metals stay on China's dual-use control list, so exporters need a license for every overseas sale. That means a lapse would be mostly a political signal, and an extension would not mean free flow. The exposure sits in Western chip packaging, optical fiber and defense optics. Beijing's clock runs in weeks, not months.

So what is Washington building against that clock? The US and Qatar signed a critical-minerals framework on November 23rd, alongside an AI cooperation pact. That's the same day as the Megaspeed listing. A senior State Department official, Jacob Helberg, said the framework aims to secure supply chains, quote, "from mining to refining to manufacturing." That's ambitious language. Here's the catch. No financial commitments were disclosed as part of this framework.

The only quoted Qatari money anywhere near this deal is older. Qatar's sovereign wealth fund put five hundred million dollars into Ivanhoe Mines last year, for a four percent equity stake. Ivanhoe is a miner, not a refiner. And refining is where China's chokepoint actually sits. So the framework talks about refining, but the only money on the table bought mining equity. It follows earlier Gulf arrangements of the same kind. That's the gap between symbolism and capacity, in one deal.

The market read is our inference. Ex-China rare-earth names like MP Materials and USA Rare Earth still trade on Beijing's January decision. Their premium doesn't rest on Gulf frameworks with no disclosed capital and no refining timeline. A framework is not a refinery. Whatever capacity Gulf money eventually funds will arrive in years. Beijing's decision arrives in weeks.

Underneath both of those supply chains sits the power bill. Yesterday we cited Goldman Sachs's estimate that up to 52 gigawatts of additional US data-center capacity could come online through 2028. A share of demand growth is load that's already arriving, not a pipeline of announcements. That makes it the most physical trend in today's briefing.
