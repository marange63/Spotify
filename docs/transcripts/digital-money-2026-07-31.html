Digital Money, Crypto, and Market Structure

Transcript · July 31, 2026

Good morning. It's Friday, July 31st, and this is your digital money briefing.

Yesterday's Fed backdrop hasn't changed. The committee is still holding at 3.50 to 3.75 percent, and it got there on a nine-to-three vote, with three governors dissenting in favor of a hike. That higher-for-longer posture is the macro current running under everything today. We led on it yesterday, so I won't relitigate it. But keep it in mind, because it's the reason the rest of today's story hangs together. The development that actually moved is Coinbase, because the numbers we've been previewing all week finally landed.

Start with the headline Coinbase itself wants you to hear. Crypto trading volume market share hit an all-time high of 10.3 percent in the second quarter, up from the first. Subscription and services revenue hit a record 555 million dollars, 48 percent of net revenue. And average USDC held in Coinbase products reached an all-time high of 20 billion dollars. That's the diversification thesis, in the company's own numbers. Three record lines that have almost nothing to do with how many Bitcoin changed hands on the platform.

Now the other side of that same report. Total revenue came in at 1.2 billion dollars, missing the roughly 1.35 billion dollar consensus, and Coinbase posted a net loss with earnings per share of negative 1.36 dollars against an expected negative 1 cent. That's not a rounding-error miss. The Street was looking for roughly breakeven, and the company printed a dollar-thirty-six loss per share. Bitcoin-related transactions are now just 12 percent of total revenue, down from more than half historically. The stock fell after hours on the print.

Here's what closes the gap on that loss, and it's the piece we didn't have yesterday. Follow-up reporting attributes the loss mainly to a 209.5 million dollar non-cash markdown on crypto assets Coinbase holds on its own balance sheet. Those are its own tokens, revalued lower as prices fell during the quarter. Add a 52.4 million dollar one-time restructuring charge tied to layoffs, plus 238 million dollars of stock-based compensation. Strip those out and adjusted EBITDA stayed positive, around 208 million dollars, Coinbase's fourteenth straight quarter above zero. So the headline loss is mostly an accounting markdown on Coinbase's own crypto holdings, not the operating business collapsing. That single non-cash mark is roughly 58 percent of the reported loss.

But don't let that excuse the trading side entirely. Transaction revenue still fell 21 percent quarter over quarter, to 599 million dollars. So the core fee engine genuinely shrank. Both things are true at once: the GAAP loss is inflated by non-cash items, and the trading business underneath it contracted hard. And that restructuring charge tells you something the adjustments can't paper over. A company confident that trading volume was about to rebound doesn't take a one-time headcount charge in the same quarter. Coinbase is resizing for a lower-volume world, not betting on an imminent snap-back.

And remember the context. Industry-wide crypto market cap fell 11 percent quarter over quarter, and spot volumes fell 25 percent. So Coinbase's record 10.3 percent share is a bigger slice of a shrinking pie, a better cut of less. That reframes the record. Winning share in a contracting market is real operational progress, but it does not, by itself, grow the dollars. The market's verdict was to sell the stock anyway, which tells you investors haven't yet accepted a valuation re-rate away from pure trading-volume beta, even with the float and subscription records in hand. And here is the mechanism that makes that re-rate debate matter. Coinbase is Circle's largest distribution partner, and it shares in the yield earned on that 20 billion dollar USDC float. The higher the Fed holds, the more that idle float earns. So a growing slice of Coinbase's economics now tracks the reserve interest rate, not trading commissions, which is exactly the kind of recurring, rate-sensitive revenue that should command a different multiple than transaction fees. The stock drop says the market isn't ready to pay for that yet.

That same shrinkage shows up in the wrapper that was supposed to institutionalize this asset class in the first place. Spot Bitcoin ETFs have pulled in just 205 million dollars in net inflows for all of July, the lowest monthly total on record. And that is after May saw 2.43 billion dollars of outflows and June saw 4.52 billion. Yes, July 30th itself looked strong: 233 million dollars in a single day, with BlackRock's IBIT pulling in 183.4 million, its best day since July 6th. But one strong day is doing the narrative work for an otherwise record-weak month. The marginal institutional buyer through the ETF wrapper has essentially stalled.

That doesn't mean nobody is buying. It means the buyer changed. Even as ETF demand flatlined, long-term holders kept accumulating. The cohort that has held for more than 155 days hit a fresh all-time high on July 21st, at 16.64 million Bitcoin, about 83 percent of supply. So the bid didn't vanish; it moved from ETF allocators, who show up in the flow data, to on-chain holders, who don't. That's a less liquid, less visible, and less price-supportive bid than the one the 2024 ETF launch was supposed to install. And worth noting, Ether ETFs actually out-drew Bitcoin funds relative to their size, taking in 342.85 million dollars in July. So the rotation isn't just out of crypto. It's partly within it.

So where are the idle institutional dollars actually going instead of the spot Bitcoin bid? Increasingly, into on-chain Treasury and money-market products that pay a competitive yield without leaving the ecosystem. BlackRock's BUIDL fund added 436 million dollars on Avalanche in a single week in mid-July, pushing its total assets under management across all networks to roughly 2.87 billion dollars. That's a small number next to the ETF or stablecoin complex, and it comes via a secondary aggregator, not a BlackRock filing, so treat it as directional. But it's the concrete instrument behind an argument that's been building all week. With the Fed leaning higher for longer, a dollar sitting idle can now earn a real yield on-chain instead of waiting on Bitcoin to appreciate. That's a quantifiable opportunity cost, not an abstraction, and it's the same dynamic sitting underneath Coinbase's own 20 billion dollar USDC float.

You can see the pull in the stablecoin data, too. On Ethereum, both of the big dollar tokens shrank through early July. USDT supply on the chain fell to 78.43 billion dollars, down about 2.2 percent over thirty days, and USDC fell to 46.55 billion, down about 4.1 percent. Some of that is genuine redemption. Some is migration to lower-fee chains, and that piece is analyst inference rather than confirmed on-chain data, so hold it loosely. But the direction fits the theme. Dollars in this system are increasingly parked where they earn, in tokenized Treasuries and yield-bearing reserves, not sitting still as non-yielding crypto exposure.

Two threads to carry out of today. Institutionalization is real, but it has migrated out of spot Bitcoin exposure and into custody, stablecoin float, and subscription revenue, visible both at Coinbase and in the ETF flow data. And the on-chain risk-free rate is quietly becoming the competing asset that spot Bitcoin has to beat. Watch whether the market starts paying Coinbase for its float the way it pays a payments company for recurring revenue, because that re-rate, if it comes, is the real institutionalization trade.
