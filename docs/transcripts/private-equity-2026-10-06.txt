Good morning, and welcome to Private Equity and the Deal Economy for Tuesday, October 6th, 2026.

The thesis today is that the private equity liquidity problem is moving. It started in the exit window. It is now showing up in the wealth channel, where the promise of semi-liquid access meets a distribution drought.

Start with Partners Group. According to Bloomberg on October 2nd, the firm is restructuring its Global Value SICAV, the evergreen private equity fund that capped redemptions in June. Trade coverage of that report puts the fund's latest net asset value at six point six billion euros. That makes it a flagship, not a side vehicle.

Here is what the reporting says. The fund would become an umbrella with two portfolios. One is a distributing fund, which holds older assets and sells them to meet redemption requests. The other is a compounding fund, which concentrates on new investments and targets higher returns. Investors could keep their exposure, switch from the distributing fund into the compounding fund, or redeem from one or both. All of it is subject to shareholder approval, so this is a plan, not a completed change. And we are working from trade coverage of Bloomberg's report, not a fund notice from Partners Group itself.

The background is simple. In the second quarter, net redemption requests exceeded the fund's limit of five percent of NAV per quarter. Partners Group's own first-half release shows the wider pressure. Redemptions totaled three point eight billion dollars, and seventy-nine percent came from three mature evergreen strategies. The firm said its evergreen platform could slow overall net AUM growth by one to two percent of AUM in the second half of 2026, with a similar drag in 2027. Press coverage of those results also reported that the firm expects current redemption trends to continue for several quarters.

Now, do not read a gate as a failure. A quarterly cap is the vehicle working as designed. It protects the holders who stay from a fire sale. The fair critique is narrower. These products were sold as semi-liquid. The gate shows how conditional that liquidity was.

The new structure supports two readings, and the disclosed details do not let us choose between them. The first is orderly segregation. Holders who want out sit in one sleeve while holders who want to stay are not forced to fund the exit of others. The second is ring-fencing. The legacy book is parked in a distributing fund that has to sell into whatever market exists, while fresh money goes into a clean sleeve. Both readings fit the language. What would separate them is how the distributing fund's sale prices compare with its carrying values.

Here is the main point, as our analysis. A gated fund's NAV is a paper number until redemptions actually clear at it. Every quarter that requests exceed the cap, the NAV goes untested. The distributing fund, by design, now has to test it through asset sales. One more inference. Holders can switch from the distributing fund into the compounding fund without redeeming. That could make headline outflows look smaller even as the legacy book shrinks. So watch the size of the distributing fund, not just the redemption figure.

One more piece of context. Over the past week, the private credit desk on this show has tracked redemption queues at Blue Owl and Ares. The same capped-queue mechanics have now reached an equity-side evergreen fund. So the semi-liquid stress is no longer a credit-only story. I will leave the credit mechanics to that briefing.

So where does an evergreen fund find cash it cannot get from exits? One answer is the secondaries market. Evercore reported in July that global secondary volume exceeded one hundred twenty billion dollars through June, a record. That shows the valve exists. If evergreen redemptions force holders or funds to sell stakes, that supply lands here.

A record volume, though, says nothing about the price at which the valve clears. It could reflect healthy demand. It could equally reflect distress selling and exits engineered by insiders. We do not have current secondaries pricing relative to NAV, so I will not guess at a discount. The open question is whether evergreen-driven supply pushes that discount wider. If it does, the marks on every sibling fund get a free, uncomfortable cross-check.

The reason the valve is under strain sits upstream. On October 3rd, Bloomberg Opinion reported that buyout executives are beginning to concede that returns will fall short of what was promised on many existing funds. The concern is especially about those that deployed between 2017 and 2021. A column on September 30th went further and called it the end of the leveraged buyout era.

Treat that with care. It is opinion writing, and the executives are not named. So this is a reported shift in tone, not evidence of realized returns. But it connects to something this show reported on October 2nd. Warburg Pincus chief executive Jeffrey Perlman said software businesses cannot be sold easily. On our read, the vintages now being conceded are the same ones that bought software at peak multiples.

The so-what is that a concession is not a markdown. The test is whether marks or carry guidance actually move at third-quarter earnings.

Now, the software problem may be narrower than Perlman's line suggests. On October 5th, Schneider Electric agreed to buy PTC for about twenty-two point six billion dollars. That is a strategic buyer writing a large check for software. But PTC's software is wired into physical manufacturing and industrial workflows.

PTC is a public company, and no sponsor is involved as reported. It is a signed agreement, not a closed deal. So it is evidence about the strategic bid, not about private equity exits. Our inference is that the bid follows proximity to the physical economy, not a blanket appetite for software. If so, the stuck inventory is more likely horizontal SaaS bought at 2021 multiples. That means the logjam is probably narrower than the word software suggests.

If old buyouts cannot be sold, the biggest managers have another option. They can put money where returns do not depend on exits, which is the plumbing. The Wall Street Journal reported on October 5th that KKR struck a deal to buy Gen II, a private-capital fund administrator, valuing it at roughly five billion dollars including debt. That is secondhand reporting, and KKR has not announced it, so treat it as reported. And five billion including debt is an enterprise value, not the equity check.

There are two readings, both our analysis. The first is a picks-and-shovels bet. Evergreen and wealth vehicles multiply the operating burden. They need frequent NAVs, more investor accounts, and more reporting. On that reading, the recurring-revenue services business underneath all of that is a better place for capital than a 2021-style buyout that struggles to clear return hurdles. The second reading is simpler. KKR is putting capital to work in a sector it knows from the inside.

One distinction matters here. Administrators calculate and report NAVs. They do not set the marks. The GP and its valuation process do. So the deal does not hand KKR control over anyone's valuations. But if KKR's competitors are Gen II clients, then the administrator's independence becomes a diligence question for limited partners. That is conditional, because we do not know the client list.

The pattern also fits last week's story of Bain Capital bidding for Koch's data-center business. Sponsor capital keeps rotating toward picks-and-shovels assets rather than toward clearing legacy inventory. Fee streams and infrastructure-like assets grow, while realized returns on older vintages lag.

So where does the cycle stand? This week brought no fresh evidence that sponsor exits are clearing. The only large software sale was a strategic buying a public company. The stress is now surfacing in retail-facing vehicles. And managers are leaning on the fee lines that do not depend on selling anything.

The biggest unresolved risk is retail-facing NAVs that have not been tested by actual exits. If more flagship evergreen funds follow Partners Group into restructuring after gates, wealth-channel inflows become a less certain substitute for institutional re-ups. That matters most for managers whose AUM growth story now leans on retail.

Three things would confirm or refute that read. First, Partners Group's shareholder vote and the next redemption window, which will show how the umbrella's terms play out. Second, the alternative managers' third-quarter prints over the coming weeks, with Carlyle confirmed for Thursday, November 5th. The test there is carry realizations versus fee-related earnings. Fee-related earnings should hold up. Carry is what the conceded vintages threaten. Third, any fresh data on secondaries pricing. If the discount to NAV widens as evergreen supply arrives, the marks on sibling funds get tested from outside.

That is the state of the machine this morning. Thanks for listening, and we will be back tomorrow.
