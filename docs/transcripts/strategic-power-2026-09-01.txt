Good morning. It's September first, and for a week this beat has located the constraint on AI power in one place: the supply side. Turbine order books that can't clear. Siemens quoting lead times of three years or more. Transformers and switchgear in short supply. A pipeline of announced gigawatts converting into actual construction at something like a nine percent rate. Today the constraint flips sides. A state has stopped taking the demand.

On August third, Texas Governor Greg Abbott issued a directive — not an executive order, a directive — to the Public Utility Commission of Texas and to ERCOT. It orders a pause on new data center interconnection approvals pending a verification and audit. The headline number attached to this all month has been ERCOT's interconnection queue: roughly four hundred seventy-four gigawatts of requests, more than five times the state's record peak demand, with about ninety percent of the new requests coming from data centers. The directive's own text narrows that considerably. It applies to all data centers in the ERCOT queue, regardless of type or size. But there are two carve-outs. The first is projects in the roughly fifty Texas counties outside ERCOT's footprint. The second is projects that are purely behind-the-meter — generating their own power, never touching ERCOT's wires. The population actually being audited is closer to two hundred fifty to three hundred projects of seventy-five megawatts or larger, the ones already furthest along in the process. Together they account for about two hundred gigawatts of future demand. That's twice ERCOT's peak-demand record, not five times it. So the real target here is the most advanced, most credible slice of the queue, not the speculative pile the headline figure implies.

There's no published end date. The directive runs until the audit is, in its own words, fully completed. ERCOT's internal target is a December tenth filing to the commission, but that is ERCOT's own planning goal, not a deadline written into the governor's order. And seventeen large loads, mostly data centers, cleared a stability assessment before the pause and are still scheduled to come online later this year. Those total about six point six gigawatts. So this isn't a freeze. It's an open-ended audit with no legal trigger to end it — the cheapest form of state action available. Authority held, not spent.

The Energy Information Administration didn't wait to find out how it resolves. In its August outlook, it cut its Texas 2027 load-growth forecast from fourteen percent down to six. That is the first time on this beat that an official AI-power demand forecast has moved down instead of up. But hold it carefully, because a forecast cut is not a load cut. Texas electricity demand hasn't fallen. An expectation did. And since the audited population is a fraction of the full queue, some of what the EIA marked down may be phantom load that was never going to be built anyway, pause or no pause.

Here's why this becomes durable rather than a one-state story: cost. PJM, the mid-Atlantic grid operator, has already put a number on what data center load does to power markets. Its independent market monitor found that data center growth drove twenty-nine point four billion dollars of added capacity-market revenue across the last four auctions. And PJM's capacity price has gone from about twenty-nine dollars per megawatt-day two delivery years ago to over three hundred twenty-nine dollars today. That is more than an eleven-fold jump, and a price cap is holding that second number down.

Keep the caveat this beat has carried twice already: assigning cost to one customer class is a monitor's attribution, not a metered bill, and no counterfactual auction exists to check it against. But it is an allocable number, and a state legislature or utility commission can act on one of those. That's the mechanism by which the Texas model spreads. And it operates at the state level rather than at the federal regulator, which is exactly where this beat argued last week the fight would land. So here is the trade. Value accrues to generation that's already interconnected, and to behind-the-meter supply that never has to ask a queue's permission. That is the same destination turbine scarcity and transformer scarcity pointed to earlier this week — three different routes, one conclusion. And Texas has now written that second half into the directive itself, as an explicit exemption.

Now, from a state rationing a queue, to a state having capacity removed by force. Ukraine's drone campaign against Russian oil refineries escalated again in August. At least twenty-one strikes since the start of the month, the most of any month since the war began. Russian refining is running somewhere around four million barrels a day, on estimates that vary meaningfully by source, against five to five and a half in a normal year. The new fact this week is domestic. Russian petrol production has fallen to about eighty thousand tonnes a day, against roughly one hundred fifteen thousand tonnes of normal summer consumption. That's a thirty percent shortfall. Lipetsk and Orenburg have introduced rationing — odd-even sale days and thirty-liter caps per vehicle — and authorities in Sochi have asked residents to limit driving because stations are running dry. One sourcing note: those petrol and rationing figures come from Ukrainian outlets, an interested party, so treat the magnitude as directional.

Keep the limit in view as well. There is no confirmed figure showing this campaign has cost Russia meaningful export revenue, because crude that can't be refined can often still be exported raw. So the honest claim is narrower: this is hitting Russian consumers and the domestic political cost of the war, not demonstrably the war chest. Compare that with the paper instrument meant to do the same job. The Senate's Russia sanctions bill, with its authority for tariffs up to one hundred percent on Russia's top oil and gas buyers, passed in early August and has sat all month in a House on recess. Twenty-one strikes moved a fuel market. A finished bill hasn't moved at all. And the transmission channel worth watching isn't Brent crude. It's diesel and the other middle distillates, because any product Russia diverts to cover its own shortage takes barrels out of global export markets without a single new sanction.

Back up the stack. If the escape route from a blocked interconnection queue is your own generation, and eventually your own reactor, the next bottleneck is fuel. Uranium spot broke out of a five-month range between eighty-four and eighty-seven dollars a pound, hitting ninety dollars on August twenty-eighth. Before calling that a structural deficit, note that Kazatomprom, the world's largest producer, is raising 2026 output by about nine percent. That's a real supply response, and spot uranium is a thin market where one print is not a verdict. The Department of Energy has also committed two point seven billion dollars over the next decade to expand domestic enrichment, alongside a stated goal of quadrupling US nuclear capacity by 2050. Two tests there. The funding mechanism behind that number hasn't been confirmed by a primary release. And a 2050 target is a policy goal, not a count of reactors under construction.

Every item so far has been a state converting a hard asset into leverage — a grid, a refinery, a fuel cycle. The last one is about who is quietly buying the asset states hold for when leverage fails. On gold, the new fact is the World Gold Council's quarterly number. Central banks bought a net two hundred eighty-eight point nine tonnes in the second quarter, up sixty-two percent year over year, and the strongest second quarter on record. They accumulated that while prices were softer. This beat concluded on Sunday that the debasement trade was pure positioning riding rate expectations. That was too dismissive. A buyer that keeps accumulating through a price decline isn't chasing momentum; it's a structural reserve bid. Don't overturn the whole call, though. A quarterly aggregate doesn't explain August's ten-percent-plus rally, which was a faster and more speculative move. And it says nothing about bitcoin, for which there's no comparable fresh figure to cite. Reserve managers and speculators are two different bids in the same asset, and only one of them just posted a record quarter.

Watch two things into September: whether any other state copies the Texas audit model, and whether Texas ever publishes an actual standard for resuming approvals. That's the briefing. Have a good day.
