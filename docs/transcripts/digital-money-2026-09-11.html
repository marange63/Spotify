Cautious Optimism Briefings

Digital Money, Crypto, and Market Structure

Transcript · September 11, 2026

Good morning. It's Friday, September 11th, and this is your digital money briefing.

On this show yesterday, we said a Bitcoin futures book that had already deleveraged would grind rather than gap into next week's binary calendar. Within hours, that call got its first live test.

The catalyst was macro, not crypto. August producer prices rose four tenths of a percent month over month, firmer than the street looked for, and long-end yields pushed higher on the print. Bitcoin rejected resistance just under seventy-eight and a half thousand dollars and traded down to roughly seventy-six and a half, a 5.75% decline on the week. That is a real move driven by a real data release. It is worth separating from the noise. This was not a crowded-trade blowup. It was a rates shock landing on a crypto market.

Here is how the futures side graded. Liquidations across the whole move totaled about seventy-six million dollars, ninety-three percent of them longs, with the largest single forced close near thirty-eight million. For scale, that is a fraction of the three hundred and sixty-eight million dollar cascade this show covered on September 2nd, when the book was still levered. Open interest fell about two percent, to roughly fifty-three billion dollars, while funding stayed only mildly positive. Put those together and the futures book absorbed the shock instead of cascading through it. That is the forecast holding.

But hold the self-congratulation. A single mid-tier data release is a weak test of a book's resilience. It is not the gap risk we were actually hedging against. And mild funding is exactly the condition that lets net-long positioning rebuild cheaply between now and Wednesday, which would restore the very gap risk this result appears to have retired. I'll also flag, as we have before, that the open-interest and funding figures trace to an automated data aggregator rather than a named provider like CoinGlass or Coin Metrics. We went looking for a cleaner source today and came up empty. Treat the direction as reliable and the decimals as soft.

The futures book held. Spot ETF demand did not. Farside-sourced flow data now covers the entire selloff window, and it shows US spot Bitcoin ETFs bleeding money through all of it. Forty-seven million dollars out on September 8th, the figure we flagged here yesterday. Then a hundred and twenty million out on the 9th. Then two hundred and eighty-three million on the 10th, the day of the producer-price print itself. That is a cumulative three-day redemption of roughly four hundred and fifty million dollars, led on the worst day by ARKB, Grayscale's GBTC and Fidelity's FBTC.

So the two halves of this market told different stories. The regulated futures wrapper stayed calm. The regulated spot-ETF wrapper withdrew in size, and the outflows accelerated as the shock landed rather than fading with it. That argues flows amplified this macro move rather than cushioned it, and it complicates any clean "the book absorbed it" narrative. One part of the institutional plumbing took the hit. The other transmitted it.

There is a portfolio consequence here that matters more than the tape. If the inflation calendar is setting Bitcoin's price, then any allocator still modelling this asset as equity beta is mismarking the hedge. The practical version of that is a sizing problem. A macro or basis book carrying Bitcoin into Wednesday's Fed decision needs to size it against real yields, not against the Nasdaq, because real yields are what moved it this week. Get that wrong and the hedge sleeve is correlated to the thing it was bought to offset.

Which is also why the digital-gold read keeps failing. This is now its third live failure inside two weeks, alongside the payrolls beat and the Middle East escalation we logged. The ninety-day correlation to gold was reported near point eight six in early September, a six-year high, but that is a secondary report with no published methodology attached. As Bloomberg's Eric Balchunas has pointed out, Bitcoin's own correlation to equities has always sat near point four. It is gold and Treasuries whose behavior shifted, not Bitcoin's. Analysts quoted alongside that reading also flagged that sudden decorrelations tend to be short-lived rather than structural regime changes, and today's tape agrees with them.

If macro is setting the price, Washington is setting the perimeter, and that's the other half of this week. The Senate's Digital Asset Market Clarity Act faces a possible cloture vote as soon as Tuesday, September 15th. That vote has not happened. Treat it as pending, and remember cloture only opens debate — it is not enactment. The prediction market on passage has already faded to roughly one in five, so the fade itself is not news.

What is new since we last covered this bill is the CFTC. Chairman Selig has now said on the record that if the legislation keeps stalling, his agency will use its existing authority to start building a crypto market-structure regime on its own. We inferred exactly that outcome on this show on Monday, without a named source. The agency's own chairman has now confirmed it.

Don't read that as a safety net. A perimeter built by agency rulemaking is narrower than statute. It is slower to implement than a headline suggests. And it is contestable in court, which is precisely the reversibility risk we flagged when we made the original inference. Nothing in the current reporting tells us the scope of the authority Selig is invoking, or any timeline for using it. So this is a direction, not a plan. What it buys the sector, in practical terms, is legal probability where it wanted legal certainty. That distinction is what keeps a bank's product committee cautious about custody, listing and the higher-risk categories waiting on this bill, because a rule written without a floor vote can be unwritten without one too.

While Congress stalls, balance sheets are not waiting. We've already covered the twenty-one-bank consortium — Goldman, Citi, Bank of America and others — building toward a 2027 dollar stablecoin. The new piece is a parallel effort. Bankers' associations across thirty-nine states have formed the BankChain Alliance, representing more than three thousand two hundred banks, also targeting a 2027 launch of deposit tokens and stablecoin issuance. The number attached to it is twenty-one point eight trillion dollars, and it's worth saying plainly what that is and isn't. That is the member banks' combined balance-sheet assets. It is not committed capital, and it is not a stablecoin issuance figure. What's actually new is the shape: two bank coalitions, one built around money-center banks and one around community banks, now aimed at the same calendar year. Neither has disclosed reserve composition, chain of issuance or governance, and nothing is live.

Here's the pool they're launching into. Tether's USDT and Circle's USDC together hold two hundred and fifty-eight billion dollars of stablecoin supply, which leaves under fifty-three billion for every other issuer combined. The newest entrant with real traction is World Liberty Financial's USD1, at four point three billion, still ahead of PayPal's token below three billion. And the aggregate pool has been roughly flat. So what two bank coalitions are chasing is not a growing market. This is a fight over redistribution, not adoption.

That has a specific consequence for the incumbents. A bank token competes first in institutional settlement, and that is the segment that throws off the least float yield per dollar of volume. So Circle and Tether lose share before they lose revenue, and the first thing that actually moves is price. Against a flat pool, the near-term effect of two coalitions announcing for 2027 is fee compression across every existing issuer, well before any new entrant clears meaningful volume.

Zoom out and the shape of the week is consistent across all three stories. Price is being set by the inflation and rates calendar rather than by crypto-native positioning. The rulebook is being drafted by regulators rather than by Congress. And the settlement rails are being built by bank balance sheets against a dollar pool that isn't actually expanding. None of that resolves today. Consumer prices print this morning, cloture comes as soon as Tuesday, and the Fed decides Wednesday. Three live catalysts, none of them decided yet.

That's the briefing. Have a good weekend.
