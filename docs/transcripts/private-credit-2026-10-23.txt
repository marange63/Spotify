Good morning, and welcome to your private credit and corporate credit briefing for Friday, October 23rd, 2026.

All week we have pointed at the late-October alternative manager earnings as the catalyst that would test the private credit story. The first one landed yesterday. Blackstone reported, and the print says private credit is now a two-track machine. Institutional and insurance money keeps pouring in. Net returns stay thin. And that thin net return is exactly what keeps the retail exit queue full.

Start with the scale. Blackstone's own earnings release, filed yesterday with the SEC, shows credit and insurance inflows of 35.8 billion dollars in the quarter. Reporting from Alternative Credit Investor puts the trailing twelve-month figure at 142.8 billion, and puts the credit and insurance franchise at 480 billion dollars of assets. The same reporting says Blackstone deployed 13.4 billion in the quarter.

Look at that gap between inflows and deployment. Roughly 35.8 billion came in, and 13.4 billion went out the door. That is a distribution story. It tells you Blackstone can raise money almost on demand. It tells you very little about whether the loans being made are good ones.

Now the number that matters more. The release says private credit returned 2.6 percent gross in the quarter, and 1.8 percent net. On the call, CFO Michael Chae specified that this line covers Blackstone's non-investment-grade private strategies. Liquid credit did a little better, and barely anything leaked out between its gross and net figures. The private book is different. The fee wedge takes far more out of it.

The trailing twelve months make the point harder. Per Alternative Credit Investor, private credit returned 6.7 percent gross over the last year, and 3.9 percent net. So about 2.8 points of the gross return disappears before it reaches the end investor.

I would be careful here. The release does not decompose that wedge, so we cannot say how much is fees and how much is loss drag or unrealized markdowns. And this is a strategy-level figure. It is not what any one BDC shareholder earned.

But the direction is clear. A net 3.9 percent on an illiquid, model-marked, gated product is thin compensation. The Fed hiked 25 basis points in mid-September. Cash alternatives now pay a real income stream with daily liquidity. The so-what is simple. The reported yield on the asset class and the return the allocator keeps are two very different numbers, and the gap is where the marketing lives.

That brings us to who is being asked to hold the illiquidity. Blackstone's flagship retail vehicle, BCRED, is the cleanest test. On the call, Jon Gray said repurchase requests were 10 percent of net asset value. The quarterly limit is 5 percent. He attributed much of the excess to carryover from the second quarter.

Blackstone fulfilled about half of the requests. Gray put BCRED's net outflows at 1.4 billion dollars for the quarter, against gross sales of nearly 750 million. I am taking these from a machine-transcribed copy of the call, so treat them as the call's language rather than filing data.

Those two figures together say the flagship retail franchise is shrinking in net terms. It is shrinking in the very quarter the institutional side gathered tens of billions.

The carryover matters too. Part of that 10 percent is the same investors re-tendering after being prorated last quarter. So the ratio overstates fresh demand to exit. But it confirms the queue is not clearing, and a queue that does not clear is a persistent overhang on the vehicle.

What the call did not give us is just as important. We found no BCRED non-accrual rate. We found no payment-in-kind share. And we found no confirmation of the Blackstone Secured Lending dividend reset that we flagged earlier this week. That fund has not reported its own third quarter yet. Until it does, treat any reset as unconfirmed, not as fact.

Next, where is the marginal dollar going? Alternative Credit Investor reports that Blackstone's inflows included 11.9 billion dollars for global direct lending. It puts infrastructure and asset-based credit at about 18 billion. So the largest single bucket of fresh money is not corporate direct lending.

That fits what we said in mid-October about too much capital chasing too few good corporate loans. When the corporate book is crowded, capital migrates to asset-backed structures and to insurance balance sheets. That is rational. But it does not retire risk. It moves it onto annuity-funded balance sheets, where a downturn would surface later, more quietly, and in a different regulatory setting. Watch where the next stress shows up, not just whether it does.

Now to the retail counterweight, which you have heard before. Blue Owl's two largest non-traded BDCs drew an estimated 4.2 billion dollars of third-quarter repurchase requests. That is down from 4.7 billion in the second quarter and 5.4 billion in the first. Both funds capped tenders at 5 percent again.

I would read that decline carefully. It is real, and three quarters running. But unfilled requests do not carry over, so holders must re-tender each quarter. Pro rata gating can wear down the request count without restoring any confidence. And the picture differs by fund. The larger vehicle, OCIC, saw requests ease to 16.8 percent of shares. The tech-focused OTIC held near 39 percent, with only about 13 percent of requests filled.

That OTIC number links the retail exit queue to the software loan market. We covered the distressed loan tape on Tuesday. Leveraged loans trading at or below 80 cents totaled 139.8 billion dollars, per JPMorgan, with technology the largest concentration at 39 percent. Software is also the biggest sector in the BDC books. So the question for the coming BDC reports is whether private marks on those same borrowers are catching down to where the public loans trade.

The default picture is why the marks question matters. Fitch's private credit default rate hit a record 6.3 percent for the twelve months through August. That measure counts interest deferrals and extensions. Narrower trackers and BDC non-accrual rates do not. So the environment is not benign. It is flattered by amend-and-extend, and the stress shows up in the definitions before it shows up in the losses.

One brief item gives the other side of the machine. U.S. CLO issuance slipped to 41.1 billion dollars in September, the weakest month since April. But refinancings overtook resets for the first time since April. The reason is tighter AAA spreads. Managers are lowering their liability costs, not creating much new risk. So the securitization plumbing is working, and it is a tailwind to manager economics. But one month of refi dominance is not a cycle signal.

Pull it together. Where does the cycle stand? This looks like a grind in net returns, not a default turn. Gross yields are high. Net returns are thin. Distressed pricing is concentrated in software and tech. And the retail channel is paying for the liquidity promise made at the top.

The biggest unresolved risk is the liquidity mismatch in retail vehicles, sitting on top of marks that no real downturn has tested. Capital gathering by institutions does not cure that. It only means the institutions can wait for better terms while retail investors cannot get out.

The catalysts are close. Ares Capital reports on October 27th, and its non-accruals and PIK share will say whether the Blackstone read generalizes. The Fed meets October 28th. Ares Management reports on the 29th. Apollo discloses asset-level marks on the 30th, and reports earnings November 3rd. If marks on software borrowers hold while public loans trade at distressed levels, the lag is the story. If they drop together, the cycle has turned.
