Cautious Optimism Briefings

Strategic Power, Energy, and Institutional Change

Transcript · August 31, 2026

Good morning. It's Monday, August 31st, and this is your strategic power briefing.

Start with a reversal. On the metric of gas-fired power capacity under development, the United States has passed China, and data centers are why. The Global Energy Monitor's August report puts it plainly. Gas capacity in development in the US rose fifty percent in the first half of this year, from 252 gigawatts to 378. GEM's comparator is explicit too. The US now has twice as much gas-fired capacity in development as China, whose own pipeline sits at 24 gigawatts.

Narrow that to the slice tied directly to data centers and it very nearly doubled in six months, from 97 gigawatts at the end of last year to 189 gigawatts by midyear. That is the number the AI-needs-power thesis has been waiting for. It is also the number to be most careful with.

Before you take it as a verdict, adjudicate it. This beat has spent the past week at the bottom rung of a ladder. The ladder runs from announcement, to permit, to financial close, to construction start, to interconnection, to an actual operating asset. On Friday it was Pentagon critical-minerals money that turned out to be conditional commitments nobody had closed. On Saturday it was a reactor construction contract with a 2032 delivery date and no site yet selected. Apply the same test to a much bigger number.

GEM's own figures do that work for you. Capacity that has actually moved into construction rose seventy-six percent in the first half, but only to 52 gigawatts in total. Of that, just under 17 gigawatts is tied to data centers. Set that against the 189-gigawatt data-center pipeline and the conversion rate from announcement to construction is roughly nine percent. GEM adds that more than three-quarters of the global pipeline is still in early-stage development. So the honest read of 189 gigawatts is a demand signal from developers filing interconnection requests. It is not 189 gigawatts that anyone is building.

The geography sharpens the point. Texas alone accounts for 122 gigawatts of the US pipeline, and 77 gigawatts of that is tied to data centers. All of it sits inside ERCOT, a grid with no capacity market and its own political economy. Concentrate the AI power buildout that heavily in one grid and you concentrate two things there at once. Price formation is the first. The ratepayer backlash is the second, and it lands at a state legislature rather than a federal regulator. So the political risk to this buildout is state-level, and it is not diversified.

The more revealing number is what developers are doing instead of waiting. Engine capacity in the US development pipeline more than doubled in six months, from 31 gigawatts to 67. These are reciprocating engines, not turbines. Developers do not switch prime movers for fun. They switch when the queue for the machine they actually want runs past their need-by date. Engines are less efficient and harder to site at scale. Choosing them anyway is the market telling you plainly what it cannot get.

The queue is long for a structural reason. Wood Mackenzie's release states that global turbine orders stood at 110 gigawatts at the end of last year, against global manufacturing capacity of only 60 to 70 gigawatts a year. An order book that far ahead of annual capacity does not clear in a year even if nobody places another order. That is why Wood Mackenzie projects turbine prices near 600 dollars a kilowatt by the end of 2027, close to triple where they sat in 2019. Note the tense on that one. It is a projection for 2027, not a price anyone is paying today.

Siemens Energy's own disclosure fits the same story from the supply side. Its gas services backlog reached 69 gigawatts after its most recent quarter, and the company says lead times across the business now run three years or more. So the bottleneck is not political will, and it is not capital. It is a manufacturing base that cannot convert orders into turbines fast enough, and will not for several years.

Sit with what that does to asset values, because this is where the gigawatt totals stop being trivia. If new capacity is rationed by a factory floor rather than by money, then value accrues to whatever generation is already interconnected today. An existing gas plant with a firm interconnection is worth more than a permit for a better plant that shows up in 2030. The turbine makers capture the scarcity rent directly, through multi-year pricing power on equipment and on the service contracts that follow it. And for the hyperscalers, behind-the-meter gas stops being a hedge against a slow interconnection queue and becomes the base case. That is now the fourth unrelated constraint this week to point at the same trade, which is what makes it structural rather than a week's headline.

Now hold the other side of the ledger to the same standard. China's answer to the same demand for power runs on a different clock and a different technology. On July 31st, China's State Council approved construction of eight new reactors, four of them using the new Hualong One 2.0 design for the first time. That is a month-old approval, not fresh news, and it deserves the identical skepticism. A State Council approval is also an announcement. None of those eight units has concrete poured, and Hualong One 2.0 is an unbuilt design.

So the honest contrast between Washington and Beijing is not that one system builds while the other only talks. Both are being judged today on paper. What separates them over a decade is conversion rate, and neither this week's gigawatt totals nor a summer approval tells you that.

The same test, announced versus real, carries from the energy layer to the compute layer. There the constraint has changed hands entirely. Power is something Washington can at least throw money and permits at. Chips were supposed to be the lever it controlled outright, through export licensing.

Bloomberg's reporting now puts a number behind what this beat could previously only argue directionally. Nvidia's licensed H200 sales into China came in at under one percent of its data-center revenue for the quarter ended July 26th, well short of what the US license permits. Before you read that as Beijing rationing American chips, name the duller alternatives. Chinese buyers may simply find H200 economics unattractive once the tariff attached to those licensed sales is priced in. Or Nvidia may be steering scarce supply toward higher-margin Western customers. Neither explanation is excluded by the reporting, and no Chinese official has confirmed a motive. The gatekeeping read is inference, not testimony.

It is still the most plausible inference, taken alongside Beijing's push behind its domestic champions. Washington wrote the license. Beijing decides how much of it clears customs. If that is right, two things follow. Any sell-side model that sizes Nvidia's China revenue off what Washington permits is mis-specified, because the throughput variable sits in Beijing rather than in Commerce. And the residual beneficiary of a legal channel throttled at the Chinese end is domestic Chinese silicon, not Nvidia. One quarter's revenue share is not a trend, so treat this as the first hard data point on that thesis rather than a run-rate.

Put the two layers together and the pattern this beat has been circling all week comes into focus. On energy and on compute alike, the binding constraint has stopped being policy authority and become physical or administrative gatekeeping. A turbine factory that cannot build fast enough. A customs process in Beijing that will not clear what is already legally allowed. Neither yields to another announcement.

Two things to watch from here. Whether US gas construction starts begin closing the gap to that 189-gigawatt pipeline, or whether the nine percent conversion rate holds through the rest of the year. And whether Beijing's clearance for licensed chips loosens or tightens, because that, and not the size of the license, is what will show up in Nvidia's China line next quarter.

That's the briefing for August 31st. Have a good day.
