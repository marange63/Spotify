Good morning, it's August 17th, and this is your orbital economy briefing.

The Starlink IPO question is finally answered, and the answer complicates the thesis everyone had settled into. SpaceX didn't spin Starlink out — it took the whole company public in June, and this month it filed its first quarterly results as a public company. Total revenue rose 92 percent year over year to $7.814 billion. But look at where the profit and the capital actually sit. The Space segment — that's launch plus Starship — brought in $962 million in revenue, up 29 percent year over year, and it posted an operating loss of $542 million, with R&D costs for Starship still climbing. Connectivity, meaning Starlink, was the only segment to generate an operating profit at all, at $1.66 billion for the quarter. AI — cloud infrastructure and Grok subscriptions — grew revenue 247 percent to $2.561 billion, now bigger than the entire Space segment. And capital allocation tells the same story even louder. Total capex jumped to $18.369 billion for the quarter, up from $2.825 billion a year earlier, and AI infrastructure absorbed $15.828 billion of that — roughly 86 percent. Starship development and launch infrastructure got $1.174 billion. So the old assumption — that Starlink cash bankrolls Starship and eventually Mars — needs revising. It's now Starlink profit and AI-driven capital raising funding a Starship program that is itself losing money. That makes SpaceX's own capital allocation, not NASA or the FAA, the more plausible binding constraint on Starship's cadence and on Artemis.

There's a second-order read-through here too. Starlink subscribers doubled year over year to 12 million, but ARPU held at $66 a month, down from $85 a year ago, as growth skews toward lower-priced international tiers. That's a falling monetization floor that Amazon's Kuiper and AST SpaceMobile now have to price against. And there's a structural consequence to SpaceX being public at all. Every quarter now, it hands competitors, the defense primes, and the Pentagon a real cost-and-margin benchmark for space-based services that used to be entirely private. Kuiper's economics, a Starshield contract price, a OneWeb margin — all of them now get judged against numbers SpaceX is obliged to disclose. The market, meanwhile, isn't simply celebrating the growth. SpaceX shares only closed back above their $135 IPO price on August 10th for the first time since mid-July, after the earnings beat. Investors are re-pricing a company whose money is flowing into compute, not rockets.

That capital story lines up with what's actually happening on the pad. Starship's Flight 13 flew July 24th, and it inverted the usual pattern. The upper-stage ship splashed down in the Indian Ocean and, for the first time, stayed intact after tipping over — good enough for a drone to inspect the heat shield and engines afterward. But the Super Heavy booster's landing burn only relit 10 of 13 engines, and by splashdown sources describe a badly degraded burn and the loss of the booster in a hard landing. Exact engine-failure counts differ across reporting, but the direction is consistent: booster reuse, the piece of the stack that actually compresses cost per kilogram, regressed on this flight. Put that next to the earnings — Starship got roughly one-thirteenth the capital AI did this quarter — and the engineering result looks less like bad luck and more like a program that isn't being resourced as the priority. And that has a downstream cost. The orbital refueling demonstrations Artemis needs from Starship sit behind a reliable booster, so every degraded landing burn keeps NASA's lunar timeline exposed to a cadence SpaceX itself isn't funding as job one.

Now the same gap between an announced number and a harder one shows up at Rocket Lab. Second-quarter revenue hit a record $234 million, up 62 percent year over year, and backlog reached a record $2.36 billion, up 137 percent. Space Systems — satellite manufacturing, riding the Space Development Agency's Tranche 2 and 3 ramp plus the newly closed Mynaric and Motiv deals — grew 93.6 percent year over year to $189.5 million and now dwarfs Launch Services, which actually fell 30 percent quarter over quarter to $44.6 million despite a similar launch count. So Rocket Lab is quietly becoming a satellite manufacturer first and a launch provider second. And yet the stock fell on this print, because Neutron — the medium-lift rocket carrying Rocket Lab's valuation multiple — is looking shakier. Management still guides to a fourth-quarter 2026 debut, and stage-one tank production is aligned with that target, but the company itself says the window is narrowing, and prediction-market traders on Kalshi are pricing just a 14 percent probability of a pre-2027 launch. That's market sentiment, not company guidance, but it's the market telling you it doesn't believe the date. Real revenue, real backlog, real manufacturing ramp — sitting next to an unflown vehicle the multiple depends on. And the timing cuts against them. A Neutron slip into 2027 delays Rocket Lab's entry into the medium-lift national-security and commercial market at exactly the moment SpaceX's public disclosure is exposing how much Falcon 9 pricing pressure is coming.

The same pattern shows up in national-security space, with the direction reversed. The Space Development Agency just awarded roughly $1.75 billion combined to Sierra Space and L3Harris for 36 more Golden Dome missile-tracking and missile-defense satellites, 18 each, all targeted for launch readiness by the end of 2028. Days later, at the Space and Missile Defense Symposium, the program's chief warned that Golden Dome could be forced to halt development as early as October without additional congressional funding — reported, not confirmed by a direct quote, so treat it as a warning rather than settled fact. That $1.75 billion award itself appears to be separately funded and isn't described as at risk. But it's a reminder that in this sector the usual bottleneck is manufacturing capacity, and here, for once, the constraint might be Congress instead. Contractors who've already tooled up production lines for Golden Dome tranches are exposed to a working-capital mismatch if appropriations lag the award announcements. For a prime, that turns a headline win into a financing question: you carry the tooling and the labor now against dollars Congress may not release until later.

Pull the threads together and the theme across all three stories is the same: value keeps accruing to whoever can actually fly, recognize revenue, or get funded — not to whoever has the biggest announced number. SpaceX's launch and Starship business is unprofitable even as AI absorbs the capital. Rocket Lab's real financials beat while its vehicle date slips against the market's own odds. Golden Dome's award is real while its funding runway is in question. None of these are stories of a sector cooling off — launch cadence, satellite manufacturing, and missile-defense demand are all still climbing. The point is narrower and more useful: this cycle, the reward is moving from the company with the boldest announced number to the one that can actually convert it into a flight, a recognized dollar, or an appropriated one. Watch three things from here: whether SpaceX's next quarter shows any capital rotating back toward Starship, whether Rocket Lab locks a firm Neutron pad date, and whether Congress funds Golden Dome before that October deadline.
