Good morning, and welcome to your semiconductors and compute briefing for September 20th, 2026.

This week three different blocs ran into the same wall, and it is not a design wall. Huawei announced a system it concedes it cannot yet build in volume. ASML's ceiling in 2028 is reportedly how fast it can assemble tools. And Washington is now trying to regulate where the AI rack itself gets assembled. The spine of today's briefing is that announced capability is outrunning buildable capability.

Start with Huawei Connect, held September 17th and 18th. Huawei said its next accelerator, the Ascend 960DT, arrives in the first quarter of 2027. That is three quarters earlier than its own roadmap said. The chip carries up to 288 gigabytes of memory and four petaflops of FP4.

The system built around it is the Atlas 960E SuperPoD. It holds 4,096 NPUs and delivers 8 exaflops at FP8, linked by Huawei's UnifiedBus interconnect with near-package optics. Huawei claims interconnect latency falls from seven microseconds to two. One sourcing caution up front. Outlets are inconsistent on the product names, so I am using The Next Web's, and every specification here is Huawei's own claim relayed by secondary reporting, not measured by anyone else.

Now the part that matters. The Next Web puts the 960DT at roughly a third of Nvidia's B300 in FP4, and roughly half in FP8. The FP4 ratio holds up against Nvidia's own published board-level figure, though sparsity assumptions on both sides are unclear. The FP8 ratio is The Next Web's statement alone. So treat the per-chip gap as real but approximate.

Huawei is not trying to close that gap with transistors. It is competing on topology. An export-control regime that caps the quality of the silicon does not cap how many chips you wire together, or how well you wire them. So system scale, optics and interconnect are the rational axis to compete on. Huawei's chairman, Eric Xu, defended the strategy in nearly those terms. His quoted line was that now Huawei can make its own chips, and even if they are inferior, at least it is going down the path of solving those challenges.

But the cost of that strategy is easy to miss. Scaling by interconnect multiplies the number of parts you must actually fabricate. A weaker chip in a bigger pod means more wafers, more memory stacks and more optics per unit of delivered compute.

That is where the announcement and the reality diverge. The pod announced this week holds 4,096 NPUs. Huawei's own release from Huawei Connect a year ago described an Atlas 960 SuperPoD of 15,488. Analyst Rui Ma told The Next Web that the chip is coming much earlier but the SuperPoD is much smaller than what Huawei originally laid out. Be careful with that comparison. The two products carry different names, and nothing I have seen says whether the 4,096 configuration replaces the larger one or sits beside it as a nearer-term, smaller product.

The million-processor claim deserves the same discipline. That figure was already in Huawei's 2025 release, attached to a SuperCluster of multiple pods. It is a year-old roadmap number, observers call it theoretical rather than proven, and no deployment has been disclosed in the twelve months since.

The Next Web also reports that Xu acknowledged Huawei cannot make enough of these chips, and put the moment China catches up with its own hardware demand at 2030. Both are the outlet's paraphrase of his remarks to reporters, not a transcript. They still matter, because they amend something this show argued yesterday.

Yesterday this briefing said the binding constraint on Chinese AI silicon was memory access rather than SMIC wafer capacity. The reported concession points instead at production volume. I would not swap one for the other. Carry both. Nothing is established about the SMIC node, the source of Huawei's high-bandwidth memory, or any volume. What would separate them is a disclosed shipment count together with a named memory supplier.

The so-what is that Chinese compute deployment is a manufacturing forecast, not a demand forecast. Chinese hyperscalers are choosing between licensed but unshipped Nvidia parts and supply-limited domestic ones. Demand there is constrained by supply, not by economics.

The same throughput ceiling shows up one layer up the stack, on the other side of the export-control line. JPMorgan analysts said on Monday, after meeting ASML's chief financial officer, that ASML is looking at ways to produce more than 110 EUV tools in 2028. That is an analyst read-through of a management meeting. ASML has not published the number, the sources conflict on the 2027 baseline, and the mix between low-NA and High-NA tools is not specified.

The analysts characterize the constraint as assembly speed rather than the supply chain, and that framing is their paraphrase too. Management indicated demand remains very, very strong, and most new orders are reportedly for 2028 delivery. That speaks to a gap this beat flagged on the 17th, when Intel and ASML's High-NA milestone arrived with no tool count at all. It is a partial answer at best. This is a production plan for EUV in total, not an installed High-NA count, and it is still a sell-side note rather than a company disclosure.

Now the skeptical turn. A three-year order queue is ambiguous evidence. It fits structural demand. It fits just as well with slot-hoarding in a shortage, where buyers book capacity they may not need because the queue itself is the scarce asset. And a slot is cancellable in a way a wafer is not. The evidence cannot separate the two readings.

What it does support is entrenchment. A queue that long favors whoever already has tools installed, meaning TSMC, Samsung, Micron and SK hynix. It raises the effective barrier for Intel's 14A and for any Chinese entrant, whatever either announces.

If assembly is the constraint, then where you are allowed to assemble becomes a competitive variable. That brings us to Mexico. The Wall Street Journal reported on the 17th that the United States has proposed capping the share of components sourced from outside North America in AI hardware built in Mexico, as part of the USMCA revision talks. I am working from secondary outlets relaying that story rather than the Journal itself. Mexico has pushed back, arguing a US content requirement could effectively neutralize the agreement.

Be clear on where this stands. The talks are early. The United States has not presented a threshold, and there is no timeline. The near-term probability-weighted effect is close to zero, and the market will likely treat it as a headline. One aggregator reports Mexico's AI-server exports at 83 billion dollars in the first half, up more than 170 percent from a year earlier. That is a single secondary source, but the scale is why the proposal gets attention.

The Journal named Foxconn, Nvidia's largest rack assembler, as exposed. The significance is the direction rather than the odds. Origin scrutiny is migrating from final assembly down into sub-components: boards, power, optics and memory. That is the change that would reprice the contract-manufacturer rack model. Wistron, Quanta and Foxconn would face a bill-of-materials re-sourcing exercise, and the bill lands on hyperscaler capex per delivered gigawatt, not on any chip price. Note which inputs those are. Optics now sits alongside memory and packaging as something both blocs are trying to secure at once, and nobody pre-builds capacity for it.

The same system logic sits behind reports from Nikkei that Japan and the United States held working-level talks on a US logic chip plant operated by GlobalFoundries, as part of a tariff-linked investment package. Neither the companies nor the governments have confirmed it, and I would not put a dollar figure on it. Read it as another sign that trade deals and fab siting are now one negotiation.

One last beat closes the loop between capital and capacity. Enflame, the Tencent-backed Chinese AI chip designer, listed on Shanghai's STAR Market this month and raised about 912 million dollars. Debut pops in that market measure administrative pricing and a scarce supply of listed alternatives, not competitive position. Money for Chinese AI silicon is being raised faster than domestic fabs can turn it into parts. That is the same concession Huawei's chairman is reported to have made, expressed as a share price.

So here is the thesis in one sentence. Capital is not the constraint anywhere in this system, and design is not either. The constraint is the physical throughput of building things, and every roadmap slide overstates it.

Two tests for the coming weeks. First, whether Huawei or any customer discloses an actual Ascend 960 shipment or deployment count. Second, whether ASML itself puts a 2028 unit figure on the record, instead of leaving it to a sell-side meeting note.

That is your briefing for September 20th. Thanks for listening.
