Good morning, it's August 10th, and this is Capital Markets Cross-Asset Radar.

Let's start with the live wire. The Strait of Hormuz standoff has re-escalated since we last covered it here on the 7th, when the framing was a deal in principle inching toward reopening. That framing broke this weekend. Iran's Foreign Minister, Abbas Araghchi, ruled out direct talks with the United States on August 9th, saying negotiations can't resume while American violations of the June memorandum of understanding continue, and that any exchange of messages is now running through intermediary countries rather than direct channels. On the same day, Iranian-backed Houthi rebels claimed responsibility for striking Saudi Aramco's Jizan refinery. Saudi authorities confirmed a fire, quickly extinguished, no injuries reported — but note that's a Houthi claim of responsibility, not an independently confirmed attribution, and this is at least the second incident at that facility in a month. Brent crude settled above $83 a barrel on August 9th as traders weighed those two developments against each other. To see how twitchy this premium is, go back to August 6th: Iran published a more restrictive draft shipping-lane plan and Brent jumped 3.8% in a single session, to $82.49. Meanwhile, transit through the strait itself remains severely constrained — roughly 8 vessels a day as of an August 5th count, versus something like 130 a day before the conflict began. That figure is five days old and hasn't been refreshed for today, so treat it as the last confirmed read, not a live tape.

On the diplomatic track, Araghchi describes the parallel Iran-Oman pact on shipping-lane coordinates as "very close" to finalization — but he's been explicit that even a finished Oman pact would not by itself reopen the strait, since Iran's list of conditions includes a region-wide ceasefire, removal of the US naval blockade, war-damage compensation, and sanctions relief. None of that has moved. The one force arguably pulling toward a deal is Iran's own economy. Trump has said the US is "low-keying" the talks, pointing to Iran's strain — by his citation of Iran's central bank, inflation running near 77% year over year, though that figure reaches us secondhand through his remarks, not a direct Tehran release. So what you have is a two-sided risk premium, not a resolution in either direction: escalatory signals and near-done diplomacy sitting side by side, with the strait carrying roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil sitting underneath equity markets at record highs that are not pricing in a supply shock.

Here's why this reaches beyond the oil tape. The Fed held rates on July 29th, but the vote was split, with three members dissenting toward a hike and the statement citing energy among the supply shocks keeping inflation above target. A persistent Hormuz premium keeps exactly that energy-inflation risk live, which cuts directly against the benign-disinflation read the rest of the complex has been pricing off one soft jobs number. And there's a quieter cost underneath the headline barrel: war-risk insurance and reinsurance on Gulf shipping stays elevated as long as this drags, a hidden tax on regional trade flows that sits independent of where crude actually prints. So the supply shock is doing work on the inflation side even on days when the oil screen looks calm.

That same look-through pattern shows up on the other side of the commodity complex, in copper, though the mechanism there is regulatory rather than geopolitical. COMEX copper hit a record $6.703 a pound back on August 5th — that print is stale news at this point, but the policy story underneath it isn't. The Commerce Department's June 30th deadline to recommend Section 232 tariffs on refined copper imports — proposed at 15% starting in 2027, rising to 30% in 2028 — has now blown past by more than a month with no announcement and no public explanation for the delay. In the vacuum, US buyers have been front-running the decision: July imports came in around 200,000 tonnes, the largest monthly volume in shipping data going back to 2014. That's a stockpile being built against a policy outcome nobody has confirmed, which is precisely the setup for an asymmetric move — if the tariff disappoints or gets shelved indefinitely, that hoarded inventory has to clear somewhere, and price downside is the release valve. So oil and copper are running the same basic pattern from opposite directions: one carries upside supply risk from geopolitics, the other carries downside risk from a stalled policy decision, and equities are looking through both.

Now let's pivot from external supply risk to the internal credit question this show has been sitting with all week. On Sunday we flagged Blue Owl's June 30th non-accrual jump — from 2.0% to 2.8% of the portfolio at cost — and asked the obvious question: is that idiosyncratic, or the first domino in private credit? The first real answers arrived this week from the two other major listed BDCs. Ares Capital reported July 29th with non-accruals at amortized cost rising to $708 million, or 2.4% of the portfolio, up from 2.0% a year earlier — a modest move, and one that reporting frames as below Ares's own historical norm, though I'll flag that comparison isn't independently verified with a hard quote, so take it as directional. Core earnings per share held steady at 47 cents, though net asset value per share slipped to $19.35 from $19.59 the prior quarter. Blackstone's BXSL told a calmer story still: no new non-accrual assets added in the quarter at all, and its non-accrual rate actually fell, to 1.8% at fair value and 3.6% at cost, down from 3.1% and 4.7% in the first quarter, as two assets came off the list after restructuring. Net investment income came in at $174 million, 75 cents a share, a net-investment-income return on equity of 11.4% annualized. Put those two prints next to Blue Owl's jump, and the read tilts toward idiosyncratic rather than sector-wide — this tempers the private-credit-crack thesis without resolving it. Goldman Sachs BDC hasn't reported yet, and neither Ares nor Blackstone breaks out which sectors are actually driving even their modest moves, so call this provisional.

Before we leave credit, bank the nuance, because it matters. Net asset value fell at both Ares and Blackstone's BXSL — Ares to $19.35, BXSL down 2.8% to $25.53 — even as non-accruals held steady or actually improved. That's marks eroding for reasons other than realized default risk: portfolio marking, spread widening on existing positions, something in that direction. It keeps alive the tension between mark-to-model private valuations and the reality a continuously priced market would show, a thread worth tracking independently of whether defaults themselves show up.

So here's the cross-asset synthesis for today. This week, one external tail risk escalated — Hormuz — and one internal tail risk calmed down — the Blue Owl non-accrual scare. And a complex that's been pricing benign outcomes largely off one soft jobs report is repricing neither of them. Equities are sitting at records, high-yield spreads remain near their tightest levels of the cycle, and none of that reflects either a live supply-shock premium in oil or a private-credit story that, while calmer, is still only one BDC away from being unsettled again. The tells worth watching from here aren't the rate path — they're whether the Hormuz premium holds or the Oman pact actually defuses it, and whether Goldman Sachs BDC's numbers, when they land, confirm the calm that Ares and Blackstone just showed.
