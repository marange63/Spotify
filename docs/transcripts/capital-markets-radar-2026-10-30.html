Capital Markets Cross-Asset Radar

Transcript · October 30, 2026

Good morning. It's Friday, October 30th, and this is Capital Markets Radar.

Here's the thesis. Amazon's print confirmed that the hyperscaler cash squeeze is an industry story, not an Alphabet story. The market is now charging the sponsors for it. But it is sorting them by who delivered earnings, not by who spends the most. And outside that leadership group, the rest of the tape is refusing to confirm a broader risk-off.

Start with the catalyst we flagged yesterday. We said Amazon's print would tell us whether the AI cash squeeze belonged to Alphabet alone or to the whole group. Amazon answered. Its own earnings release shows trailing twelve-month free cash flow swinging to an outflow of 42.4 billion dollars. A year earlier, that same line was a 14.8 billion dollar inflow. The operating business is not the problem. Operating cash flow rose 29 percent over the same period. Capital spending simply grew faster.

And the spending isn't slowing. Amazon reaffirmed full-year capex at roughly 220 billion dollars. CEO Andy Jassy went further, saying the company expects to spend "meaningfully more" in 2027. That puts Amazon alongside Alphabet, whose free cash flow turned negative in the quarter, as we covered yesterday. It also puts Amazon alongside Meta, which posted negative free cash flow of its own. So three of the four big spenders are now showing negative free cash flow on at least one measure. Microsoft is the exception, and we'll come back to how it's paying.

Here's the part that matters for the stock. Amazon missed anyway. It earned 1.89 dollars a share, against the 1.96 that analysts polled by LSEG expected. Revenue also came in a hair light.

That miss landed despite AWS revenue growing 39 percent year over year. Amazon's stock fell 4.25 percent in the regular session on Thursday, then roughly another 6 percent after hours, per Investing.com. So demand for the product is not what's being questioned. A cloud business growing that fast, paired with a falling stock, means the market is pricing capital intensity and the length of the payback.

Now widen the lens to the whole group over two days, because the dispersion is the real signal. Start with Alphabet. Its stock fell about 11 percent in Thursday morning trading, per 24/7 Wall Street. That's an intraday figure, not a verified close. Capital spending jumped to 51.3 billion dollars, from 44.9 billion the quarter before. And headline earnings of 2.12 dollars a share looked like a large miss.

But Alphabet's own filing softens that picture. An 8.7 billion dollar loss on equity securities cut diluted earnings by 55 cents a share. That's a mark-to-market hit, not an operating one. Strip it out, and earnings were closer to 2.67 dollars. Secondary sources, including MarketBeat and Investing.com, put consensus near 3.02. So Alphabet still missed on an operating basis, by roughly 35 cents. That's a real shortfall, but it's well under half the headline gap. If analysts separate the mark from the operating result in the coming days, Thursday's reaction will look harsher than the fundamentals justify.

Now contrast Microsoft. It beat, with adjusted earnings of 4.89 dollars a share against a 4.72 consensus. It also guided capex above 50 billion dollars next quarter. And its stock edged higher rather than lower.

Meta fits the same pattern. Per the same 24/7 Wall Street roundup, Meta guided this year's capex to 135 to 145 billion dollars while posting negative free cash flow. Its shares edged higher too.

Put it together, and the split in this earnings cycle tracks earnings delivery, not capex restraint. Microsoft guided spending higher, beat, and was rewarded. Alphabet and Amazon missed, and were punished, capex and all. So the market isn't rejecting AI capex yet. It's refusing to pay for capex that arrives alongside an earnings miss. That's a narrower and more reversible signal than an AI capex top. It's the template to apply the next time one of these names reports.

That brings us back to the financing ladder this show has been climbing all week. We went from neocloud bonds, to private lenders, to a lab's public filing to fund its losses, to a chip vendor backstopping its customer's lenders. Today the sponsors' own shareholders are absorbing the cost directly. And there's now direct evidence that the gap is reaching debt markets as well.

Per Bloomberg, Microsoft priced a 25 billion dollar senior unsecured bond sale on Thursday. It's Microsoft's first U.S. high-grade sale since 2017, and it drew more than four times demand. The proceeds are earmarked for capex, buybacks, and general corporate needs. That matters, because Microsoft is the name the market didn't punish this week.

There are two ways to read the Microsoft deal. The benign read is balance-sheet efficiency. A cash-rich issuer that is also buying back stock can tap an order book that deep, and the use of proceeds is mixed. The less benign read is that even the disciplined hyperscaler is choosing not to fund this build purely from operations. We lean toward the second, and that's our inference, not a reported fact. The timing lines up with Microsoft guiding capex higher again next quarter. Either way, the credit market just showed it will fund the buildout at scale. The so-what: investment-grade supply is where the next rung of this financing story is most likely to show up.

The read-through for suppliers runs the other way. Nobody in this group is cutting. Amazon is spending more in 2027, Microsoft more next quarter, and Meta is holding its guided range. So the revenue line for chips, power, and data-center real assets stays intact, even as the sponsors' own equity gets repriced. Yesterday we said the spenders' cash was funding the winners' rally. Now the spenders' shareholders are the ones paying for it. That's a potential decoupling worth watching. Sentiment toward the sponsors' stocks can move one way, while the supplier and real-asset trade moves the other.

Now to the rate those long-payback cash flows are discounted at. The ten-year Treasury yield closed at 5.045 percent on Wednesday. That was the day the Fed held, and two voters, Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack, dissented in favor of a hike, as we covered yesterday. The ten-year eased to 4.989 percent on Thursday, the same day Alphabet sold off. We're not claiming the tech selloff moved the bond market. A move of about six basis points is noise. The honest read is that duration didn't sell off further on the hawkish dissent. Nor did it rally hard on the mega-cap drawdown. It's still pinned near the 5 percent line this desk has tracked all month. The so-what: a higher-for-longer discount rate and a longer capex payback compound against each other, and that's the backdrop for every earnings reaction this week.

Step outside mega-cap, and the alarm isn't ringing. CNBC had the VIX at 17.20 on Thursday, up less than 2 percent on the day. That's a modest tick on a day when one of the index's largest weights fell double digits. It's also below where volatility sat the week before the Fed and earnings double catalyst. On Tuesday, this desk argued that hedges bought into that double catalyst would run off unused if it passed without broad damage. It passed with single-name damage only. Those hedges look to have run off rather than paid out.

There are two readings of that calm. Either the market correctly sees this as single-name dispersion, not systemic risk. Or index volatility is mispriced, and cheap relative to what just happened in two mega-cap names. The risk sits with the second reading. If either the capex story or the hike path extends, the hedge that just ran off gets re-bought at a higher price.

Crypto tells a similar non-confirmation story. Spot Bitcoin ETFs took in 17 million dollars on Thursday, per KuCoin's flow report, a fourth straight day of inflows. But that's noise against a 30-day net outflow of about 255 million dollars, per crypto.news. Spot Ethereum ETFs lost 105 million the same day, extending the outflows we noted yesterday. Call it stabilization without conviction. Bitcoin isn't catching a flight-to-safety bid off the equity wobble, and it isn't being dumped either. The so-what: the speculative bid is waiting, not leading.

So three things to watch, none of them settled yet. First, whether Alphabet or Amazon follow Microsoft into the bond market to fund next year's capex. Second, whether index volatility finally reprices if the earnings damage spreads beyond these two names. Third, whether Bitcoin ETF inflows grow into something more than noise, in either direction. And the October payrolls report isn't due until next Friday, so don't anchor to any number for it yet.

That's the radar for October 30th. Trade carefully, and I'll see you tomorrow.
