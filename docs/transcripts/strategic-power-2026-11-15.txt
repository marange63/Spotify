Good morning. It's Sunday, November 15th, 2026, and this is Strategic Power. Today's through-line: the bill for capacity the West hasn't built is landing before the capacity does.

Start with the grid. PJM's Independent Market Monitor issued its annual report on November 12th. It found that capacity costs rose more than 80 percent in the first nine months of this year, compared with the same stretch last year. That isn't a projection. It's the Monitor's measure of costs already incurred.

On Tuesday, this desk asked whether the Senate permitting bill reaches PJM's capacity charges, since its FERC rewrite targets transmission pricing. The Monitor's numbers say that cost line is still climbing. On Saturday, we said forecast data-center load keeps getting cancelled or delayed. Here's the twist. Discounting the forecast has not discounted the bill.

The next test is PJM's regular annual capacity auction, covering 2029 and 2030 delivery. PJM says it still plans to hold it in December. The Monitor expects it to clear at the maximum price again.

For comparison, the last auction cleared at the ceiling of $325 per megawatt-day for a third straight year. That ceiling comes from Pennsylvania's settlement with PJM, and it has been extended to cover December's auction too. The Monitor estimates that auction adds five to ten billion dollars in data-center costs to electricity bills. That's a forecast for an auction that hasn't run, not a realized cost.

Now a distinction the headlines blur. December's auction isn't the only proceeding in play. PJM also designed a separate, one-time Reliability Backstop Procurement, meant to buy incremental capacity tied to large data-center load. In late September, FERC accepted most of that plan but suspended it until February 28th, 2027. FERC questioned how PJM planned to divide the costs among customers. PJM says it has no revised dates for that procurement yet.

So here's the asymmetry. The proceeding aimed squarely at data-center load is frozen until next year. The proceeding that prices 2029 and 2030 capacity for every customer in the footprint goes ahead this December. Ratepayers are prepaying, years ahead, for load whose forecasts keep shrinking. And the mechanism meant to settle who pays for that load is on ice.

Layer the institutional fight on top. The Monitor calls the current path "not an efficient or competitive market solution." PJM's response is that it "recognizes the impacts of rising wholesale costs on regular people and businesses." That's sympathy, not a claim of authority to reallocate anything. And the backstop's own design points to the states. Per Utility Dive, it relies on each PJM state to define which retail loads carry the costs. So the Monitor objects, PJM sympathizes, FERC has paused, and the states hold the allocation pen. Our read is that no institution is clearly on the hook to change the bill before December.

Two complications keep this honest. First, clearing at the cap is a scarcity signal. It doesn't by itself prove that data centers are the whole cause. Could slow new supply and plant retirements be doing part of the work, more than the Monitor's attribution allows? Second, the cap cuts both ways. It protects households from shock bills. But it also mutes the price signal that's supposed to call forth new generation. So the fix that protects ratepayers today may lengthen the shortage underneath it. That's our inference, not anyone's finding.

For markets, capped clearing gives independent power producers revenue visibility, but at a ceiling rather than an open scarcity price. Utilities in PJM states carry the political risk of retail bills rising into that auction.

And when the grid bills you at the cap and makes you wait, you buy your own power.

That's what Anthropic just did. First, the standing disclosure: Anthropic makes the model behind this show. The Financial Times, as relayed by Fortune and Bloomberg, reports that Anthropic agreed to pay $3.4 billion for 1.7 gigawatts of heavy-duty natural-gas generators. Anthropic itself hadn't confirmed the deal as of our research. Anthropic took over generator commitments that a San Antonio supplier, OnePWR Solutions, had already placed with Caterpillar.

Twice this week, on Tuesday and again on Friday, this desk inferred that data-center developers would route around the grid with their own gas, rather than build less. Here is a frontier lab writing the check.

Keep the caveat in view, though. The generators are expected to become available within the next two years. That's equipment, not operating plant. No site, air permit, gas supply contract, or interconnection has been reported.

The sharper read is what Anthropic actually bought. It's less a block of megawatts than a place in Caterpillar's manufacturing queue. In a capacity-constrained buildout, equipment slots are becoming assets in their own right. A lab with capital decided that slot was worth taking ahead of everyone else.

Now scale it. Anthropic's IPO filing discloses about $900.9 billion in aggregate committed computing and infrastructure spending. By this desk's arithmetic, the generator deal is roughly four-tenths of one percent of that. Power equipment is a rounding error in the budget. Yet it gates everything the rest of that budget buys.

There's an angle here for IPO investors too. The same filing shows $70.7 billion due in the twelve months after September 30th. Non-core power hardware is now part of what a model lab is committing to buy. That invites a fair question about whether frontier labs are becoming partial utilities.

The supply-side effect is our inference. The higher and more certain the grid's capacity bill, the more balance-sheet buyers will purchase generation outright. That hands Caterpillar and its peers pricing power. When the grid can't move fast enough, the deepest pockets buy around it, and the line gets longer for everyone behind them.

Power, at least, is something cash can buy its way around. Magnets are the input where cash doesn't buy capacity yet.

On Wednesday, this desk said Beijing's rare-earth leash is temporary, but the collar is permanent. Now the collar has a price. The truce extension has been the headline all week. But the licenses governing dysprosium, terbium, and yttrium were never suspended. And the Platts benchmark for dysprosium oxide is up 242 percent since its launch at the end of March, to $4,100 a kilogram.

One caveat. That benchmark is brand new, so part of the move may be price discovery in a thin market, not pure scarcity. Still, the direction is the point. The truce headline didn't touch the elements that matter most to Western magnet and defense supply chains.

Here's the less obvious read, and it's our inference. That ex-China premium is two things at once. It's the cost of dependence for magnet buyers. It's also the margin a non-Chinese refiner would earn. So the truce didn't erase the business case for Western refining. The risk is that Beijing can collapse that premium whenever it chooses.

Now the American side, where the problem is a capacity wall. Fortune, relaying Arthur D. Little data via Reuters, reports that U.S. demand for the most common rare-earth magnet type reached about 48,000 metric tons last year. Domestic sources supplied just 300 tons of that.

U.S. capacity is expected to reach about 5,000 tons by the end of this year. That's roughly a tenth of last year's demand. And it's capacity, not production. The real output benchmark is still that 300-ton figure.

That gap collides with a deadline. Starting January 1st, 2027, defense contractors are supposed to stop buying rare earths, magnets, and tungsten from China, Russia, Iran, and North Korea. Washington has repeatedly granted waivers because domestic supply falls short. At a tenth of demand, our read is that another waiver is the likely outcome. If so, the Pentagon's new FORTRESS America stockpile push is symbolism running ahead of state capacity, at least for now.

Note the calendar, too. Washington's deadline lands nine days before January 10th, when, per the reporting, Beijing's suspension of its wider control package is due to lapse. For Western refiners and magnet makers, the persistent premium supports their economics, truce or no truce. Defense primes and EV-motor makers keep paying it, against a sourcing deadline domestic capacity can't meet. The premium is the market's running price for refining the West hasn't built, and Washington's own deadline will probably blink first.

A short watch list. First, PJM's December capacity auction, expected at the cap. Second, the Fed's December 9th decision, with futures pricing an 89 percent chance of another quarter-point hike.

Then the January calendar: the defense sourcing deadline on the 1st, and Beijing's suspended package due back in play on the 10th.

Each is a bill arriving before the capacity that's supposed to cover it.

That's Strategic Power for November 15th. Stay cautiously optimistic.
