Good morning, it's Saturday, November 7th, 2026, and this is Strategic Power.

This week markets priced words faster than facts. An Iran peace that nobody has signed stripped away much of oil's war premium in five sessions. Meanwhile, Ukraine was physically knocking out Russian refining capacity. And the one piece of language that actually changes market structure barely registered. Treasury quietly dropped two words from its auction guidance.

Start with oil, because that's where the gap between price and reality is starkest. Brent closed the week at $98.32 a barrel, down more than 16 percent, according to CNBC. Oilprice.com attributes the slide to "expectations of US-Iran diplomatic progress." Rewind to Monday. On November 2nd, this desk said Washington's next move on Iran was being sequenced to the election calendar. Military options were on the table, and so was a quiet back channel through Qatari mediators. Five sessions later, the market has decided the move is a deal. Nobody has signed one.

Look at what the mediator itself is saying. Qatar describes both sides as "committed to mediation" and "reviewing proposals." Reviewing proposals is not a ceasefire. The most direct statement this week came from Tehran. Mohsen Rezaei, who heads Iran's Supreme National Security Council, spoke during a visit to Russia on November 5th. His words: "As long as they do not execute our seven conditions, we will neither open the Strait of Hormuz nor have negotiations." That's a senior Iranian official ruling out the very outcome the market spent the week pricing. And he said it the day before Brent's weekly close. One honest caveat on the price move. Some of a drop this size is probably crowded longs unwinding, not pure deal pricing. We don't have positioning data to split the two. So don't give diplomacy credit for the whole move.

The shipping market tells the same story from a different angle. If the war premium were really coming off, the cost of moving crude should be easing too. It isn't. Reuters reported this week that a Valero trading executive puts freight for US Gulf Coast crude to Asia at thirty to thirty-five dollars a barrel. That's roughly a third of what the barrel itself now costs. To be precise, that route doesn't run through Hormuz. So it's a read on a global tanker market still pricing disruption, not a direct measure of the strait's toll. But it's the opposite of normalization. And it has a concrete consequence. Asian refiners buying American crude get far less relief than the Brent screen suggests, because their freight bill hasn't fallen with the benchmark.

Two more implications follow from the price move. The first is inflation. On Monday, this desk noted that the Fed's most recent hold came with two dissents in favor of a hike. We said oil in the one-teens strengthened that hawkish case. Falling oil removes one leg of it. But a premium that came out on sentiment can go back in on sentiment. The second is the asymmetry. A market that has already priced peace has more room to snap back if talks fail than it had with Brent near $118 at the end of October. That's my inference, not a forecast. The so what: the price is ahead of the paper, and the paper still says no.

Now the other oil war, where the damage is physical, not priced. Ukraine's military intelligence, known as HUR, says strikes have knocked 15 of Russia's 38 major refineries out of operation. HUR says another 12 are running at reduced output. Treat that count carefully. It comes from an interested party, and we've found no neutral source that corroborates it. "Offline" at a refinery is also often temporary, because damaged units can be repaired within weeks. What is independently reported is the pace. Bloomberg says Ukraine hit a sixth Russian crude-processing plant in a single week, a sign the campaign is intensifying. So treat the stock of damage as contested, and the tempo as the confirmed signal.

HUR makes one more claim worth flagging. It alleges that Russia has begun importing fuel through opaque arrangements designed to get around sanctions. If true, that's striking. It would mean a major net energy exporter is buying refined product to cover its own shortfall. But it's an allegation from the same interested source, not an established fact.

Here's my read on why the campaign matters. Refinery outages cut into Russia's refined-product revenue. They also squeeze domestic fuel supply heading into winter. That's inference from HUR's claims, not a measured number. The campaign shapes the diplomacy, too. Russia, Ukraine, and the US meet in Abu Dhabi on November 10th and 11th. This desk has said to treat those talks as choreography until someone moves on territory. My inference is that Kyiv is building leverage against the Kremlin's energy revenue going into that room. So in the Gulf, the market took a premium off the table on words. In Russia, drones are taking capacity off the table in barrels. A premium priced on words can come back in a single session. A damaged refinery takes weeks of repair, and Kyiv's strike tempo is still rising.

Pledges cut both ways, and there's one at home I owe you a correction on. On Friday, this desk said we didn't have a comparable data-center statement from Georgia's governor-elect. Now we do. Fortune reports that Keisha Lance Bottoms pledged "a temporary statewide pause on new construction to study the effects on electricity bills, communities, and the environment." Set that beside the other two governors-elect who ran on the issue. In Texas, Gina Hinojosa vowed to halt data-center construction and end subsidies and tax breaks for major developers. In Ohio, Amy Acton proposed a conditional pause that would apply unless developers pay all their utility costs, use union labor, and meet environmental standards. Both of those come from the same Fortune report. Against them, Georgia's position is the softest of the three. It's a pause to study, not a halt. It's also a campaign pledge, with no force until she takes office in January. But it lands in the state that hosts OpenAI's Project Camellia, the pilot site for the Grid Protectors deal with Georgia Power we've been tracking. And the stakes in these three states are large. Texas alone is projected to hold nearly 30 percent of US data-center capacity by 2028, according to a Bloom Energy report cited by Fortune. Note that Bloom sells on-site power to data centers, so it has an interest in a grid-hostile narrative. The so what: all three governor-elect positions are now on record, and none of them is law before January.

Pledges are cheap until they're written into policy. Treasury wrote two words out of one on Wednesday. In its November 4th quarterly refunding, Treasury dropped "at least" from its promise to hold coupon auction sizes steady. Per Bloomberg's report, the new wording covers "the next several quarters" and "reflects projected borrowing needs." We're working from that report, not from the primary statement.

That matters more than it sounds. The open-ended promise of flat coupon supply is gone. Sizes now track projected borrowing needs. Don't read a direction into that, up or down, because the language doesn't state one. Some coverage frames it as opening the door to less long-end issuance. Nothing in the quoted wording says so. What it does say is that duration supply is a live variable again. For months, investors could simply assume flat issuance. Now the term premium has to price the chance that sizes change.

Treasury also changed the plumbing. The seven-year note moves to a quarterly auction with two reopenings. That's a shift in how supply is sold, not how much. And Treasury will buy back up to $70 billion of its own securities across the curve, explicitly for liquidity purposes. That's a tell. Treasury is managing market functioning, not just financing a deficit. Next week it sells $125 billion of three-, ten-, and thirty-year paper, the first coupon auctions under the new language. The so what: everyone spent this week trading an oil story built on unsigned diplomacy. Meanwhile, the quieter and more durable change in how the US finances itself got almost no attention.

Two things to watch into next week. First, whether those $125 billion of auctions clear smoothly under the new guidance. Second, whether the Qatari mediation on Iran produces anything signed, rather than merely reviewed. Until it does, oil is trading narrative, not structure.

That's Strategic Power for November 7th. Stay cautiously optimistic.
