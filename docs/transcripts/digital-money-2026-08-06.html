Cautious Optimism Briefings

Digital Money, Crypto, and Market Structure

Transcript · August 6, 2026

Good morning. It's Thursday, August 6th, and this is your digital money briefing.

Start with the price, quickly, because it's the least interesting thing happening this week. Bitcoin is stuck in the low-to-mid sixty-thousands, going nowhere. But the flow data underneath it just broke a pattern. Spot Bitcoin ETFs took in a hundred seventy million dollars on August 3rd and two hundred eleven and a half million on August 4th, both led by BlackRock's IBIT. That's a real break from the tape this show has been describing all week, a market that had been weak and two-sided since June's record 3.4-billion-dollar single-week outflow. One week of inflows doesn't undo that; the June reversal and a further two-day redemption of roughly 465 million dollars in late July both argue against reading this as a new regime. So the price is quiet, and the flows just turned. That tells you the real story this week isn't Bitcoin at all. It's underneath it, in the plumbing that moves the dollars.

That plumbing story starts with Circle. Morgan Stanley downgraded Circle Internet to underweight from equal-weight on Monday and cut its price target to thirty-eight dollars from a hundred and six. The stock slid six percent on the news and is now down about thirty percent for the year. The bank didn't just trim a number, it rebuilt its model: it cut its USDC supply forecast by roughly a third for 2027 and by nearly half for 2028, which drags GAAP earnings-per-share estimates three percent below consensus in 2027 and twenty percent below in 2028. The thesis is structural, not sentimental. A stablecoin like USDC pays its reserve yield to the issuer, Circle. A tokenized money-market fund pays that same yield straight to the holder. As those tokenized funds become a credible on-chain dollar wrapper, there's less reason to park money in a stablecoin that keeps the yield for itself. Morgan Stanley also flagged that USDC's use in commercial and agentic payments, the use case that was supposed to be the growth engine, is still thin, running around forty-two thousand dollars a day. So what matters here isn't the stock move, it's that a sell-side model just put a hard number on an argument that's been theoretical until now: the regulated-issuer-as-GENIUS-Act-winner thesis is being tested against the balance sheet, and it's losing.

Now follow the money to where it's actually going, because this is the other half of the same trade. BlackRock's CFO disclosed on the Q2 call that the firm already manages sixty billion dollars of Circle's own reserves, about a quarter of the entire three-hundred-billion-dollar stablecoin market. On the same day as the Circle downgrade, BlackRock launched a second tokenized money-market vehicle, a stablecoin reserve fund with daily dividend reinvestment, alongside its tokenized Treasury liquidity fund on Ethereum, and said both are intended to qualify as eligible reserve assets under the GENIUS Act. That's not just marketing language. The statute itself explicitly permits money-market-fund shares that invest solely in approved reserve assets, and separately permits tokenized versions of most of those categories. So the legal path is real, which is exactly why it matters that BlackRock is building the product to walk down it. BlackRock's original tokenized fund, BUIDL, has grown to roughly two and a half billion dollars and is now accepted as collateral for leveraged trading on venues including Crypto-dot-com and Deribit. That's the preview of what happens once the two new funds scale: assets meant to back stablecoin redemptions doing double duty as rehypothecated trading collateral elsewhere in the system. Put the two stories together and you get one repricing, not two separate ones. The reserve income leaving Circle's model is the same income BlackRock is now packaging into a product it sells directly, and routing a quarter of the stablecoin market's reserves through a single asset manager is its own concentration risk, whatever else it is.

Coinbase's earnings, a week old now, complicate that bear case rather than settle it. Revenue came in at 1.22 billion dollars against Wall Street's roughly 1.29 to 1.31 billion, and the company posted a GAAP net loss of 359.5 million dollars. Transaction revenue fell 21 percent as crypto's total market cap dropped 11 percent and spot volumes fell 25 percent, so the miss reads as cyclical, tracking a market-wide slowdown, not something specific to Coinbase. And buried in the release is a genuinely inconvenient data point for the Circle bear case: average USDC held on Coinbase hit an all-time high of twenty billion dollars in the quarter. Circle's single largest distribution partner is holding record balances at the exact moment Morgan Stanley is modeling systemic float erosion. Both things are true at once. Coinbase also earns a revenue share on that balance, so it's not disinterested evidence, but it is real evidence that at least one major venue for USDC isn't shrinking. Coinbase itself is also getting less dependent on that reserve-yield economics generally, since subscription and services revenue is now 48 percent of net revenue, up from 29 percent less than two years ago.

Now follow this migration one step further, into the Treasury market, because this is where the skeptics tend to overreach. The reflex is to say that if stablecoin float shrinks, so does one of the newer sources of demand for short-dated Treasury bills. But the float doesn't vanish, it changes hands. A stablecoin issuer buys bills to back its coins. A tokenized money-market fund buys the same bills to back its shares. When a dollar moves from USDC into a BlackRock reserve vehicle, the bill demand moves with it, from an opaque, issuer-controlled reserve book to a conventional money-fund manager that reports its flows the ordinary way. That's a maturation of the demand base, not a reduction in it. The Treasury-demand story that got bundled into the stablecoin trade survives this repricing almost intact; it just gets a more familiar owner. And that reframes how to value a stablecoin issuer as an equity in the first place. Strip away the crypto framing and Circle is a levered bet on one spread: the yield it earns on reserves minus what it pays to distribute the coin. Morgan Stanley's downgrade is really the claim that both sides of that spread are now contestable, the yield side captured by tokenized funds, the distribution side split with partners like Coinbase. That's a far more fragile business than the compliant-winner-of-the-stablecoin-era label made it sound.

Last, the regulatory clock that frames all of this. Senate Majority Leader John Thune said Monday the CLARITY Act, the market-structure bill that would split SEC and CFTC jurisdiction over digital assets, would get a floor vote before the chamber's August 10th recess. That's a firmer commitment than the drift this show has tracked all week, where the bill kept slipping and prediction markets sat around thirty percent for 2026 passage. But the math hasn't moved with the rhetoric. Passage needs sixty votes, Republicans are still roughly seven Democrats short, and Senator Thom Tillis said negotiators are, in his words, not quite there on an ethics side agreement, while Senator Elizabeth Warren remains opposed. So watch the gap between the announcement and the delivery. If the window closes without a vote, the jurisdictional ambiguity that keeps banks cautious about crypto market infrastructure stays embedded in Coinbase and Circle valuations for another two quarters at least, right as those valuations are already being repriced from the inside.

That's the through-line for today: the GENIUS Act was supposed to crown the compliant stablecoin issuer. Instead the reserve economics are migrating to the asset manager supplying the rails underneath it, and this week Wall Street started pricing that in.

That's the briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
