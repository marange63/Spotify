Good morning, it's Wednesday, October 7th, 2026, and this is your strategic power briefing.

Today's thesis has three parts. Real new megawatts are starting to show up, but in small increments and on slow timelines. The demand claims behind the AI buildout are finally being audited. And in oil and rare earths, supply is being set by chokepoints and deadlines, not by the targets governments and cartels announce.

Start with the lead. Google and Constellation Energy announced a long-term deal yesterday that brings, in Constellation's words, 890 megawatts of new, reliable nuclear capacity onto the PJM grid. This matters because it is genuinely additive. On this beat we've covered two earlier hyperscaler nuclear deals that mostly weren't. Amazon's Calvert Cliffs agreement added only about 190 new megawatts out of 690. Oracle's Point Beach subscription, as reported, added none; it reallocated output from a running plant. On Tuesday we set a simple test: a deal like this adds supply to the grid only if it underwrites an uprate. Google's deal is the first of the three that passes it.

But pace the timeline. Constellation's release says the first uprate is expected by 2028, roughly two years out. It gives no completion date for the rest of the 890 megawatts. It also doesn't say whether the remaining uprates still need sign-off from the Nuclear Regulatory Commission. The work spans eleven Constellation-owned nuclear units across Illinois, Pennsylvania and New Jersey. The deal carries more than 4.3 billion dollars of new investment. But that is Constellation's money, not a payment from Google. The release attributes the capital spend to Constellation alone.

So what is Google actually buying? It is buying certainty for Constellation's lenders and shareholders. A long-dated hyperscaler contract moves uprate output from merchant pricing to contracted pricing. That lowers the risk on the capex, even though Constellation still carries it. That is the logic of the structure, not a disclosed financial term. But it is a template other independent power producers with uprate-eligible fleets will want to copy.

There's a second, larger piece of this deal that cuts the other way. Alongside the uprates, the two companies signed a separate fifteen-year supply agreement for an additional 2,700 megawatts in PJM. That is three times the size of the new capacity. The release frames it around keeping Constellation's existing assets economically viable. It never says outright that this is existing output. But that is the clear implication. If so, most of the megawatts in this headline deal are reallocation, even though the new part is real.

Now size the new part against the actual gap. PJM's most recent capacity auction procured 145,777 megawatts. Utility Dive puts that about 6,625 megawatts below the grid operator's own 20 percent reserve-margin target. Once fully delivered, on a timeline that runs past 2028 with no end date, 890 megawatts closes roughly an eighth of a single year's shortfall. So what: hyperscaler money is finally buying new nuclear megawatts instead of just renting existing ones, and that is the trend to watch. But one deal, arriving slowly, does not close PJM's reliability gap. Expect continued upward pressure on capacity prices in that market regardless of this announcement.

If that's what real new supply looks like, Texas is showing what paper demand looks like. ERCOT is reviewing about 474 gigawatts of connection requests. That is more than five times the state's record peak demand, and roughly 90 percent of it is data centers. Governor Greg Abbott directed ERCOT and the state's utility commission on August 3rd to audit data-center projects in the queue before any more advance. In September he followed with a halt on data-center permits at the state's environmental regulator, pending the same review. That regulator owes the governor a compliance update by October 19th, though it's not clear the update will be made public. ERCOT itself says the full audit won't wrap until December.

Don't read this as Texas turning against data centers. Read it as the most build-friendly state in the country trying to find out how much of its own queue is real. A request pipeline five times peak demand is, almost by definition, full of speculative and duplicate requests. Nobody yet knows what share. That is precisely what the audit is for, and I won't guess at a number the state hasn't produced.

The second-order effects run well beyond Texas. Many national forecasts of data-center power demand are built on interconnection request volumes. If ERCOT formally de-rates its queue, those forecasts look overstated. Developers who can't get through the audit may also turn to behind-the-meter generation, or to other grids with faster interconnection. That is a possibility to watch, not a shift anyone has observed yet.

So what: set 890 real megawatts against a 474-gigawatt request pile. The gap is more than two orders of magnitude, and it tells you where the binding constraint sits. It isn't chips or capital or announcements. It is interconnection, and the verification of the demand behind it. October 19th and December are the first real data points on how much of that headline demand survives scrutiny.

The same gap between a stated target and physical reality runs through oil. OPEC+ agreed on October 4th to hold its November production target unchanged at 31.1 million barrels a day. The market barely reacted, with Brent up less than one percent on the decision. The headline looks like discipline, but it isn't, really. According to trade-press reporting, Gulf OPEC+ producers have been exporting at only 60 to 80 percent of normal levels because of continuing disruption from the US-Israeli war on Iran. Treat that range as reported, not tanker-tracked. Still, if members can't hit their targets anyway, holding the quota costs them nothing. It is symbolism, not a supply decision. So what: the chokepoint, not the cartel's arithmetic, is setting physical supply, and a quota headline tells you little about barrels.

And the strategic input with a hard date attached is rare earths. Last November, after the Trump-Xi summit, China suspended the second wave of its rare-earth export controls. That wave, as reported, covers heavy rare earths including holmium and erbium. The suspension expires on November 10th. Nothing in the available reporting shows renewal talks underway in either direction. So the honest framing is that the outcome is genuinely open, not a presumed rollover.

Beijing hasn't been passive during the pause, either. A secondary aggregator reports that China widened existing export controls to ten US firms in June and fourteen EU firms in July. Treat those counts as reported rather than confirmed from a primary Chinese notice. But the pattern is the point. Beijing keeps using firm-specific pressure as a lever even during a formal truce.

So what: in about five weeks, markets face a binary with real teeth. A lapse would hit inputs for magnets, defense electronics and precision optics. Nothing in today's reporting suggests Western refining could replace that supply by then. And it's not clear the market has been forced to price that yet.

Here is what to watch. First, whether Constellation's first uprate stays on track for 2028, and whether the remaining tranches get a date. Second, the Texas compliance update due by October 19th and ERCOT's audit conclusion in December, which will tell us how much of that 474-gigawatt queue is real. Third, November 10th, when China's rare-earth truce either rolls over or doesn't. Each of those dates will tell you more about the real state of strategic power than any press release did this week.

That's the briefing. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
