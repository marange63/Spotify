Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Credit & the Corporate Credit Cycle

Transcript · September 16, 2026

Good morning, it's September 16th, and this is your private credit briefing.

We start with a warning from inside the club. At the Milken Institute's Canada summit on Monday, Fortress Investment Group co-CEO Jack Neumark told other credit managers to watch themselves on AI data-center lending. His language was blunt but restrained: "As credit gets more and more involved, I think everyone's going to have to be careful." Fortress manages roughly 55 billion dollars, so this is not a bystander's opinion. It's a competitor's. And take that literally. Neumark runs a firm that is not winning these mandates, so this is an interested view, not a disclosure. On Monday this briefing flagged data-center debt pouring into the leveraged finance pipeline with no named borrowers behind it. Now a credit manager has put a name on the risk itself.

The shape of the worry, as his remarks were reported, is straightforward. Private lenders financing AI infrastructure are taking fixed coupons on very long-dated assets. The equity in those projects keeps the upside if the buildout pays off. That's a paraphrase of the argument rather than his verbatim words, so hold the framing loosely. We do now have a real number to test it against. The bond financing Meta's Hyperion data-center campus in Louisiana, structured by Blue Owl and Pimco, priced its senior secured bonds at a 6.581 percent coupon, Treasuries plus 225 basis points, maturing in May of 2049. That spread is tight. It prices closer to investment-grade infrastructure debt, the kind backed by a long lease to a highly rated tenant, than to the wider, floating spreads direct lenders demand for amendable corporate credit. That's context from earlier this year, not this week's deal. But it's the concrete evidence behind Neumark's warning. If hyperscaler lease economics or AI revenue growth disappoint over the next two decades, that coupon does not reprice and does not renegotiate. It just sits there, fixed, while the asset underneath it is re-marked. Layer on the broader wave. More than 40 billion dollars of data-center bond placements from Applied Digital, CoreWeave, Hut 8 and Related Companies have priced in the 144A market since November, and that is a cumulative total since last autumn rather than a monthly run-rate. The so-what here is simple. The fastest-growing use of private credit dollars right now has no loss history at all. Nobody has tested what happens to twenty-three-year data-center paper in a downturn, because there hasn't been one yet.

Before judging paper with no loss history, it's worth looking at how the book that does have one is being kept alive. Fitch's monthly private credit tracker, covered on this show yesterday, put the trailing twelve-month default rate at a record 6.3 percent. What wasn't reported then is the composition, and the composition is the real story. Stressed maturity extensions, meaning borrowers pushing out loan maturities rather than repaying or refinancing at market terms, were the single most common form of default in August, accounting for 45 percent of the month's events. They have held that top spot for three straight months. The other dominant modes are payment deferrals and switches to payment-in-kind, which are the same family of forbearance rather than outright payment failure. Put it together and you get a default rate built overwhelmingly on borrowers being given more time, not on borrowers failing. That composition data reaches us through press coverage of Fitch's report rather than the release itself, so treat it as reported, not primary. And nothing in it speaks to loss severity. Recovery rates on these defaults are not disclosed, and recovery is the number that would actually settle whether the spread compensation was adequate.

Here's the definitional point worth sitting with. A lender who grants an extension typically keeps that credit marked as performing on its own books, even as Fitch, counting honestly, logs it as a default. The record default rate and the calm-looking fund marks are not contradictory numbers. They're the same fact viewed from two different ledgers, and Fitch's is the more conservative of the two. Extension is the shock absorber of this cycle. Shock absorbers wear out.

That absorber works because the loans behind it are short-dated and amendable. Which is exactly what the new AI infrastructure paper is not. A middle-market sponsor loan can be extended, re-priced, or restructured with a phone call between lender and borrower. A twenty-three-year fixed-rate bond wrapped around a data-center campus cannot flex the same way. There is no extension optionality built into a structure already written to a 2049 maturity. To be fair to the other side of this trade, the counter-case deserves real weight. Rated 144A data-center tranches are underwritten against hyperscaler lease covenants, not against AI revenue directly. If that lease structure holds, the risk of the debt genuinely can decouple from the risk of the AI market above it. That is where the market may be over-discounting the risk rather than ignoring it. Nobody has data yet to say which read is correct, and that is precisely the point. It's an open question, not a settled one.

And the money funding all of this is getting shorter, not longer. On the 13th, this briefing cited first-quarter redemption pressure at Apollo Debt Solutions and Ares Strategic Income, running at 11.2 and 11.6 percent of net assets, and explicitly left the second-quarter figures unconfirmed. Now there's a named number. Cliffwater Corporate Lending Fund, a nontraded interval BDC, received redemption requests in the second quarter equal to 17 percent of outstanding shares. Caps of roughly five percent a quarter are typical of these vehicles. A request is not a redemption, and a cap binding is the structure working as documented rather than failing. But the real question isn't whether investors get paid this quarter. It's what the manager sells to pay them. The liquid, performing loans in a portfolio are the easiest to sell. The ones already granted maturity extensions are not. That's adverse selection happening inside the vehicle itself, on top of whatever adverse selection happened at origination. Run it forward. If extended credits aren't marked down the way impairments would be, net asset value stays elevated, tenders get priced off that elevated NAV, and a queue running more than three times the cap keeps rolling forward rather than clearing.

There's one more link in this chain worth naming, even though I can't size it. Banks lend to non-bank financial institutions, that money funds private credit managers, and those managers are now among the largest lenders to data-center developers. That is an indirect banking-system exposure to AI capex that nobody underwrites as such. We went looking for a dated 2026 figure isolating data-center or AI-infrastructure lending inside bank exposure to private credit, from the Fed, the IMF, the BIS or the rating agencies, and there isn't one. So take the channel as real and the magnitude as genuinely unknown.

So where does the cycle actually stand? Deterioration is real, but it's being managed by extension rather than resolved. The binding risk in private credit right now isn't the headline spread level. It's duration and liquidity mismatch. A book of loans is getting quietly termed out on one side, and a growing pile of AI infrastructure paper that can't be termed out at all is building on the other. Both are increasingly funded by vehicles that promise their holders quarterly liquidity. The biggest unresolved question is what a long-dated, untested asset class does once it sits inside a redeemable wrapper under real stress. Nobody in this market has lived through that.

Watch four things from here. First, this afternoon's Fed decision, which lands after we go to air, so I take no position on the outcome. Second, the next Fitch print, for whether that 45 percent extension share holds above 40. Third, third-quarter BDC nonaccrual, payment-in-kind and tender disclosures due in November. And fourth, the next earnings calls from Blue Owl, Ares and Apollo, for the first real disclosure of how much data-center exposure sits on their books.
