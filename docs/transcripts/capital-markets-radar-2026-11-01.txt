Good morning, it's Sunday, November 1st, and this is your Capital Markets Radar.

The thread running through this week's price action is simple: the cost of money is repricing the edges of the market. The weakest credits, the debasement hedge in gold, and the carry trades built under crypto are all getting squeezed at once. Meanwhile the AI buildout's binding constraint has shifted from willingness to spend to the ability to take delivery. That pushes its biggest financing need out into 2027. Let's start where the repricing is now showing up in price, not just in data.

For a week, private-credit stress showed up only in default rates and surveys, not in prices. On Wednesday we argued that pricing had run ahead of risk, and that the repricing would come later, and in lumps. The first lump just showed up in the public CCC market. Spreads on CCC-rated US leveraged loans widened about 280 basis points from the end of September, to roughly 2,449 basis points, per Reuters reporting. That's the widest since May 2009. This is the market price confirming the stress. The Moody's default rate of 5.8 percent, which we flagged Wednesday, is now just one input behind a price that's moving on its own.

The same stress shows up in who's willing to hold these loans. Listed business development companies, the public vehicles that hold private-credit books, are trading at about 0.72 times net asset value. Their long-run average is about 0.92. So public holders are marking these books well below where the managers mark them. There are two honest readings. Either the market is front-running losses the managers haven't booked yet, or this is mostly a liquidity discount that overstates the real damage. The software and services concentration we flagged Wednesday argues for a sector and vintage problem, not a systemic one. But the discount alone can't settle it.

What we don't know is whether this has spread up the stack. We found no clean evidence yet that broad high-yield or investment-grade spreads are moving with the CCC tier, so stress is confirmed at the very bottom of the credit stack and not yet above it.

One more piece belongs here, and our Private Credit desk raised it last week. Barclays puts its total private-credit exposure at about 15 billion pounds, the vast majority in its banking book. That comes amid reported Bank of England scrutiny of whether its traders misrepresented illiquid private-credit loans. It's one bank, not a system-wide signal. But it's a reminder that private credit threads back into regulated balance sheets, through marks that regulators are now checking.

Why does stress at the bottom of the stack matter now? Because the same hurdle rate squeezing the weakest borrowers is also draining the trades built on carry and hedging elsewhere. The Fed, as we covered Thursday, is debating a hike into December, not a cut. That was a hawkish hold, with two dissents in favor of tightening further. After the decision, futures put the odds of a December hike at roughly 83 to 85 percent, up from about 78 percent the day before. It's backdrop, not news today. But leveraged loans are floating-rate, so by our inference a December hike would raise the interest burden on exactly the cohort now repricing.

That same rate backdrop is showing up in crypto positioning. US spot bitcoin ETFs posted about 643 million dollars of net outflows on Friday. Our Digital Money desk covered that figure yesterday, along with the two readings: plain de-risking, or a mechanical unwind of hedged positions. Three issuers, BlackRock, Fidelity, and ARK 21Shares, made up nearly 88 percent of the withdrawals. The new piece is an attribution. Galaxy Research, relayed secondhand, ties hedge-fund exits from the ETFs to the cash-and-carry basis compressing toward Treasury yields. That's a single research note and it's unconfirmed. The Binance hack also hit the same tape, so it's a competing cause for the same day's selling. But if Galaxy is right, this is a rates story. The risk-free leg of the trade got relatively more attractive, and that's not a verdict on bitcoin itself.

Now to the clearest break with the recent pattern. On Thursday we noted gold was still failing to catch a stress bid near the four-thousand-dollar handle, even with a geopolitical risk premium sitting in oil. It has now broken below that level. Spot gold was at roughly 3,876 dollars an ounce in Saturday's snapshot, a one-year low. That's about 30 percent below the record near 5,600 dollars set in late January.

A hedge against debasement and geopolitical risk is unwinding this hard while that risk premium is still live. That is the non-consensus point. Our inference, and it is an inference, since we have no quoted real-yield figure, is that the real-rate and hike-path hurdle is beating the haven bid. The competing explanation is simpler. Gold ran parabolic into January, and a 30 percent round trip looks a lot like crowded length unwinding rather than a fresh macro verdict. Either way, the debasement trade is losing to the hike path for now.

And the one place where the cost of money isn't the binding constraint is the AI buildout. There, the constraint is delivery. Microsoft trimmed its full-year capital spending forecast to 170 billion dollars from 175 billion, even after beating on revenue and earnings. Chief financial officer Amy Hood tied it to delays at third-party GPU suppliers. Those delays push some available capacity into the first half of 2027. She expects spending to accelerate then, because of higher component costs and delayed deliveries.

Our Enterprise Technology desk made the supply point on Friday, so the operational read isn't new. The capital-markets read is. The trim looks like restraint, but it isn't. Microsoft still guided next quarter above 50 billion dollars, so the missing five billion looks like spending that slips later, not spending that's cancelled. So this is deferred spend, not reduced spend, and it lands later at a higher unit cost. One caution: the GPU delay is one CFO's account, and we can't yet say it's industry-wide.

For suppliers and power, that implies a lumpier 2027 rather than demand destruction. That's our inference. The investor who sells the chip and power trade on Microsoft's trim is over-reading it.

There's a supporting signal in earnings estimates. One analyst estimate has the combined forward earnings growth forecast for the Big 7 tech companies falling from 33.6 percent to 22.6 percent in a month. If it holds, that group would trail the rest of the S&P 500 for the first time since the end of 2022. Treat that as one data point, not a confirmed rotation. But it rhymes with a market sorting AI spenders by delivery and cash generation, not by the size of the capex number.

Loop that back to the lead. Deferred capex means a bigger financing need arrives later, into whatever credit market exists by then. By our inference, that's a market whose bottom tier is already repricing today. That's the connective tissue across this episode. Money is getting more expensive for exactly the borrowers, hedges, and trades that can least afford it.

Here's what to watch into next week. First, whether broad high-yield and investment-grade spreads start moving with the CCC tier. That would turn a contained problem into contagion. Second, whether business development companies cut dividends or mark down their books, which would tell us which reading of that discount is right. Third, whether gold holds the high 3,800s or keeps falling. And on the calendar, October payrolls land Friday, November 6th, with CPI following on November 10th. Neither has printed, so don't trust any number you hear for either before then.

That's your radar. Stay cautious, stay optimistic, and I'll see you next time.
