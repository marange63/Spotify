Good morning, and welcome to Cautious Optimism Briefings for August 24th, 2026.

Over the past week this show has been tracking two separate stories — how the AI buildout gets financed, and how compute scarcity gets priced. Today those threads converge inside a single company's week. Broadcom's position at its largest custom-silicon customer is being contested on one side of its book, and on the other it is reportedly raising tens of billions of dollars of debt to fund a single AI lab. The pattern across today's briefing is that competitive position in custom silicon is increasingly being bought with financial instruments, not just won with better chips.

Start with the lead. On August 19th, Marvell filed an 8-K disclosing a commercial agreement it signed with Google on July 29th. The scope is the story, not the dollar figure. The filing covers custom silicon that attaches to the TPU ecosystem: AI inference accelerators, storage controllers, network interface controllers, memory interface controllers, and near-memory compute. That is five product categories, not one socket — Google is qualifying Marvell across the entire stack that surrounds its Tensor Processing Units, not just second-sourcing a single component.

To seal that access, Marvell issued Google a warrant on August 18th for up to 58,970,907 shares at $206.58 apiece, exercisable until August 18th, 2033. Now the structure that defuses the headline number. Only 1,360,867 of those shares vest on a fixed schedule, in the first year. Everything else vests in 240 separate tranches, one tranche for every $500 million of Marvell custom-product revenue Google actually chooses to buy. That purchasing is discretionary, running out to fiscal 2033. So this is not a $120 billion order book.

Size that against Marvell's own stated ambition. On its May earnings call, management said it remains confident in delivering over $10 billion of custom revenue in fiscal 2029. Vesting the whole warrant would take $120 billion of cumulative Google purchases — more than a decade of custom revenue at that target run rate, with Google the dominant customer inside it. So it's a performance ladder Google can decline to climb, and Marvell paid for the right to offer it with its own equity — roughly 59 million shares of potential dilution.

The market read it as a competitive shift regardless. Marvell shares rose roughly 7 to 10 percent, depending on which outlet's reference point you use. Broadcom shares fell about 5 percent the same day. But here is the caveat that matters: Broadcom has disclosed no change to its own Google arrangement.

Broadcom's own 8-K, filed back on April 6th, confirms that its custom-TPU development agreement and a separate networking supply deal with Google both run through 2031. That duration is on the record. What is not on the record, anywhere in that filing, is the word "exclusive." There is no exclusivity language at all. So the accurate description of Broadcom's position is sole-supplier-by-practice through 2031, not a contractual lock.

The concentration at stake is real and quantified. Broadcom's fiscal 2025 10-K states that one customer accounted for 32 percent of net revenue, up from 28 percent the year before and 21 percent the year before that — a rising three-year trend. But the filing never names that customer. Identifying it as Google is analyst and press inference from the TPU relationship, not Broadcom's own words.

So here is what actually happened. Google has bought a call option on a second full-stack vendor, and Marvell financed the win through its cap table rather than through price or performance. If warrants become the standard currency for hyperscaler design wins, the effective take rate on custom silicon falls for every merchant vendor, because the competition moves off the spec sheet and onto the balance sheet.

If that's Broadcom's position being contested on one side of its book, look at what it was reportedly doing on the other. Broadcom is said to be in talks to raise up to $100 billion in debt — structured as roughly $60 to $70 billion of senior notes and about $30 billion of junior notes — with Blackstone and Apollo as counterparties, to fund AI-chip infrastructure for Anthropic. This extends the AI XPV Platform the three parties launched earlier this year, which already put $35 billion behind Anthropic's data-center construction, aiming for more than 1 gigawatt of compute online this year and more than 20 gigawatts through 2028. Treat those gigawatt figures as the platform's stated targets, not delivered capacity.

This is reported, unconfirmed, and the outlets don't even agree with each other — headlines range from "upwards of $70 billion" to "up to $100 billion," and no Broadcom filing or on-the-record statement backs any of it. But the direction matters more than the exact number. Last Wednesday and Thursday this show covered AMD's record $4.75 billion bond and Nvidia's $105 billion contingent, indemnified guarantee, and argued that investor scrutiny would slow the cadence of these mega-deals. Within a day of that call, this report landed — at the top of its reported range, roughly twenty times the size of AMD's bond, and structured as funded private debt rather than a contingent public guarantee. Either that discipline isn't binding, or it's being routed around by moving the financing off public markets and into private credit. This briefing can't settle which, but the shift itself is the signal.

And the harder question is who actually holds the risk. Senior notes placed with Blackstone and Apollo mean much of the exposure sits with those asset managers and their limited partners, not visibly on Broadcom's own balance sheet. So Broadcom is trading diversified customer exposure at Google for concentrated counterparty exposure to a single frontier lab's trajectory.

Both of those are ways of buying a customer relationship. There's a third way, and it belongs to a company that can afford neither a warrant nor a hundred-billion-dollar syndicate. Cerebras launched its CS-4 system on August 19th, built from three WSE-3 Turbo wafer-scale processors, each with 4 trillion transistors — what Cerebras calls the largest AI semiconductor ever built. The company's claim, and it should be heard as Cerebras's claim specifically, is 30 times the tokens-per-second-per-user of GPU-based solutions on frontier models. CEO Andrew Feldman said the system "delivers industry-leading speeds on the largest frontier models, fundamentally changing the paradigm."

Treat that skeptically. No model, workload, or comparison GPU configuration was disclosed, and no independent benchmark has validated the number. Tokens per second per user is also the most flattering possible metric for a wafer-scale part, because it measures single-stream latency rather than throughput per dollar or per watt. What is genuinely interesting is architectural: the CS-4 uses on-wafer SRAM instead of high-bandwidth memory, which is a real dissent from the assumption that all frontier inference is HBM-bound. But the decisive commercial question isn't peak speed, it's whether a customer will port off CUDA for it — and there's no named customer, contract, or shipment yet. First shipments this quarter is a company forecast, not a confirmed event.

Three companies, three ways to fund a position — equity, debt, and architecture. One participant is funding its own expansion out of the shortage itself rather than out of someone else's balance sheet. On August 20th, Micron committed $10 billion to build Micron Research Labs, an AI-focused facility in Boise, with construction beginning in phases from 2027, layered on top of its already-underway $50 billion Boise fab buildout. It's unclear whether that $10 billion is incremental to the existing $50 billion program or already counted within it. Meanwhile Samsung's push for a roughly 20 percent DRAM price increase for the third quarter — a dispute whose underlying reporting dates to early July and which remains unresolved — is now meeting real resistance. Trade reporting says downstream PC and smartphone makers are no longer able to absorb further cost pass-throughs. That's the first visible sign that memory pricing power, while still firmly with the suppliers, has a ceiling somewhere.

The competitive dimension that actually moved this week wasn't architecture, manufacturing, or packaging — it was the balance sheet. And it's worth naming what that read is built on: one primary filing, one unconfirmed financing report, and one unverified vendor benchmark. Nvidia reports on the 26th and Marvell on the 27th, and neither should be asked to carry today's conclusion.
