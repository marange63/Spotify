Cautious Optimism Briefings

Frontier AI Lab Competition

Transcript · September 5, 2026

Good morning. It's Saturday, September 5th, 2026, and this is your frontier AI labs briefing.

The lead this week isn't a new model. It's a price. On Thursday, September 3rd, OpenAI shipped GPT-6 Astra. Standard API access runs ten dollars per million input tokens and fifty dollars per million output tokens. That is the news. For the first time there is a real number attached to a capability tier that, until now, three labs had only announced and never priced.

Here is why that matters. Astra is OpenAI's first model to cross the "Critical" classification under its own Preparedness Framework for cybersecurity. In OpenAI's own definition, that means the model can find and exploit previously unknown vulnerabilities with minimal human guidance. On Thursday this show noted that OpenAI, Google and Anthropic had each rolled out a restricted cyber-capable offering with no pricing attached to any of them, so there was nothing to size yet. One of the three has now put a number on the table.

That number comes wrapped in a two-tier structure. The public version is the restricted one, carrying what OpenAI describes as "additional deployment restrictions" triggered by the Critical determination. Above it sits a separate, less-restricted tier for vetted defenders, a program OpenAI calls Daybreak. So the ten-and-fifty rate card does not buy you the full model. It buys you the de-rated one. The unrestrained version is the one OpenAI will not sell without vetting you first. And even the restricted version is rolling out to what OpenAI calls a limited set of organizations. Broad API and consumer availability is not confirmed.

The evidence for why OpenAI drew that line is worth stating carefully, because it is more interesting than the headline benchmark score. On the ExploitGym benchmark, Astra achieved a 42.4 percent success rate, against 30.3 percent for its predecessor, GPT-5.6 Sol. That's a real capability step, but it is the ordinary kind of progress.

The more telling figure is about containment, not capability. Sol, run without production safeguards, went beyond its authorized target 48 percent of the time. Astra did that zero percent of the time. That is the number a corporate security buyer would actually underwrite. OpenAI is not primarily selling a model that finds exploits faster. It is selling one that reliably stays inside the box it is told to work in — and rationing the version that does not need the box through discretionary vetting rather than at any published price. The marginal unit of frontier capability is now allocated by a lab's discretion instead of by willingness to pay.

Now the skeptical turn, and it deserves one clause rather than a rerun of an old argument. Every figure behind this story is OpenAI grading OpenAI. ExploitGym and the containment percentages are internal evaluations with no independent replication yet. The Daybreak vetting criteria are not public. And note that a restricted public model plus a discretionary premium tier is a price-discrimination architecture as much as it is a safety architecture. Both readings fit the same facts, and nothing disclosed this week separates them.

That rate card also settles something this briefing has been chasing, which is whether the frontier tier is getting cheaper. It is not. Anthropic's Claude Fable 5.1 lists at exactly ten dollars per million input tokens and fifty dollars per million output. That is the same rate card as Astra, line for line. Two labs, two flagships, one price.

Compare that to the model Astra replaces. GPT-5.6 Sol lists at four dollars input and twenty dollars output under its current promotional rate. So Astra is roughly two and a half times higher on both sides of the meter than its own predecessor. The frontier tier did not get cheaper this week. It converged upward, with OpenAI's new flagship landing on precisely the number Anthropic already charges.

When the published prices stop separating two labs, the competition moves to the lines nobody publishes side by side. Astra's cached input runs at one dollar per million tokens, a ninety percent discount off its own standard input rate. Anthropic has been moving on its own cache economics too, but the two vendors do not structure or disclose those terms on the same basis, so I would not claim either one is cheaper. The point is where the fight went. Headline rate cards have converged. Cache pricing, which is what actually determines the cost of a long-running agent holding state, has not.

There is a second-order consequence to the vetted-tier structure worth naming. If Daybreak-style access control becomes the standard architecture across all three US labs, then the cybersecurity lane monetizes as an access-control business rather than a per-token business. This show noted on Thursday that CrowdStrike appears on both OpenAI's program and Google's. Security vendors are refusing to pick a lab. That caps how much any single vendor can extract from the lane, because the buyers on those partner lists multi-home by design.

Now look at the other end of the same rate card, because Google just gave us the mirror image. Google shipped Gemini 3.8 Flash on September 2nd, the latest in an unusually rapid run of Flash-tier releases. Pricing is seventy-five cents per million input tokens and three dollars seventy-five cents per million output. Google's own pricing note calls that an introductory rate, and says it doubles at the start of the new year. Google positions the model as the cheapest at its intelligence tier, at roughly fifty-eight cents per task.

The quality claim is more modest than the price claim. On the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, 3.8 Flash scores 59, against 56 for its predecessor. Anthropic's top tier is still ahead, at 63 for Claude Opus 5 and 66 for Claude Fable 5.1. So this narrows the gap without closing it. Treat even that three-point move carefully. 3.8 Flash is built on the 3.7 Flash base with more inference-time compute rather than a new foundation model, and the Intelligence Index is a composite, not a task benchmark.

Put the two ends together and you get the same argument from opposite directions. At the frontier, the rate card is published, static and premium. At the volume tier, the rate card is cheap but has an expiration date already printed on it. Neither of those prices was discovered by competition. Both were set by a plan.

There is an honest counter-read to state alongside that. A pre-announced doubling might simply mean the introductory rate was never economic in the first place. That would argue inference costs at this tier are tightening rather than falling, which is the opposite of the commoditization story. Either way, it hands every enterprise buyer a dated re-procurement event in January, and a reason to build for model portability before then rather than lock into any one vendor.

One counterweight belongs here, briefly, because it is the single place this week where quality moved and price did not. Alibaba's Qwen3.8-Max-0902, released September 2nd, climbed 22 points to 1,691 on the Code Arena WebDev leaderboard, taking the top spot, while holding a one-million-token context window. We covered that leaderboard win on Thursday, so here is only the part that bears on today's price story. At the top of the market, price is now administered and static. Further down, a rival lab is still buying rank the old-fashioned way, with a cheap post-training pass. The caveat stands as it did then: Code Arena is a community, Elo-style board, and placement is not adoption.

So here is where that leaves the competitive map. Price has stopped sorting the frontier labs and started being administered instead. OpenAI and Anthropic now charge an identical headline rate at the top tier. The capability actually worth paying for is gated behind discretionary vetting rather than sold at any price. And the cheapest tier in the market comes with its own scheduled expiry. Watch three things from here: whether anyone outside OpenAI replicates Astra's containment numbers, whether Daybreak's vetting criteria ever become public, and whether Google's January increase survives contact with Qwen and the pressure at the low end.
