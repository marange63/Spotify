Good morning, it's Sunday, August 9th, and this is your robotics and physical AI briefing.

Today's clearest signal comes from autonomous driving, where Waymo is converting a gated pilot into an open, revenue-live market, even as the humanoid side of the industry remains stuck at a hardware-versus-policy standoff.

Start with Dallas. On August 4th, Waymo dropped the waitlist that had gated its robotaxi service since it went semi-public in February. Roughly 150,000 riders used the Waymo app during that gated period — that's app sign-ups, not confirmed rides delivered, and Waymo has not disclosed a Dallas fleet size or a post-waitlist trip run-rate. Airport service to Love Field also isn't live yet. But removing a waitlist is a different kind of signal than a demo or a capability claim. It's Waymo telling the market it's comfortable with the unit economics and the safety margin at that scale, not just the technology.

One structural detail under the Dallas expansion is worth flagging, because it's a template as much as a market. Waymo runs the Dallas fleet in partnership with Avis Budget Group, on all-electric Jaguar I-Pace vehicles, with Avis handling fleet operations. That matters because owning and maintaining a robotaxi fleet is a capital and labor sink, not a software margin. Outsourcing the depot, the charging, and the cleaning to an existing rental-fleet operator lets Waymo scale city access without carrying all the fleet-maintenance capex itself. Expect other AV operators to copy that split rather than vertically integrate the garage.

That confidence claim gets tested by the freeway story, and here the full picture is more specific — and more serious — than a simple pause-and-resume. Waymo's freeway trouble traces to a formal voluntary recall filed with the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration on June 18th, covering roughly 3,900 to 4,000 vehicles. The company told regulators its robotaxis drove into closed highway construction zones at least 13 times. In Phoenix, vehicles failed to recognize ramp-closure signage. In the Bay Area, on May 18th, seven robotaxis entered active construction lanes because the software prioritized avoiding other hazards over recognizing the work zone itself. No injuries or collisions were reported, but the pattern was serious enough that Waymo pulled every vehicle off every freeway nationwide the next day, May 19th, while a fix was developed.

That fix — described by Waymo as software updates for enhanced scene recognition and routing around construction zones — began rolling back out on July 29th. But it's a staged restart, not a single network-wide flip. Phoenix is the confirmed market so far; Los Angeles, the San Francisco Bay Area, Miami, and Austin were still pending as of the most recent reporting, with Waymo describing those markets as following "in the coming days." So the accurate read is restoration underway, led by Phoenix, not restoration complete everywhere.

Here's the so-what on both halves together. The freeway failure wasn't just a company-driven, unverified tweak — it has a documented regulatory paper trail through a formal NHTSA recall, which is a harder form of accountability than "we updated some software." That's a point in Waymo's favor on transparency. But the staged, city-by-city rollout also means the hardest test — does the fix generalize outside Phoenix — hasn't happened yet. Removing the Dallas waitlist while freeway service is still only partially restored elsewhere tells you Waymo is comfortable scaling surface-street commercial access faster than it's comfortable scaling back onto freeways. That's a rational, risk-differentiated posture, and it still leaves Waymo well ahead of Tesla, whose robotaxi and FSD efforts remain geofenced and safety-driver-supervised rather than open, unsupervised, and waitlist-free in a major market.

Now the industrial and policy side of physical AI, where two catalysts this show has been tracking all week have both now landed, without producing a clean economics answer.

On July 28th, the FCC added "advanced robotic devices" — its language for mobile humanoids and quadrupeds — to its Covered List, citing the risk that these machines "collect data that could be leveraged by malign actors to surveil Americans, enhance the capabilities of foreign intelligence services, or remotely commandeer the robots." FCC Chairman Brendan Carr framed it as supply-chain security policy. But the action is narrower than "ban" suggests: it blocks equipment authorization only for new device models going forward. Robots already purchased or already authorized are grandfathered, and the federal government itself is exempt. There's also a conditional-approval path that runs through the Department of War, which turns the measure into a licensing gray zone rather than an absolute wall. Still, it's the first hard US market-access barrier specifically for Chinese humanoid and quadruped hardware.

Eight days later, on August 6th, Unitree priced its Shanghai STAR Market IPO at 150.8 yuan a share — a valuation of roughly $9.04 billion, raising about $904 million. It's China's first listed humanoid robot maker, and the listing cleared China's fastest-ever approval-to-registration turnaround for that exchange, just 104 days. Unitree's 2025 revenue quadrupled to 1.7 billion yuan, on roughly 5,500 humanoids shipped and cumulative quadruped sales past 33,000 units, with gross margins on its core business around 60 percent.

Put those two dates together and you get the bifurcation this show flagged as the thing to watch: the US is walling off finished Chinese humanoid and quadruped hardware imports at the exact moment China is capitalizing its national champion on public markets. The practical pressure lands on US integrators and research labs that have used Unitree or similar Chinese platforms for R&D — they now have a supplier-requalification problem, pushing demand toward US and allied hardware makers like Figure, Apptronik, and Boston Dynamics. And because so much of the actuator, motor, and rare-earth supply still sits in China, a bifurcated hardware chain doesn't only reroute demand — it can raise the bill of materials for US and allied makers that still buy Chinese components underneath the finished robot. That's a real capital-allocation shift. What it isn't, yet, is proof that any humanoid maker, American or Chinese, has cracked paying deployment at scale — the FCC action and the IPO are both about market structure and capital access, not about a robot doing new, verified, revenue-generating work.

One layer down, worth a brief note: Nvidia keeps building out the model layer underneath both the walled-off hardware and everything on the open side. Isaac GR00T N1.7 now runs on an upgraded Cosmos-Reason2 and Qwen3-VL backbone, and Nvidia's Cosmos Reason component is built to turn vague natural-language instructions into physics-grounded, step-by-step plans rather than requiring bespoke task engineering. The FCC's Covered List reaches hardware, not software — so an open-weight robot brain like this is available to exactly the makers the FCC just restricted from selling finished robots into the US. That's the second-order effect to watch: value in embodied AI may keep concentrating in the compute and simulation layer, the way it did in generative AI, even as the hardware layer gets walled off by country.

So where does that leave the picture. Autonomous driving is the subsector actually crossing from gated pilot to open, revenue-facing market this week, even with the freeway restart still incomplete and unverified beyond Phoenix. Humanoid robotics remains a strategic-industrial standoff — a market-access wall on one side, a national champion going public on the other — with capital and policy moving faster than any disclosed unit economics. The near-term tell is Phoenix itself: because the restart is staged, any fresh construction-zone incident there before Los Angeles and the Bay Area come back online would say far more about whether the fix generalizes than a quiet re-enablement would. So watch for independent confirmation that Waymo's freeway fix holds beyond Phoenix, and watch whether any humanoid maker, in the US or China, discloses a real paying-deployment number instead of a shipment or manufacturing count.

That's the briefing. Have a good Sunday.
