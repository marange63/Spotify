Good morning. It's Monday, August 10th, 2026. Today's story is that the AI power bill has stopped being a forecast and started clearing as an actual price.

Start with PJM, the grid operator that covers thirteen states from Chicago to Virginia. On July 14th, PJM ran its capacity auction for the 2028-2029 delivery year, and the results are now public. PJM itself put it plainly: the clearing price was $325 per megawatt-day, procuring just over 138,000 megawatts of generation at a total cost of $16.4 billion. That is the third straight year this auction has cleared at an elevated level, and PJM's own release names the cause. Quote: this auction saw "the continued trend of the addition of large data center loads to the load forecast that forms the basis of the reliability requirement." In plain terms, the grid operator is telling you, in a market-clearing number, that AI data centers are why your capacity costs are rising.

And this number is not free-floating. Last year's auction, for 2026-2027, hit a regulatory price cap of $329.17 per megawatt-day, a cap that exists only because of a political deal with Pennsylvania's governor. Without that cap, PJM says the price would have been nearly $389. So the modest step-down to $325 this year is not proof the market has found equilibrium. It's still landing inside a range that was artificially held down the year before.

So what does this mean. It means the marginal cost of running AI compute in PJM territory now includes a real, audited grid tax, not a hypothetical one, and that tax is showing up in both hyperscaler contracts and ordinary ratepayer bills at the same time. The beneficiaries are the companies that supply the generation PJM is scrambling to procure: independent power producers like Vistra, Talen, and Constellation, and gas turbine makers like GE Vernova sitting on years of backlog.

That auction result is the delivered, priced half of a story this show has been tracking for days: does regulatory authority keep up with the capacity crunch, or does the market outrun the rulemaking. Here's where the regulator stands. Back on June 18th, the Federal Reserve's electricity counterpart, FERC, issued what are called Section 206 show-cause orders to all six major U.S. grid operators, ordering each one to either justify its current interconnection rules or propose reforms covering how fast big data centers can plug into the grid. That deadline is not vague. It is August 17th, 2026, exactly 60 days out, one week from now.

There was also an earlier checkpoint, August 3rd, the last date an operator could ask for a 90-day extension to instead pursue its own separate filing. That date has already passed, and FERC has been explicit that it will not grant extensions automatically, warning it will "strongly disfavor requests for additional extensions." So the off-ramp most grid operators might have used to buy time has quietly closed already. What we don't know yet is whether any operator actually took that extension route before it shut, or whether any has filed a real reform proposal. Nobody has confirmed that publicly. We also don't know whether FERC's mandate will ultimately force data centers to bear the full cost of the transmission upgrades they trigger, or simply require more transparency about those costs. The two available accounts of the order disagree on how far it goes, so treat any claim that data centers will be forced to pay full freight as unconfirmed for now.

So what does this mean. The extension escape hatch is gone, the real compliance test is exactly one week away, and if it lands on the side of making large loads pay their own interconnection cost, that decision alone will help determine which states get the next wave of AI data centers and which get priced out. Authority is still chasing a cost the market has already priced.

Now the macro backdrop that finances all of this. Friday's July jobs report showed nonfarm payrolls actually fell, by 23,000, a shock against expectations, and May and June were revised down by a combined 103,000 jobs. Unemployment ticked down, but that's a function of people leaving the labor force, not a hiring boom. A weakening labor market is exactly the kind of data that pushes the Federal Reserve toward faster rate cuts in September. So what does this mean for the power story. Cheaper capital is arriving right as the cost of building grid capacity, gas turbines, and nuclear plants is going up. That's a tailwind for the utility and infrastructure capex this whole briefing is about, even as it sits awkwardly next to rising prices elsewhere in the economy.

Which brings us to that other price pressure: trade policy. Three weeks ago, the administration signed proclamations invoking Section 338 of the 1930 Tariff Act for the first time in its 96-year history — an authority even Franklin Roosevelt's administration considered and never used. The result: 50% tariffs on a wide range of Canadian goods, from autos and dairy to alcohol and even hockey sticks, taking effect August 19th. The Trade Representative's office puts total exposure at nearly $20 billion, about 5.2% of the $382 billion in goods the U.S. imported from Canada last year.

The headline rate is not the real story here. What matters more is that these tariffs apply even to goods that qualify under the USMCA trade agreement, meaning a treaty that companies built supply chains around no longer guarantees protection. So what does this mean. Reaching for a dormant, untested statute and overriding a standing trade agreement raises the risk premium on every North American manufacturing investment made on the assumption that USMCA rules were durable — a direct hit to the reshoring and friend-shoring thesis that's supposed to underpin a lot of that same grid and industrial buildout. And the timing compounds the tension from the jobs report: a new inflationary tariff wall lands August 19th just as a softening labor market is pushing the Fed toward cutting rates. That's a stagflationary combination bond markets will have to sit with, and Section 338's legal footing is untested enough that a court challenge is likely, so don't assume this tariff structure survives intact.

Put it together. This week's running theme has been that political authority keeps announcing capacity it hasn't yet delivered. Today, for the first time, one side of that ledger closed the gap: PJM's auction is a real, audited price, not a press release. FERC's interconnection authority is still one week from its own test. And layered on top, a labor market that's cooling and a trade policy that's escalating are pulling monetary policy and inflation in opposite directions at exactly the moment the country needs cheap, abundant capital to build the power grid AI depends on.
