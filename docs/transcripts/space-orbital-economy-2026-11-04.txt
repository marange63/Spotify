Good morning. It's Wednesday, November 4th, and here's where the orbital economy stands.

SpaceX reported its first quarterly results as a public company yesterday. The print is the cleanest evidence yet that Starlink has crossed from subsidized land grab into real profit engine. The rest of the company tells a messier story. SpaceX now reports Starlink as its Connectivity segment. Per SpaceX's release, as relayed by the Motley Fool, that segment generated five point two billion dollars in revenue, up sixty-nine percent year over year. Segment operating income doubled to two point four billion. By our own arithmetic, that's roughly a forty-six percent operating margin. So Starlink has passed the test that matters most for a constellation. This is recognized, profitable revenue, not an order book or a subscriber count dressed up as a story.

But don't extend that framing to the whole company. SpaceX's launch business, reported as the Space segment, had a rough quarter. The earnings exhibit SpaceX filed with the SEC puts Space revenue at one point eight billion dollars. That's up eighty-four percent from the prior quarter, but only six percent from a year ago. Operating income in that segment swung to a loss of seven million dollars, from a profit of two hundred fifty-nine million a year earlier. Call it breakeven, but the swing is the point. When you hear that SpaceX is printing cash, that's a Starlink story, not a company-wide one. The rocket business that built SpaceX contributed no operating profit this quarter.

Now the segment generating the most excitement: Super Intelligence, SpaceX's AI compute business. Revenue was seven point six billion dollars, up eight hundred thirty percent. That makes it bigger than Starlink by revenue. Reported operating income was four hundred thirty-one million dollars, a margin under six percent by our arithmetic. But read the fine print before calling that thin. The filing says the figure already absorbs one point two billion dollars of stock-based compensation tied to the Cursor acquisition. Add that charge back, and the segment earned about one point six billion, roughly a twenty-one percent margin. Again, that's our arithmetic. The filing doesn't make clear whether the charge recurs. So the honest read sits between the two numbers. Super Intelligence is not barely breaking even, but it's nowhere near Starlink's margin either. And the release says nothing about who its customers are, whether any are related parties, or what its backlog looks like. Treat the eight hundred thirty percent as a top-line story, not proof of an AI-infrastructure-grade profit center.

What is unambiguous is where the capital is going. Total capital expenditure in the quarter was nineteen point six billion dollars, more than the entire quarter's revenue. Super Intelligence alone took sixteen point four billion of that. Connectivity and Space took roughly one and a half billion each. That's a hyperscaler's AI buildout bolted onto a satellite company, not a satellite company dabbling in compute. Consolidated operating income was two point eight billion dollars, against a loss of two hundred seventy-three million a year ago. That's a genuine swing. But with capex above total revenue, SpaceX is almost certainly free-cash-flow negative this quarter. That's our inference from the capex line, not a disclosed figure.

Which is why the stock tells a different story than the income statement. Per the Motley Fool, shares traded around one hundred forty dollars on November 2nd. That's down thirty-eight percent from the two hundred twenty-six dollar high set on June 16th. Note the timing. That price predates the print, so it isn't the market's verdict on these results. Two explanations compete. One is a technical, time-limited overhang. Under the IPO's staggered lockup, up to one point three billion additional shares became eligible for sale following the earnings announcement. The other read is structural. The market may be re-rating SpaceX away from a satellite-and-telecom multiple and toward an AI-spender multiple. On that view, capex above revenue draws the same skepticism Wall Street has applied to hyperscalers funding their compute buildouts. If Super Intelligence keeps compounding, the space sector's flagship stock could end up trading with the Nasdaq AI complex more than with space peers. That's a scenario, not a forecast. The data can't tell us which explanation dominates, and both are live.

Now a callback. On Saturday, this desk argued that Starlink and launch cash flow are the collateral that lets SpaceX borrow cheaply for compute. We said a Starlink spin-out would pull that collateral out from under the AI build-out. Yesterday's print sharpens that tension. The launch half of the collateral contributed no operating profit this quarter. So, on our reading, Starlink alone is now the funding base for a bigger, faster-growing, far more capital-hungry segment. A spin-out, which has been speculated about but never announced, would remove the one segment generating real cash.

Now to the other side of the Starlink story: the rivals trying to copy its economics. They're stuck at the deployment stage. Amazon's Leo constellation, formerly Project Kuiper, has three hundred ninety-six satellites in orbit, per Broadband Breakfast. The planned initial build is more than three thousand. And Leo hasn't launched a single new satellite since early July. Even so, Amazon CEO Andy Jassy told analysts on October 29th that Leo remains on track for initial service this year. Last week, this desk noted that non-SpaceX dates were coming from analysts and trade press, not operators. Jassy's statement breaks that pattern. A chief executive has put his own name on a deadline. That makes management accountable, but it doesn't make the date credible. The first check is a ULA Vulcan launch carrying forty more Leo satellites, scheduled for November 13th. And "initial service" with roughly an eighth of the planned constellation deployed is a coverage-limited beta. It doesn't compete with Starlink's operational scale.

AST SpaceMobile faces the same constraint. Its target of forty-six satellites on orbit, a step toward global direct-to-cell service, has slipped out of this year. Per Advanced Television, AST now expects to get there by roughly the first half of 2027. The company says that timeline depends on the availability of Blue Origin's New Glenn rocket. AST needs ninety satellites on orbit just to begin global service, and it targets two hundred forty-three by the end of 2028. New Glenn hasn't flown since a vehicle exploded during a static-fire test in May. Blue Origin is reportedly targeting a November return to flight, but that date comes from trade press, not a company commitment. So AST's 2027 revenue timeline now carries Blue Origin's return-to-flight risk stacked on top of its own. One more New Glenn slip moves a public company's revenue start date.

Step back, and the pattern across Amazon and AST is the same. The binding constraint visible in their schedules is launch supply. We have no direct evidence on demand either way, so don't read this as demand proven. But launch is exactly the layer SpaceX owns. Every quarter these rivals slip, Starlink keeps compounding two point four billion dollars of quarterly segment operating income with no scaled low-Earth-orbit broadband competitor live. That pushes out the rivals' payback periods while Starlink's keeps running.

So where does that leave things? Genuinely inflecting: Starlink's profitability, now on a public income statement for the first time. Still open: whether Super Intelligence's growth turns into real operating leverage, and whether that Cursor charge recurs. Also open: whether SpaceX's launch business returns to profit, and whether Amazon and AST can convert operator promises into deployed, revenue-generating satellites. Watch three near-term catalysts. First, Starship Flight 15, which would attempt the first-ever catch of both booster and ship on the same flight. An October trade report pegged it for no earlier than November 8th, while later reporting we covered Saturday points to mid-November. SpaceX hasn't confirmed either. Second, the Vulcan launch with Amazon's next batch of Leo satellites on November 13th, alongside New Glenn's return-to-flight target. Third, SpaceX's forthcoming 10-Q, for any detail on Super Intelligence's customers and backlog that this release didn't disclose.

That's the state of orbit. Thanks for listening, and I'll catch you next time.
