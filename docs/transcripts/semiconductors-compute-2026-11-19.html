Good morning. It's Thursday, November 19th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

Nvidia's full earnings-call transcript is out, and it answers three questions the press release left open. What is Vera Rubin actually bringing in? What happened to China? And where does the margin slide stop? None of the answers is simple good news. After that, we'll weigh Intel's first signed outside customer for its eighteen-A process, and what that signature is worth.

Start with product. Rubin generated nearly twenty billion dollars of revenue in its first shipping quarter. Until this call, Nvidia had said only that Rubin was shipped and ramping, with no dollar figure attached. Nvidia calls it the fastest product ramp in its history, and that's Nvidia's own characterization. Against the hundred-and-eight-billion-dollar data-center line, Rubin is already a little under a fifth of the total. On the ladder from announcement to sustained adoption, it has moved from shipped to quantified.

Now discount it properly. This is one quarter of initial fill. First-quarter ramps typically carry initial builds and channel loading that don't repeat at the same pace. "Fastest ever" also partly reflects scale. Nvidia's base and its customer commitments are far bigger than at the Hopper or Blackwell launches. So a record ramp is partly a size effect, not purely execution. Management also gave no Rubin-versus-Blackwell split for the quarter ahead. Its only forward line was that sequential growth comes from AI demand and "the continued ramp of Vera Rubin."

On supply, management said only, "We've secured memory, we've secured advanced packaging." It never named CoWoS or HBM4. That's reassurance, not detail. But it carries a competitive implication, and this part is our inference. The memory and packaging Nvidia has secured come out of the same pool that AMD and the hyperscaler custom-chip programs are drawing on. A twenty-billion-dollar Rubin quarter tilts that allocation further toward Nvidia. So for the challengers, the binding constraint is capacity they can't get, not just chip design.

That supply line also bears on Nvidia's leadership. The company is being run by an interim chief executive. Colette Kress, Nvidia's chief financial officer, took the top job after Jensen Huang resigned last week, and she still holds the CFO role. On Monday, this desk argued that Nvidia's supply position runs on signed contracts, not on Huang's personal pull with suppliers. A twenty-billion-dollar Rubin debut under Kress is a second straight data point for that reading. The so-what: Rubin is a quantified product now, but not yet a proven run-rate.

The second gap the call filled is China. Nvidia confirmed it is "no longer shipping licensed Hopper Data Center products to China." In the third quarter alone, those sales came to two point four billion dollars. That's a change in kind, not degree. Before the call, the China zero was an assumption in Nvidia's outlook. Now it's an operating fact, with a measured run-rate behind what was lost.

Now run the arithmetic. The fourth-quarter guide is a hundred and thirty billion dollars, against a hundred and sixteen billion just reported. By our math, that's roughly fourteen billion dollars of sequential growth, after absorbing the loss of that China business. It's the cleanest evidence yet that demand outside China is carrying the guide on its own.

So why did the shipments stop? Nvidia didn't say. Last Saturday, we reported that Congress and the FBI were scrutinizing the export licenses that let Nvidia's H200, a Hopper part, into China. A policy-driven halt would fit that backdrop. So would two other readings. Beijing may be discouraging purchases. Or demand may simply have thinned for a part two generations old, now that Rubin is shipping. All three are inference, and the transcript doesn't let anyone pick a winner.

What we can say cleanly is the second-order point. With China at zero in practice, any future reopening is upside that Nvidia's guide doesn't include. Meanwhile, that market defaults to domestic accelerator makers. That was true on paper before. Now it's true in operation.

Growth without China is one half of the picture. What it costs to deliver is the other. Until this call, Nvidia's margin step-down had no stated end point. Now it has one. Management said it expects the fourth quarter "to mark the bottom of gross margins." After that, it sees margins improving to a range of seventy-two to seventy-three percent in fiscal twenty-eight.

Hold those two numbers apart, because they're easy to conflate. The fourth-quarter guide is seventy-one to seventy-two percent, GAAP and non-GAAP alike. That's the trough investors are being asked to look through. The seventy-two to seventy-three range describes fiscal twenty-eight, a year out. So the recovery management has sketched is about one point off the bottom, spread across a full year. That's a slow climb, not a snap-back.

As for why margins are falling, Nvidia still hasn't said. The closest the call came was a backward reference to "a significant change in terms of the cost of supply." Management also said the fourth-quarter guide reflects customer purchase orders it is honoring. Neither HBM pricing nor Rubin ramp costs was named.

Here's our inference, and it ties back to supply. Nvidia's only margin explanation is the cost of supply, and its only supply comment is that memory and packaging are secured. Put together, that suggests Nvidia may be paying up to lock in scarce inputs. On Monday, this desk called 2027 accelerator demand constrained by availability, not by economics. A margin squeeze that comes from input costs, while revenue keeps climbing, fits that reading. But it's our reading, not management's words. Weigh the messenger too. This forecast comes from Colette Kress, the interim CEO who is also the CFO, on her first call running the company. The so-what: Nvidia has put a date on the bottom, but still not a cause, and the market should price it as guidance, not evidence.

Here's the bridge to our second story, and it's our inference. A twenty-billion-dollar Rubin quarter is also a twenty-billion-dollar pull on one foundry's leading-edge and packaging capacity. That foundry is TSMC. So the scale of the ramp raises the strategic value of any credible leading-edge option outside Taiwan. That's where Intel comes in.

At its TomorrowX summit in Austin yesterday, Intel chief executive Lip-Bu Tan said the company "just signed the first customer" for its eighteen-A process. Intel's technology chief, Pushkar Ranade, clarified that the customer signed for the eighteen-A-P variant. That variant entered risk production in June.

Track the arc. On Monday, this desk said Intel Foundry had no confirmed external leading-edge customer. On Tuesday, we corrected that. Tesla had agreed to use Intel's fourteen-A process. But fourteen-A is a later node, and it was still at an early design kit. Now there's a signature on eighteen-A-P, a node already in risk production. That shortens the distance between Intel's customer claims and actual wafers. It's genuine movement up the ladder, from risk production to signed.

But score it on the right dimension. This is a customer-relationship gain, not a production-leadership gain. The customer isn't named. There's no volume, no revenue figure, and no production timeline. Intel also hasn't said whether this is a committed design-in or an early engineering engagement. For a foundry trying to prove it can hold hyperscaler-scale business, those are very different things.

That's why the missing name matters more than the signature. A hyperscaler ASIC program would make Intel a credible leading-edge option outside Taiwan. A small test-chip engagement would not. The announcement doesn't let anyone tell which. And outside estimates of eighteen-A yield conflict and don't state their method, so they can't settle the question either.

There's also a less flattering reading. The signing was announced from a summit stage, by a CEO who needs the foundry story to work. Intel is financing capacity ahead of proven outside demand, including October's equity raise. So an unnamed customer is a signal to future customers and capital providers, as much as a commercial milestone. Either way, it does nothing for 2027 accelerator supply. That point, from Monday and Tuesday, still holds. The so-what: Intel has a signature, not yet a second source.

So here's where things stand, dimension by dimension. Product: Rubin is quantified, but its durability isn't proven. Supply: Nvidia says memory and packaging are secured, and on our read that leaves less for the challengers. China: an assumed zero has become an operating zero, with the cause still unstated. Margins: a dated trough, a slow recovery, and no stated driver. Manufacturing alternatives: Intel has a signed customer, without a name or a wafer. As for who gained ground, on our read, Nvidia gained on product evidence and on supply allocation. Intel gained, provisionally, on customer relationships. And China's domestic accelerator makers gained by default.

Three things to watch. First, a Rubin-specific number or mix for the quarter now underway. Second, any statement from Nvidia or Washington on why the China shipments actually stopped. Third, whether Intel puts a name, a wafer commitment, or a production date behind its eighteen-A-P customer.
