Cautious Optimism Briefings

AI in Biopharma R&D

Transcript · September 7, 2026

Good morning. It's September 7th, and this is your briefing on artificial intelligence across the biopharmaceutical value chain.

Yesterday I corrected something I'd been saying all week. I'd been telling you that none of the AI-drug-discovery pipeline touches the expensive end of development, and that was wrong. Insilico's rentosertib and Generate's GB-0895 are both in registrational trials. The correction was right. But it was incomplete, because I didn't have the number it needed to sit inside. Today I do.

An analysis presented at ASCO this year counted 117 AI-enabled therapeutic assets, across 63 companies, that have entered interventional human trials. Let me handle the sourcing before the substance, because it matters. That tally traces to a named conference abstract in the Journal of Clinical Oncology, with data current as of the first of December last year. The abstract itself sits behind a paywall I could not get through, so I am relaying the figures as a secondary source reports them, not as numbers I have read in the primary document. Treat them as well-attributed and nine months stale, not as independently audited.

With that caveat in place, here is the shape of the funnel. Of those 117 assets, 60 had completed Phase 1. Only 8 had completed Phase 2. That is the denominator this beat has been missing. And note the word completed. Completing a Phase 2 is not the same as passing one. Eight completions tells you nothing yet about eight outcomes — it tells you only that the field has arrived at the door of the room where efficacy gets decided, eight times.

So yesterday's correction was about direction, not scale. Two registrational programs are real, and I'm not walking that back. I also can't tell you that rentosertib and GB-0895 sit inside that count of eight, because the tally's data cutoff predates where those programs are now and I have no line-by-line list. What I can tell you is the structural point: the field's evidence base for whether AI actually improves clinical outcomes is still sitting almost entirely upstream of the stage where that question gets answered.

Now, the field has a standard defense against that thinness, and you've probably heard it. AI-designed drugs are supposedly clearing Phase 1 at 80 to 90 percent, against a historical industry rate around half that. I want to be precise about what that claim is and isn't. I could not find a primary study, a denominator, or a published methodology behind it anywhere. It traces only to aggregator articles citing unspecified industry data. So I am not going to state it to you as a fact, and you shouldn't repeat it as one either.

What I can give you is the audited half of that comparison. A peer-reviewed phase-transition analysis of conventional, non-AI candidates finds that Phase 1 transition rates have declined from roughly 70 percent to roughly 50 percent over the past two decades. The Phase 2 transition rate for the 2011-to-2019 window was 28.8 percent, the lowest of any phase. That is the wall every AI-originated asset eventually has to walk into.

Here's why the Phase 1 claim would be close to uninformative even if it were audited. Phase 1 tests tolerability and dosing. Phase 2 tests whether you had the right target in the first place. AI's strongest and most demonstrated contributions today — structure prediction, molecule design, property optimization — act on the part of the funnel the industry was already reasonably good at. A high Phase 1 pass rate would mostly tell you about medicinal chemistry, about dose selection, and about which molecules a sponsor was confident enough to put into humans at all. It would tell you very little about whether the underlying biology was right. And notice the pattern: this is the same self-scoring problem I've been flagging on this beat all week, now showing up inside the statistic the field uses to defend its clinical record. No denominator, no named methodology, nothing you can check.

So if the efficacy evidence is this thin, what does the market actually pay for a validated AI-derived target today? On September 1st I told you Recursion's collaboration with Genentech carries a disclosed ceiling running past twelve billion dollars across up to forty programs, and that the market had been pricing a conversion curve with no observable points on it. It has one now. Genentech exercised its option on the first validated neuroscience target out of that collaboration, and the milestone attached to that exercise was three million dollars.

Three million dollars against a twelve-billion-dollar ceiling is not a rounding error. It is the market's own discount on conversion probability, written into the contract. Read it either way you like. The bear case is that an early milestone is a contractual formality dressed up as scientific validation. The bull case is that value is deliberately back-loaded into later development and sales milestones, so small first payments are structural rather than diagnostic. Either way, the headline deal size tells you almost nothing about what is changing hands right now.

The second-order point is the one I'd actually trade on. If option conversion across those forty programs turns out to run at single digits, then the honest description of this business is a data-licensing contract with option upside, not a pipeline. That caps the multiple, and it means the number to track is dilution rather than program count. For context on the scale involved, the same quarter that carried the three-million-dollar milestone was one in which Recursion booked $7.67 million of revenue and missed consensus by roughly 37 percent. I'd resist reading that miss as evidence about the science — lumpy partnership revenue at a platform company is a recognition-timing artifact. Read it as evidence about the financing clock.

That brings me to where the next money is going, which is a subject this beat has never covered. On September 2nd, Japan's Ono Pharmaceutical signed a drug discovery partnership with Aitia, a causal-AI company whose platform builds what it calls Gemini Digital Twins — models of disease biology assembled from patient clinical and omics data through causal simulation rather than pattern-matching. Ono takes an exclusive worldwide option to research, develop and commercialize candidates against whatever targets Aitia identifies in neurological disease.

Neither company disclosed an upfront, a milestone schedule, or a deal value, so nothing here can be sized. I think the absence of a number is itself the information. An exclusive option is the cheapest possible way for a large pharma to buy exposure to a method that has never produced a clinical-stage asset. And that is the honest status of causal target identification: no digital-twin-derived molecule has reached the clinic to be judged, so there is no prospective validation to point at. The method's core claim is that it recovers causation rather than correlation from observational patient data, which is precisely the claim observational data is worst at supporting.

But note the direction of travel, because it breaks this week's pattern. For six days the capital story on this beat has been molecule design — the overwhelming majority of disclosed financing going to two design shops. This is a large pharma paying instead for disease-mechanism modelling and target identification, upstream of chemistry, in neurology, where target validity rather than molecule quality is usually what kills a program. Money is starting to move toward the layer where the Phase 2 failures actually originate. If that continues, value accrues to whoever holds longitudinal patient omics and clinical data, not to whoever runs a design model on top of it.

On real-world data, one line, because I've covered it at length this week and I'd rather tell you nothing moved than restate a standing read. Nothing moved. No new regulatory position, no new dated deal, no new measured outcome on whether federated architectures actually solve the fragmentation problem. It remains the one mechanism that could cut cost at the expensive end, and it remains unproven.

I want to close with something that isn't an AI story at all, because it's a useful check on how much any of this changes the picture. Over the past two weeks Novartis halted its autoimmune CAR-T trials after three cases of a rare immune complication, IEC-HS, with fatal outcomes. Bristol Myers Squibb separately paused enrollment in its own autoimmune CAR-T program after what it describes as transient and reversible inflammatory events. Those are materially different events and I won't merge them into one narrative. Some analysts suspect the rapid-manufacturing technology shared by both therapies is implicated, but that is speculation, not an established finding. What matters here is that nothing in the AI stack — not structure prediction, not trial-design agents, not real-world-data platforms — had any purchase on that outcome. It is a biology and manufacturing failure, and it sits exactly where the cost of drug development actually lives.

So, pulling it together. The realistic near-term effect of AI on clinical success rates is not yet visible in the data, because the data barely exists. Eight completed Phase 2 assets against 117 in trials is not a sample you can conclude anything from. R&D spend, on the same logic, is being reallocated toward earlier and cheaper decisions rather than reduced, and today's numbers are the reason why: the savings all sit at the wide end of the funnel and the costs all sit at the narrow one. The honest test to watch isn't the next funding round or the next benchmark paper. It's the ninth Phase 2 completion, and the tenth, and whether that trickle ever produces a rate that beats 28.8 percent. Until then, the money is telling you more than the marketing is — three million dollars for a validated target, and an undisclosed option for an unvalidated method upstream of it.

That's the briefing for today. I'll talk to you tomorrow.
