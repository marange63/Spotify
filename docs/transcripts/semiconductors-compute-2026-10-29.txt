Good morning. It's Thursday, October 29th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

On Tuesday, this desk carried TrendForce's preview of a record SK hynix operating margin near 78 percent. We warned that one quarter's print couldn't settle whether the memory boom is structural or just the top of a cycle. SK hynix reported yesterday. It came in higher, at an implied 80 percent margin. Then management tried to settle the argument anyway, by pointing past the end of the decade.

Start with the horizon, because that's the real news. On the earnings call, SK hynix management said DRAM and HBM volume for the next three years, through 2029, is, in their words, "effectively sold out." Its CFO went further. He said the company expects supply to fall short of demand through 2030. Those quotes come from a third-party transcript of the call, but they are the company's words, not a press paraphrase. Compare that with yesterday's baseline. This desk carried Micron's CEO saying a large part of 2027 HBM is already sold out. In one day, the scarcity horizon a memory maker is willing to name has moved from 2027 to 2030.

Now listen to how SK hynix qualified its own claim. On the same call, management said volume secured under long-term agreements accounts for just over half of total capacity for 2027 and 2028. So "sold out" blends signed contracts with demand still under discussion. The headline and the fine print say different things. And the fine print is what tells you how much pricing power is actually locked in.

What does that do to buyers? A seller claiming a deficit through 2030 is telling hyperscalers and labs to sign long now, before the next round of price talks. Every buyer that does so converts SK hynix's forecast into contracted revenue. On our read, that is the point of the 2029 line.

Next, the scale of the quarter. Revenue crossed 100 trillion won for the first time. Operating profit rose more than sevenfold from a year earlier, to a record 83.33 trillion won. That's a little over 62 billion dollars, and it's what implies the roughly 80 percent margin. The memory complex rallied on it. SK hynix rose as much as 8 percent, and Micron and SanDisk each gained more than 4 percent.

So which is it, structure or cycle? Here's the structural case. SK hynix has signed agreements covering more than half its capacity into 2028. Management also says it has finalized next year's HBM volume and prices with major customers. And it's now naming a 2030 horizon. Here's the cyclical case. An 80 percent margin is exactly what the peak of an ordinary memory cycle looks like. Peak margins pull in capacity. SK hynix itself said its 2027 capex is expected to "increase significantly" compared with 2026, though it gave no figure. And a multi-year deficit claim is precisely what a seller says when it wants buyers to lock in price at the top. The deciding evidence is whether 2028 through 2030 contract prices get fixed. On what the company disclosed, only next year's pricing is described as finalized.

The same quarter has a capital-allocation tell. SK hynix is pursuing roughly 100 trillion won of shareholder returns for fiscal 2026. The board also approved a fresh 20 trillion won share acquisition this quarter. A company certain of a deficit through 2030 might be expected to pour that cash into capacity. Instead it is returning cash at scale while keeping its capex guidance qualitative. Read one way, that's confidence. Read another way, it's a management team that doesn't want to be the one that overbuilds into the down-cycle.

The number investors mostly skipped is the one that deserves attention. Net income was 39.8 trillion won. That's less than half of operating profit, and well short of the roughly 61 trillion won consensus that TradingKey cited. CFO Kim Woo-hyun explained the gap on the call. There was an 11.6 trillion won foreign-exchange loss. There was also a 22.9 trillion won valuation loss on investment assets. Together those two items are larger than the company's entire net non-operating expense, so other items partly offset them. The shortfall isn't an operating problem. It's currency and portfolio losses sitting below the line. The market rallied anyway. The takeaway: the operating margin is clean, but the earnings that reach shareholders are now hostage to the won and to the value of whatever SK hynix holds on its books.

If memory is setting the price, the next question is who pays it. Nvidia's answer arrived on Monday. Jensen Huang is pitching Vera Rubin at roughly 40 billion dollars of revenue opportunity per gigawatt of deployed capacity. That compares with about 25 billion for the outgoing Grace Blackwell generation. It is Nvidia's own sales estimate, not booked revenue or backlog. But it means buyers are being asked to spend about 60 percent more per unit of power.

Set that against Tuesday's story. Nvidia quietly cut Rubin's listed HBM4 bandwidth per GPU by about 13 percent. So the price per gigawatt is going up while the memory spec on each GPU went down. This next part is our inference. Some of what Nvidia is charging per gigawatt looks like memory scarcity being passed through to the customer. That makes the memory vendor, not the GPU maker, the one setting terms at the margin. The same squeeze reaches AMD and the custom-ASIC programs, because they all buy the same HBM stacks. Be clear about the competitive dimension here. The 40 billion figure is evidence of Nvidia's pricing power and customer relationships. It is not proof of architecture leadership.

Rubin is reaching customers, too. A joint Nvidia and Oracle release on Tuesday describes Oracle Cloud scaling Rubin systems and adding Nvidia's Vera CPU. The release is a deployment claim, not a shipment count, and it gives no volume figure we could verify.

And the buyers paying that higher price are increasingly borrowing to do it. Recall SpaceX's roughly 40 billion dollar lease for Rubin chips, arranged through an entity managed by Apollo Global Management. Yesterday this desk reported it as a binding commitment that hadn't closed. It is still expected to close before year-end. This week Fitch kept SpaceX's rating unchanged. According to 24/7 Wall St., Fitch said the lease "finances compute capacity already in Fitch's forecast." That's not new comfort. It means the leverage was expected, not that it's safe.

Behind the lease sits real revenue. SpaceX's Super Intelligence compute business brought in 7.6 billion dollars in the third quarter. A year earlier it was 800 million. That's the classic compute-constrained buyer. It has demand it can't serve, so paying more per gigawatt can be rational. But an economics-constrained buyer, one whose returns depend on revenue per token, faces a harder sum at 40 billion per gigawatt. And growth of nearly tenfold from a small base is exactly the kind that doesn't survive a demand pause.

The broader pattern is that higher capex per gigawatt pushes more of the buildout onto leases and private credit, rather than operating cash. It's the same trend as yesterday's lead, where Broadcom put its own balance sheet behind Anthropic's chip financing. The memory supplier's pricing power is now a direct input into how much of this buildout has to be borrowed.

One more read on the same cycle comes today. Samsung pre-announced record third-quarter operating profit of 107.4 trillion won, up 782.5 percent from a year earlier. That's a company-wide figure that includes non-memory businesses, so don't set it against SK hynix's number as a like-for-like memory comparison. Samsung's divisional results are due out today. They are the real test of whether SK hynix's margin reflects its own HBM position or pricing power across the whole memory industry.

So, the watch list. Samsung's divisional breakdown today. Intel's third-quarter results after today's close, where the question remains whether volume sits behind the Tesla 14A win. AMD on November 3rd. China's pause on expanded rare-earth export controls, which is due to lapse on November 10th. And whether SpaceX's Apollo-backed lease actually closes before year-end.
