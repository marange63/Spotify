Good morning, this is The Throughline for Monday, August 17th, 2026. Today's headline: private capital is moving decisively on the enterprise franchises public markets spent all year discounting — Electronic Arts' record fifty-five-billion-dollar take-private has closed and Silver Lake is now circling Workday — but the terms tell you as much as the size, because it's patient sovereign equity, not leverage, writing the biggest checks.

Start with the macro backdrop. Capital Markets Cross-Asset Radar has the S&P 500's 27th record close of the year resting on a breadth illusion: strip out Alphabet and Amazon, and the record earnings surprise FactSet flagged falls from 29.2 percent to 10.9 percent, meaning the rally's durability now hinges on Nvidia's next print, not broad participation. Digital Money picks up the same theme in the plumbing underneath the rally, with Circle's Arc blockchain going to public mainnet September 16th and rival tokenized-deposit networks from JPMorgan, Citi, Bank of America and Wells Fargo aiming for 2027 — a race over who settles on-chain dollars, not who issues them. Private Credit and the Corporate Credit Cycle complicates the "cycle is turning" story the Journal ran last week: Blue Owl's nonperforming rate really did hit a five-year high, but Ares and Blackstone Secured Lending's own filings look closer to normal or improving, and FS KKR's board buying back its own stock at a 40 percent discount to its stated net asset value is the more damning tell on whether the marks can be trusted at all. Private Equity and the Deal Economy rounds out the macro tour with the EA close: at fifty-five billion dollars it's the largest LBO ever, but it ran nearly two-thirds equity and one-third debt, financed by Saudi Arabia's sovereign fund rather than a leveraged-loan desk — the record isn't a sign leverage is back, it's a sign patient sovereign capital is substituting for it.

Now the AI and compute stack, where the same "who captures the value" question runs underneath every story. Semiconductors and Compute Infrastructure has demand and supply both reacting to the same memory bottleneck: Cerebras wafer-scale silicon just won real production distribution inside OpenAI's own API running fourteen times normal speed, while Samsung is weighing converting a fab to churn out two-nanometer HBM logic dies for future Nvidia demand. Frontier AI Lab Competition has capability frozen at the top for about a week now, so Alibaba and Moonshot are both walking back free open weights with licensing tolls on large commercial users, while Anthropic made Claude Code's Auto Mode the default, trading manual safety review for agentic speed. AI Products and Applications has Google's newly-priced Gemini 3.7 Flash undercutting Claude and GPT-5.6 Terra on cost, even though independent benchmarks show it merely tied, not ahead — and Anthropic separately extended its compliance audit trail to cover agentic coding sessions, the unglamorous infrastructure that actually unlocks regulated-industry deployment. Enterprise Technology closes that leg with the Workday story: Silver Lake's take-private talks sent the stock up nearly 18 percent, an implied bet that seat-based software survives the agent transition for franchises with defensible cash flow, even as Microsoft's 30-million-seat Copilot growth shows the category isn't dying broadly — it's being repriced franchise by franchise.

Turning to the applied frontier. Robotics and Physical AI has government, not the lab, setting the pace this week: a new 100 percent tariff on large drones targets China's near-monopoly on the physical layer, while Zoox started charging fares in Las Vegas under the first federal exemption letting a robotaxi run with no steering wheel at all. Space and the Orbital Economy has SpaceX's first public earnings revealing where the capital actually goes: AI and cloud infrastructure pulled in eighty-six percent of an $18.4 billion capex quarter, while Starship — the program everyone assumed Starlink profits were funding — got barely a thirteenth of that and is still losing money. AI in Biopharma R&D closes the tour with the sharpest version of today's pattern: Leica Biosystems won a genuine FDA clearance for AI-assisted pathology quality control, but its headline 24-percent and 69-percent improvement figures trace to a single unpublished company study, while Tempus AI's foundation-model deal with AstraZeneca and its Personalis acquisition show real revenue accumulating at the diagnostics layer — even as AI-native drug-discovery valuations, like Chai Discovery's tripled 3.8-billion-dollar mark, keep climbing without a single clinical readout to back them.

One thread is now impossible to ignore, because this is the fifth straight day this show has tracked it. A marketed number sitting next to a harder one keeps showing up as the organizing test across almost every domain: the S&P's record close against its two-stock breadth, Blue Owl's five-year-high delinquency against FSK's own discounted buybacks, Gemini's benchmark marketing against its independent tie, and Leica's clearance against its single unpublished study. Five days running, the pattern hasn't been that these headline numbers are false — it's that whenever a harder number exists to check them, it keeps landing softer than the story built on top of it.
