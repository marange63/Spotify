Good morning. It's Saturday, October 17th, 2026, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

Here's the thesis up front. US chip stocks have lost something like two trillion dollars in value since last week, but nothing in the physical supply chain broke. What the market repriced was who can pay for the AI buildout, not what's scarce inside it.

Start with the rout itself. Reuters estimated that US chip stocks were set to lose about two trillion dollars in market value in the selloff since the prior week. Treat that as approximate. It was a mid-move estimate, not a settled close-to-close number. The firmer figure is the index. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell about ten percent over six straight down sessions.

Two triggers did the damage, and the reporting suggests they landed in sequence rather than together. The first was rates. On Wednesday, October 14th, the September consumer price index came in at 3.7 percent year over year, against 3.6 percent expected. Core CPI rose 2.6 percent, against estimates of 2.5. Yahoo Finance's live coverage tied that session's chip selloff specifically to growing expectations that the Fed may have to hike. The second trigger was financing. A chart of neocloud providers' bond yields circulated on social media, and CoreWeave's shares fell more than sixteen percent. Note what that was. It was a reaction to a chart, not to anything CoreWeave disclosed. CoreWeave isn't a chipmaker, but it is a leveraged buyer of GPU capacity, so its credit is a direct read on how the marginal buildout gets funded. So this wasn't one shock. It was a rates leg first, then a financing-fear leg compounding it.

And that financing scare fits a pattern. It's the third valuation shock in under two weeks, and each has rested on thinner evidence than the last. On October 8th, a Financial Times report put OpenAI's annualized revenue well short of the figure other outlets had been reporting. On October 12th, Broadcom CEO Hock Tan said a lot of investors set extreme expectations without knowing what they own, almost like gambling in a casino. Tan sells AI silicon for a living, so that was a remark about investor expectations, not about Broadcom's order book. The same day, China's CXMT claimed progress on a new DRAM cell architecture, a claim no one has independently verified. This week, the trigger was a chart on social media. The evidentiary bar for moving these valuations keeps falling. The thinner the trigger, the more the move tells you about fragile positioning, and the less it tells you about AI demand.

And the damage was worst exactly where demand looks most secure. On Thursday, October 15th, South Korea's Kospi closed about eight percent lower. Samsung Electronics closed down nine percent that day, and SK hynix down eleven. That came on top of Samsung's CXMT-driven drop earlier in the week. Memory had one more worry of its own. The same live coverage that tied the selloff to rates also pointed to fears of rising Chinese memory competition behind the extended declines in Micron and SK hynix. So Seoul's pain this week has several plausible sources, tangled together. For a sector whose story is built on memory scarcity, that's an uncomfortable result.

Now set that tape against the supply side. The two companies furthest upstream both reported earlier this week, and neither said the physical bottleneck has eased. TSMC raised its 2026 revenue growth forecast to about 44 percent. ASML said its planned 2027 and 2028 supply won't fully meet customer demand, though that's a seller's claim with no bookings figure behind it. And no chip supplier we can find has cut a forecast since the selloff began. The gap between what suppliers say and what the tape prices is widening.

That gap is the cleanest illustration yet of a distinction this show keeps drawing. Demand can be constrained by compute availability, or it can be constrained by economics. Every supply-side print says the first constraint is as tight as ever. This week the market started pricing the second, without any change in the first. That's the risk worth naming for investors. A funding scare can easily get extrapolated into a structural demand break, and so far the evidence supports only the funding scare.

That brings us to memory, which is the week's real analytical turn. Micron has already locked in contracts for most of its 2027 HBM output, with HBM prices expected to more than double next year. That's not new, so take it as the premise rather than the news. Yet memory was the layer that fell hardest. That's the opposite of what a simple demand-cooling story predicts. If buyers were walking away, the least-contracted layer should suffer most, not the most pre-sold one.

Here's the tension. Contracts record what buyers promised. Equity prices reflect whether investors believe those buyers can keep paying. This show has leaned toward the equity market as the better signal, and Seoul's tape this week puts that lean to the test. But be honest about what the tape can and can't tell us.

Three explanations compete, and the evidence can't yet pick a winner. The first is counterparty risk. A locked 2027 contract is only as good as the buyer's ability to pay for it, and this week's financing jitters raised exactly that question. The second is rates and crowding. Korean memory names had run hardest into this rally. They sell first in any broad de-risking, regardless of anything memory-specific.

The third is competition inside HBM itself. Samsung has said its third-quarter HBM4 revenue should more than triple from the second quarter. It also expects HBM4 to exceed 60 percent of its second-half HBM sales. That's company guidance from a small base, so it says more about mix than about scale. Still, it targets the incumbent. Counterpoint Research estimates SK hynix took about 50 percent of HBM revenue in the second quarter, against 33 for Samsung and 18 for Micron. Other trackers put SK hynix higher, so treat that split as one estimate. If Samsung is gaining ground in HBM4, that alone could explain SK hynix falling eleven percent on Thursday against Samsung's nine, with no financing story required. These readings don't cancel each other out. The market voted, but it didn't say why.

Here's what that implies, and mark it as inference. Expect a split between financial and physical bottlenecks. If neocloud and other debt-funded buyers face tighter capital, the marginal GPU-leasing buildout slows. HBM and leading-edge lithography stay just as scarce. Scarcity rents should hold best at suppliers whose customers are hyperscalers with strong balance sheets. They should weaken fastest where demand runs through leveraged neocloud financing.

The memory contract book is where those two bottlenecks meet. A locked book like Micron's 2027 HBM commitments carries counterparty concentration that a spot market doesn't. It is only as durable as its weakest buyer's funding. That's the channel through which stress in AI credit could reach chip earnings, not just chip multiples. So far, it has reached only the multiples.

The next hard data point is SK hynix's third-quarter report, due October 28th. It will be a reported quarter rather than guidance, landing after this rout. Watch whether SK hynix's HBM share and pricing hold against Samsung's HBM4 push. And watch whether any chip supplier, anywhere, actually trims a forecast. Until one does, this is a repricing of who pays, not of what's scarce.
