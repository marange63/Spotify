Capital Markets Cross-Asset Radar

Transcript · August 22, 2026

Good morning, it's Saturday, August 22nd, and this is your Capital Markets Radar.

We've been running one question on this show for three days: is Treasury's buyback intervention actually removing duration, or is it just signal without flow. On Wednesday, Treasury doubled its long-bond buyback operations, and the 30-year fell nine basis points to 5.196 percent, with the 10-year down almost six to 4.647 percent. On Thursday, Bessent escalated, naming no ceiling on how far the buybacks could go, on the argument that yields don't reflect the underlying fundamentals. We said gold would be the tell for whether that relief held. By Friday's close we have the answer, and it's a clean round trip. The 30-year finished the week at 5.27 percent. That is roughly seven basis points above the low the announcement bought, and essentially back where the long bond stood before Treasury said anything at all. The 10-year did worse than a round trip. At 4.73 percent it closed above its pre-announcement level outright.

Don't over-read that as "the buyback failed." Nothing has actually been bought yet. The operations don't start until September 9th and run through November 4th. What round-tripped is the announcement effect, not the program. What the round trip does tell you is how shallow the conviction behind that relief was. An intervention this size bought the long end three days of calm.

Here's the split verdict that makes this the lead. Bonds failed the test. Gold and the dollar did not. Gold traded at 4,622 dollars an ounce on Friday, up over five and a half percent on the week and its best level in three months. The dollar index sat near 98.8. Against Thursday that's roughly flat, but on the week it was tracking a loss of close to one percent, and over the trailing month it's down about 2.3 percent. The currency kept drifting in the debasement direction even in the week the bond relief died.

That leaves two separate clocks running. There's a duration clock, where intervention buys you days before the bond market tests it again. And there's a fiscal-credibility clock, where the intervention itself gets read as confirmation that Washington is worried, and that clock doesn't reset just because yields round-trip. Notice the combination underneath it. Nominal yields are back up while gold rises and the dollar falls, which means real yields are climbing at the same moment demand for a zero-yielding hedge is climbing. Normally, higher real yields suppress that demand. Both rising at once tells you the market has stopped treating this as a rates story and started pricing fiscal and currency risk as a standing feature of the regime.

There's a funding wrinkle in that worth holding into September 9th. A weaker dollar alongside sticky-high long yields cuts both ways for the foreign accounts that buy Treasuries. It raises their cost of dollar funding while it lowers the return once they hedge the currency. That is historically the setup that thins foreign official demand at auction, and it arrives right as Treasury starts actually buying at the long end.

One honest complication before we leave gold: don't hang the whole move on debasement. Brent crude is also up roughly six percent for a second straight week on Middle East risk, and some of that geopolitical premium is almost certainly sitting inside the same 4,622 print. Call this directional evidence for the debasement thesis, not clean single-cause confirmation.

That same impulse shows up, levered, in crypto. Bitcoin closed the week around 77,300 dollars, up 22 percent, its strongest week in nearly two years. Ether did better still, up 29 percent, breaking above 2,430 dollars, with Ether ETF net assets reaching 13.58 billion dollars, the highest since May. Yesterday on this show we flagged a single 517 million dollar Bitcoin ETF inflow day and told you to watch the next flow print rather than the price. We got it, twice more. Thursday brought 606 million dollars into Bitcoin ETFs, Friday another 307 million, alongside a parallel four-day, 512 million dollar run into Ether ETFs. The follow-through is real. Those daily figures come from different flow trackers, so treat them as confirming prints rather than one clean series.

But the composition problem hasn't gone away. On Friday, IBIT alone took 239 million dollars of that 307 million Bitcoin total, roughly three-quarters of the flow. On the Ether side, BlackRock's ETHA supplied about 173 million of Thursday's 221 million dollar haul. Two products from one issuer are doing most of the work on both legs. Bernstein is calling the move liquidity-driven, and even Bernstein hedges it, telling clients that bitcoin historically has had a positive reaction to liquidity expansion. That's a pattern match, not a mechanism. A trade this concentrated turns off as fast as it turned on. It takes one allocator's committee, not a broad market, to reverse it.

Now to where the macro turns into a margin story. All three major indexes fell on the week even though Friday closed green, with the S&P down 1.4 percent and the Nasdaq down 2 percent. Underneath that, a much bigger rotation has been building for two months. The biotech complex is up more than 20 percent since mid-June while chip stocks are down roughly 20 percent over the same stretch. That's a forty-point relative gap hiding under nearly flat index prints, and it's a directional comparison — the market wrap behind it doesn't name the specific ETF or chip index.

Pair that rotation with the memory market. A single DDR4 chip hit a record 42 dollars and 45 cents on August 7th, more than double May's 20-dollar level, with supply partners guiding customers to expect further increases of 10 to 20 percent a month through year-end. That's a spot chip price, not a blended contract cost, so don't run it straight into a server bill of materials. But the equity market is already reacting: the Roundhill Memory ETF fell nearly nine percent on August 18th alone as rising yields hit crowded AI-memory positioning.

That's a tension worth naming rather than resolving. The same memory scarcity that proves AI demand is real is also the input-cost shock squeezing AI-server margins, which makes the shortage a brake on the very capex cycle it is a symptom of. And the "chip stocks fell 20 percent" framing almost certainly papers over a divergence. Samsung, SK hynix and Micron sell that 42-dollar chip and should benefit from the spike. The server builders and chip designers who consume DRAM are the ones getting repriced on cost. Two different trades, collapsed into one headline number.

Brent deserves a mention on its own terms. It's trading just below 94 dollars, on pace for a second straight weekly gain near six percent, with the reporting tying that to expected US pressure on Iran — a causal story, not a documented event. Take the price as fact and the cause as attribution. What matters is the pattern: every other asset we've tracked this week round-tripped or reversed. Oil didn't. It remains the most plausible mechanism for forcing an inflation repricing that nothing else has managed to force.

Which brings us to the asset that still refuses to move at all: credit. High-yield option-adjusted spreads printed 2.75 percent on August 18th, against 2.70 percent the day before. Be precise about that timing, because it cuts against the easy version of this point. August 18th is the most recent reading we can confirm, and it predates Wednesday's intervention and Friday's reversal. What it does establish is that the spread absorbed a 19-year-high 30-year yield print without flinching, and that the 273 basis points we cited midweek is the same number in different clothes. We've now flagged this spread five straight days. The honest framing: a spread this quiet either means credit genuinely is fine, or it means nothing has yet arrived that credit is obliged to price. From the spread alone, those two states look identical.

The longer it stays suppressed, the sharper the eventual repricing if a real catalyst hits. And the calendar just handed us one. Fed funds futures have essentially priced out a September cut and carry real odds of a hike — the aggregators don't agree closely enough to quote an exact split, but the direction isn't in dispute. Into that, Fed Chair Kevin Warsh delivers his first Jackson Hole keynote as chair this coming Friday, August 28th, roughly three weeks ahead of the September Fed decision. A hawkish surprise there would test the bond market's fragile stability, the crypto liquidity trade, and the gold-and-dollar debasement pricing in one session. That is the first event capable of separating what has been a single indistinguishable trade all week.

So here's the thesis in one line: Treasury's intervention was tested and the bond relief failed, but the debasement pricing in gold, the dollar and crypto survived that failure — which tells you this market has stopped arguing about where rates go next and started pricing the fiscal regime itself.

That's your radar for the week. Have a good weekend, and I'll see you at the next briefing.
