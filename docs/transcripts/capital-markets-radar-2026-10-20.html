Capital Markets Cross-Asset Radar

Good morning, this is your Capital Markets Radar for Tuesday, October 20th, 2026.

Here is the frame for today. Monday's equity bounce got no confirmation from either leg of last week's trade. Bonds started giving back their rally, and the AI credit that was supposed to be the fear signal stayed stressed. We'll take those in order, starting with rates.

Start with a question this desk left open. In Friday's briefing, we asked whether the long end would give back its credit-scare rally once the hot producer-price print was digested. On Monday, it started to. Treasury's own daily par yield curve has the ten-year bottoming at 5.09 percent on Friday. By Monday it was back at 5.15 percent, a level CNBC's figures confirm. That's roughly six basis points given back off the week's low, not a one-session spike. In short, the long end is no longer treating credit fear as a reason to own duration.

The front end tells a different story. In Saturday's briefing, we said the two-year had the most to give back if the credit-scare read turned out wrong. So far it hasn't given much. Treasury's table has the two-year at 4.74 percent at Friday's low and 4.77 percent on Monday. That's about three basis points, half the ten-year's move. So the long end is repricing faster than the front end. If hike risk were really rebuilding, you'd expect the two-year to lead. It isn't leading yet, though one session can't settle that.

So why did yields rise? CNBC framed Monday's move as investors assessing the U.S. inflation backdrop. One same-day account had yields easing on an oil retreat, but Treasury's own numbers contradict it on direction. The better test is credit. If Monday were pure relief, with bonds selling simply because fear eased, AI credit should have healed alongside stocks. As we'll see in a moment, it didn't. That points to inflation and term risk, not relief, as the driver. That's our inference, not a quoted catalyst.

The cost lands on multi-asset portfolios. Last Friday, this desk said sixty-forty and risk-parity allocators had just got their bond hedge back. Within a week, it's slipping again. If yields keep rising while AI credit stays distressed, bonds and AI-linked risk can fall together.

The inflation read has a fresh wrinkle from oil. Brent eased to $108.80 a barrel on October 18, according to Egypt Oil and Gas. The relief traces to last week's executive order waiving sanctions tied to Russian energy. But it's provisional. Secretary of State Rubio said the administration needs up to sixty days to assess the waiver's impact. And a waiver moves sentiment, not barrels. No confirmed increase in physical supply has been reported.

The bigger point is the buffer underneath that price. The Strategic Petroleum Reserve has fallen to 283 million barrels, its lowest since 1982, per Crux Investor rather than official Energy Department data. A policy-driven dip sitting on the thinnest physical buffer in four decades means oil's upside tail is fatter than the headline price suggests. That boxes in the Fed. With inflation risk rising through oil and at the long end, it has little room to ease into AI credit stress, even if that stress spreads. That's the policy-error risk this desk first raised on Sunday, and Monday's rates move made it sharper.

If rates are rising on inflation risk, the market that should mind most is AI credit. That's where financing cost meets a thin margin of safety. CoreWeave is still the cleanest gauge. Per CNBC, CoreWeave's bond due 2032 was yielding about 14.9 percent on Monday. That's only slightly down from over 15 percent the prior week, and still distressed. Its five-year credit-default swaps sat around 1,082 basis points, also distressed.

The break is in the co-movement. All last week, CoreWeave's stock and bonds fell together. On Monday, AI equities rebounded broadly, and CoreWeave's credit barely moved. There's a fair objection here. Distressed bonds trade thinly and quotes go stale, so a one-day gap is weak evidence. But the simplest reading is that bond buyers haven't bought the relief rally that stock buyers did. Credit is not confirming the equity rebound.

Here's why rising rates and credit stress compound each other. Forbes noted that CoreWeave lost $626 million in the quarter, with net interest expense of $640 million. By our arithmetic, the interest bill alone was larger than the entire loss. The bull reads a business near breakeven before financing costs. The bear reads a capital structure that can't carry its own debt. Either way, the problem is financing cost, not operations.

That's where Treasuries come back in. A higher risk-free rate under a distressed spread lifts the all-in cost of new data-center financing on both legs at once. One qualifier matters. Much of the existing debt is likely fixed-rate, so Monday's move bites on new issuance first, not the coupons already on the books. Bottom line, the buildout's hurdle rate rose on Monday even though nothing about CoreWeave's operations changed.

Is that stress spreading? Not yet, on the evidence. The Fed's FRED data shows the CCC-and-lower spread at 12.97 percent on Friday, up from the 12.78 percent we cited Saturday. Meanwhile the broad high-yield index sat at a far calmer 3.23 percent. Two cautions apply. A one-day move in a small, illiquid index can come from one or two issuers. And Friday's print predates Monday's rebound. For now, the stress sits at the bottom of the stack rather than moving through credit broadly.

Crypto isn't flashing contagion either. Yahoo Finance had bitcoin opening Monday at about 82,000 dollars, up slightly from Sunday. That's barely above the 81,000 level it broke during the October 8th liquidation flush this desk covered Monday. Bitcoin isn't trading as a liquidity barometer this week, so don't read its calm as an all-clear for AI credit.

So what did Monday's stock buyers actually buy? Citadel Securities gives the honest answer in its October outlook. Micron and Nvidia alone account for 36 percent of expected S&P 500 earnings growth. Strip out the top ten names, and expected growth falls to about 11 percent.

That matters for the valuation case. Citadel puts the index at 19 times forward earnings, roughly its ten-year average. That looks reasonable until you remember whose earnings are holding it up. Those are estimates, not results. And they're concentrated in exactly the names whose customers' financing costs just rose. Monday's rebound re-bought the same concentration whose funding is repricing. That's not diversification. It's a tightening loop.

The firms financing that concentration got paid well for it, at least through the third quarter. Some context first. As this desk reported Monday, Goldman's prime-brokerage data showed hedge funds cutting gross exposure to AI stocks through mid-October, selling longs rather than adding shorts. Goldman also said its prime-book leverage was still near five-year extremes. Morgan Stanley's third-quarter print shows what that leverage was worth to a bank going into October's hedge-fund de-grossing. Morgan Stanley's equity-trading revenue climbed to $5.80 billion, from $4.12 billion a year earlier. Its fixed-income revenue went the other way, down 7 percent, which its results attributed to weaker credit activity.

Two caveats. The quarter ended September 30th, so these numbers capture the leverage build, not the de-grossing. Fourth-quarter results are where lower prime balances would show up. And falling fixed-income revenue in a quarter of volatile yields hints that client flow in credit is thinner than the macro noise implies. One bank doesn't prove industry-wide illiquidity. But thin credit flow is exactly the fragility that matters if stress climbs out of the CCC tier.

Pulling it together. Last week's bond rally assumed credit fear would keep the Fed away. On Monday, the rates leg moved against that assumption while the credit leg stayed stressed. Two things to watch into next week's Fed meeting. First, does the long end keep selling off while CoreWeave's credit stays wide? Second, what does the Fed signal about the path beyond this meeting? We still don't have a reliable, dated probability for a December move, so I won't manufacture one. The meeting itself hasn't happened, so treat all of this as positioning ahead of it, not a forecast of the outcome.

That's the radar for today. Trade carefully, and I'll see you tomorrow.
