Cautious Optimism Briefings

Enterprise Technology & Software

Transcript · November 9, 2026

Good morning. It's Monday, November 9th, 2026, and this is your enterprise technology briefing. Last week's AI-software earnings verdicts are already covered on this desk. Today's question is different. Which AI commitments can a customer walk away from, and which ones are vendors locking down so they can't be?

Start with Oracle, because this is the first real test of the customer side of its backlog. Bloomberg reported on November 6th that Microsoft is winding down an arrangement to rent AI computing capacity from Oracle. Microsoft declined to comment. Oracle shares fell roughly four percent as the story spread. On Wednesday, this desk said Oracle's twenty-billion-dollar raise bought it time, not independence from the capital markets. Two days later, the exposure flipped. The question is no longer whether Oracle can fund its build. It's whether Oracle's customers stay committed to using what it builds. That's a different risk from a funding gap, and a bond sale can't fix it.

Here's what we actually know, and it's more specific than the first wave of coverage. The arrangement traces back to a 2023 deal that let Microsoft run its AI-powered Bing search on Oracle's servers. People familiar with the matter told Bloomberg the hardware ran an older generation of Nvidia chips. Those chips no longer matched what Microsoft needed. So the stated reason is a generational mismatch on an aging, Bing-era deal. Be precise about what kind of event this is. It's a third-party rental being unwound, not a cut to Microsoft's own capital spending. That supports a reallocation story over a demand-collapse story. It doesn't prove the pattern stops at this one arrangement. And the reason comes from unnamed sources, not from Microsoft.

Oracle's public response doesn't settle it either. A company spokesperson told Reuters that demand for Oracle's cloud AI infrastructure remains strong, including for earlier-generation GPUs. Notice that Oracle answered a different question than the one being asked. It defended demand in general. It did not confirm or deny this specific contract. My read is that the mention of earlier-generation GPUs is an implicit concession. Chip generation is now part of the demand story.

Now the backlog. As this desk noted Wednesday, Oracle's own fiscal first-quarter disclosures, for the quarter ended August 31st, put remaining performance obligations at six hundred sixty-four billion dollars. That's up two hundred nine billion from a year earlier. Oracle says it booked more than thirty billion dollars of new AI cloud contracts in that quarter alone. But Oracle discloses no customer-concentration breakdown of that backlog. So the public record can't tell you how much of it depends on any single counterparty, Microsoft included. Nor can it tell you whether this Bing-era rental sits in that number at all. Don't read this as a dent in the six hundred sixty-four billion. Read it as a reminder of what the number is. A backlog is only as firm as the willingness of the customers behind it to keep using what they signed for.

The second-order effect is inference, but it's the one to carry forward. If hyperscalers let older rented capacity lapse as their own builds come online, demand risk concentrates at the debt-financed lessors. That means Oracle, and the neoclouds that rent out GPU capacity the same way. For buyers and investors, contracted backlog at those vendors becomes less predictive than the headline implies. So watch the staying power of the counterparties, not the size of the backlog.

If rented capacity can be handed back at the infrastructure layer, the application incumbents are moving the opposite way. They're pricing their agents into the most committed revenue terms they have: seats and contract value.

Salesforce said on November 5th that usage of its Agentforce Coworker product rose twenty-one-fold over three months. It says more than one hundred eighty-five thousand users are now putting Coworker to work. Coworker reaches general availability on Microsoft Teams, iOS, and desktop this month, at fifty dollars per user per month. Those usage figures are Salesforce's own, with no independent verification and no revenue attached. There's no base for the twenty-one-fold multiple, so the starting level is unknown. And there's no revenue history behind the fifty-dollar price, because general availability only begins this month. On the evidence ladder, this is a usage claim plus a list price. It's not yet a revenue contribution.

The pricing choice is the interesting part. On Friday, this desk concluded that the marginal AI dollar was metered, not seated. A flat per-user price on an agent whose usage is climbing fast is a direct counterexample. Two readings compete, and neither is settled. The first is that a predictable seat price is exactly what chief financial officers will sign. It offers the same budget certainty that Snowflake's hard AI spending caps, which this desk covered Wednesday, were built to provide. The second is that this is an incumbent defending its seat model against the drift toward metering. On that reading, if usage outruns the flat price, the margin risk lands on Salesforce rather than the customer. That second reading is inference, not a disclosed fact. Treat it as a hypothesis to test.

There's a structural anchor for it. Per Morningstar's summary of Anthropic's IPO filing, consumption-based revenue was eighty percent of Anthropic's revenue in the first half of 2026. The standing disclosure applies: Anthropic makes the model behind this show. The point is that the model layer underneath these agents mostly bills on usage. When the layer below you bills by consumption and you charge a flat seat on top, rising usage becomes your cost unless the price moves. Salesforce hasn't disclosed which models power Coworker. So this is a point about how the stack is priced, not a claim about Salesforce's own costs. The so-what: seat pricing on agents buys the customer predictability, and it hands the vendor the inference bill.

ServiceNow is making a related move by a different route. According to secondary reporting, it raised its 2026 AI annual-contract-value goal to one point seven billion dollars. That's a contracted measure, not a metered one. The prior target wasn't given in that reporting, so there's no delta to judge. At the same time, ServiceNow confirmed a fresh round of job cuts touching the UK, the US, and India, with the scale undisclosed. Whether AI revenue is funding margin expansion or substituting for labor is an open question. Nothing in the reporting links the two causally, so don't assume a link.

The same lock-in instinct shows up in security, through acquisitions. On Thursday, this desk covered CrowdStrike's deals for Fiddler AI and Dreadnode, reportedly worth about four hundred ninety-five million dollars combined, both aimed at policing AI agents. Now The Information reports that Palo Alto Networks is in talks to buy Clutch Security, another agent-security startup. These are reported talks, not a closed deal, and no price has been confirmed. But the pattern is building. Platform incumbents are buying their way into agent security rather than building it. They're doing it ahead of clear evidence that production agents exist at a scale that needs this much policing. My inference is that this narrows the independent exit market for the category's remaining startups.

Put it together, and this week shows a split in who holds the leverage. At the infrastructure layer, a major buyer appears able to hand rented capacity back. At the application layer, vendors are pricing agents into seats and contract value, terms a customer can't easily walk away from. My read is that bargaining power, for now, sits with whoever can still walk away. Remember that the next time a vendor rolls out a record backlog or contract-value number.

Three things to watch. First, whether Microsoft or Oracle says anything further about the scope of the wind-down. Second, whether Palo Alto Networks' November 18th earnings call brings any confirmation on Clutch Security. Third, whether Salesforce puts a revenue or attach-rate number behind Agentforce Coworker now that general availability has arrived.

That's the briefing. Stay cautiously optimistic.
