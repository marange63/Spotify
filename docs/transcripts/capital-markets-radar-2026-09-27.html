Capital Markets Cross-Asset Radar

Transcript · September 27, 2026

Good morning, it's Sunday, September 27th, and this is your Capital Markets Radar.

All week this desk tracked the ten-year Treasury climbing to a 5.17 percent close on Friday, near its highest level since 2007. Through all of it, the S&P stayed inside the same narrow range it has held since August. Yesterday we described that split as equities trading the peace leg while bonds traded the persistence leg. Today there's a better explanation, and it comes from the earnings side, not the rates side.

Goldman Sachs, as reported by 24/7 Wall Street, says S&P earnings per share surged 51 percent year over year in the second quarter. Goldman also estimates that AI-related investment drove almost half of this year's S&P earnings growth. That is the answer to the week's puzzle. A higher discount rate should have compressed the multiple. Instead, a fast-growing earnings base absorbed it. The market didn't ignore the ten-year. It out-earned it, at least on paper.

Here's the catch, and it lives in that phrase, on paper. Large tech companies booked roughly 150 billion dollars of unrealized investment gains in the second quarter, on their stakes in private AI companies. By Goldman's count, that's about 12 percent of S&P earnings. That is not operating cash flow. It's a valuation mark on a company that doesn't trade, so there's no daily price to check it against. The market may be over-reading the growth rate and under-reading the quality behind it. And the risk is lopsided. If those private marks come down in a risk-off move, the hit would likely land abruptly at a reporting date rather than drift lower. That would take out reported earnings at the same moment higher yields are compressing multiples. It's a pro-cyclical loop that the headline growth number hides.

Now the bill that comes due. Goldman projects that depreciation on this buildout will subtract roughly five points from S&P earnings growth in 2027. By 2028, it says, depreciation could fully offset the earnings contribution from the additional AI investment. The scale explains why. Secondary coverage of the same Goldman note has the largest US hyperscalers on track for about 800 billion dollars of capex this year, with more in each of the next two. Every one of those dollars eventually runs through the income statement as depreciation. This extends a thread the Throughline carried last Tuesday, when Goldman was cited putting AI infrastructure near 40 percent of the S&P's market value. The same concentration now shows up on the earnings line, not just the valuation line.

Pair that with the discount rate. Trading Economics puts the odds of another quarter-point hike in October at around 66 percent. That's a different gauge from the CME figure we cited yesterday, so don't read the two as a trend. Either way, October is live. Put the pieces together and you get a double squeeze on the same AI-exposed names. A higher rate presses on the multiple, while depreciation presses on the earnings. The market's calm, with the ten-year at a nineteen-year high, has been financed by an earnings base that is partly non-cash. And that base is set to lose its own growth contribution. In short, equities' immunity to rates has been borrowed against the next two years of earnings.

There are two honest counterpoints. The first is that Goldman itself is not bearish. It projects S&P earnings per share of 415 dollars next year, and about 11 percent growth in each of the next two years. It still sees upside in the index over the coming twelve months. So Goldman's base case is slower growth, not a collapse. The real risk is narrower than a bear call. The market's own base case needs a deceleration from 51 percent to about 11 to happen smoothly, with no reversal in those private marks along the way.

The second counterpoint is that depreciation is a schedule, and management writes the schedule. Companies choose the useful lives of their servers and data-center equipment, and they can lengthen them. So next year's drag is a management lever as much as a mechanical certainty. That makes third-quarter earnings season the tell. Watch whether companies stretch useful lives, or guide to next year with the drag intact. If the drag shows up in guidance, a rotation out of AI capex beneficiaries has a trigger that doesn't depend on the Fed at all.

That capex also has to be financed, and that's where the marking problem shows up again. T. Rowe Price's recent read has investment-grade corporates yielding about 5.90 percent, on a spread of just 79 basis points over Treasuries. Do the subtraction, and more than five of those points are the Treasury base rate itself. So to the extent the buildout is funded in the bond market, every further leg up in the ten-year raises its cost directly, whatever credit spreads do. Tight spreads are not the same thing as cheap money.

The marking problem also lives in private credit, which this desk covered in depth on Wednesday, when Fitch's default count hit a record. What's new is a Bloomberg piece putting private credit default estimates at 1 percent, 6 percent, or 19 percent, depending on who you ask. The gap reportedly turns on definitions, especially whether selective defaults and liability-management exercises count. You can argue those are value-preserving restructurings rather than losses. But that's exactly the point. In private credit and in the AI gains alike, how much stress or profit you see depends on who does the marking. Right now, both the market's calm and its worry live in model marks rather than traded prices.

So is anyone pulling money back from risk? Not yet. US spot Bitcoin ETFs took in 2.4 billion dollars in the week ending Friday, per SoSoValue data reported by The Block. That was their largest weekly haul since October of last year, led by BlackRock's IBIT. It also flips year-to-date flows positive, to about 934 million dollars, from 5.8 billion negative as recently as mid-July. That answers the question this desk left open on Thursday. We said then we couldn't tell whether institutional demand held through Wednesday's yield-driven reversal. On the weekly evidence, it held. Money kept arriving in the same week the ten-year set its high. One line on a caveat we've already made: some of that flow may be basis or carry trades rather than directional conviction, and a weekly total doesn't settle that. The takeaway is that risk appetite is intact. It isn't yet paying for higher rates.

There's a counterweight inside the same story, and it matters more for Treasury than for crypto. Stablecoin supply has been shrinking. Bloomberg reported earlier this month that Tether's USDT fell nearly 3 billion dollars in the first half, to about 184 billion. That puts it on pace for its first contraction since the 2022 crypto crash. Circle's USDC fell by a similar amount. This is first-half data, so treat it as backdrop rather than a new print. But it undercuts Treasury Secretary Bessent's hope that stablecoin growth would create a durable new buyer base for bills. Keep the channel straight. Stablecoin reserves sit largely in bills, not coupons. So this bears on the front end and bill financing. It does not explain the soft indirect bids this desk flagged at the five-year and seven-year auctions. The practical effect is that bill demand leans harder on money funds and foreign buyers, just as a hiking Fed keeps the front end in play.

Now the dates, with no outcomes to assert yet. August core PCE lands this Wednesday, September 30th, after core ran at 3.3 percent in July. Payrolls follow on October 2nd, CPI on October 14th, and the Fed decides on October 28th. Layer onto that the start of third-quarter earnings season. That is the first real read on whether companies stretch useful lives to soften the coming depreciation hit. It's the marking problem one more time, now inside the income statement. If the marks and the schedules hold, equities can keep out-earning the ten-year. If either one gives, the squeeze arrives from both sides at once.

That's Capital Markets Radar for September 27th. Have a good week.
