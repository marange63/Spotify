Cautious Optimism Briefings

Digital Money, Crypto, and Market Structure

Transcript · September 23, 2026

Good morning, it's Wednesday, September 23rd, and this is your digital money briefing.

All last week this desk asked the same question every session: is anyone actually writing a check, or is this rally just short sellers being forced out? On Monday, someone wrote a check. U.S. spot Bitcoin ETFs took in $998.95 million in net inflows, the largest single day in eleven months. That is the print we said was missing, and it breaks the pattern this desk has been describing.

Start with who bought. BlackRock's IBIT led with $381.4 million. Ark and 21Shares' ARKB brought in $289.1 million. Fidelity's FBTC added $238.8 million. Those three funds carried roughly nine-tenths of the day, and the rest of the complex supplied the remaining tenth. Compare that with last week. Last Thursday, IBIT alone carried the inflow. On Friday, two funds carried about 97 percent of it. Monday's bid ran through three large distribution channels, and a lot of the purchase came from outside BlackRock. So the demand side looks less concentrated than it did a week ago, even if a handful of issuers still did the heavy lifting.

Spot Ether ETFs moved the same day, taking in $269.98 million, their best single session since last October. That matters because Ether funds were the series that snapped a four-week inflow streak last week. So Monday was not a Bitcoin-only event. Money came back into the digital-asset wrapper broadly.

Now look at what happened on the same tape. Bitcoin touched roughly $87,300, its highest level since January. By 3 a.m. Eastern on Tuesday it had settled back to $85,400. That is still up 4.7 percent over 24 hours, but well off the peak. Underneath that move, $1.06 billion in crypto positions were liquidated, and $844 million of that was shorts. One caution on those numbers. That 24-hour window overlaps the squeeze we reported yesterday, so don't add the two together as separate events.

The shape is the same one we've seen on every leg of this rally. Roughly four of every five liquidated dollars were shorts being forced out. Real money and forced covering arrived on the same day, and this data cannot separate the two. Some meaningful share of Monday's price action was mechanical. The honest read is that we don't know the split, and anyone who claims to know it is guessing.

Here is what makes this print harder to dismiss than the ones before it. It happened into a tightening Fed, not an easing one. The Fed's quarter-point hike on September 16th, to a range of 3.75 to 4 percent, is old news on this desk. What's new is the path. The Fed's median projection for the year-end rate moved up to 4.1 percent, from 3.8 percent in June. That pencils in one more hike this year. So this is not just a hawkish print, it's a hawkish path. The easy explanation, that crypto is rallying on easier money, is not available.

That cuts two ways, and both deserve a hearing. A higher expected policy rate raises the carry cost of holding a zero-yield asset. Demand that shows up anyway, against a rising median dot, is a stronger signal than the same demand would be into a cutting cycle. But the same fact makes the move more suspect. If the macro backdrop is getting harder and price rises anyway, positioning is the obvious alternative engine. And positioning is what the liquidation data points to.

So if it isn't easier money, what is it? This is where the strongest bull case comes in, and it predates Monday's rally. In the first week of September, Bitcoin's 90-day correlation to gold sat near a six-year high, the highest since 2020. The link to stocks moved the other way. On a shorter 30-day window, Bitcoin's correlation to the S&P 500 fell toward zero during the August rally. Bitwise's André Dragosch put the interpretation plainly. When currency-debasement concerns dominate, in his words, bitcoin has recently started to look like "an amplified version of gold." Bloomberg's Eric Balchunas made the same point from the other direction. What has changed, he said, is that gold and Treasurys "have become much more correlated" with Bitcoin. He called it a small window of time, but notable. And in his words, it "blows up the claim that it's just QQQ."

If that framing holds, the implication is bigger than one day of flows. An asset that behaves like a debasement hedge rather than leveraged tech beta can sit in portfolios whose mandates exclude high-beta risk. That would widen the allocator base behind the ETF wrapper. Near a billion dollars arriving into a hawkish dot plot is exactly the kind of evidence that thesis would predict.

Glassnode isn't convinced. Its pushback is that similar breaks from equities during bond selloffs have, in its words, "previously been short-lived." And there's a timing caveat that matters. The correlation data is about three weeks old. It predates the hike and it predates this rally. It describes the regime going into the move, not the move itself, and it hasn't been re-measured since. So the debasement-hedge story is the best bull case available, and Monday's inflow is consistent with it. It doesn't prove it.

There's a third reading worth naming, even though we can't size it. ETF inflows are not automatically a bet on price. They can be the long leg of a hedged basis trade. An institution buys the fund, shorts the equivalent futures, and collects the spread with no view on direction. The Block describes the derivatives backdrop around Monday only as "positive funding and elevated open interest." That description fits a carry trade. It also fits longs re-levering into the rally. We looked for a dated, quotable futures basis or open-interest figure for Monday's session and did not find one, so we won't invent one. The carry explanation stays on the table as an alternative, not a finding.

That leads to the second-order point on positioning. If much of Monday's move was covering, the shorts that fuelled it are now gone. Those $844 million of short liquidations were forced buyers, and they can't be forced to buy again. That leaves price more dependent on the spot bid alone. So the variable that matters from here is not leverage. It's whether the ETF flow shows up again.

And here's the discipline this desk has to hold. On Sunday we said the daily flow series was noise, because every time we tried to read a direction from one print, the next print reversed it. We have to apply that standard now that the surprise is bullish. One large inflow is a data point. It is not yet a trend. The test is whether it repeats, not how big Monday was.

So where does that leave the thesis? On Monday we described this sector as gaining permission, not money. That framing has to be revised. One large check has arrived. It has not been repeated yet, and it came alongside a short squeeze that makes its true size hard to isolate. There are two tests ahead. First, does ETF demand persist through the rest of this week? Second, does price hold through Friday's quarterly expiry, which this desk has flagged all week as the next real pressure point? If flows fade as the next hike gets priced, the positioning reading wins. If they hold, the debasement reading earns a promotion. Until then, this is a pattern break worth taking seriously, not a regime change worth calling.

That's the briefing. Thanks for listening, and we'll pick this back up tomorrow.
