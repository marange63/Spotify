Good morning, it's July 28th, and this is your private credit briefing.

For most of this week we've circled the retail-redemption front of private credit: who's gating, who's calming down, who's still bleeding cash out of non-traded vehicles. Today the fresher signal sits on the other side of the ledger — the asset side. Is the credit these funds actually hold as healthy as the redemption story implies? The data says: not evenly, and the gap itself is the story.

Start with the cross-sectional numbers on business development companies. Non-traded BDCs — the retail-facing, semi-liquid vehicles — averaged 0.6% non-accruals in 2025. The largest twenty listed BDCs, which trade and mark daily, averaged 2.1%, more than three times higher. Payment-in-kind income tells the same story: 4.6% for non-traded funds versus 8.1% for the big listed names, with Blue Owl's OTIC running as high as 12% PIK income against Goldman's GSCR at under 1%. That listed-versus-non-traded gap is an open question, not a verdict — it could reflect genuinely different portfolio vintages, or it could mean the less-liquid, less-frequently-marked retail NAVs are simply lagging what the more disciplined, market-priced listed cohort is already showing. Either way, the marks least tested by real price discovery are the ones retail investors are holding.

Widen the lens and the pressure is portfolio-wide, not a handful of bad names: of roughly five thousand companies held across BDCs as of the end of March, just over ten percent are showing signs of credit pressure. And remember what PIK income actually is — it's a borrower deferring cash interest rather than paying it, the textbook amend-and-extend move. It inflates reported net investment income and flatters dividend coverage on paper, while the cash itself never arrives. More than a third of this book comes due by 2028, so the deferral has a deadline. Not every PIK uptick is distress, though. Golub Capital's BDC posted a low 1.4% non-accrual rate this quarter, and its PIK increase traced to a single borrower using a pre-structured toggle rather than a distressed amendment. That's the honest counter-case: PIK by design, not PIK by desperation. The takeaway is that the deterioration is broad enough to be a trend, but it's being smoothed by structures that keep the cash-flow reckoning a year or two away.

Here's the trap that sits underneath all of it. One number is conspicuously missing from this picture: a fresh default rate. The most recent agency print anyone can point to is KBRA's middle-market read from late last year — 3.5% of borrowers, 2.1% of the roughly one trillion dollars in notional debt outstanding. That data is now nine months stale. In a market repricing this fast, a nine-month-old default rate isn't a measurement, it's a memory. And the reported rate stays low precisely because PIK and amend-and-extend keep borrowers current on paper. So the cleanest-looking number in private credit is also the most backward-looking one, which is exactly why the non-accrual and PIK trend matters more right now than the headline default figure.

One thread ties this stress to where the growth actually is: software exposure across these portfolios averages nearly 20% directly, rising above a third once you count software-adjacent credits. That's private credit's direct wiring into the AI capex cycle. The same spending story that's inflating hyperscaler valuations is sitting, in loan form, inside the retail credit funds — which is exactly where the bigger story is happening.

Morgan Stanley now projects roughly $570 billion of global AI-related debt issuance this year, four times last year's pace, with $236 billion already priced by the end of May. The bank frames the total data-center financing need through 2028 at about $2.9 trillion against roughly $1.4 trillion of Big Tech operating cash flow — worth stating plainly, because the commonly repeated "$1.5 trillion funding gap" figure gets misquoted as new debt that has to be raised. It isn't. It's capex minus cash flow, and only a sliver of it, roughly $200 billion, is expected to come from traditional corporate bonds. Morgan Stanley puts the rest — an estimated $800 billion opportunity through 2028 — on private credit's plate: Blackstone, Blue Owl, Apollo, PIMCO, BlackRock. Treat those totals as sell-side projections, not booked flows, but the direction is unambiguous.

Here's why the timing matters. Hyperscaler bond order coverage — how many times over an offering gets subscribed — has fallen from nearly five times in February to below two times by July. Public bond investors are visibly pulling back from this credit. And the structural response is to move it off the public market entirely. Meta's Hyperion joint venture with Blue Owl keeps most of that data-center debt off Meta's own balance sheet, with Blue Owl holding an 80% stake. So the residual financing demand public investors are stepping back from is exactly what's migrating into private credit's least transparent corner — off-balance-sheet joint ventures that make true hyperscaler leverage harder for any analyst, equity or credit, to see. Put the two exposures together and the risk isn't diversified at all: a single bout of AI-capex digestion would hit the software loans already sitting in BDC books and these new data-center loans at the same time. That's a correlated bet being marketed as diversification.

Now the deployment side. Blackstone's combined credit platform is nearly $550 billion, with $84 billion of dry powder — more than double where it stood at the start of 2024. That capital is piling up against a direct-lending origination market that fell roughly 55% quarter over quarter. Capital accumulating faster than it can be deployed at defensible terms is exactly the mechanism that compresses spreads into thin compensation for the risk being taken. Late-cycle, the binding constraint isn't raising money — it's finding a home for it without loosening terms.

And the redemption story itself is holding up less cleanly than the headline suggests. Blackstone's Jon Gray told investors BCRED redemption requests are "down materially" early in the third quarter, framing it as vindication against calls for a private-credit "calamity." Hold that loosely — it's guidance on a quarter only weeks old, not a disclosed number, and it may just mean the nervous money already left. The second quarter itself was rougher: repurchase requests ran near 10% of shares, up from 7.9% in the first quarter, Blackstone again capped fulfillment at the standard 5%, and BCRED still posted $1.2 billion of net outflows despite $1 billion of gross sales. It also cut its monthly distribution to 18 cents, the second cut in nine months. A calmer tone on top of a second distribution cut is not the same as a healed vehicle.

Apollo, for its part, is pushing the opposite narrative — framing private credit as a $38 trillion investment-grade opportunity rather than the roughly $2 trillion levered-lending slice the market fixates on, alongside a preliminary 9% annualized return on its alternatives book. Treat that as the industry's own marketing rebuttal to the stress story, not confirmation of it. It's a preliminary, unaudited figure still awaiting full results, and the $38 trillion is a total-addressable-market number, not a description of what any BDC actually holds.

So where does the cycle actually stand? The binding risk isn't spread levels — it's valuation opacity in the non-traded marks and an untested concentration in software and AI-adjacent credit that the industry is still calling diversification. The next two days bring the confirming data: Ares Capital reports tomorrow, KKR the day after, and both will refresh the non-accrual and PIK figures this briefing has been built on. Watch whether Ares's non-accrual rate, at 2.1% of cost as of the first quarter, moves up or down from there. That one print will tell you more about the cycle than any amount of management reassurance.

That's the briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
