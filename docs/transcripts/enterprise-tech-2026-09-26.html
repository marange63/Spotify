Cautious Optimism Briefings

Enterprise Technology & Software

Transcript · September 26, 2026

Good morning, it's Saturday, September 26th. This week brought no fresh earnings or deployment disclosures from the big platform vendors, so the marginal news sits elsewhere. This week's new enterprise-AI products didn't come from the model labs, and they didn't come from the big systems of record. They came from the layers in between. One routes work across frontier models. One claims to replace the application itself. And one polices the agents. All three rest on the announcement, not on revenue.

The most consequential of the three is in security. On September 22nd, Palo Alto Networks launched Unit 42 Continuous Frontier AI Defense. It's an agentic offensive-security service that perpetually finds, validates, and remediates enterprise exposures. The mechanism is what matters. Palo Alto built what it calls a proprietary multi-model harness. It routes each task to whichever model suits it best, spanning Anthropic's Claude Mythos and OpenAI's GPT models. And Palo Alto says the harness exists partly to manage, in its words, "the cost of frontier AI at scale."

That answers a question this beat has been asking all week. On Wednesday, we looked at Cloudflare's remediation service built on OpenAI's Daybreak models. The question then was whether security vendors were becoming a channel for one lab's intelligence. On Thursday, CrowdStrike showed up as a product partner inside Anthropic's Claude Marketplace. Palo Alto has taken a third path. It buys from both labs and keeps the routing for itself.

There are two ways to read that choice. Read one way, it's leverage. Models become interchangeable inputs, and Palo Alto keeps the customer relationship and the pricing power. Read the other way, it's dependency. A security vendor with no model of its own is exposed to whatever the labs charge, and both labs already sell direct to enterprises. The cost language is the tell. If Palo Alto had to engineer a harness to manage inference spend, then part of this product's gross margin is being paid to the labs. No margin figure has been disclosed, but the vendor itself is conceding that the cost is material.

What's genuinely new is also narrower than the marketing. Periodic penetration testing is work that services firms and vulnerability-management tools already do. The new part is continuous, automated remediation replacing a human-paced engagement. Palo Alto's rationale is that attackers using AI have compressed breach cycles by almost 97 percent in some cases, from weeks to hours. That's Palo Alto's own framing, not an independent measure, and "in some cases" is doing real work. If the product delivers, the exposed budget is red-team services and traditional vulnerability-management tooling.

Now the market reaction, which deserves care. CrowdStrike shares slipped on the news, according to GuruFocus, but no size for the move was reported. Meanwhile CrowdStrike's own latest quarter showed annual recurring revenue of five point eight four billion dollars, up 25 percent year over year. Palo Alto disclosed no pricing, no customer count, and no bookings for the new service. CrowdStrike reported no customer loss. So a rival's stock moved on a launch that came with no numbers attached.

So the takeaway is this. Security is starting to look like a model-routing business, but that is still a narrative, not a result. One unsized dip in a rival's shares doesn't show the market has priced anything. The test is whether Palo Alto ever discloses what this service earns.

Palo Alto's launch is software absorbing a services job. The next story is a startup claiming software can absorb another piece of software entirely. Ema builds what it calls universal AI employees, which automate HR, IT and finance workflows. On September 23rd, TechCrunch reported that Ema had raised seventy-seven million dollars. That brings its total funding to one hundred forty million. Ema says the round more than quadruples its valuation from 2024, but it withheld the new number, so that multiple can't be checked.

The operating claims are bigger. Ema says revenue bookings have surpassed one hundred fifty million dollars, and that net dollar retention runs around 180 percent. Keep the discipline here. Bookings are multiyear commitments, not recognized annual revenue, and no revenue figure appears anywhere in the reporting. Ema also claims fifty-fold revenue growth, but from an undisclosed base. That tells you the shape of the curve, not its size.

The real headline is the replacement claim. Chief executive Surojit Chatterjee says that many of Ema's customers are, quote, "already on the way to replace" large SaaS applications "completely, removing dependency on them." That's a direct test of whether AI is a new front end on existing software, or something that removes the software underneath. Grade it as a vendor claim. No named customer, anywhere in the coverage, has confirmed decommissioning a system or cutting seats at Workday, ServiceNow, SAP or Salesforce. And "on the way" is a trajectory, not a switch-off.

Chatterjee's own explanation actually undercuts the stronger reading. He says those applications are "mostly becoming like a database." An application reduced to a database is still running, and still under contract. That's close to the headless posture Salesforce laid out at Dreamforce last week. Keep the data and workflow layer, and let some other interface sit on top. If that's the real pattern, incumbents lose the screen but keep the record.

The threat to the incumbents would then show up in seat counts at renewal. That's conditional, and it isn't in evidence yet. What would settle it is a named customer switching off a system of record, or a disclosed seat cut at an incumbent's renewal. Until then, the replacement thesis has a named claimant but no named victim.

Whether agents are routing security work or fronting applications, somebody has to count them, and be able to switch them off. That's where Dataiku comes in. On September 24th, Dataiku launched Agent Management. It's a standalone product that finds every AI agent an enterprise is running, whichever platform built it. It measures each agent's business and technical performance, and flags the riskiest ones. General availability is planned for October, so there's no customer production data yet.

Dataiku's pitch rests on one statistic. It says fewer than one in five organizations maintain a complete, current inventory of their AI systems. Two caveats apply. Dataiku sells the fix for the gap it's citing, and it gave no methodology. Now set that against the Guild.ai survey this beat covered on Thursday. There, IT decision-makers expressed near-universal confidence that their agent inventories were complete. Guild also sells agent-governance tooling.

So two governance vendors, in the same week, describe opposite realities. Part of the gap may be the question asked. Confidence is not the same thing as a complete, current inventory. Either way, the finding is that the data on agent sprawl is as unaudited as the agents themselves, even as policing them becomes a product you can buy.

There's a structural risk for Dataiku too. The platforms that build agents, like Salesforce, Microsoft and AWS, could bundle governance into their own suites. Bundling caps what a standalone governance product can charge.

Put the three layers together. Palo Alto routes security work across two frontier labs and gets paid for the plumbing. Ema says it's replacing the software underneath enterprise workflows, on the word of its own chief executive. And Dataiku sells the ability to find agents that, by its own account, most organizations can't fully see. All three are running well ahead of any audited dollar. Three disclosures would settle it. First, pricing or bookings for Palo Alto's new service. Second, a named Ema customer confirming it switched off an incumbent system. Third, real customer counts once Dataiku's product reaches general availability in October.

That's the briefing for today. Have a good weekend.
