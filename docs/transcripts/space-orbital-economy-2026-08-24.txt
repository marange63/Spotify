Good morning. It's Monday, August 24th, and this is your briefing on the space and orbital economy.

We start with SpaceX's first quarterly numbers as a public company. The call was August 4th and the transcripts landed the following week, so this isn't breaking news. It's material we haven't put in front of you yet. Total revenue was $7.8 billion, up 92 percent year over year. But look at where it came from. Starlink, reported as the Connectivity segment, brought in $4.3 billion, up 66 percent. The Space segment, which is launch and Starship, brought in $962 million, up 29 percent. That's about a quarter of what Starlink generated, and roughly twelve percent of company revenue. So when you hear "SpaceX earnings," understand that you are mostly hearing about a satellite internet company. Rockets are the smallest of the three disclosed segments.

The constellation now stands at roughly 10,200 operational satellites, and Starlink added more than 1.7 million net subscribers in the quarter at a flat $66 of average revenue per user. That is volume, not price.

And here is the disclosure that actually moves analyst models. Segment profitability was reported. Connectivity posted $1.656 billion of operating income, up 79 percent year over year, and about $2.6 billion of adjusted EBITDA. That is roughly a 39 percent operating margin on segment revenue. It is also about 73 percent of the company's total adjusted EBITDA from a segment that is only about 55 percent of consolidated revenue. Until this quarter, nobody outside the company could do that arithmetic. Now Amazon's Kuiper, Eutelsat OneWeb and the Chinese megaconstellations get measured against a published broadband-satellite margin — the first real benchmark this industry has had.

Now the capital allocation, because this is where it gets interesting. Quarterly capex was $18.4 billion. Of that, $15.8 billion — 86 cents of every capex dollar — went to AI compute buildout, not to launch or pad infrastructure. That spending feeds the newly disclosed AI segment, built on the xAI and Grok businesses folded in after February's all-stock merger, which posted $2.6 billion of revenue, up 247 percent year over year. For scale, that is more than sixty times the entire $250 million round Starcloud, the orbital-compute startup we covered yesterday, closed last week.

Here is the through-line worth naming directly. This show has spent the past week calling launch capacity the binding constraint on the orbital economy. SpaceX is the one company with both the cash and the vehicles to relieve that constraint fastest. And it has just told the market, in its own numbers, where the marginal dollar goes. Overwhelmingly into compute. So launch scarcity, at the margin, is partly a choice the dominant supplier is making. It is not purely a physical ceiling. That partly breaks our own framing, and you should hear us say so.

Be careful with the tempting version of that story, though. It is true that Starlink is the profit engine, and that is now reported rather than inferred. It is not true that Starlink's operating cash is paying for the compute. Roughly $2.6 billion of segment EBITDA does not fund $15.8 billion of quarterly capex. The consolidated company still posted a net loss of $541 million, narrowed from about $1.01 billion a year earlier. What is actually paying for the AI buildout is the balance sheet. SpaceX ended the quarter with $100 billion of cash and marketable securities, built from IPO proceeds and a June bond sale. Starlink's margins make that equity and debt cheap to raise. They are not the source of the cash.

Which brings us to the guidance. Chief financial officer Bret Johnsen pointed to $100 billion of annualized recurring revenue by year end — that is this December, not next. Do the arithmetic. $7.8 billion in a quarter annualizes to about $31 billion, so reaching $100 billion by December means more than tripling the run rate in two quarters. Now be precise about the guidance. Johnsen tied it to expected December revenue, annualized, across all three segments plus the newly closed Cursor acquisition. That is an exit run-rate, not a full-year revenue figure, and recurring revenue here is a company-defined metric. Only the AI segment is growing anywhere near fast enough to bridge that gap, so the headline financial target at the world's most important space company is substantially a forecast about its AI business. And don't conflate two round numbers: the quarter-end cash is realized, the run-rate is a projection.

If that is where the money is going, here is where the calendar is going. Starship Flight 14, which was to attempt the first operational Starlink V3 deployment, has slipped into September, and SpaceX says it will not attempt a booster catch this time. The vehicle that would eventually widen the launch bottleneck keeps moving right while the AI capital moves forward.

Now, when launch doesn't work, somebody eats the cost — and this quarter we finally got a number. Blue Origin confirmed the root cause of its May New Glenn static-fire explosion: an anomaly at the main oxygen valve on one of the BE-4 engines. The company says it can retrofit a small valve modification quickly, and it is guiding to a return to flight before year end. That is company guidance, not a licensed launch date, and Blue Origin's dates have slipped before. An earlier New Glenn flight in April had already done real damage. Its upper stage left AST SpaceMobile's BlueBird 7 satellite in an orbit too low to sustain operations, and the satellite was written off. Reporting on AST's second-quarter results puts the charge at $125.9 million, of which only about $30 million was covered by insurance. AST's entire quarterly revenue was $31.5 million. So one bad insertion cost this operator four times what it earns in a quarter.

The uninsured three-quarters is the more interesting number. It tells you operators are already self-insuring most of a satellite's value, because at current premiums the coverage doesn't pencil. That cuts against the reflexive read: underwriters may well reprice New Glenn payloads after this, but it matters less than it sounds when the coverage was already thin. The cost of launch unreliability lands on equity holders, not on underwriters. That is a balance-sheet line, not an operational footnote, and it is the first time this week's launch argument has shown up as a dollar figure on a public company's books.

Reliability is one gate on the calendar. Orbital mechanics and weather are another, and China just ran into it. Chang'e-7 — an orbiter, lander, hopper and rover bound for the Shackleton region near the lunar south pole — was set to fly from Wenchang in a window that opened today and closes tomorrow. Tropical Depression Narra over the Gulf of Tonkin forced the scrub. Chinese officials say the mission slips at least a month. The exact new date is genuinely ambiguous: outlets read the official language two different ways, and this is secondary reporting, not a statement from the space agency. Then do the arithmetic. Once it launches, the orbiter needs roughly ninety days of scouting before any landing attempt, which pushes a touchdown toward year end at the earliest.

On Friday we told you that China's booster recovery meant the capability was no longer uniquely American, but that the scoreboard hadn't moved. This is the same lesson from the state-program side. Chinese execution is not uniformly ahead of schedule, and what stopped Chang'e-7 was weather, not technology. South-pole lighting geometry and a monsoon-exposed coastal pad bind lunar timelines regardless of political urgency, and they bind NASA's own south-pole landers the same way.

One closing thread, and it is the one thing here that compounds while everything else slips. ESA's 2026 Space Environment Report — and we are reading that through secondary summaries, not from ESA directly — found low-Earth-orbit collision probability up roughly 20 percent year over year. It puts the 600-to-900 kilometer band past the Kessler threshold, where collisions breed debris faster than natural decay clears it. But try to size the operating cost and the data falls apart. Published counts of Starlink's collision-avoidance maneuvers range from 144,000 over six months to more than 300,000 over a year, with no reconciled methodology. Nobody, not the operator and not a regulator, publishes an agreed figure. That gap is the story. The best proxy for orbital congestion has no agreed measurement, at a moment when one constellation alone runs 10,200 satellites and yesterday's national transportation policy addressed ground and airspace while saying nothing about traffic in orbit.

So where does that leave things. What is genuinely inflecting is Starlink's profitability, now measurable at a 39 percent segment operating margin, and SpaceX's revenue scale. What is still a demo, or worse a write-off, is launch reliability outside SpaceX's own fleet, and lunar execution on anybody's declared timeline. Watch four things. Whether Flight 14 actually flies in September. Whether Chang'e-7 gets a firm new date rather than a vague one. Whether Blue Origin turns year-end guidance into a licensed return-to-flight date. And whether SpaceX's third-quarter print puts that $100 billion run rate within reach or exposes it as aspiration.

That's your briefing. Have a good Monday.
