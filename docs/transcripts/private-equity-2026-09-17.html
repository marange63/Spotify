Good morning, it's September 17th, 2026, and this is your private equity briefing.

Start with the terms, straight from the company's own filing. The Baldwin Group, the insurance broker, is going private for thirty-two dollars and fifty cents a share in cash. That values the business at about seven point seven billion dollars of enterprise value. And it is an eighty-eight percent premium to Baldwin's unaffected closing price on June 17th, the last clean print before reports of go-private talks surfaced. Say that plainly. This is a premium to the price before the leak, not to yesterday's trade. It is about as decisive a number as this market produces.

Now the part that actually matters this week, which is who is paying it. The buyer isn't a buyout fund. It's Sequence Holdings, described in its own release as a permanent holding company, together with DFO Management — Michael Dell's family office. No general partner. No ten-year fund clock. No limited-partner capital call standing behind this check.

Break the price down before drawing anything from it. Of that seven point seven billion in enterprise value, about four point six billion is the actual equity purchase price. The rest is net debt assumed or refinanced. Don't let the headline number stand in for capital deployed. The equity check is the figure to hold onto. Against trailing adjusted EBITDA of about three hundred ninety-six million dollars, the price works out to roughly twenty times earnings. Twenty times — and the financing structure behind it isn't disclosed anywhere available, so don't guess at the leverage. One more discipline. The deal is agreed, not closed. Completion is targeted for the first quarter of 2027, subject to a shareholder vote and regulatory approval. No cash has moved.

Here's the so-what. Twenty times earnings only pencils for a buyer with nowhere to be. A drawdown fund underwriting that multiple has to get out inside a defined hold period. That means betting on multiple expansion, or on leverage, to bridge the gap between the entry price and the exit math. Permanent capital doesn't carry that problem. It can compound instead of flip. Which is exactly why it can out-bid the funds that usually win auctions like this one.

Follow that one step further, because it lands on fund economics. If unbounded-hold capital is the marginal bidder for durable, asset-light, cash-generative businesses, a buyout fund has two choices in those auctions. Lose them, or pay through its own return math. Both outcomes surface later in the same place — the entry multiples carried by 2026-vintage funds, and then the cash those funds eventually return. That's the quiet cost of an auction you either lose or win at the wrong price.

The question hanging over this market has been which general partners can still raise and deploy. Apollo's flagship fund is running behind target. Mega-funds are spending roughly three times as long on the road as they did five years ago. Baldwin flips the question. The richest multiple paid in public this week wasn't paid by a fund at all. The competitive question is starting to shift from which GP wins the auction to whether a GP is needed in the room in the first place.

That also narrows what the fund model is actually selling. Take away the leverage tailwind and the multiple-expansion tailwind, both of which have faded in this cycle. What's left as the differentiator is operational change. For a handful of managers that is a genuine edge. It is a much thinner edge than a decade of falling rates made it look, and it has to carry a twenty-times entry price now.

To be fair to the other side of this argument. A competed, arm's-length buyer paying full price in public is real price discovery, and it is the kind this market has been short of. If an outside, non-conflicted buyer will pay twenty times for a durable, asset-light, cash-generative business, that is a data point validating the high end of sponsor marks on comparable companies. It cuts against the skepticism this briefing has carried toward valuation marks all week, and I'd rather say that than bury it. Just bound it correctly. One competed price on one insurance broker is not a clearing price for the industry's much larger backlog of unsold portfolio companies. It tells you what the best asset can fetch. It doesn't tell you what the median one is worth.

Turn now to where the fund model is still working, at a very different scale. Carlyle is backing a new platform, Avenrock Energy, in its acquisition of Parallax Energy, a Western Canadian oil and gas producer. The price is roughly one billion Canadian dollars, about seven hundred nineteen million U.S. dollars. It's Carlyle's second Canadian energy buy-and-build in a year. The deal is subject to customary closing conditions and regulatory approval, so nothing is closed here either. And there's no disclosed production or multiple detail to hang on it beyond the headline price. It matters as contrast. The fund model is still deploying capital — just at roughly a tenth the size of Baldwin, and in a corner of the market the permanent-capital buyers aren't fighting over. It's also, structurally, a sponsor selling to a sponsor-backed platform. So somebody in this market did get paid this week. Call that a small check against the running theme that nothing is returning cash.

Now the limited-partner side, where the same asymmetry shows up in a pension's own numbers. CalPERS posted a preliminary net return of fourteen point eight percent for the fiscal year ended June 30th, with private equity the standout at seventeen percent. Both of those numbers ran on this show on Tuesday. The new layer is the multi-year picture. An independent analysis published September 15th argues that even after that headline year, CalPERS' five-year annualized return sits essentially level with its six point eight percent actuarial discount rate. A razor-thin cushion, after what looked like a banner year.

I want to be precise about sourcing on that. The comparison comes from that outside analysis, not from CalPERS itself. A follow-up check couldn't stand up an exact five-year figure against CalPERS' own primary materials. So treat it as one analyst's framing rather than an audited number. What is not in dispute is the absence. Nowhere in CalPERS' disclosures is there a private-equity distribution or cash-on-cash figure for the year. A seventeen percent mark is a change in carrying value. It is not cash landing in the pension's account. And the one-year figures are preliminary and unaudited, which matters more than usual in an asset class whose marks are known to lag.

Then sit with the incentive that creates. A board looking at fourteen point eight percent and an eighty-five percent funded status — comfortable enough to avoid hard choices, thin enough to need the returns — has every reason to hold, or even raise, its private-markets allocation. It can do that without ever asking what actually came back in cash this year. Pair that with a marquee take-private clearing at a full multiple and you have the cover story for standing pat. That's the complacency risk worth naming. It isn't a prediction of losses. It's a prediction that the cash question stays unasked for another year.

Pull it together. The entry side of this market keeps clearing at full, even record, prices. And increasingly the winning bidder isn't a fund at all, which narrows the edge drawdown vehicles can claim once leverage and multiple expansion stop doing the work. Meanwhile, nothing this week put meaningful cash back into limited partners' hands. That's the cycle in one sentence — pricing power at entry, silence at the exit. Watch three things from here. Whether Apollo signs the reported carve-out of Johnson and Johnson's DePuy Synthes orthopedics unit, a deal said to be weeks from a decision and the kind of scale take-private that would show a fund can still win a big auction. The Baldwin shareholder vote and regulatory path toward that first-quarter close. And the late-October third-quarter manager earnings, which will be the next real read on realization pace rather than paper marks.
