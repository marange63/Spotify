Cautious Optimism Briefings

Strategic Power, Energy, and Institutional Change

Transcript · October 11, 2026

Good morning. It's Sunday, October 11th, and this is your Strategic Power briefing.

Here's the thread running through today's news. Washington is allocating the cost of scarce energy by legal carve-out rather than letting markets clear it. Abroad, it is licensing Russian diesel back into world trade to protect the pump, even as Kyiv vows to keep burning the refineries that make that diesel. At home, a state has written the grid cost of AI data centers into statute. Two arenas, one move: scarcity priced by decree instead of by market.

Start with the diesel license. On October 9th, Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control issued General License 135. Its title says exactly what it does. It authorizes transactions related to, quote, "the sale, delivery, offloading, and importation of diesel fuel of Russian Federation origin." That includes imports into the United States, and it runs until April 7th, 2027. It followed Trump's announcement of an agreement with Putin to let Russian diesel back into world markets.

On its text, this is not broad sanctions relief. As reported, the license, quote, "does not repeal all U.S. sanctions on Russia or authorize every petroleum transaction." It also does not let American banks debit the accounts of Russia's central bank, its National Wealth Fund, or its Finance Ministry. The financial architecture of the sanctions regime stands. What moved is one product, diesel, for price reasons. Read literally, this is a pump-price instrument, not a peace dividend.

But the text is not the whole story, and the timing shows why. Yesterday, this desk reported that the Graham sanctions act is now law, per CSIS. Its tariff authority on buyers of Russian energy goes live October 18th. Treasury issued this license before that authority has even switched on. Congress tightened by statute, and the executive loosened by license, inside the same few weeks. My read is that Washington is treating Russian sanctions as a dial tuned to pump prices, not as a ratchet.

That is no longer just this desk's inference. Senator Blumenthal called the license, quote, "directly contrary to Congress's intent in our bipartisan sanctions bill." Peter Harrell, a Georgetown sanctions expert, went further. He said the episode, quote, "pretty much proves the point that the Graham Russia Bill was not going to force the Trump Administration to increase economic pressure on Moscow." The bill's namesake cannot weigh in. Senator Graham died in July, before the license was issued.

So there are two honest readings. On the legal text, the license is narrow. On credibility, named critics read it as meaningful easing, because it shows the new law will not bind. Both can be true, and the second matters more for markets. Third-country buyers and traders have just learned that American secondary pressure on Russian energy is negotiable. That blunts the deterrent value of the new tariff authority before it is ever used. That is still an inference. But it is now an inference with named sources behind it.

There is also a durability question. One legal analysis argues the license's standing against the 2022 statutory ban on Russian oil imports is unresolved. It writes that, quote, "General License 135 does not qualify for the earlier-license exception merely because Treasury retained that authority." That is a contested argument, not a ruling. But it means the carve-out carries legal risk on top of its April 2027 sunset.

Now the deeper contradiction. On October 6th, this desk argued that two forces were squeezing toward the pump. Washington's own blockade of Iran tightens crude. Kyiv's drone campaign against Russian refineries tightens products. And Washington controls only one of those two levers. General License 135 is Washington reaching for a third lever, aimed at offsetting Kyiv's. Kyiv has answered. Ukraine's Defense Ministry, in remarks relayed from Financial Times reporting, put it bluntly: quote, "We will burn refineries." Treat that as declared intent, not a measured strike tempo. But the logic is plain. The license assumes a flow of Russian diesel that Washington's own wartime partner says it will keep trying to destroy.

Markets took the license at face value anyway. Ultra-low-sulfur diesel futures settled at four dollars and seventy-three cents a gallon on October 9th, per DTN. That was a drop of about four and six-tenths percent in a single session. The physical case for that move is thin. Energy News Beat reports that the first tranche Trump cited, three hundred thousand tons, is less than one day of American diesel demand. And none of those volumes appear in the license text. So the futures move looks like a repricing of risk, not of barrels. That cuts both ways. If the strike campaign takes more Russian capacity offline, the same risk premium can come straight back.

Two more cautions on the lead. This is a unilateral American license. I found no on-the-record European or British statement either way, so do not assume a clean global reopening of Russian product trade. And the inflation read-through stays conditional. September CPI is not out until Wednesday, October 14th. There is no print yet showing diesel softness reaching the broader price picture.

So here's the takeaway on the lead. Washington's newest Russia sanctions law bent before its main tool went live, the moment it collided with pump prices. And the policy rests on output that Washington's own ally says it will burn. Expect diesel volatility to persist, whatever the license's paper terms say.

From who pays at the pump to who pays at the meter. A Minnesota law took effect October 1st, and it extends an arc this desk has tracked all week. On October 6th, we followed the cost of AI power into a Wisconsin rate case. On October 8th, we covered FERC's suspension of PJM's backstop procurement over how its costs would be split. Now the question has reached binding state statute. The Minnesota law requires that, quote, "contracts for large data centers, 150 megawatts or more, cover the extra costs of serving them." Minnesota's Senate Bill 6 adds a second provision. It, quote, "lets utilities curtail newly connected large customers during firm load-shedding events."

That second provision deserves more attention than it's getting. It turns a new large data center from firm load into partly interruptible load. The utility can switch it off when the system is stressed. For a hyperscaler sizing a site, that changes the reliability math. Mandatory cost coverage raises the bill. Curtailment risk raises the need for backup. Together they strengthen the case for on-site or behind-the-meter generation, and for contracts with incumbent firm generators. That is an incentive, not an observed shift.

It also fits a structural argument S&P Global made back in June. S&P said the biggest barrier to data center growth is a lack of transmission and substation capacity, not a shortage of generation. Minnesota's law takes that constraint and puts its price directly on the new load, rather than on existing ratepayers.

Now the skeptical note. Minnesota is one state, with limited hyperscale concentration. Treat it as a template, not a national trend. The real test is whether Virginia, Texas, or Georgia copy it, and nothing in the record shows that yet.

So what's the takeaway here. In this desk's coverage, the fight over who absorbs the grid cost of AI has gone from a rate case, to the federal regulator, to binding law, within a single week. Any state that copies Minnesota raises the effective cost of grid-connected sites there. That is a relative headwind for siting in those states, and a relative tailwind for firm power that can sit behind the meter.

Put both stories together and the pattern holds. When energy is scarce, neither Washington nor the state capitals are letting price clear it. They are allocating the pain by carve-out and by statute. Watch four markers from here. First, October 18th, when the Graham Act's tariff authority goes live, and whether anyone is actually targeted. Second, the tempo of Ukraine's refinery strikes. Third, whether another large-load state follows Minnesota. And fourth, the April 7th, 2027 expiry of the diesel license. Keep Wednesday's September CPI in view only as a pending print, not as evidence yet.

That's the briefing for October 11th. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
