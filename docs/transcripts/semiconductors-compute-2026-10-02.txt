Good morning. It's October 2nd, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

Yesterday we read the headline out of Micron's fiscal fourth quarter. We called the conventional-DRAM strength inside it the more cyclical leg of the business, separate from the structural HBM story. The full earnings call shows how much of Micron's future is already signed. That forces a partial revision of our own read, and it's today's lead.

Start with depth. Micron told analysts that more than 75 percent of fiscal 2027 shipments are already under customer allocation or contract. That phrase merges two different things. Allocation is the supplier rationing scarce output. A contract is a buyer's binding commitment. So treat three-quarters as the upper bound on what is truly locked, not the floor.

The longer-dated number is cleaner. Micron now has 26 Strategic Customer Agreements, which it calls SCAs, covering roughly 35 percent of production volume through 2030. And management said plainly that the SCA agreements cover both DRAM and NAND. So this is not just an HBM lock-up. The multi-year contracting we've tracked in AI memory is reaching into the commodity side of Micron's book.

Here's how far that revises yesterday's read. Part of what we called cyclical is being converted into contracted, multi-year visibility. That's a real change. But 35 percent through 2030 still leaves roughly two-thirds of Micron's output exposed to the cycle. And yesterday's point about margins stands. Conventional DRAM is still riding scarcity pricing that a downturn would erode. The revision is to the duration of the windfall, not to its nature.

Now the question this show posed on Wednesday about Samsung's long-term deals. Do these contracts fix price, or only volume? For Micron, the call gives a partial answer. Management said three quarters of expected SCA revenue has a defined pricing framework. A majority of those frameworks use price bands with a floor and a ceiling. The remaining quarter is negotiated periodically against market prices. So this is real but partial price protection, not a bare volume commitment. Management also said any new SCA discussions are being negotiated at higher pricing, reflecting current conditions.

The so-what is a trade. A ceiling caps how much Micron can capture if the shortage tightens further. In exchange, a floor protects its revenue out to 2030 if the shortage breaks. Micron is selling some of its own upside to buy duration.

That contrast sharpens against SK hynix. Back in July, TrendForce, relaying unnamed sources, reported that SK hynix was adopting long-term agreements without the industry-standard price cap. Under that structure, spot-market increases flow straight into contract pricing when supply is short. TrendForce explicitly called it a departure from Micron's approach. Micron's own call now confirms the Micron side of that contrast. Two caveats, though. The SK hynix report is three months old and secondhand. And it describes a new contract structure, not the terms on every agreement SK hynix holds today.

Put the two together and you get two different bets on the same shortage. Micron is buying duration and giving up ceiling upside. SK hynix keeps its upside exposed and passes volatility onto its buyers. If spot stays above contract, SK hynix captures more of the next two years. If the cycle turns, the floors Micron kept are what protect its revenue. Which supplier made the better trade depends on whether this shortage holds through 2030. That's genuinely unknown, so treat it as a live question, not a verdict.

One more signal on contracting pace. Micron said ten additional agreements were signed in the fiscal fourth quarter alone. Management described them as spanning multiple business units, ranging from small to large customers. So the contract book isn't just a handful of hyperscaler megadeals. It's broadening.

Now the capital side, where the call overturns a story we might otherwise have told. Micron's full-year fiscal 2026 capital spending was $27.37 billion. Against $133 billion of revenue, that's roughly twenty cents of capex per revenue dollar, which looks restrained. But management guided first-half fiscal 2027 capex to approximately $25 billion. That includes about $11.5 billion in the first quarter alone. So the first six months of the new fiscal year will very nearly match all of fiscal 2026.

Where is it going? Management said the majority of the construction-spending increase versus prior plans is for clean rooms that come online in late 2028 and beyond. That's not a supplier declining to underwrite its own demand story. It's a supplier that held back in fiscal 2026 and is now accelerating. This show's read is that the SCA book is what gives it the confidence to pour concrete. The restraint was a 2026 posture, not a standing policy.

That timing also tells you something about the cycle. Clean rooms that land in late 2028 don't add bits in 2027. So the new spending extends the shortage window rather than closing it near term. The supply response arrives while many of these contracts are still running. If demand softens by then, the floor prices become Micron's cushion. That's the cycle bet sitting inside its capex plan.

Two second-order effects before we move on. First, buyers without an SCA. Smaller labs, enterprise OEMs and PC makers are competing for an uncommitted pool that Micron keeps contracting away. That's the same access-is-scarce dynamic this show flagged on Wednesday with Samsung. Now it has a company-disclosed number behind it. The squeeze on smaller buyers is our inference, not Micron's claim.

Second, Nvidia and the custom-ASIC vendors. Earlier this week we noted Nvidia's component commitments surging, mostly on memory. Banded multi-year contracts turn part of that memory bill from a cyclical swing into a semi-fixed input. That cuts both ways. Buyers get volume certainty and, on much of the priced book, a ceiling. They also lose the ability to time purchases around a memory trough.

Shift now from memory sellers whose buyers are lining up to sign, to a foundry with the opposite problem. Intel Foundry needs buyers for its next node, and it doesn't have an anchor signed. According to a Piper Sandler research note from September 25th, relayed by TechSpot, eight companies are evaluating Intel's 14A process. They are Amazon, Apple, AMD, Google, Tesla, Microsoft, Nvidia and Qualcomm. That's nearly every major fabless and hyperscaler name in the industry. But the list comes from an analyst, not from Intel or any of the eight. And evaluating costs a buyer almost nothing. In this show's judgment, it doubles as leverage in pricing talks with TSMC. Evaluation is not a design win, and it isn't committed volume.

The deadline is what makes this news. The same reporting says Intel needs to sign an anchor customer in late 2026 or early 2027, ahead of a planned 2028 production ramp. Yesterday we said Intel's 18A execution was beating internal targets, but that the real gate was a named external customer committing volume. Intel's 14A is where that gate actually sits, and now it has a clock on it. Score this as customer interest, not customer commitment, until a name is attached to signed volume.

A brief contrast on the same theme. Samsung's Taylor, Texas fab reportedly reached full booking before 2-nanometer volume production begins. That's from an early-September report relayed by Sammyfans, so treat it as context, not news. Booked capacity is not shipped output, and the site is only moving toward trial operations. But it shows that a US leading-edge fab can attract commitments before it proves yield, when the node and the timing line up. Intel's 14A problem may be less about the node than about the offer wrapped around it.

Put it together by competitive dimension. On memory pricing power and visibility, the sellers hold the cards. Micron chose capped but durable, SK hynix chose uncapped but volatile, and the cycle will decide between them. On capacity, Micron is now spending to stretch the window, not just harvesting it. On foundry customer relationships, Intel has broad interest and no signature, while Samsung's Texas fab is booked but not yet shipping. Watch two dates next. TSMC reports on October 15th, and ASML's results are expected in mid-October. Both will show whether the capacity buildout is still outrunning the economics, or whether the economics are catching up.
