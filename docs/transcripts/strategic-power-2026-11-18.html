Strategic Power, Energy, and Institutional Change

Transcript · November 18, 2026

Good morning. It's Wednesday, November 18th, 2026, and this is Strategic Power.

Here's the through-line for today. The demand side of the AI power story isn't the constraint anymore. The grid's rules are. This week the institutions that write those rules moved on who waits and who pays. They did not move on how many electrons exist. And on chips, China's leverage has moved from what it buys to what it supplies.

Start with PJM, the largest grid operator in the United States. On Monday we covered its market monitor's November 12th report. The Monitor demanded that December's capacity auction be postponed until forecast data-center load could be removed from it. PJM's own answer, filed with federal regulators on November 16th, points the other way. It doesn't price that load out. It tries to connect it faster.

The filing, which PJM calls fast energization, would cut the study period for new data centers from about twenty-four months to twelve. It gets there by studying power plants, transmission upgrades, and large loads together, instead of in separate queues. PJM is asking the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission to open a sixty-day comment period and rule within one hundred twenty days. On our arithmetic, that points to a decision around mid-March.

Two caveats matter. First, this is a filing, not an approval. Second, the twelve-month figure is a study-completion target, not the date anything gets plugged in. PJM's own transmittal makes that concrete. It says the first fast-energization cycle would finish in 2029. So even a clean, on-time win at FERC next spring doesn't move real megawatts for years.

The customers themselves doubt the clock. Before the filing, the Data Center Coalition and the Corporate Energy Buyers Association wrote to PJM's board. In their words, they "remain deeply concerned that the proposed approach could have the opposite effect." The worry is the annual cycle. A project that clears its transmission owner's review just after the yearly cutoff could wait nearly a year for the next cycle. Then it would spend another year inside PJM's study. That's nearly two years, which is roughly today's wait, not half of it.

There's a cost story in the filing too. PJM isn't setting an allocation formula. Instead, it would publish, in one place, the transmission-upgrade costs driven by specific large loads. States could then use their power over retail rates to decide whether those data centers pay. A PJM spokesman put the intent plainly: large loads should bear the costs of the upgrades they drive. The filing also moves financial and readiness screening earlier, to weed out speculative applications. And it lets some customers take interruptible service while upgrades are built, provided spare transmission capacity exists. On our read, that flexible-load option is the one piece that could bring power forward before a full cycle runs.

Now the market read, which is our inference. A first cycle that finishes in 2029 does nothing for December's auction. The Monitor has warned that auction will likely clear at the maximum price. So near-term scarcity stays where it was. That keeps capacity revenue firm for independent power producers in the footprint, such as Constellation, Vistra and Talen. It also keeps the bill on ratepayers until supply catches up. The plain read: PJM has effectively conceded that its own queue is the binding constraint on new compute. But what it filed is a faster clock and a cost ledger, not new generation.

A faster queue still doesn't settle who pays today. North Carolina just answered that question directly. On November 17th, state regulators approved Duke Energy rate increases. Alongside them came a separate rate schedule for data centers of fifty megawatts or larger. Its minimum payments are set at seventy-five percent of contracted demand.

That turns a speculative reservation into a contracted obligation. If a developer books capacity and then scales back, it still owes most of the bill. On our read, that's the first concrete tool aimed at the phantom-load problem: developers reserving grid capacity they may never use. It came from a state commission, not from PJM or FERC. And it's exactly the kind of billing decision PJM's filing hands to the states.

Keep the price effect in proportion. Dallas Federal Reserve researchers, as relayed by a regional outlet, estimate existing data centers have raised wholesale power prices by two to six percent on average. That's a bounded estimate for today's installed base. Don't confuse it with North Carolina's broader rate case. There, residential rates are expected to rise more than nine percent over two years. Nothing in the record ties that increase specifically to data centers.

The second-order effect is our inference. If minimum-take tariffs spread, speculative reservations get culled, and load forecasts converge toward real demand. But a seventy-five percent minimum protects other ratepayers only if the tenant stays solvent. Stranding risk moves onto data-center balance sheets. So tenant credit quality becomes a utility's problem, not just a developer's. Optionality on grid capacity now has a price tag.

And the demand pressing on both of those stories is not slowing down. Nvidia reported fiscal third-quarter results on November 17th. Revenue was 115.8 billion dollars, about double a year ago, and nearly all of it came from the data-center business.

Then the guide. Nvidia projected 130 billion dollars for the fourth quarter, plus or minus three percent. And the release states plainly that it is not assuming any data-center compute revenue from China. Nvidia has taken that posture before, so the exclusion itself isn't the news. The news is the size of a guide built entirely without China.

That closes a loop from Monday, when we noted Nvidia's filing showing Beijing restricting H200 purchases. Now the door is shut from both sides, in the guidance itself.

What the guide doesn't settle is Washington's newest lever. On Monday, November 16th, the Commerce Department issued an interim rule on remote access to advanced AI chips. It requires export licenses for those chips going to data centers in five countries, among them Singapore and Malaysia. The trigger is a data center renting more than a modest cluster's worth of compute to China-linked companies. In short, it targets the rented rack rather than the shipped chip. Nothing in the reporting we have shows what Nvidia's management said about it.

Here's the inference worth sitting with. With China already at zero in the outlook, further US export restrictions cost Nvidia shareholders little in the near term. That likely weakens corporate resistance in Washington to tightening further. It also pushes Beijing's response toward what it supplies, rather than what it buys. China has been written out of Nvidia's numbers. It hasn't been written out of the supply chain.

Which is where Beijing still holds real leverage, and where this desk owes a correction. Yesterday we said China's rare-earth export-control suspension had lapsed on November 10th with no extension. That was wrong. Reporting that relays China's commerce ministry and customs authority says the suspension runs from November 11th, 2026 through January 10th, 2027. Both yesterday's read and today's rest on secondary reporting of the commerce ministry's notice, so treat the January 10th date as reported, not confirmed.

The point that survives the correction matters more. The suspension was never the real lever. China's April 2025 licences on seven elements, among them dysprosium, terbium and yttrium, stay in force whatever Beijing decides. Those are the heavy rare earths that matter most for defense magnets. So the extension doesn't defuse the risk that Beijing answers that Commerce remote-access rule. It just moves the decision point to January 10th.

On the supply side, allies are building around that leverage, slowly. On November 17th, France and Malaysia agreed to a rare-earth mining and processing venture, expected to start operating in early 2027. Malaysia carved out an exception to its own 2024 raw rare-earth export ban, for France alone. That comes alongside a thirteen-thousand-tonne processing joint venture in Perak. Our read is that the carve-out is the tell. The ex-China chain is being built on the same selective-access leverage Beijing uses.

In New York, a state incentive package of roughly fifty-four million dollars, confirmed earlier this month, backs a Rochester separation plant. It's planned to handle about eighteen thousand tons a year from 2027. Both projects carry start dates, and Rochester carries state money, which is more than press-release industrial policy usually offers. But both are 2027 plans, not operating plants. The truce has an expiration date now. The licensing regime that actually controls the inputs does not.

Three things to watch. First, tomorrow's first-ever three-way summit between the US, Chinese and Russian presidents, in Shenzhen on the sidelines of APEC. It's the nearest room where the chip and rare-earth tracks could be traded against each other. No outcome is known, and any reported deal from that room should be treated as unconfirmed until it's independently verified. Second, PJM's December capacity auction, whose window opens December 9th, and the FERC comment period on fast energization. Third, January 10th, when the rare-earth suspension comes up for renewal or lapse.
