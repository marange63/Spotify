Good morning, it's Wednesday, November 4th, and this is your Capital Markets Radar.

The headline this week is that the AI trade is now being priced in the bond market, not just the stock market. Credit spreads, not equity multiples, are becoming the real scoreboard for the buildout. And so far the scoreboard says the money is still there, just at steeply different prices.

Start with the lead, because it's the next rung on the financing ladder this desk has been climbing since late October. On Friday's show it was Microsoft's bond. Yesterday it was Oracle's debt-and-stock raise. Now it's Meta, reportedly seeking at least twenty billion dollars in its first investment-grade bond sale since April. What's new isn't the size. It's the price. Meta's initial spread guidance runs one hundred thirty basis points over Treasuries on the five-year, and one hundred eighty-five on the ten-year. It's two hundred thirty on the twenty-year, and the thirty-year carries the widest spread of all. That slope is the market charging for the length of the AI payback. The longer you ask investors to wait, the more they want paid.

The reporting on the deal adds the qualitative piece. Investor demand for AI-related debt has cooled since the spring. Money managers now want higher premiums for the risks of Big Tech's spending commitments. The comparison point is Meta's own last deal. In April it raised twenty-five billion dollars and drew peak investor orders of ninety-six billion. Two cautions before anyone calls this a crack. These are initial guidance levels, and guidance often tightens into final pricing. And we don't have a verified final order book for this deal yet. So "cooled" is not "closed." The market is funding the buildout at a price, and that price now has a term structure.

One number to strip out of the conversation. Some coverage this week adds Meta's bond deal to a newly disclosed sixty-billion-dollar at-the-market equity program, and arrives at a much bigger financing headline. Meta's own SEC filing doesn't support that. It says the equity program is capped at sixty billion dollars. It says the program is tied to the start of trading in Meta's Class C stock, which hasn't happened. It says no agreement for the program exists yet. And it says the program isn't expected to begin until the first quarter of 2027. So that's not money being raised this week. Treat it as an option Meta is holding, not capital in hand. If anything, it lets Meta see where its stock sits next year before it dilutes anyone.

Now the price discrimination inside that same market, in the same week. Microsoft's roughly twenty-five-billion-dollar bond drew orders topping one hundred billion. So the deepest demand for the strongest name is still there. Oracle sits at the other end of the spectrum. S&P cut it to BBB-minus in July, one notch above junk. Its February bond carried a six-point-seven-percent coupon. It sold ten billion dollars of new bonds on Monday, and its shares fell almost nine percent that day. A correction on our part: yesterday this show put that drop at five percent. The figure for the day is almost nine. So what we're watching isn't a shut window. It's a steeply tiered one, sorted by credit quality and by tenor.

Zoom out, and the scale makes this a credit-portfolio story, not just a tech story. Large AI spenders have raised at least two hundred fourteen billion dollars of debt in 2026. That's up a hundred fifty-seven percent on the eighty-three billion raised in all of 2025. One estimate now puts AI at roughly a quarter of all US corporate bond issuance, up from four percent two years ago. The so-what: passive investment-grade holders are absorbing that sector concentration whether they chose to or not.

The stock market is telling the same story, just more narrowly. On Monday, the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index closed twenty-nine-point-four percent below its June 22nd record. That was its lowest close since late April. The Nasdaq 100 closed ten-point-four percent off its October 6th record, its third correction of the year. The S&P 500, by contrast, was only about three-point-six percent off its own record. That gap says the drawdown is concentrated, not systemic.

And it's concentrated almost exactly where the credit stress sits. Measured from each name's own 2026 peak, CoreWeave is down sixty-three percent and Oracle is down fifty-five. Nvidia is down a milder seventeen. The names that lean hardest on outside capital have fallen furthest. Here's the part the market may be under-reading. For a levered issuer like Oracle, a falling share price thins the equity cushion under its debt, right as it returns to the bond market. Weaker equity and tougher credit terms can then feed each other. That's our inference, not a reported fact. On timing, chip stocks did lift the Nasdaq in Tuesday's session. But one up day after a near-thirty-percent drawdown in semis is a bounce, not a bottom. The so-what: this is rotation inside AI-linked names, not a broad risk-off, but the reflexive loop is live in the weakest credits.

If the public bond market is charging Meta one hundred eighty-five basis points for ten-year money, what is private credit charging? Apollo's third-quarter call claimed excess spread of two hundred eighty-five basis points over Treasuries on investment-grade origination. On sub-investment-grade origination, it claimed four hundred thirty. That's management's own figure, and the structures differ from Meta's bonds, so treat the comparison as indicative. Still, it's a hundred basis points wider than Meta's ten-year guidance. Our read is that this helps explain why the strongest AI spenders keep crowding into the public market. It's still the cheaper window. The borrowers pushed out of it land in a market whose bottom tier is already under strain. The CCC tier of leveraged loans we flagged on Sunday was last reported at its widest spreads since 2009. So stress is visible at the bottom of the quality stack, even as top-tier borrowers pay up and still get funded.

Underneath every one of those spreads sits the policy rate. As of Monday morning, Polymarket priced a quarter-point December hike at seventy-two-and-a-half percent. A December cut was priced at around one percent or less on both Polymarket and Kalshi. That's close to the Sunday-night snapshot we cited yesterday, so treat it as a level, not a trend. The point is that the AI selloff hasn't bought the market any policy relief. The next wave of AI issuance faces two premia stacked together: the hike path, and the AI-specific spread. October payrolls land Friday, and October CPI follows next week. Neither has been released, so there's no number yet, only a setup.

The most rate-sensitive asset on the screen is already voting. US spot Bitcoin and Ethereum ETFs lost about one-point-three billion dollars combined across Friday and Monday. BlackRock's IBIT alone shed four hundred five million dollars on Monday. That was more than the entire group's net outflow that day. So the redemption is concentrated in the flagship institutional wrapper, not spread across the category. Bitcoin closed Monday at seventy-six thousand seven hundred nine dollars, a thirty-day low. And October ended with the first monthly ETF outflow since June. Our inference is that institutional and carry money is stepping back from the highest-beta risk asset on the board, just as cut expectations have evaporated. The timing fits, but timing isn't proof of cause. The so-what: bitcoin's marginal flow now answers to the same institutional risk budget as the AI equity book.

Three things to watch from here. First, Meta's final pricing and order book, measured against April's ninety-six billion dollars of demand. Second, whether the next AI-linked bond has to pay a real concession to get done. That would be the clearer tell that credit appetite is cracking, rather than just repricing. Third, Friday's payrolls print, the next real catalyst for that December rate call.

That's the radar for November 4th. Trade carefully, and I'll see you tomorrow.
