Good morning, it's Tuesday, September fifteenth. Tomorrow afternoon the Fed delivers its decision, and the hike odds have owned every market conversation for a week. Today, deliberately, none of that. The thing that has actually been repricing for a month is not the risk complex. It is the hedge complex. Going into a binary event, that is the more interesting story.

Start with gold, and start with the arithmetic rather than the tape. Gold set a record on January twenty-eighth at five thousand five hundred and eighty-nine dollars an ounce, per Fortune's gold tracker. On Friday it held near four thousand three hundred and eighty-five. That is a drawdown of more than a fifth from the record, roughly twenty-one percent. And understand what has been holding gold up alongside the rates story. Part of its bid this cycle has been a geopolitical premium out of the Strait of Hormuz, where shipping risk has been repricing crude all month. Keep that premium in mind. It comes back at the end.

The standing view on this desk has been that a meaningful share of gold's bid is a hedge on fiscal and inflation credibility rather than a pure real-rates trade, and that a hawkish Fed therefore would not break it. That view is wrong. What is new today is not the direction but the size of the hole. This is not a whipsaw around a record. The insurance is down more than a fifth heading into the event it was bought to protect against.

The mechanical story is real yields. The thirty-year Treasury yield sat at five point three six percent as of September eleventh, the highest since 2007. Higher yields raise the opportunity cost of holding an asset that pays nothing, and gold is paying that cost. But do not take the mechanism at face value. Five point three six is a nominal yield. Suppose this cycle's hawkish repricing is coming from an energy supply shock rather than demand-side inflation. That is what this desk has argued since the August prints. In that case breakevens are doing part of the work, and the real-rate move is smaller than the nominal move implies. A drawdown this large is also big enough to be a positioning event rather than a clean fundamental re-rate, a record-long book liquidating into a hostile tape. We have no positioning data to settle that, and I am not going to pretend otherwise. What we can say cleanly is what the hedge has cost, and it has cost a fifth of its value.

And the other hard assets are not confirming each other either. Copper closed at six dollars and twenty-eight cents a pound on September fourteenth, per Trading Economics. That is down nearly three percent on the day and just under five percent over the month, though still up thirty-five percent year over year. Reported commentary ties some of the monthly softness to tariff concerns, which is a policy variable rather than a rates one. The standing split here has been gold as a rates trade and copper as a scarcity trade. That split was right in direction and wrong in timing. The scarcity bid has been bleeding for four weeks. Do not over-read it either. A five percent pullback inside a thirty-five percent annual gain is a pause, not a breakdown. The so-what is narrow but real. If you were holding copper as an inflation hedge rather than as an electrification trade, the last four weeks have not paid you.

Uranium is the exception. It was at ninety dollars and fifteen cents a pound as of September eleventh, up three and a half percent over the month and nineteen percent year over year, on continued nuclear buildout. It is the only leg of the hard-asset complex still climbing on a one-month basis. It is also the only one with essentially no Fed variable in it. So here is the ordering. Gold worst, hit hardest by the rates channel. Copper fading, the scarcity bid eroding despite a huge annual gain. Uranium intact, running on structural demand the Fed cannot touch. That is not a clean two-basket split. It is three assets driven by three different things that happen to be resolving in the same week. And the flip side for uranium is worth saying out loud. A trade the Fed cannot hurt is also a trade the Fed cannot rescue, so tomorrow's decision will not resolve it in either direction.

The dollar sits in the middle of all this without taking a side. The dollar index rose about a third of a percent on Monday, to ninety-nine point four four. Zoom out and that move disappears. Up on the day, down two tenths of a percent on the month, up two point two percent on the year. That internal contradiction is the point. The currency has not decided what tomorrow means either.

Which raises the question that actually matters this week. If the recognized hedges against fiscal and inflation risk are all repricing lower into the meeting, the interesting problem is not what the Fed does tomorrow. It is who is buying the government's paper at all. And the fastest-growing new buyer sits at the very short end.

That is where stablecoins fit, so let me put the boundary on the claim before I make it. This is a front-end story, not a long-end one. The channel that is genuinely straining is long-end demand at a nineteen-year-high yield, and short-dated stablecoin reserves do not reach that part of the curve. What they do reach is the bill market, and there they are already sovereign-scale. Stablecoin issuers' Treasury-bill holdings now rival those of large countries. Collectively they are a top-twenty foreign holder of short-term US Treasuries, with Tether alone around seventeenth. The GENIUS Act requires reserves to be held one-for-one in cash and short-dated Treasuries, with monthly disclosure. So growth in stablecoin supply converts mechanically into bill demand.

Tokenized Treasuries are the smaller adjacent channel. Circle's USYC held about three billion dollars and BlackRock's BUIDL about two point seven billion. Onchain Treasuries more than doubled across 2025, from three point nine billion to eight point seven billion, with Ethereum the dominant venue. Date that last one carefully. It is a full-year 2025 figure, not a recent move.

So what does a hike do to that bid? It cuts in two directions at once. Higher front-end yields raise issuer float income, and the GENIUS Act bars passing that yield through to holders. So tightening improves the issuer's economics and weakens the product against a plain money-market fund in the same stroke. Better business, worse deal for the customer. That means the direction of future supply growth, and therefore the size of the bill bid behind it, is genuinely ambiguous rather than simply positive. Which is the honest answer to who is buying the paper. A large and fast-growing buyer at the front end, whose growth rate the Fed is about to push forward and backward simultaneously.

There is a data problem underneath all of this worth naming. We went looking for a dated figure showing whether stablecoin supply is actually rolling over. The one number that survives a direct check of its source is an April report putting the sector's market capitalization above three hundred and twenty-one billion dollars, described as an all-time high at the time. A competing account of a later and higher peak could not be traced to any page that contained it. So this is a sovereign-scale buyer of government paper whose aggregate size cannot be pinned down to the month. This desk made a smaller version of that point last week, about business-development-company marks. Loans that look current on a manager's own books read differently once someone adjusts them. Same problem, one rung up the ladder. Treasury and Fed data get checked by people with a reason to check them. This market's numbers are self-attested, at exactly the scale where self-attestation stops being good enough.

So heading into tomorrow, watch the hedges, not just the decision. The clean test this desk named Sunday is the one still in front of us. That Hormuz premium fades, and the Fed hikes anyway, leaving gold to take the rate penalty with no war bid to offset it. That configuration has never been run in this cycle. Tomorrow afternoon is the closest it has come.

That's the radar for today. Talk to you tomorrow.
