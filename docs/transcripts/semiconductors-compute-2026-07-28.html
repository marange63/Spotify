Good morning, it's Tuesday, July 28th, and this is your semiconductor and compute briefing.

For four straight sessions the story in chips has been the Korean memory duopoly selling scarcity forward. Nvidia locked SK hynix into a deal it says could be worth $500 billion over a number of years, tied to data centers coming online in 2027 and a targeted 2 gigawatts of power capacity. Samsung landed Broadcom on a five-year memory-and-foundry agreement estimated at more than $200 billion through 2030, covering Samsung's 2-nanometer-and-below logic process and HBM supply for Broadcom's next-generation AI accelerators. Today that bet gets tested from both directions at once — a fresh supply-side challenger from China, and fresh demand-side doubt out of the hyperscalers.

Start with the challenger. ChangXin Memory Technologies, China's leading DRAM maker, went public on Shanghai's STAR Market and raised roughly 58 billion yuan, about $8.6 billion, in the country's largest semiconductor listing ever. Shares closed around 49 yuan on debut day, roughly a 470 percent pop from the IPO price, which put CXMT's market capitalization near 3.3 trillion yuan — enough to overtake Industrial and Commercial Bank of China and make a DRAM maker China's single most valuable listed company overnight. The fundamentals underneath the pop are real: CXMT held a 7.67 percent share of global DRAM in 2025, the world's fourth-largest producer, and its first-quarter revenue this year hit 50.8 billion yuan, up more than 700 percent year over year.

So what does that actually mean. Separate the capital-markets headline from the competitive reality. A 470 percent single-day surge on the STAR Market is a listing-day speculative pattern, not a stable enterprise value you can compare against Samsung, SK hynix, or Micron. That 7.67 percent global share makes CXMT the world's number four, but the top three sit far ahead on both scale and process, and a war chest doesn't close a lithography gap that export controls actively widen. The proceeds language is general — production-line upgrades and DRAM technology advancement — with no confirmed HBM roadmap or timeline. Equipment export controls still cap how fast CXMT can move into advanced lithography, and therefore into leading-edge HBM. What this IPO really is, is Beijing capitalizing its memory-sovereignty push through domestic capital markets rather than direct subsidy, giving a state-aligned champion a war chest to chase the DRAM oligopoly over a multi-year horizon. And it doesn't stop at DRAM. Paired with YMTC, China's NAND champion, Beijing now has two capitalized memory players attacking the parts of the stack that export controls don't fully wall off. It doesn't change this year's HBM allocation. It does put a new name in the room for the next several years of that fight.

Now the demand side wobbled the same week. Chip stocks sold off sharply again today, extending a rout that first hit SK hynix and Samsung Electronics on July 2nd, when SK hynix fell 14.5 percent and Samsung dropped 9.1 percent in a single session, a combined $290 billion of market value gone in one day. That selloff deepened further today, hitting SK hynix, Samsung, and SoftBank again — I'll flag that today's exact percentage moves aren't independently verified yet, so take "deepened" as the reported direction rather than a precise number.

The proximate trigger is a report that Meta is building an internal unit, reportedly called Meta Compute and led by infrastructure chief Santosh Janardhan alongside Meta Superintelligence Labs' Daniel Gross and president Dina Powell McCormick, to monetize AI capacity Meta built for itself. The reporting describes two paths under consideration: reselling raw GPU capacity the way a neocloud like CoreWeave does, or selling API access to Meta's own hosted models, including its closed-weight model Muse Spark. That distinction matters. If Meta goes the bare-metal resale route, that's a much more direct signal of GPU overbuild relative to workload demand than if it simply monetizes model access on infrastructure it's already using. Either way, this is Meta weighing what to do with excess capacity underneath a committed $182.9 billion AI infrastructure buildout — and note, Meta itself hasn't confirmed this on the record. It traces to Bloomberg reporting, and Meta didn't respond to a request for comment.

Here's the analytical distinction the prompt world keeps blurring: compute-constrained demand versus economics-constrained demand. Micron told you last month its entire calendar-2026 HBM supply, including HBM4, is sold out on price and volume — that's compute-constrained, real scarcity. A hyperscaler quietly shopping around its own excess GPU capacity is the opposite signal, economics-constrained, meaning the build may be outrunning near-term workloads at the margin. Both can be true in different corners of the market simultaneously, and there's a circularity worth naming: if today's selloff is a durable repricing rather than a crowded rally correcting, it raises the cost of the capital the Korean memory makers need to fund the very supply expansions that Friday's mega-deals assumed would happen. And remember what Friday's deal actually committed Nvidia to. Not just selling chips, but underwriting a 2-gigawatt buildout — hundreds of thousands of GPUs of power capacity. That blurs the line between chip vendor and infrastructure financier, and vendor-financed demand of that kind is exactly what a durable derating would call into question. If it sustains, it could slow the pace of new HBM capacity commitments just as the Korean makers try to lock in 2026-through-2028 allocation at higher prices.

While the tape was punishing memory names, the quarter's cleanest execution signal came from the logic side. Intel's second-quarter revenue grew 25 percent, the company's fastest growth in almost 15 years, with non-GAAP earnings per share of 22 cents matching consensus. More importantly for the foundry thesis, Intel's Foundry segment narrowed its operating loss to $2.1 billion on $5.8 billion of revenue, down from a $3.2 billion loss a year earlier, and Intel's 18A process reportedly climbed to about 85 percent yield from roughly 65 percent the prior quarter — those yield figures come from secondary reporting, not Intel's own primary materials, so treat them as reported rather than confirmed. A twenty-point yield gain in a single quarter, if it holds, is the kind of move that changes unit economics. Intel also said 18A now has a manufacturing commitment from a major cloud service provider, its first commercially recognized external volume customer — but it wouldn't name the company. So the yield trajectory is the most encouraging internal data point Intel Foundry has produced in a while. The unnamed customer is exactly why skepticism about the foundry turnaround stays elevated: until Intel can put a real name next to that commitment, the market has no way to independently size how much volume is actually coming.

Where does this all resolve. Fast, actually. SK hynix reports earnings tomorrow, July 29th, and Samsung follows July 30th. Don't anchor on the headline profit numbers — watch the blended DRAM average selling price trajectory and how much detail they give on HBM4 ramp timing. That's where you'll see whether the scarcity the Korean makers priced into $500 billion and $200 billion of forward contracts last Friday is holding, or whether this week's selloff and CXMT's entrance are the first cracks in that story.
