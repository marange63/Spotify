Good morning. It's Friday, August 28th, and this is your briefing on artificial intelligence across the biopharmaceutical value chain.

For the better part of a week, this beat has told one story: no layer of the AI drug stack has produced an independent, positive, prospective result. Target discovery, molecule design, and finally even the operations layer — all promise, no randomized proof. Today that changes, at least partially. And it changes at the end of the stack nobody expected.

The result is INTerpath-001. Merck and Moderna announced on August 19th that a Phase 3 trial met its primary endpoint of recurrence-free survival and a key secondary endpoint of distant-metastasis-free survival. The regimen is intismeran autogene, an individualized neoantigen mRNA therapy, given alongside Keytruda, against Keytruda alone. The trial enrolled 1,137 patients with completely resected stage IIB through IV melanoma, randomized two-to-one after surgery. Each patient's dose is built from their own tumor. It is a synthetic mRNA construct encoding up to 34 neoantigens specific to that person's cancer, sequenced, ranked, and manufactured one patient at a time.

Be precise about what was validated here. This is not a model discovering a target or designing a molecule. It is computational tumor sequencing and neoantigen ranking, feeding a personalized manufacturing line, sitting on top of an already-approved checkpoint inhibitor. Do not file intismeran under "AI-designed drug." But it is still a meaningful first. A blinded, randomized, prospective trial cleared the bar this field has been failing all week.

The win is thinner than the headline suggests. Merck and Moderna's release gives no hazard ratio and no magnitude of benefit. The endpoints were simply met. The companies say they plan to present the data at an upcoming international medical meeting, and to engage regulators on filing submissions from there. So the size of the effect is unknown today. That matters, because the market did not wait for it.

Tempus AI is the genomics and AI-data company, and it was not a party to this trial. It rose 23.91 percent on August 19th anyway, opening at just under 50 dollars a share and trading up to just over 61. The reason sits one layer down. Personalis is the tumor-sequencing company Tempus agreed to buy in a roughly one-and-a-half-billion-dollar, mostly-stock deal announced July 20th. Those are reported terms, not figures pulled from a filing. Personalis makes the ultrasensitive sequencing test that, according to secondary reporting, underpins the neoantigen-identification workflow behind this vaccine.

Say the weak link plainly. Merck and Moderna's own trial release does not mention Personalis or Tempus at all. That linkage comes from market and press commentary. Personalis itself appears to have described a version of the technical relationship in an investor release well before this readout, but that could not be confirmed against the primary text, so treat the attribution as probable rather than proven. Either way, the market repriced the sequencing company sitting underneath somebody else's clinical result. That is the pattern this beat keeps finding. Leverage accrues to whoever owns the data or the assay, not to whoever runs the trial or the model. Today it just arrived with a positive sign in front of it.

It also explains a repricing already underway. Tempus signed enterprise real-world-data agreements with Merck in March and with Gilead in April. Treat those as pricing context, not news. Industry analysts, not company filings, put the deals in the hundreds of millions of dollars over multiple years, against a prior norm of somewhere between tens of thousands and a million dollars a year for real-world-data access. That is a press estimate, not a contract term. Directionally, though, it tells you why a 24 percent move on someone else's trial is not purely reflexive. Investors are capitalizing a toll booth on genomic and longitudinal patient data whose price already reset upward this year.

One second-order effect before we move on. Per-patient manufactured therapeutics shift the binding constraint away from model capability and toward logistics: sequencing turnaround, per-patient dose manufacture, cold chain. If this modality scales, the R&D question stops being whether AI cuts discovery cost. It becomes whether personalization structurally raises cost of goods sold. Expect, too, that platform companies start disclosing which late-stage partner programs run on their assay. If the market will re-rate a genomics name by a quarter off a partner's readout, that disclosure is itself an asset.

Now down the stack, to the layer this beat had been calling the only checkable win in the whole thing. Faro Health raised a 37.3-million-dollar Series B on August 26th, co-led by Merck's Global Health Innovation Fund and S32, to push agentic AI into clinical trial protocol design. Faro's platform turns narrative protocol language — objectives, endpoints, eligibility criteria — into structured data that both people and software agents can act on. Merck's venture investor calls it a technology that, by saving time and improving quality, has the potential to transform clinical development. Notice the tense. That is a venture thesis, not a measured result. No valuation was disclosed for the round.

The evidence gap underneath it is the real story. Faro has had a live, multi-year deployment with Bristol-Myers Squibb since the end of March, to scale what BMS calls autonomous AI across its global clinical portfolio. Five months in, there is still no published, independent measurement of timeline or cost reduction from that deployment, or from any other. Earlier this month, a Perspective in Nature Reviews Drug Discovery put the peer-reviewed version of the same point on the record. AI applications in drug discovery have attracted high interest, it says, but evidence for clinically relevant impact so far is limited. That is the journal-grade echo of the independent test this beat led with yesterday. So strategic pharma capital is funding the operations layer in the same week that layer failed its first real evidence check.

There is one clean read-through if the tooling does eventually work. Protocol authoring and amendment cycles are billable manual labor at the contract research organizations. Agentic protocol design pressures CRO pricing at exactly the point where those margins are thickest. That is the trade to watch if a measured number ever shows up.

One rung further down, adoption widens as evidence thins. Lundbeck and Eversana announced on August 25th an expanded partnership to scale AI-enabled commercialization across Lundbeck's US commercial organization — marketing, medical communications, market access, and media operations. Eversana's chief executive frames it as a hybrid of human expertise and AI-driven execution. No financial terms were disclosed. Neither were any outcome metrics from whatever deployment preceded this expansion. That is a full-organization rollout justified by results nobody outside the companies can see.

Put the stack together and the relationship inverts as you go down it. The narrowest and hardest intervention to run — a randomized Phase 3 of a personalized, manufactured therapy — carries the strongest evidence and the least deployment. The clinical-operations layer has strategic pharma capital and no measured outcome. The commercial layer has the broadest deployment of the day and no disclosed metric at all. Evidence and adoption are moving in opposite directions.

What would settle the lead is the hazard ratio Merck and Moderna present at that medical meeting, and it cuts both ways. A modest benefit would confirm that the market moved on the mere existence of a result rather than its magnitude. A large one would be the first data point this beat has had that could plausibly bear on probability of technical success. Not proof that AI improves clinical success rates broadly, but the first item all month that touches the question rather than gesturing at it. Note what today's result still does not touch: Phase 2 attrition. So the standing read on R&D spend holds — reallocation toward earlier, cheaper failure, not reduction. What is different today is that for the first time, the reallocation has a randomized trial behind it instead of a vendor slide.

That's the briefing. Have a good weekend.
