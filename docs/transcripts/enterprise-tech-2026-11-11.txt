Good morning. It's Wednesday, November 11th, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

This week's AI revenue prints are answering the demand question. The question they leave open is what that revenue costs. At CoreWeave, it costs the balance sheet. At EPAM Systems, it costs the legacy business sitting next to it.

Start with CoreWeave, because yesterday's third-quarter print is the first fundamental data since the credit market spent the week pricing this company like a problem. Per the company's earnings release, revenue grew about a hundred sixty-eight percent from a year ago, to roughly three point seven billion dollars. Revenue backlog reached roughly a hundred thirty-two and a half billion dollars. Chief executive Michael Intrator called it a breakout quarter. That is paying demand, not a rumored buildout.

Now the cost side. CoreWeave raised its full-year capital expenditure guide to thirty-seven to thirty-nine billion dollars. By our arithmetic, that's roughly three times the revenue it expects this year. And adjusted operating margin fell six points from a year ago, to ten percent.

Here's where the cost actually lands. Adjusted operating income was a positive three hundred seventy-nine million dollars, and it still grew from a year ago. The GAAP bottom line went the other way, to a loss of seven hundred nine million. That's a swing of about one point one billion dollars between the two lines. On the earnings call, management put third-quarter interest expense at eight hundred forty-six million dollars. So interest alone is most of that swing. And it's guided higher again, to more than a billion dollars in the fourth quarter. The cost of this growth isn't hiding in operations. It's sitting in the financing line, and it's rising every quarter.

Here's the part that matters for the rest of the AI infrastructure trade. Last weekend, this show's capital markets desk reported one CoreWeave bond yielding above sixteen percent. On yesterday's call, management said CoreWeave will not use the unsecured debt market to fund the business at current yields. Instead, it plans to finance the majority of the remaining capex needed to deliver its backlog through delayed-draw term loans. Management also put its weighted average cost of debt at seven point nine percent, less than half that bond yield. So the refusal is rational. CoreWeave is declining to pay the marginal price of credit while its blended cost stays far lower.

The second-order effect is about who carries the risk. My inference is that this shifts incremental exposure away from unsecured bondholders and toward lenders secured by the assets themselves. In this business, the assets are GPUs. So the real underwriting question moves from CoreWeave's credit standing to how long those chips stay rentable. Demand looks answered. What has changed is the shape of the funding.

That brings us to margin, where management offered a specific story. It said CoreWeave raised pricing across SKUs by approximately twenty-five percent. It said margins reached the low double digits a quarter earlier than planned. And Intrator argued that margins on older GPUs should improve over time, as price and operating costs remain fixed but depreciation dissipates. Here's my read. If prices really rose by a quarter, the six-point margin decline is hard to pin on pricing pressure. It points instead to ramp timing, with new capacity carrying depreciation before it carries full revenue. That's management's story plus our inference, not an audit. But next quarter's margin can test it.

Notice what Intrator's older-GPU argument assumes, though. It assumes the price on older chips holds. That's where the week's other data point comes in. On Monday, this desk covered Bloomberg's report that Microsoft is winding down a 2023-era deal to rent AI capacity from Oracle. People familiar with the matter said the hardware ran an older generation of Nvidia chips that no longer met Microsoft's needs. Microsoft hasn't confirmed the report. The worry is simple. If hyperscalers hand back older rented capacity, the risk lands on debt-financed lessors like CoreWeave. This quarter's revenue and backlog don't show that happening yet. But the older-GPU pricing assumption is still untested. If older capacity can be handed back at renewal, price doesn't stay fixed. That leaves a sector-wide question open: are GPU useful lives being set too long?

CoreWeave did give a partial answer on who stands behind its commitments. Management said more than seventy-five percent of committed capex through 2027 is tied to investment-grade and enterprise customers. The rest sits with AI labs and AI-native companies. That describes committed capex, not the backlog itself, and no single-customer concentration was disclosed. But it locates the weakest link in the AI-lab slice. Those are the customers most exposed to a generational non-renewal like the one Microsoft reportedly exercised against Oracle.

Now flip to the buyer's side of those contracts, because this is where the CoreWeave print becomes an enterprise story. For a company renting GPU capacity from a neocloud, the lessor's financing is now part of the product. My inference is that the lessor's balance sheet belongs in the vendor risk review, right next to price. If the new term loans sit against the hardware, the lessor's lenders have a claim on the very chips your workloads run on. So the questions at signing change. What are your refresh rights to newer chips? What happens to your capacity if the lessor's financing tightens? Can you exit older hardware at renewal without starting over? The Microsoft episode shows the leverage a tenant has at renewal. Enterprises should write that option into the contract up front rather than assume they'll have it.

If CoreWeave shows AI revenue that costs balance sheet, the services layer shows AI revenue that costs the business it replaces. EPAM Systems reported last Thursday. On the earnings call, management said AI-native revenue was more than a hundred eighty-five million dollars in the quarter, about thirteen percent of the total. It raised its full-year AI-native target to at least six hundred fifty million dollars, from six hundred million. That's the kind of disclosure this desk said on Friday the market now wants. We argued that an AI story without its own disclosed number was starting to cost multiple. EPAM has a number. What the number reveals is the cost.

Here's why. Per EPAM's release, total revenue grew just two point three percent, an increase of thirty-two point one million dollars year over year. EPAM didn't give a year-ago AI-native figure. But the logic is simple. If AI-native added more than thirty-two million dollars over the year, then everything else at EPAM shrank. For a line now running above a hundred eighty-five million a quarter, with a target management just raised, that's a low bar. And management effectively confirmed it. Chief executive Balazs Fejes conceded that parts of EPAM's services experienced some level of shrinkage in the quarter.

The simplest reading is substitution. AI-native delivery is replacing headcount-priced work rather than sitting on top of it. Think about what that means from the client's side. The client gets the same outcome with fewer billable hours, which is exactly the productivity gain AI was sold on. The integrator books that gain as lost revenue. Two caveats keep this honest. AI-native is EPAM's own classification, with no outside audit of what qualifies, so some of it may be existing engagements under a new label. And one quarter can't separate AI substitution from ordinary cyclical softness in discretionary IT spending. Still, the inference for the sector is uncomfortable. If AI-native delivery cannibalizes headcount-priced services, integrators capture less revenue per project even as AI demand rises. That would pressure pricing power at headcount-based peers, though we have no peer data yet to confirm it. The way out is pricing on outcomes rather than hours, and that's the shift to watch for in EPAM's commentary.

Axon Enterprise is the contrast case. Per an earnings-highlights summary of its call, Axon's AI products surpassed a hundred million dollars in annual recurring revenue, in a quarter where total revenue grew twenty-eight percent. That rests on one secondary summary, and annual recurring revenue is a run-rate, not recognized revenue. But it shows the other path: AI attached to a sticky installed base, adding rather than replacing. Here's what separates the two paths. AI is additive when it's sold as a new, separately priced product on top of something the customer already owns. It's substitutive when it's sold as a cheaper way to deliver work the customer was already paying for by the hour. Axon is the first kind. EPAM is the second. A vendor's pricing model tells you which side of that line it's on.

So here's the throughline. Disclosed AI revenue is finally showing up in the numbers. And each time it does, it exposes a cost the headline growth hides. At CoreWeave, that cost is a rising interest bill and a bet on how long GPUs stay rentable. At EPAM, it's a legacy book shrinking under the line that replaces it. Three things to watch. First, whether CoreWeave funds its remaining capex the way management described, through delayed-draw term loans rather than unsecured bonds. Second, whether EPAM's fourth quarter clears its six hundred fifty million dollar AI-native target while total growth stays near flat. Third, Microsoft Ignite runs November 17th through 20th, so watch for anything on how Microsoft sources its AI capacity.

That's the briefing. Talk tomorrow.
