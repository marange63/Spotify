Private Equity & the Deal Economy

Good morning. It's Wednesday, August 26th, and this is your private equity briefing.

Start with one number pair that reframes the whole liquidity conversation. In the first half of 2026, the slice of the secondaries market that limited partners use to sell grew 4 percent. The slice general partners control grew 35 percent. That is not a rounding difference. That is the market telling you who controls the exit.

The source is Evercore's first-half secondary market review, as relayed in the trade press rather than the primary document, so hold the decimals loosely and the direction firmly. The composition, not the headline total, is what's new. GP-led volume reached 65 billion dollars, and for the first time it overtook LP-led volume, which came in at 56 billion. We already knew this market was setting records. What we didn't know until now is that the record is being set by sponsors, not by allocators.

Go one level down and it gets sharper. Single-asset continuation vehicles alone hit 34 billion dollars, up 88 percent year over year, and they now account for a little over half of all GP-led activity. So the fastest-growing part of the fastest-growing channel is sponsors rolling their own best assets into vehicles they continue to control. The channel a limited partner uses to exit on its own terms barely moved. That is the takeaway. Liquidity in private equity is increasingly manufactured by the general partner, for the asset the general partner chooses to keep. It is not made available to the limited partner who simply wants out.

The pricing data points the same way. Single-asset continuation vehicles priced mostly at par, with some clearing as much as 14 percent above net asset value. Multi-asset vehicles went the other way, with 71 percent of them pricing below par. That is a distribution, not an average, so don't read it as single-asset vehicles systematically clearing above their own marks. Read it as adverse selection. A sponsor re-underwrites its trophy asset at or above its own mark, and validates that mark in the process. Then it clears the rest of the book at a discount through the multi-asset wrapper.

Follow that through to the limited partner who stays put. Once the best asset rolls out into a single-asset vehicle, what's left in the legacy fund is the residual, and this same secondary market prices that residual below par. That's a quiet transfer of quality out of the old fund, at a price the sponsor had a hand in setting.

One honesty note on the data itself. A rival data provider had a materially different composition split last week. The providers roughly agree on price and disagree on share, and that disagreement is itself a finding. This market still isn't transparently measured, and anyone quoting a share of GP-led volume to a decimal place is quoting a provider, not a fact.

The 4 percent also deserves a counter-reading before we lean on it. Flat LP-led volume could mean bids are absent. It could equally mean allocators simply aren't distressed sellers right now, after the kind of year CalPERS reported on Friday, a preliminary 14.8 percent total return, with the private equity book up 17 percent. A pension that just grew its whole book has no allocation-math reason to dump stakes at a discount. What would settle it is pricing on LP-led portfolios, and that number isn't in front of me.

Now put the volume data against something this show flagged Monday. Staff at CalSTRS have been opting to take cash rather than roll existing stakes into new GP-led vehicles. Be careful about what that shows. Taking cash there is liquidity, and it's the GP-led market that delivered it. What it isn't is liquidity on the allocator's terms. CalSTRS got paid on the sponsor's timing, for the asset the sponsor chose to keep, at a price the sponsor helped set. And for whatever it still holds in the legacy fund, it is back in the channel that grew 4 percent. That's the shape of the problem. The fast-growing channel answers the sponsor's liquidity question first, and the allocator's only where the two happen to coincide.

Now the other side of the realization question. On Carlyle's second-quarter call, chief executive Harvey Schwartz said U.S. buyout, the firm's largest private equity strategy, returned 23 percent of its fair value to investors over the trailing twelve months. He benchmarked it himself, calling that more than twice the current industry average and above a long-term industry average of 20 percent. That is Carlyle's own disclosure, and it's a different ratio from cash returned as a share of firm-wide assets, which is the version this show ran on Sunday. The two aren't comparable, and the strategy-level one flatters. Note too that a shrinking book mechanically lifts it. This show reported Thursday that Carlyle's private equity assets under management actually fell about 1 percent year over year. The 23 percent is still the better number, because it's Carlyle's disclosure rather than my arithmetic. It just isn't the industry's number.

And don't take it as a clean rebuttal to the adverse-selection story either. It is one firm, one strategy, self-selected for disclosure. Carlyle does not break out how much of that 23 percent came from third-party sales versus continuation vehicles or sponsor-to-sponsor trades. We went into the transcript looking for exactly that split, and the transcript describes the realizations only by asset class and geography. So the question stays open, and the two answers point opposite ways. If those realizations are genuinely third-party, the distribution problem is more manager-specific than industry-wide, and manager selection matters more than asset-class exposure. If they're continuation-vehicle driven, it's the lead item's mechanism showing up again under a different name.

One more Carlyle data point worth a beat. Transaction fees hit a record 111 million dollars, more than double the level a year ago. That is not an annuity line like fee-related earnings. It's advisory and structuring work tied to transactions actually closing, which makes it a forward indicator of deal flow rather than a measure of assets gathered. It suggests activity is picking up even where full-cycle realizations still lag.

From selling to buying. Silver Lake is reportedly in talks to take Workday private, first reported by Reuters on August 13th, with the stock's reaction implying a move from roughly 43 billion dollars of value to about 51 billion. Nothing is signed. No price has been disclosed, no financing structure has been disclosed, and that 51 billion is market-implied, not a term-sheet figure. Nothing has surfaced since roughly the 17th to confirm or kill it.

But the context matters. This show noted on Friday that the marginal dollar in Electronic Arts' 55 billion dollar buyout, the largest leveraged buyout ever, which closed August 4th, was sovereign capital from Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund alongside Silver Lake. That is capital with no fund life and no redemption clock. If Workday converts to a signed deal, that stops looking like an anomaly and starts looking like a template. I'd stress that extending the sovereign structure to Workday is my inference, not a disclosed fact. No co-investor has been named. And every megadeal signed today adds to the same unexited inventory this briefing has been tracking all week. Financing capacity at the top of the market isn't the constraint. Exit capacity for everything below it is.

Smaller scale, same direction. Francisco Partners agreed on August 20th to take Weave Communications private for about 650 million dollars, at 7 dollars 40 a share, a 34 percent premium to the unaffected price three days earlier. That deal is announced, not closed, with completion expected in the fourth quarter. It's the small-cap cousin of the Thoma Bravo deal for Accelerant this show covered Sunday, at a 49 percent premium. Two take-privates of recently listed software companies, announced a week apart, both at real premiums. One caution. A 34 percent premium to a marked-down reference price is not the same as a full price, and I don't have Weave's level against its own listing price, so I won't claim it repeats the Accelerant pattern in that specific respect. Two deals aren't a dataset. They mark where a premium is landing below the megadeal tier, and nothing more.

So where does the cycle stand. The dominant liquidity channel in private equity is now the one the seller controls, not the one the buyer chooses, and the pricing gap between single-asset and multi-asset vehicles is the clearest evidence of it. Carlyle's 23 percent is a genuine data point against that read, and it's unresolved as to source. Watch three things from here. Whether Workday actually signs. Whether a marquee GP-led vehicle still prices at par once more allocators follow CalSTRS in declining to roll. And whether September quarter-end marks, landing in late-October earnings, hold up against what these secondary prices are already telling you.
