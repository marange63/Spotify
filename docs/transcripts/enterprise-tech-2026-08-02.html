Good morning, it's Sunday, August 2nd, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

All week this show has been chasing one question: is hyperscaler AI capital spending actually converting into paying demand, or is it running ahead of what customers will use? Thursday we named Amazon as the last data point owed on that question. It reported Thursday night, and it answers on the pass side.

AWS grew revenue 37% year over year to $42.2 billion, its fastest pace in eighteen quarters, according to Amazon's own earnings release. Operating income came in at $16.6 billion, up from $10.2 billion a year earlier — doing that math on Amazon's own reported figures puts the operating margin near 39%. So this is not growth bought by giving the product away. CEO Andy Jassy told investors AWS is now a $169 billion annualized revenue run-rate business, which he said would rank it 24th on the Fortune 500 if it stood alone. And the backlog — customer commitments signed but not yet recognized as revenue — now stands at $496 billion, Jassy said on the call, growing triple digits year over year. For context on how fast that's moved: Amazon's own disclosure around its Q1 print this year put that same backlog figure at roughly $364 billion, so the level has climbed sharply in a single quarter. I want to be precise about that jump, because it matters how it's sourced. Amazon's Q2 call itself only restated the new $496 billion number, not the prior one side by side — the $364 billion base comes from the earlier quarter's disclosure. So treat it as two figures from two prints, not one clean quoted leap. Either way, a few hundred billion dollars in contracted, not-yet-billed revenue is a very different animal from a bullish capex slide.

To fund it, Amazon is raising capital expenditure guidance to roughly $220 billion for 2026, up from a prior estimate of about $200 billion. And here's the detail worth sitting on: Jassy attributed that increase directly to higher memory costs. Not more chips, not more buildings — memory. That's a small phrase with a large implication. When the largest infrastructure buyer on earth says its capital plan went up because memory got more expensive, it's telling you the hyperscalers are now bidding against each other, and against everyone else, for the same scarce DRAM and high-bandwidth memory. That input-cost pressure doesn't stay inside Amazon. It flows through to every enterprise vendor renting server or GPU-backed capacity, and eventually to the price of the SaaS and AI features built on top of it. The cost of the AI buildout is starting to leak out of the hyperscalers' income statements and into everyone else's.

And Jassy went further. Even at $220 billion, he said AWS will not have enough capacity to meet 2026 demand, and expects the same to hold true in 2027. That's the headline. Demand is outrunning a build that's already accelerating. Sit with the second-order effect of a two-year capacity shortfall at the biggest cloud provider. It means some enterprise workloads simply won't get the AWS capacity they want, when they want it — and that scarcity is a tailwind for anyone selling the alternative. Multi-cloud strategies, hybrid deployments, and providers like Oracle that pitch themselves as the overflow valve all get more attractive when the market leader is telling you, out loud, that it's sold out through next year.

So the backlog picture across the three hyperscalers is now complete. Google's cloud backlog sits around $514 billion, a figure we anchored on last week. Microsoft's commercial remaining performance obligation is $678 billion, up 84% year over year. AWS now sits at $496 billion. Backlog is becoming the metric CIOs and investors shop on, because it shows multi-year commitment that a single quarter's growth rate can't. And notice what it implies about the years ahead: at both Microsoft and AWS, backlog is growing faster than already-strong revenue. That gap is forward visibility — it's the justification for raising capex again in 2027, which locks the cloud business into ever-higher capital intensity. These are becoming heavy-industry balance sheets wearing software margins.

That visibility also explains why the market is discriminating between the three. Alphabet's stock fell more than 6% on its own capex increase despite beating estimates, while Microsoft and Amazon were rewarded for raising spend tied to a metered, external consumption line investors can see billed and growing. Build spend that shows up as recognized cloud revenue gets a pass. Build spend without that visible attribution gets punished. I'd flag one caveat: Alphabet's selloff is the firmly sourced reaction here — the positive moves at Microsoft and Amazon are reported, not something I'd stake the argument on. But the direction of the discrimination is clear enough.

Now move up a layer, from infrastructure to the product built on top of it. Does that consumption show up as monetized software inside an incumbent, or does it stay a subsidized feature? Microsoft's answer, from its own July 29 earnings call, is that it's monetizing. Microsoft 365 Copilot has surpassed 30 million paid seats. Separately, GitHub Copilot has reached 50 million users, with revenue growing more than 60% quarter over quarter — driven by usage-based billing, not just seat counts. Keep those two numbers distinct: the 30 million figure is explicitly paid enterprise seats, while the 50 million GitHub figure blends free and paid tiers, so it's a weaker signal of committed spend. The number that carries the argument is the GitHub revenue growth, because it's usage-based. It rises when developers actually run the tool, not when a procurement team buys licenses that sit idle. That's the clearest incumbent proof so far that AI is moving from bundled feature to billed product line. Nadella also previewed a unified Copilot app merging chat, coding, and agents, expected to ship this quarter — but that's an announcement, not evidence of adoption yet, and I'd keep it in the promise column until seats show up against it.

So the cloud layer's monetization case now looks proven. The customer's return on it does not. McKinsey's own research, published in May, found that adoption and capital investment in generative and agentic AI keep climbing, but sustained impact on performance stays elusive. Only about 6% of companies McKinsey surveyed count as high performers attributing five percent or more EBIT impact to AI, and McKinsey says the differentiator is organizational practice, not which technology a company bought. That's an older data point, not this week's news, so I'm using it as tension rather than a fresh headline. But it's the necessary counterweight to everything else in this briefing. Backlogs, seats, and consumption are all accelerating at the vendor level. The number of enterprises that can point to a bottom-line result from all that spending remains small, and concentrated in the firms that changed how they work, not the ones that bought the most software.

Put those two facts side by side and you get the real shape of the week. The hyperscalers raised their capex again, on demand they say they can't even fill through 2027, and the market paid the ones whose spend converts into a visible revenue line. That widens the gap that matters. On one side, the vendors are getting paid, provably, today, and are pouring the proceeds into an even bigger build. On the other, the customers financing that build through their cloud bills and their seat licenses still can't mostly show what they got for it. The cloud layer's monetization is settled. The enterprise-level payoff is the open question heading into next week, and it's the one I'll keep watching.

That's your briefing for August 2nd. Have a good day.
