Good morning, it's Friday, August 28th. This is Cautious Optimism Briefings.

This show has spent a week watching outside parties test private credit's own marks. Every test so far cleared worse than the inside number. This week a bid arrived that didn't clear at all. And it turns out this wasn't even the first round of this particular fight.

Start with the fresh piece. Cox Capital Partners, working alongside Saba Capital Management, offered to buy up to ninety million dollars of shares in five non-traded business development companies. The funds are run by HPS, Apollo, Ares and Blue Owl. The average price was a twenty-six percent discount to net asset value. By the deadline this week, Cox had received under five million dollars of orders. Call that what it is. It's a rejection by non-response. Investors facing a certain twenty-six percent loss chose to stay locked in rather than take it.

There's a second Cox and Saba offer in circulation, and it's tempting to pair the two. That one was for shares of Blue Owl Capital Corporation Two, about thirty million dollars at a thirty-three-point-two percent discount, and it was unanimously rejected by that fund's board. But the dates don't support treating these as one story. That board's press release is dated in March, five months before this week's five-fund tender. And it targeted a sixth fund entirely, one that isn't among the five in the August program. So this isn't one stress test with two data points. It's the same two sponsors running the same playbook twice, against different targets, and losing both times in different ways. In March, the board said no on the investors' behalf, backed by a BofA Securities fairness opinion that called the price inadequate to anyone other than Cox and Saba themselves. That board also pointed to a sweetener. Shareholders were set to receive thirty percent of net asset value back by the end of March, part of a plan to return fifty percent or more of net assets over the course of the year. In August, the board didn't need to act at all, because the holders simply didn't sell.

So treat this as a standing arbitrage program rather than an event. Cox and Saba first disclosed an intention to tender for several Blue Owl vehicles back in February, and they were still at it in August. That matters because neither round produced a market-clearing price. A bid somewhere between sixty-seven and seventy-four cents on the reported dollar met an ask at par, and almost nothing traded either time. That's a spread, not a price.

So why won't holders sell, even at a discount that would be a real loss anywhere else? Because the exit they're comparing it against is rationed. Non-traded BDCs typically cap redemptions at around five percent of net asset value per quarter, and the queue behind that gate is long. Bloomberg's reporting put the industry-wide backlog near fifteen billion dollars. A separately sourced Fitch Ratings figure puts investor redemption requests in the second quarter alone at fifteen point six billion. Those aren't quite the same measure, one a flow and one a backlog, but they land in the same neighborhood. Fitch also found that ten of the sixteen BDCs it tracks couldn't meet their caps. So a holder is choosing between a certain twenty-six percent loss today and an uncertain wait at a mark that may or may not be real. Refusing the tender is a statement about the queue, not a vote of confidence in the portfolio. Don't read it as vindication of the marks.

It's worth steelmanning the other side for a moment. If the marks were badly wrong, you'd expect at least some well-informed holders to grab seventy-four cents while it was on offer. Almost none did. That's weak evidence the reported values are broadly credible. It's weak because those same holders have no cheaper way out and every incentive to wait for one. Both readings survive the data, and that is precisely the problem. This market has no mechanism that forces it to choose between them.

So what is a twenty-six percent discount actually bidding on? Here the underlying credit data argues the discount is far too blunt an instrument. Fitch's trailing twelve-month private credit default rate hit a record of roughly six-point-one percent in July, up from six percent the prior quarter. Leveraged loans are running at three-point-eight percent, and high yield at two-point-seven. That record was set while both of the public comparables sit below four percent, which is the part that should bother you. But Fitch's own expectation is that the institutional, larger-borrower slice of that number could fall to two-point-three percent, while smaller borrowers stay under genuine duress. Those are the companies without the financial cushion or the refinancing flexibility that bigger issuers enjoy. One reported number, two very different markets inside it. A flat haircut across five funds from four managers doesn't distinguish between them at all.

The same split shows up one layer down, inside the BDC portfolios themselves. Across the top ten business development companies, non-accruals rose to just under four percent of debt at cost in the second quarter, up twenty basis points, according to PitchBook. Compare that with Barings BDC, which reported non-accruals at two-tenths of one percent of its portfolio at fair value, outside its credit support agreement. Now name the asterisk. That figure excludes positions covered by a manager-level backstop, so it isn't the same thing as a clean book. And the same release disclosed a payment-in-kind position gone non-accrual in a single credit called A.T. Holdings, worth about half a percent of portfolio cost. Different denominators, different disclosures, one conclusion. These portfolios are not interchangeable, and pricing them all at a single discount to net asset value is analytically lazy. The dispersion is the finding. Manager selection is doing more work here than the asset-class label.

Now put the two halves together, because that's where the retail risk actually sits. The half of the market defaulting fastest is the smallest, least refinanceable borrower. The wrapper holding a great deal of that exposure is the non-traded vehicle with a five percent quarterly gate. So the wealth channel ends up structurally short liquidity and long the deteriorating half of the market at the same time. That's the retailization problem stated precisely. It's a composition problem, not a headline default-rate problem, and no aggregate number will show it to you.

Pull it together. The binding constraint in private credit right now isn't default frequency in aggregate. It's the absence of a clearing price. The structures on both sides, the redemption cap, the fairness opinion, the promised return of capital, all function to let everyone avoid discovering one. Cox and Saba's recurring campaign is becoming an unofficial secondary print that boards now have to answer to. And a template is emerging: reject the bid, cite a fairness opinion, and accelerate a return of capital instead of transacting. Expect more capital-return announcements timed against tender deadlines.

Three things to watch from here. First, whether Blue Owl Capital Corporation Two's promised return of capital executes on the schedule its board pointed to. Second, whether Cox Capital rolls the program into another round against a different set of funds. Third, the third-quarter non-accrual and payment-in-kind prints across the BDC sector. Those prints will show whether the split between institutional and smaller borrowers is widening or narrowing. That's where the real signal lands, not in a discount tender that keeps finding no sellers.

That's the briefing. Have a good day.
