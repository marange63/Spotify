Good morning, it's September 10th, 2026, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

Yesterday this show argued that the instrument of competition in AI infrastructure had become the balance sheet. That was after Qualcomm handed Amazon roughly four billion dollars of warrants against a sixty billion dollar vesting ceiling. Today the same trade shows up again, an order of magnitude larger, and in a much harder instrument.

Nvidia's fiscal second-quarter filing discloses a commitment entered in August. The company took on guarantees capped at a total of one hundred five billion dollars. What they cover is the land, power, and shell buildout for roughly four and a quarter gigawatts at SB Energy's PORTS-Pike Technology Campus in Ohio. That campus will exclusively host Nvidia infrastructure, leased to OpenAI for twenty years. Say the caveats before the number travels. The obligation is capped. It becomes effective in phases as conditions are met. And the first data center isn't expected ready for service until fiscal 2029. Nvidia also says each generation of infrastructure deployed there could represent roughly one and a half million GPUs, or one hundred fifty to two hundred billion dollars of Nvidia revenue. That figure is Nvidia's own forward estimate about capacity that doesn't exist yet. It is not a booked number, and it should be treated that way every time it's repeated.

Why doesn't this get the same discount this show applied to Qualcomm's warrants, or to the warrant Marvell handed Google last week? Because a warrant is optionality a customer may never exercise, while a capped, phased guarantee is a contractual credit obligation with defined triggers. But don't let the size do the work either. It still ships nothing for three years. And what Nvidia has actually taken on is not GPU demand risk. It's underwriting its customer's landlord. Nvidia is the sole tenant at PORTS-Pike, which means it has no diversification inside that campus if OpenAI's ability to service twenty-year rent payments into the 2040s falters. Concentration, not circularity, is the exposure that matters here. That's a credit question, not a compute question.

One honest gap before moving on. This show could not confirm from the filing whether Nvidia has recorded any carrying value or expected credit loss against that guarantee. The only liability-recognition language located covers legal proceedings, not this obligation. So treat the balance-sheet treatment as unknown, rather than as settled off-balance-sheet.

Here's the thing. The bigger number in that same filing isn't the guarantee at all. Nvidia's supply and capacity commitments, mostly for memory procurement, jumped from one hundred nineteen billion dollars last quarter to two hundred seventy-nine billion dollars this quarter. The company's own commentary says that increase is primarily related to securing memory for the next several years. My first instinct was to call that the more solid number, contractual rather than contingent, because it's about physical scarcity rather than financing. Nvidia's 10-Q complicates that. Its own boilerplate on these supplier agreements says they may be canceled, rescheduled, or adjusted for the company's business needs. So the two numbers aren't as different in kind as they look. Both carry conditionality, just triggered differently.

What is new and confirmed is the schedule. About ninety-two billion dollars of that total is due in the rest of fiscal 2027. Another eighty-seven billion falls in fiscal 2028, and eighty-eight billion in fiscal 2029. That puts roughly ninety-five percent of the whole two hundred seventy-nine billion inside the next three fiscal years, rather than spread evenly out to 2032. That's a near-term claim on memory supply, not a slow multi-year hedge, and it's the more useful read of where the scarcity is concentrated. It also cuts against the easing case this show entertained on Sunday, when a reported TSMC delay in 2027 packaging orders suggested the bottleneck might be loosening. The largest buyer in the industry is behaving as though memory, not packaging, is still the binding constraint.

If that buyer has effectively prepaid several years of memory, the next question is who can physically make it. Micron is reportedly targeting roughly double its current high-bandwidth memory capacity by the end of this year, aiming for about one hundred thousand wafers a month. That figure is trade-press reporting, not a Micron disclosure. Even if it lands, it would still leave Micron the smallest of the three major HBM suppliers by capacity, behind Samsung and SK hynix, and this show can't put a confirmed number on either rival today. The practical effect of Nvidia's prebuy is that it removes swing volume that would otherwise discipline pricing for everyone else. That means AMD, the hyperscaler ASIC programs, and the PC and phone makers who have no AI revenue to offset memory getting more expensive. This show covered the contract-price arithmetic on Tuesday, and that direction hasn't reversed.

Every number so far is something money can buy. Allocation, prebuilt inventory, a guaranteed customer's landlord. Nvidia's own inventory rose to thirty-one point six billion dollars from twenty-five point eight billion sequentially, specifically to prepare for the Vera Rubin introduction this quarter. That is cash converted into physical position on one end, and into forward demand on the other. Here's what money can't buy. Manufacturing capability itself.

Intel and ASML disclosed this week that High-NA extreme ultraviolet lithography has now processed more than one million wafers to date at Intel Foundry. That count spans tool certification, R&D, and what the companies call volume production. Read the scope carefully, because it's narrower than the headline. The volume production is on select layers, for a subset of Intel's own Core Ultra Series 3 chips, code-named Panther Lake. That's Intel's internal product, on part of a die. It is not an external customer committing real volume to an advanced node. And a million wafers is a throughput count, not a quality metric. No independent yield or defect-density figure for those High-NA layers exists.

Intel shares still rose more than four percent in premarket trading this week, on top of a similar gain the prior session. That makes this the fourth time in five days that this show's standing Intel test has gone unmet. The test is simple: a named external customer putting real volume behind an advanced node. And it is the second straight session in which the market has paid up for a self-measured instrument. The pattern isn't really about Intel's execution at this point. It's about what the market is willing to pay for a company measuring its own homework.

So rank today's numbers by how solid they actually are. Delivered revenue: eighty-nine billion dollars in Nvidia's data center segment last quarter. Guided revenue: one hundred eight billion dollars for the current quarter. A commitment with a defined schedule but cancellation language: two hundred seventy-nine billion dollars in supplier agreements. A capped, phased contingent guarantee: one hundred five billion dollars. And a company's own forward estimate about capacity that doesn't exist: one hundred fifty to two hundred billion dollars per generation at PORTS-Pike. The gap between the biggest number and the most solid one is the whole story this week.

That gap is also the limit of the strategy. A balance sheet can buy allocation, and it can manufacture demand. It cannot buy capability. No amount of Nvidia cash doubles Micron's HBM output a second time next quarter, and none of it qualifies an Intel node for an external customer.

So the forward tests are simple. Watch whether Nvidia's next filing states a recognized carrying value for the PORTS-Pike guarantee, rather than leaving it in a footnote. And watch whether any named external customer commits real volume to an Intel advanced node. Until one of those lands, the ledger stands where it stands today: enormous purchasing power, aimed at the one thing that isn't for sale.
