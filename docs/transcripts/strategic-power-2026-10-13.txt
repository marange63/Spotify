Good morning. It's Tuesday, October 13th, and this is your strategic power briefing.

Three developments moved the base layer of the AI buildout. A federal regulator closed a fast lane for data-center power in America's largest grid. A nuclear fuel market set a record its own equities don't seem to believe. And Moscow opened a diesel tap that Washington had just licensed. Start with the regulator, because it decides who collects the scarcity rent on everything else.

On October 9th, the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission rejected most of PJM's plan to bring large new loads onto its grid faster. PJM is the nation's largest grid operator, spanning the mid-Atlantic and the Midwest. Its plan would have let data centers connect without first securing matching new generation. In exchange, those loads would agree to be curtailed ahead of grid emergencies. The industry shorthand was bring your own power. FERC rejected the core of it, a package called the Interim Resource Adequacy Service. It approved only the narrower pieces, a definition of large load and a registry requirement. The commission's reasoning was specific. In the order's words, several provisions "appear to place obligations directly on eligible new large loads." Utility Dive's summary of the order puts it more bluntly. FERC said those elements "intrude on state jurisdiction over retail rates and appear to discriminate against large loads."

That jurisdiction point is the real story, more than the megawatts. It is the fourth stop on an arc this desk has tracked since last Tuesday. A Wisconsin rate case put data-center costs in front of a state commission. FERC then suspended PJM's backstop procurement over how its costs would be split. On Sunday, we covered Minnesota writing curtailment of new data centers into state law. On Friday, FERC told PJM it cannot do the wholesale version. The pattern across all four is consistent. Authority over who pays for data-center power, and who gets switched off first, is moving from the grid operator to the states. In practice, curtailment-for-access bargains now have to be built state by state, at retail. They can no longer be struck once for the whole PJM footprint.

Two readings of the order compete. One says FERC killed the fast lane. The other says FERC simply wants it redesigned. The redesign reading leans on FERC's own press release, titled "FERC Directs Nation's Largest Grid Operator to Create New Rules." But the substance of the order points the other way, at least for now. FERC rejected the package outright rather than approving it with conditions. No source reviewed shows a refiling or compliance deadline. PJM says only that it is reviewing the order. Jefferies expects the idea could at most be "minimally revived in future iterations, likely led by states." And a concurring commissioner, Rosner, flagged an open design flaw. He warned that leaving these loads out of PJM's capacity-auction demand curve risks a region that "sheds thousands of megawatts" involuntarily. So the better-supported reading is denial in the near term. Any redesign looks slow, and it runs through the states. A redesign with no clock, and with a sitting commissioner questioning the market design, is not a fast lane.

The beneficiaries of that delay are easy to name. Jefferies told clients the ruling will "potentially lead to higher capacity prices for longer in PJM." The same note called it "positive for existing generation and IPPs." Read that as the independent power producer category: Constellation, Vistra, Talen and NRG. It is a view on equity value, not a share-price forecast. And no stock reaction tied to this specific ruling has been reported. This desk drew the same inference when it covered FERC's backstop ruling last Thursday. Now a named sell-side note says it on the record, with the price mechanism attached. The logic is simple. Every new megawatt of load that can't take a regulatory shortcut still has to be served by someone who already owns firm generation.

What about the developers? Nobody has a quotable figure for how much load was waiting on this fast lane. So don't let anyone size the slowdown for you. But the incentives are plain. Data-center developers in PJM now have more reason to build behind the meter, to sign directly with incumbent nuclear and gas operators, or to site elsewhere. Those are incentives, not observed shifts. And elsewhere is not a clean escape. This desk reported last Wednesday that Texas is auditing its own data-center queue. For the AI capex story, the implication is uncomfortable. In America's largest grid, the binding constraint is still power access, not chips or capital. A regulatory shortcut just closed, and not a single physical megawatt was added. So the scarcity premium in PJM now belongs to whoever already owns firm megawatts, not to whoever moves fastest on paper.

If the winners are existing firm generators, the purest of them run on uranium. And that market just printed a record of its own. The long-term contract price is the rate utilities lock in for multi-year fuel supply. Per Investing News, it ended September at ninety-six dollars fifty cents a pound. That's above the prior peak of ninety-five dollars, set back in 2007. But hold that record loosely. It's a nominal record. The 2007 peak was set in dollars worth considerably more. In real terms, the market is not as stretched as the headline suggests.

Here's the part that doesn't add up. Cameco, the bellwether uranium producer, was down 6.9 percent for the year and 11.6 percent over the prior month, as of early October. The VanEck Uranium and Nuclear ETF, a broader basket of uranium and nuclear-adjacent names, was down 16.4 percent year to date. So the contract market is pricing record tightness, while the equities exposed to that same fuel are selling off.

Two explanations compete for that gap. The first says equity investors are mispricing a durable tightening that the term market has already recognized. The second says equity holders doubt the record will hold. On that view, they worry utilities won't keep signing enough new long-term contracts at these levels. The available evidence doesn't settle it, and this desk won't pretend otherwise. What ties it back to the lead is this. The hyperscaler nuclear deals this desk covered last week run on existing reactors. Those reactors are exactly where term fuel demand shows up. The regulator just preserved the scarcity that makes them valuable. The fuel market is pricing that scarcity. The equity market, for now, isn't following.

That's the slow, structural side of energy power this week. The fast, political side moved again on Russian diesel. On October 9th, Trump and Putin announced an agreement allowing Moscow to export diesel to the United States and other markets, despite sanctions. That came only weeks after Congress sent the president a tougher Russia sanctions law. Moscow's side of the trade came the next day. Russian state news agency TASS, as reported by the Kyiv Independent, said the government would permit the sale of five hundred thousand tons of diesel to foreign markets, effective October 10th. That partially unwinds a ban Russia imposed in July 2026. Russia imposed that ban after Ukrainian strikes on oil facilities disrupted its own fuel supply. That link matters more than the tonnage. The diesel Washington just licensed exists only as long as Russian refineries survive a strike campaign that Washington doesn't control. Moscow delivered a decree. The barrels still depend on Kyiv's drones.

Three things to watch. First, whether PJM refiles any version of the fast lane, and on what timeline, since none is set. Second, whether the next uranium term-price print extends the record or gives it back. Third, Wednesday's September consumer price index. It is unreleased as of this taping, so hold any diesel read-through until it prints.

That's today's briefing. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
