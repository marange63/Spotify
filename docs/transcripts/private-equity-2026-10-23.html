Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Equity & the Deal Economy

Transcript · October 23, 2026

Good morning, it's Friday, October 23rd, 2026, and this is your private equity briefing.

The print this show has been waiting on all week landed yesterday, and it scores a call we made in advance. On Monday we set a simple test for Blackstone's third quarter. If fee-related earnings kept growing while realizations stayed flat or worse, the exit logjam would hold for another quarter. Blackstone reported Thursday morning, and the test resolves cleanly. Fee-related earnings rose 6% year over year, according to the earnings call. Net realizations fell 20% quarter over quarter. That's the carry Blackstone books only when its funds actually sell assets and send cash back. On a year-over-year basis the decline is sharper still. Blackstone's own earnings release puts net realizations at $331.5 million this quarter, down 34% from $504.8 million a year earlier. Monday's bull test needed realizations stepping up from a year ago. They fell by a third instead. Fees up, carry down. The read holds.

Put those two numbers next to a third and you get the clearest paper-versus-cash picture of the week. Blackstone's corporate private equity funds appreciated 2.8% in the quarter and 15% over the trailing twelve months. That's a mark, not a dollar returned to anyone. So in the same quarter that Blackstone told investors their holdings are worth 15% more than a year ago, the realized carry that only arrives with cash fell by a third. The gap between what the portfolio is marked at and what it's paying out widened this quarter. It did not narrow.

Now, management's answer to that gap is a promise. CFO Michael Chae said Blackstone expects a "significant step-up" in net realizations in the fourth quarter. He said carry already under contract is expected to "surpass the total net realization levels for the firm from the third quarter." Treat that as guidance, not a result. The question that actually matters is where that carry comes from. Chae did offer one data point on the quarter just closed. He said the third quarter's realizations came mainly from several exits in Blackstone's Asia portfolio, specifically in Japan and India. That's geography, not exit type. We still don't know whether those were arm's-length sales to strategic buyers, sponsor-to-sponsor hand-offs, or continuation vehicles repricing the same assets among insiders. The same ambiguity carries into the fourth-quarter pipeline, because Blackstone hasn't said what's filling it.

And remember the base. This step-up is measured against a quarter whose decline management says it saw coming. Beating a weak third quarter is a low bar. It says nothing yet about recovering the ground lost year over year. So don't let "step-up" do the work of "thaw." Management's own description of the listing route points the same way. Chae called the IPO market "open but highly selective." This show watched what selective looks like on Wednesday. AirTrunk, the Blackstone-backed data-center platform, reportedly pushed its Singapore REIT listing into 2027, even after raising the offered yield. The fourth quarter is the test, and the thaw thesis hasn't passed it yet.

There is a fair bull case here too, and it deserves a plain statement. Fee-related earnings is the annuity-like base of this business. Blackstone isn't a forced seller of anything. And a lumpy realization line is normal for an industry that sells assets in lumps, not in a steady stream. One soft quarter from one manager says nothing yet about Apollo, KKR, Carlyle, or Ares. The bear case is simply that the manager's shareholders and the fund's limited partners may now be living through different cycles. One is compounding on fees. The other is waiting on cash.

And while the realization line waits on that test, Blackstone is building a second business out of the waiting itself. Its new secondaries flagship, Strategic Partners Fund Ten, has raised over $16 billion toward a target of at least $22 billion. That's a raise in progress, not a final close. It follows the ten-billion-dollar secondaries fund CVC closed, which this show covered on Monday. Read it generously and it's healthy liquidity provision, giving limited partners an exit when the traditional market won't. Read it skeptically and it's limited partners paying a second layer of fees for liquidity the deal market should be supplying. Both readings are true at once, and that's the point. Liquidity provision is turning into its own fee franchise for the managers large enough to scale it. That franchise earns whether or not the underlying assets ever actually sell.

Blackstone disclosed one more inflow channel in the quarter. Its private-wealth private equity vehicle, BXP, posted what Jonathan Gray called "record sales in the third quarter of $2.9 billion." That's fresh capital coming in from the wealth channel. There's no redemption or outflow data to cite either way, just gross sales. Still, it's a third channel, alongside institutional fundraising and secondaries capital, by which money keeps flowing into this machine while cash flowing out of it stays constrained. Firmwide, Blackstone took in $63.3 billion of inflows this quarter alone. The fundraising machine is not the problem. The exit machine is.

Meanwhile, the price of getting deals done at the top of the market keeps climbing, which raises the bar for exits still to come. The live example is Qiagen, the healthcare company that EQT has agreed to take private after a bidding contest with KKR. This show covered that deal in full yesterday. Two of the largest buyout firms competed for one scarce asset, and the competition pushed the price up. Full-price, competitively bid deployment means the vintages buying today are paying up now. That makes their eventual exit math less forgiving if the realization window stays this grudging.

If fee-related earnings is the part of this business the public market is willing to pay for, Blue Owl is the cautionary tale on the other side of that coin. Blue Owl is itself a buyer of minority stakes in other private-markets managers. The Wall Street Journal has reported that bankers approached Blue Owl's founders about a possible sale. Those approaches come after the stock fell roughly 67% from its January 2025 peak. Treat that carefully. This is a single report, relayed secondhand and unconfirmed by the company, and banker outreach to founders is not a process. Most of that drawdown is really a private-credit story that belongs to a different briefing. But narrowly, it's worth naming. Manager consolidation may now be reaching the acquirers of GP stakes themselves, not just the sub-scale targets they've been buying. The market is pricing durable fee streams generously, and discounting harshly wherever it doubts the earnings quality behind them.

The same pattern shows up on the allocator's side of the ledger. CalPERS reported its private equity net asset value at $119.3 billion as of March 31st this year, up from $92.2 billion twelve months earlier. That's backdrop data, not this week's news. The jump blends new capital being called, marks appreciating, and a deliberate increase in CalPERS's own target. It isn't a read on performance by itself. But directionally it rhymes with everything else this week. Net asset value keeps accumulating on institutional books while the cash that would validate those marks comes back slowly. An overweight allocator's natural release valve is to sell into the secondaries market. That is exactly the market Blackstone and CVC are raising big dedicated pools to buy into, and to collect fees from.

So where does the cycle actually stand? Still two-speed. Fee-related earnings and reported net asset value keep climbing for the biggest managers. Deployment at the top end stays full-priced. And the machinery for manufacturing liquidity, secondaries, continuation vehicles, and private wealth, is scaling faster than the traditional exit window is reopening. The tally of clean, arm's-length sales to third-party buyers is unchanged this week. The single biggest unresolved risk is that marks keep rising without cash arriving to confirm them. Watch three things to find out whether that changes. First, the actual source of Blackstone's fourth-quarter realizations when they're reported. Second, the Strategic Partners Ten final close. Third, the remaining majors' results over the coming weeks, starting with Ares, then Apollo and KKR.

That's the briefing. Have a good weekend.
