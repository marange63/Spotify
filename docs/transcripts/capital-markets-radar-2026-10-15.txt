Good morning. It's Thursday, October 15th, 2026, and this is your Capital Markets Radar.

Yesterday this desk called September's CPI the real catalyst. It printed hot. But the more important move came this morning, and it changes what the bond market is actually pricing.

Start with the print. Headline prices rose 0.6 percent in September, per Yahoo Finance's coverage, up from 0.4 percent in August. Gasoline did much of that work, up 9.1 percent on the month. On Tuesday, this desk warned that an energy-driven headline might not move core. That caveat didn't hold. Core prices rose 0.3 percent on the month, matching August's already-hot pace rather than cooling. Year over year, core climbed to 2.6 percent from 2.4. So the easy defense, that this was just an oil spike the Fed can look through, is weaker than it was on Tuesday. The so-what: with the Fed already at 3.75 to 4 percent after September's hike, the hawks now have a core argument, not just a gasoline one.

The first reaction was textbook hawkish. FXStreet's read of CME FedWatch put the odds of an October 28th hike at about 50 percent shortly after the release. That was up from 22 percent before it. Other trackers caught a lower peak, so treat the exact number as noisy and the direction as the signal. The dollar moved with it. The dollar index rose to 102.86, its highest since April 2025. That's above the 102.61 we cited yesterday, so the strong-dollar stretch has set a new high. A dollar at an eighteen-month high tightens global dollar funding just as AI-linked credit is repricing wider. That's our inference, but it matters. Those are compounding stresses, not offsetting ones.

Here's the twist. The hawkish spike didn't hold, even on its own day. By 7:23 on Wednesday evening, FXStreet had October hike odds back down to 31.6 percent. That unwind happened before this morning's Treasury rally even began. So these are two separate moves, and they should be read separately.

Now the second move. This morning, per CNBC, the ten-year yield tumbled almost 13 basis points to around 5.07 percent. That's its lowest level since September 25th. Recall the test this desk set on Sunday: can the long end fall on calming news? On Friday's data, it couldn't. Now the long end has finally fallen. But it didn't fall on calm. It fell the morning after a hot inflation print, as investors moved into bonds amid the tech rout.

Our working read is that the driver of duration demand has shifted, at least for now. It has moved from the inflation story to a credit and growth scare. Two other explanations fit the same move, and they deserve airtime. One is a positioning squeeze, after the ten-year touched a 24-year high of 5.364 percent last week. The other is ordinary mean reversion from a CPI-day spike. We also lack clean two-year yield data for today. So we can't confirm whether the front end stayed pinned by hike risk while the long end rallied. Hold the thesis loosely.

If it does hold, the second-order effect is large. Duration would be working as a hedge against equity and credit risk again. That's the hedge sixty-forty and risk-parity allocators lost while yields climbed to multi-decade highs. The so-what: the bond market may now be pricing the leverage behind the AI buildout more than it's pricing the Fed.

That credit scare has a name, and it's climbing the ladder. Start at the bottom rung. CoreWeave's five-year credit default swaps are quoted above 1,000 basis points, per GuruFocus. Sources disagree on the exact print, so take that as a distress-level marker, not a settled number. CoreWeave's unsecured bonds touched a 15 percent yield intraday, per 24/7 Wall St. The stock is down more than 20 percent across two trading sessions. Elon Musk's one-word reaction, "Yikes," tells you how loud this got.

One rung up sits SoftBank. On Sunday, this desk flagged SoftBank's record junk bond, with its longest tranche at a 9.75 percent coupon. SoftBank's credit default swaps now sit at 424 basis points. Then comes Oracle, a name much closer to investment grade. Oracle's five-year CDS widened 26 basis points overnight, to a record 288.

Here's why that sequence matters. Last weekend this desk called the stress in AI and CCC credit dispersion, not contagion. We said stress in the AI buildout prices at its weakest financing link first. That's still true of CoreWeave. The new information is Oracle. By our own definition, contagion is stress climbing the quality ladder. Oracle's record print is the first sign of exactly that.

The skeptical case is real. CoreWeave may be an idiosyncratic leverage story, not a verdict on AI capex returns. And 288 basis points is wide for Oracle, but nowhere near distress. Still, every basis point Oracle adds raises the marginal cost of capital for the buildout. It lands on the vendor, partner and special-purpose financing we argued the hyperscalers would push risk onto. One more inference, clearly labelled. If this repricing persists, private credit funds lending against data centers and GPUs are the next place to look for markdowns. We have no figure for that exposure yet. The so-what: the market is now testing whether the AI buildout can keep being financed cheaply, and the answer is getting more expensive.

The equity tape shows how much selling has already happened. The Nasdaq Composite fell about 1.9 percent on Wednesday, to 26,692.87. The VIX closed at 16.87, up about 10.5 percent on the day, per Investing.com. That's up from the 14.84 this desk cited on Tuesday, but still modest given the chip drawdown underneath it. The gap between bond volatility and equity volatility that we've tracked is narrowing, and it's narrowing from the equity side.

The more telling number is positioning. Goldman's model, as relayed by Yahoo Finance, puts global CTA equity length at roughly 43.5 billion dollars. That's down from about 125 billion a month ago. Citadel's gauge of trend-follower positioning swung from well above its norm to below it over September. These are modeled estimates, not measured positions. But they say the systematic sellers have largely already sold. That cuts two ways. There's less mechanical fuel for a further flush. There's also less evidence that conviction sellers think this is over. The so-what: the next leg, up or down, is a fundamental judgment on AI financing, not a flow event.

Bitcoin gave the same signal in miniature. Yesterday this desk said a single day of bitcoin ETF inflows was noise until it repeated. It didn't repeat. Per Farside, spot bitcoin ETFs saw about 261 million dollars of net outflows on Wednesday, led by Fidelity's and BlackRock's funds. Bitcoin sold off with tech, even as Treasuries went on to rally. So it's trading on risk appetite and the dollar, not on discount rates alone. Crypto gets no benefit from lower yields when the reason for lower yields is fear.

One last piece feeds back into the Fed's dilemma, and that's oil. Brent fell about 2.2 percent this morning, to roughly 103.75 dollars a barrel, per Anadolu Agency. The trigger was reports that the US may ease restrictions on some sanctioned vessels carrying Russian oil. That's reported, not decided. And one day's drop is a premium trim, not barrels restored. Brent was already trading around 103 dollars on October 9th, after tanker strikes near Qatar and the UAE. The so-what: oil near 100 dollars keeps headline inflation pressure alive into the Fed meeting.

So here's where that leaves October 28th. A Fed that hikes into widening AI credit spreads and a tech drawdown risks a policy-error narrative. A Fed that holds, with oil elevated and core not cooling, risks looking behind the data. The hike odds aren't a forecast. They're a probability caught at a moment, and they've already whipsawed within a single day. Two things to watch from here. Does Oracle's CDS keep widening? And do hike odds settle, or keep swinging into the meeting?

That's the radar for today. Stay cautious, stay optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
