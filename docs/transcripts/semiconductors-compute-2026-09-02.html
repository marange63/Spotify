Cautious Optimism Briefings

Semiconductors & Compute Infrastructure

Transcript · September 2, 2026

Good morning. It's September 2nd, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing. Everything that moved at the margin this week moved in memory, so today is a memory-allocation episode by choice — no foundry read, no export-control read, because that is where the news actually was.

Two days ago, this briefing said Samsung's HBM4 yield gain was share moving inside a fixed pool of suppliers, not more bits arriving in the market. It set a test. Does that yield gain show up as allocated volume at an accelerator vendor? Today there is a fresh number out of Seoul. It is worth being precise about what that number measures, because it is not the number the test called for.

Seoul Economic Daily, relaying a note from the Korean brokerage LS Securities, reports that HBM4 rose from about 5 percent of Samsung's own total HBM shipments in the first quarter of this year to about 35 percent in the second. That is Samsung's product mix shifting toward HBM4-grade output. It is not a stated share of Nvidia's HBM4 purchases. No source anywhere quotes a Samsung share of Nvidia's HBM4 allocation specifically. So the honest read is narrower than it first sounds. Samsung is converting its own line to the higher-grade product very fast, which is real evidence of a yield and qualification win. But it does not by itself tell you how Nvidia is splitting its orders.

The market treated that mix shift as though it had settled the allocation question anyway. LS Securities raised its price target on Samsung Electronics by 12.5 percent and cut its target on SK hynix by 27.3 percent. That is more than twice the size of the raise, running the other way. The flows had already moved in that direction. Over the trailing month, Samsung shares rose about 1.2 percent while SK hynix fell almost 9 percent, and foreign investors were net sellers of roughly 6 trillion won of SK hynix stock.

But what changed the brokerage's mind was not only Samsung's shipment mix. LS Securities also cut its own forecast for SK hynix's 2027 HBM operating margin, from about 80 percent down to about 60 percent, roughly this year's level. That is a company-specific revision to the incumbent's profitability model, and it is at least as much of the target cut as anything Samsung shipped. A brokerage marking down its own forecast is not the same event as a customer moving an order. Keep those two apart.

The analyst behind that note frames the SK hynix downgrade as the competitive structure among suppliers normalizing, rather than as a demand slowdown. The pricing data supports him. The average HBM export price reached $76.13 at the end of July, up 9.5 percent in a single month and above $70 for the first time. So the product is still setting price records while one supplier's margin outlook gets marked down. That is not a cycle rolling over. It is a repricing of who captures the rent inside a market that is still tightening.

Here is the skeptical turn. If Samsung genuinely has a fast-improving HBM4 line, the textbook outcome is a second credible supplier disciplining the incumbent's pricing. That has not happened. Prices went up anyway. Read one: the shortage is deep enough that Samsung's ramp gets absorbed without touching price at all. Read two: this is really a story about Samsung's own manufacturing mix, and SK hynix's numbers got cut mostly because a Korean brokerage revised its own margin model, not because a rival visibly took orders away. Either way, there is no confirmed evidence yet of an actual reallocation of Nvidia's HBM4 business. Treat the magnitude as reported, not established.

It helps to keep the competitive dimensions separate rather than netting them into one story. There are four of them. Qualification was settled in June, when Nvidia confirmed all three memory makers had cleared certification for the Vera Rubin platform. Yield moved in August. Samsung's own product mix is what moved now. Pricing power is the fourth, and it has not moved at all. So Samsung has plausibly gained on qualification, yield, and mix. It has gained nothing on pricing power, and the industry's memory cost curve is unaffected by all four.

There is one channel where the market's reaction feeds back into the physical outcome, and it runs against the incumbent. Defending allocation into the HBM4E generation takes capital. SK hynix is the company facing that squeeze. Its price targets are getting cut and foreign investors are selling the stock, so its financing position for exactly that capex is worse, right at the moment allocation is being decided. Sentiment is not usually a supply variable. In a capacity race funded partly on equity, it becomes one. And note what Nvidia does and does not get from a genuinely stronger second vendor. It gets supply security, which is real, since its own management named memory as its binding constraint. It does not get a lower price. Availability and procurement cost are separate problems, and only one of them is being addressed.

To understand why nobody gets price relief regardless of who wins the supplier fight, look at where the wafers are going. Trade reporting on second-quarter contract prices shows the memory makers prioritizing capacity for HBM, server DRAM, and enterprise SSDs, with consumer memory left persistently short. The sharpest move is mobile memory: LPDDR5X modules up 89 percent in a single quarter, from $77 to $146. A 16-gigabyte DDR4 module rose 51 percent over the same three months. These are contract prices as reported by trade press, not disclosed supplier pricing. AMD's own view, relayed through that same reporting, is that DDR5 does not normalize before 2028. So this is not a blip that clears next quarter. It is a multi-year reallocation of fab capacity toward the highest-value products.

That has a consequence nobody is underwriting. Phone, PC, and console makers are absorbing a bill-of-materials increase that runs on a different clock from their own product cycles, and there is no AI revenue anywhere in that chain to offset it. The AI-infrastructure buyers can pass memory cost into the price of compute. A laptop OEM cannot. So the memory squeeze shows up as an AI-margin story on one side of the industry and a straightforward consumer-electronics margin compression on the other.

There is a non-consensus wrinkle worth naming, and it needs a date attached. As of the fourth quarter of 2025, Counterpoint Research reported that general-purpose DRAM operating margins had reached the 60 percent range, surpassing HBM margins for the first time. No fresher data through this year confirms whether that inversion still holds. But if it does, the crowding-out story runs backwards. Consumer memory is not scarce because HBM is more profitable. It is scarce, and therefore became more profitable, and the memory makers now have a standing incentive to keep both markets tight rather than relieve either one. That reframes today's fight between Samsung and SK hynix as a fight over which company captures the rent, not a fight that ends it. The only thing currently testing whether that incentive is being acted on is a proposed class action filed in June in California federal court against Samsung, SK hynix, and Micron, alleging they cut consumer DDR3 and DDR4 output while ramping high-bandwidth memory. That is an allegation, not a finding.

Everything so far is reported or estimated. Broadcom reports fiscal third-quarter results today, and as of this taping it has not reported, so nothing about the actual print should be treated as fact. What is usable is the guidance already on the books. Broadcom has guided to roughly $16 billion of AI semiconductor revenue for the quarter, growth of more than 200 percent year over year and more than half of expected total revenue, against a reaffirmed full-year AI semiconductor target of $56 billion. The number to watch is the one management did not move last quarter: a standing fiscal 2027 target of, in their words, in excess of $100 billion. Management declined to raise it after the second quarter even on a beat, and the shares fell 12.6 percent. Three days ago, this briefing flagged Marvell's roughly 8 percent drop as a custom-silicon story getting priced to its calendar rather than its headline. Today is the same test for Broadcom. And here is why it belongs in a memory episode. Custom ASICs need the same constrained HBM stacks Nvidia buys, and trade estimates put ASIC buyers on a worse per-gigabyte tier than Nvidia because they lack Nvidia's volume leverage. So a strong Broadcom print tightens the same allocation queue rather than routing around it.

One sentence on what today rests on: one Korean brokerage's revised estimates relayed through one outlet, one quarter of trade-press contract prices, and one earnings print nobody has seen yet. But strip all of that out and something still holds. Wafers are committed to HBM, server DRAM, and enterprise SSDs into 2028, on the memory makers' own allocation behavior and AMD's own timeline. The bill for that lands on phone, PC, and console makers with no AI revenue to cover it, and that consumer bill-of-materials inflation is the consequence nobody in this market is underwriting. The near-term tests are whether Samsung, SK hynix, or Nvidia puts an actual allocation figure on the record, and whether Broadcom moves its long-term AI target when it reports.

That's the briefing. Talk tomorrow.
