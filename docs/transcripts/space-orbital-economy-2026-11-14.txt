Good morning. This is your Cautious Optimism Briefing on the orbital economy, for Saturday, November 14th, 2026.

Friday's flight finally answered the question this desk has carried all week. It just didn't answer it the way the setup implied.

SpaceX flew Starship's fifteenth test flight on Friday afternoon, lifting off from Starbase at 4:47 local time. The plan was the most ambitious yet. It was the first attempt to catch both the Super Heavy booster and the ship back at the tower on a single flight.

The ship side has done its job so far. After reaching orbit, it deployed 26 version-3 Starlink satellites, the same count as the previous flight. SpaceX's mission plan has the ship spending as much as 22 hours in orbit before its own landing attempt. That attempt was still ahead as this script was written. Treat it as open, neither failed nor successful.

The booster side did not work. SpaceX aborted the planned catch and diverted the booster to an offshore splashdown in the Gulf of Mexico. CNBC reports it burst into flames there. Other outlets reported only the aborted catch. And SpaceX did not immediately say why it called the catch off.

Here's what makes that notable rather than routine. SpaceX had already caught a Super Heavy booster on three previous flights. Nobody had ever caught a ship. On Monday, this desk named a partial outcome as a live scenario, with the booster coming home and the ship not. Friday inverted that. The leg that came up short was the one SpaceX had already solved, on the one flight that needed both halves at once.

Be careful how far to push that. We don't know the cause. A conservative abort onto a water splashdown is a different thing from a hardware failure, and SpaceX hasn't said which this was. One flight is also not a cadence, and it is not a cost-per-kilogram data point. But the structural lesson holds either way. Full reuse is not one milestone moving steadily forward. It is two separate problems, and Friday showed you can lose ground on the one you thought was done.

The other half of the story is that Starship is already doing commercial work, whatever the recovery result. SpaceX says one Starship can carry up to 60 version-3 Starlink satellites, about 20 times a Falcon 9's load. Two flights running, it has carried 26. By our arithmetic, that's under half its own stated capacity, flown in a mode that isn't yet fully reusable.

So Starship is a working satellite truck and an unproven reusable system at the same time. Those are two different investment theses that happen to share a rocket.

The payload matters because of what it feeds. In last month's third-quarter results, SpaceX reported Starlink consumer subscribers at 13.6 million, up from 12 million at the end of June. That's the profitable, compounding business Starship already serves, on partially recovered flights. So SpaceX can keep growing Starlink while the reuse business case waits. The cost-per-kilogram step change slips with every setback. Starlink's deployment lead doesn't necessarily slip with it.

That gap between promise and reality has a price, and it shows up most clearly in the challengers' order books. Start with Rocket Lab's Neutron. This week brought more than 515 million dollars of Neutron contracts signed in the third quarter, and a base price raised to 70 million dollars a launch.

First, a correction. On Thursday, this desk relayed a previously reported target of about 50 million dollars. Rocket Lab's own release says the prior price was 55 million.

So the real increase is 15 million dollars, not the roughly 20 we sized it at. That's about 27 percent, on a rocket that has never flown.

The same release adds two new data points. Neutron's backlog now stands at 16 missions. And Rocket Lab says the next available launch opportunities are in the 2030s.

Now set the 70 million dollar list price against Neutron's rated 13,000 kilograms to low Earth orbit. By our arithmetic, that's roughly 5,400 dollars per kilogram. That's at list price and rated capacity, before contract terms we can't see, and before a single flight.

On schedule, earlier reporting had Rocket Lab finishing a new forward dome by year end. That's the manufacturing problem that pushed the maiden flight into 2027. But a finished dome is a step toward a launch date. It is not a launch date, and Rocket Lab still hasn't named one.

Read the backlog two ways. It's genuine pricing power, because constellation customers have few medium-lift alternatives to SpaceX. It's also a hard cap. New customers effectively can't get a Neutron seat this decade, which reinforces Falcon 9's grip in the meantime. And that per-kilogram premium is exactly what a reusable Starship would eventually undercut. So every Starship recovery setback extends the window in which Rocket Lab can charge it. Rocket Lab is selling the very gap the lead story described.

When you can't buy a seat until the 2030s, the alternative is building your own rocket. AST SpaceMobile shows what the market charges for that choice.

Start with the market's verdict, which is the new part. Per Benzinga's tally, AST's third-quarter revenue of 42.3 million dollars missed a 46.6 million consensus. Its loss was also wider than expected, and the shares fell 8.7 percent.

UBS cut its price target from 78 dollars to 43, citing launch delays and execution risk. By our arithmetic, that nearly halves it. On Wednesday, this desk put AST plainly: financed, but not yet a service. The sell side has now put a price on exactly that gap.

Around the same time, AST unveiled a 1.3 billion dollar engine deal with Firefly Aerospace for its own heavy-lift launcher, Blacklift. The new detail is the hardware count. Firefly will deliver 27 Miranda engines and 9 vacuum-variant Vira engines for AST's first three rockets. Neither engine has flown an orbital mission yet.

Run that forward. Three rockets at up to five BlueBirds each is about 15 satellites of lift, by our arithmetic. None of it arrives before 2028. So Blacklift carries none of AST's near-term deployment plan. That build still rides entirely on other companies' rockets.

The fair counter is worth stating. AST reported roughly 3.2 billion dollars of cash at the end of September, which makes Blacklift affordable. And one bad day judges execution timing, not the direct-to-cell thesis. Still, AST is paying today, in its share price, for a hedge that only starts to matter in 2028.

Launch isn't the only thing that decides whether a constellation pays back. Survival is the other. The U.S. Defense Intelligence Agency says China is expanding its arsenal of weapons to disrupt or destroy satellites. It adds that Beijing probably intends to deploy additional anti-satellite missiles capable of destroying spacecraft in low, medium, and high orbits. That's an assessment of intent, not a confirmed new deployment.

It points the same way as an episode last month. Russia's Kosmos-2612 satellite shadowed ICEYE's commercial radar satellite, X-36. One tracker, cited by Militarnyi, put the closest approach at 26 meters. Other trackers' estimates vary widely, so treat that as one reading, not a consensus.

What follows is our inference, not a sourced finding. If any single satellite can be disabled, operators will favor proliferated, replaceable fleets over a few expensive assets. That means more satellites and shorter replacement cycles. And that demand lands on the same constrained launch queue this whole briefing has described. Threat doesn't relieve the launch bottleneck. It tightens it.

So where does that leave things? Starship is genuinely inflecting as a production Starlink launcher. As a reusable system it is still a test program, and Friday made plain those are different milestones. Rocket Lab's pricing power is real, but it sits on a vehicle with no launch date. Blacklift is unflown engines for a rocket that hasn't been built. The biggest open question is which recovery problem SpaceX solves first, and how quickly it gets back to the pad.

Watch four catalysts. First, whether the Starship upper stage is caught or lands on its own attempt. Second, what cause SpaceX gives for the booster abort, and any timing for Flight 16. Third, ULA's Vulcan and its first Amazon Leo mission, which was still waiting to fly as of last night. And fourth, whether Rocket Lab finally puts a date on Neutron's first flight.

That's the briefing. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you next time.
