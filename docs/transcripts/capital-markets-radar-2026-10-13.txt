Good morning, it's Tuesday, October 13th, and this is your Capital Markets Radar.

Here's the frame. Last week, mainstream investors started repositioning for a higher-for-longer rate world. Money moved into bonds, and out of equities and crypto. Today's bank earnings and tomorrow's CPI are the first live tests of that move.

Start with where the money went. A weekly US equity-market briefing published on Substack reports that bond funds took in a record 19.8 billion dollars in the week through October 7th. Equity funds lost about 5.1 billion dollars over the same week. That's one newsletter's tally, and the Investment Company Institute's mutual-fund data for an adjacent week show a much smaller shift. So take the direction, not the record.

The direction fits the rate backdrop. The Fed hiked by a quarter point in September, and markets still price one more quarter-point hike in 2026. Long yields have risen with it. One honest gap here: the sources we checked disagree on the exact 10-year level, so we won't hang the argument on a print we can't verify. What matters is the choice investors made. Facing a hiking Fed, they bought income rather than chase equities.

The same pattern showed up at the speculative end of the market. CoinDesk reports that US spot Bitcoin ETFs saw their largest single-day outflow since June 25th on October 7th. That's the same day the bond-fund week closed. One industry tally puts combined weekly outflows from Bitcoin and Ethereum ETFs at 1.22 billion dollars. That matters for how crypto is trading. If Bitcoin were behaving as an inflation hedge, an oil-driven inflation scare is exactly when it should have caught a bid. Instead, it's trading like a rate-sensitive risk asset. The so-what: on this evidence, crypto is part of the duration trade, not a hedge against it.

The breadth signal points the same way, but it's softer. The same Substack briefing says the equal-weight S&P 500 posted its first positive week in eight weeks. It also describes energy and utilities as acting better in October. What we couldn't find is a sourced figure for how far equal-weight beat the cap-weighted index. Nor could we find a clean weekly sector ranking. So call it a lean away from the mega-cap-heavy index, not a measured rotation.

There's also a simpler explanation to weigh. Equal-weight can beat cap-weight in any week when the largest index weights simply have a bad few sessions. That's a story about a handful of names, not about money broadening out. One week doesn't make a factor regime.

If it does persist, though, the second-order effect is large. Cap-weighted, momentum-heavy portfolios become the funding source for the rotation. In practice, that means mega-cap tech is what gets sold to buy income and defensives. Momentum and risk-parity allocators would then face a regime that pays for income and penalizes long-duration growth. The so-what: real money leaned toward bonds last week, but you need at least one more week of breadth data before calling a regime change.

That raises the obvious question. If money is moving toward income, is it fear or is it yield-seeking? Credit spreads are where fear would show first. And broad credit isn't showing it. The ICE BofA US High Yield option-adjusted spread sat at 3.15 percent on October 8th, per the Fed's FRED database. That's still the latest print, so there's no reading yet for the back half of last week. One gap matters here. We couldn't verify a current spread for CCC-rated paper, the bottom of the junk stack, which is where stress would surface first. So the honest read is that broad credit is calm, not that every corner of it is. This still looks like a rates and discount-rate repricing, not a credit-cycle one. The so-what: worry about duration, not defaults, at least until the banks say otherwise.

Which is why today's bank earnings matter more for their commentary than for their headline numbers. JPMorgan, Goldman Sachs, Wells Fargo and Citigroup report this morning. Bank of America and Morgan Stanley follow tomorrow. None of those results exist as I record this.

The bar is high. FactSet's consensus has S&P 500 third-quarter earnings growing 29.5 percent from a year earlier. That would be the third straight quarter above 25 percent. A bar that high means a beat is largely priced in.

For the banks, listen for two forces pulling in opposite directions. Net interest income should benefit from a higher-for-longer rate path. Provisions are where that same rate path would first show up as credit damage, especially in private-credit-adjacent exposure. The so-what: a clean earnings beat paired with cautious provision language would be a worse signal than it looks.

Beyond the banks, the margin question is energy-cost pass-through, and that brings us to oil. The EIA's October Short-Term Energy Outlook raised its fourth-quarter Brent forecast to 105 dollars a barrel. That's 14 dollars above last month's call. Here's the detail that got little air time. In the same release, the EIA says it still expects prices to generally fall from their early-October average. So the agency that just raised its number by fourteen dollars is also calling a fade from here.

You can read that two ways. The charitable read is that even the forecasters chasing this move don't see it as the new baseline. The skeptical read is that a fourteen-dollar revision in one month means the forecaster is following the tape, not leading it. And an agency that revises that much could be wrong about the fade, too.

That matters for tomorrow's September CPI report, out at 8:30 Eastern. It's the first real test of how much of the energy spike has passed through to inflation. Oil is the likeliest channel to move inflation expectations. Two cautions apply. A September print mostly reflects September energy prices, not October's. And a hot headline driven by energy may not move core inflation, or the Fed, very much. The EIA's own fade call is a reason not to extrapolate a hot energy print into a durable move in breakevens.

There's a further wrinkle. Even an oil fade may not pull long yields down. The policy backdrop is doing more of the work than the barrel is. Markets still price one more quarter-point Fed hike in 2026, and a softer oil price doesn't change that on its own. The so-what: CPI can tell you about the energy channel, but on its own it won't tell you the long end has turned.

The other energy trade in this rotation is power, not oil. Industrial Info Resources puts the long-term uranium contract price at 97 dollars a pound. That's the highest in 18 years, and 13 percent above September of last year. The stated driver is surging electricity demand. It's the commodity-market version of the power-scarcity story behind the data-center buildout. Buyers are locking in supply years out at the highest contract price in nearly two decades. One caution: long-term contract prices are broker-assessed indicators in a thin market, not exchange prints. Still, the so-what is clear. Power scarcity is no longer just a regulatory story. It's now showing up in the price of a physical input.

Put it together. The market is starting to position for a higher-for-longer rate world. Money is leaning toward bonds and away from equities and crypto, broad credit stays calm, and the oil forecasters are hedging their own hawkish call. Now the catalysts arrive in sequence. Banks report today and tomorrow. September CPI lands Wednesday morning, and PPI follows Thursday morning. The Fed decides on October 28th. Against that calendar, the VIX closed at 14.84 on October 9th, per FRED. That's a calm reading for a week this crowded, and it makes protection worth a look. By Thursday, we'll have a much better sense of whether last week's move toward income was conviction or a one-week wobble.

That's the radar for today. Trade carefully, and I'll see you tomorrow.
