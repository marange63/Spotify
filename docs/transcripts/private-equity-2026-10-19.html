Private Equity & the Deal Economy

Transcript · October 19, 2026

Good morning, it's Monday, October 19th, and this is your private equity briefing.

The thread through the past week has been capital going in, not coming out. Today adds a fourth signed take-private to that tally, still no signed third-party exit we can confirm, and a clearer picture of where fresh capital is actually pooling.

Start with the lead. EQT Infrastructure has signed a binding scheme implementation deed to take Cleanaway Waste Management private. Cleanaway is Australia's integrated waste manager, and this is no longer a proposal. It's a signed contract, entered by EQT's Infrastructure VI fund. The cash price is three dollars nine and a half cents Australian per share. EQT's release puts the implied enterprise value at roughly nine point three billion Australian dollars, and no source we have states an equity value. The Motley Fool's report of the terms frames that at twenty times Cleanaway's FY26 underlying EBIT. So this is a full price for a defensive, essential-service cash flow, not a distressed scoop.

Now the premium-base test this show has been running all week. CCC Intelligent Solutions, Bumble, and Varonis all carried headline premiums measured against stale or depressed reference prices, so each headline overstated how much the buyer was really paying up. EQT's release measures Cleanaway's thirty-two point one percent premium against the close of two dollars thirty-seven on August 12th. That was the last trading day before EQT's proposal went public, an undisturbed and contemporaneous base. The binding cash price sits a notch below the three dollars thirteen EQT offered in August. Reporting on the deal attributes that step down to the standard dividend adjustment in an Australian scheme, not to a cut through due diligence. So on the base that matters, Cleanaway breaks the pattern. The premium is real, though the price was held, not bid up.

There are two honest readings of the deal. The bull case is that a sponsor signing a binding, full-premium deal for essential-service cash flows shows real underwriting conviction, with rates still a live question. The bear case is that this is infrastructure capital buying a utility-like asset from public shareholders, and it returns no cash to a single private equity limited partner. Only the bear reading speaks to where this cycle's stress actually sits. And signed is not closed. Cleanaway shareholders vote before the end of February 2027, and the Federal Court of Australia must approve the scheme. The Foreign Investment Review Board, the competition regulator and authorities abroad must also sign off, with implementation targeted by the end of March. Cleanaway tells you sponsors can still buy. It tells you nothing about whether they can sell.

One more detail from EQT's release. With this deal, Infrastructure VI is expected to be eighty-five to ninety percent invested. On our read, that's a fund that will soon need successor capital, not one returning cash to its own backers. It also shows the cost of all this deployment. An entry at twenty times EBIT needs a buyer willing to pay at least that years from now. Every full-premium entry raises the bar for the eventual exit, and that pushes realization further out for the vintages deploying today. File Cleanaway under capital going in.

So if capital going in is visible everywhere, where is the fresh capital coming from? That brings us to the secondaries market, and to CVC. CVC Secondary Partners closed its sixth dedicated secondaries fund at ten billion dollars. That's nearly double the five point eight billion its predecessor raised. More than two hundred institutional investors committed, and roughly half were new to the platform. The close dates back to early September, so it's new to this show rather than breaking news for the market.

Recall what we said on the 13th. Dedicated secondaries fundraising looked scarce in aggregate. That's the condition that lets buyers of fund stakes ration capital and demand discounts on multi-asset portfolios. CVC's close complicates that read without overturning it. One large close doesn't refute aggregate weakness. The capital isn't gone. It's concentrating. On Friday, we flagged the same pattern in primary fundraising, with dollars holding up while the number of funds closing kept shrinking. CVC shows it on the secondaries side too. Half-new LPs could mean real demand for secondaries exposure as a liquidity tool. It could also just reflect CVC's distribution reach, and the disclosures don't separate the two. Either way, the advantage runs to managers who can offer limited partners liquidity tooling at scale, and the squeeze falls on everyone else.

For context, stated once. Secondaries volume set a record in the first half of this year, at one hundred eighteen billion dollars globally, up fifteen percent year over year. That's Jefferies' tracking, as reported by Investment Week, and other data providers put the total slightly higher. We noted the record on the 13th. The news now isn't the record. It's where inside that record the fresh capital is landing.

So what does concentrated secondaries capital do to the exit problem? Here's our read. Bigger dedicated pools at the top let continuation vehicles keep clearing for trophy assets at or near their carrying marks, because there's a ready buyer with real size. A sponsor rolls its best asset into a continuation vehicle, and the limited partners who sell out record a distribution. But the asset stays with the same manager. That's capital recycled within one sponsor relationship, not price discovery against an independent buyer.

Follow that through to the numbers limited partners care about. Distributions to paid-in capital can tick up on paper while hold periods actually lengthen. The liquidity problem gets deferred into a new vehicle rather than resolved by a sale. And the managers big enough to run those vehicles keep earning fees on the assets they kept. So the same concentration should keep the largest managers' fee-related earnings growing, even if realized carried interest stays weak across the industry. Fees up, carry flat: that's the divergence this read predicts.

Which brings us to the calendar. Blackstone reports third-quarter results this Thursday, October 22nd. It's the first hard read on whether the deals clearing this quarter turned into realized cash. No results exist yet, so this is a catalyst to watch, not a number to quote. Here's the test. If Blackstone's realizations and realized performance revenue step up clearly from a year ago, and management credits third-party sales and IPOs rather than continuation vehicles, then distribution is loosening and our read is wrong. If fee-related earnings keep growing while realizations stay flat, the logjam holds. Blue Owl follows on October 29th, the next read on GP stakes and manager consolidation.

Pulling it together. The past week has produced four signed take-privates on this show's tally. GTCR and Elliott's deal for CCC. Bumble's take-private by Ithaca Holdings, which Blackstone as an existing investor approved by written consent. Proofpoint's deal for Varonis. And now EQT's Cleanaway. Each is capital going in, or in Bumble's case, liquidity Blackstone arranged through a take-private rather than a market exit. Against that, we can confirm zero signed third-party exits. There's nothing new on the London Stock Exchange Group's talks to buy Permira's Octus, the one exit candidate we've been tracking. Deployment is visible everywhere you look. Distribution is not, and it remains the binding constraint on this cycle. What would change that read: Blackstone's realizations on Thursday, Blue Owl on the 29th, a signing on Octus, and whether Cleanaway clears its vote and approvals on EQT's timetable.

That's the briefing. Have a good Monday.
