Good morning. It's Tuesday, August 4th, and this is your AI products briefing.

This week's real contest isn't which model is smartest. It's how much authority you'll let an agent exercise on your own production systems, and whether that authority can be trusted. Three stories this week line up around exactly that question, and the answers are arriving from three different directions at once: a shipping product, a legal deadline, and a mathematical proof.

Start with the product. We described Microsoft's Project Perception on Sunday: a coordinated security system where red agents probe for attack paths, blue agents triage which risks actually matter, and green agents turn findings into fixes. What's new this week is that the research preview became a real, buyable product. Project Perception entered public preview on August 3rd. Microsoft's own blog puts it plainly: "We are bringing this vision to customers around the world through Project Perception, which enters public preview on August 3." That's the headline change at the margin — a demo became a SKU.

And it comes with a genuinely new pricing template. Microsoft is charging by consumption, in units it calls Security Compute Units, or SCUs. Its product page says, "You pay only for what you use, measured in Security Compute Units. Different agents consume SCUs at different rates depending on the intensity of the task they perform." No dollar-per-SCU rate has been published anywhere, so treat this as a billing mechanism, not a price you can compare yet. But the mechanism itself matters: it's the first real attempt to meter an agentic product by how much work an agent actually does, rather than by tokens consumed or seats licensed. If that catches on, expect CrowdStrike, Palo Alto Networks, and SentinelOne to face pressure to answer with their own task-based meters rather than static subscription tiers. And notice that the pressure isn't only competitive. A meter that bills by task intensity only makes sense if buyers believe the agent is doing real, trustworthy work — so the pricing model and the trust question turn out to be the same question wearing two hats.

There's a deeper shift underneath that meter. Once you let an agent change a production system, the thing you're really buying is no longer the model. It's whoever owns the orchestration and the sign-off controls around it — the layer that decides what an agent is allowed to touch and who has to approve it. That pushes enterprise value away from raw model access and toward the governance stack. Change management, in other words, stops being an afterthought and becomes the actual product battleground.

So how autonomous are these green agents really? Microsoft's marketing leans hard into "agents that act, not just alert." Its own product page describes green agents that "remediate and harden... proactively, without a hand-off at every step." That sounds like full autonomy. But the same page immediately qualifies it: "every high-impact action stays under human sign-off." So this is not agents rewriting production systems unattended — it's agents drafting and staging fixes that still need a human to approve the consequential ones. That's a materially different governance story than the "AI replaces the SOC analyst" framing you'll see in some coverage, and it's the more honest one.

On capability, Microsoft is more confident than the coverage of a preview usually warrants. Its own blog states the combined system "delivers 96% on CyberGym, an industry leading benchmark, +12 points above Mythos" — that's Microsoft's own primary claim, not just secondary reporting. Worth knowing, though: a separate, unverified secondary figure puts the score closer to 88 percent, and that discrepancy hasn't been resolved with a verifiable source. So take the 96 percent as Microsoft's own stated number, treat it as a synthetic-benchmark result rather than a proven field result, and wait for real customer outcomes before betting on it.

That trust gap — how much you believe an agent's output before letting it touch something real — is also being answered by regulators this week. On August 2nd, the EU AI Act's high-risk obligations became legally enforceable: conformity assessment, logging, and mandatory human oversight for AI systems in sensitive categories. A proposed delay to December 2027 has been floated but not formally adopted, so as written, the deadline stands now, this week — though that status is genuinely contested and could still change. Practically, this turns audit logging and human-oversight controls from a nice-to-have into a sellability requirement for any vendor shipping AI products into the EU, starting immediately. For a vendor, that means the audit trail and the human-in-the-loop switch are no longer features you bolt on when a customer asks. They become the price of admission to the EU market at all.

The same trust question is showing up from a completely different angle: not policy, but proof. OpenAI has previewed its next model family, Astra, not by releasing it, but by publishing new solutions to ten mathematics problems that had sat unsolved for decades — one open since a 1999 conjecture, another a sphere-packing bound unimproved since 1978, and a disproof of a decades-old rigidity conjecture from operator algebra. What makes this notable for products, rather than pure research, is how OpenAI chose to substantiate it. Instead of a benchmark score, they published machine-checkable proof certificates. As one report on the GitHub repository put it, "the repository reports a 'sorry' count of zero, meaning no step in any of the formalized proofs has been left unproven." Alongside the certificates, OpenAI posted a 249-page manuscript of the reasoning and released the whole package under an open Apache 2.0 license. Every logical step compiles and checks out independently — you don't have to take OpenAI's word for it.

Caveat this hard: Astra itself isn't a product. Nobody outside OpenAI can run it, none of the ten results has been peer-reviewed, and OpenAI chose which results to publish, which is a real selection bias. The often-cited two-thousand-dollar cost, cited by one outlet as "the cost of producing new results on ten long-standing mathematical problems," reflects only the successful, published runs — not the compute spent on the attempts that failed. The AI critic Gary Marcus called the release, in his words, "amazing but vastly oversold" — a useful reminder that a clean proof of ten hand-picked problems is not the same as a general-purpose tool you can point at your own hard question. Still, the pattern is the interesting part: if machine-checkable verification becomes standard alongside AI capability claims, it's a preview of a trust feature that could eventually show up in coding tools and research agents, not just math papers.

Put the three stories together and the shape of the week is clear. Microsoft is selling agents real authority to act on production systems, on a new pay-by-work meter. Regulators are now legally requiring proof that such systems are overseen and logged. And a frontier lab just showed what independently verifiable proof of AI output can look like when a company chooses to provide it instead of asking to be trusted. Capability stopped being the scarce ingredient in AI products a while ago. This week, trust — proven technically, mandated legally, or neither — became the actual product. And if that's right, the durable edge in agentic products stops being who has the smartest model. It becomes who can prove their agent's work, auditably and on demand — a moat a benchmark chart simply can't fill.
