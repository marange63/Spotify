Good morning. It's Sunday, October 11th, and this is your space and orbital economy briefing. This week SpaceX moved from needing a carrier to trying to become one, and the market believed it faster than the hardware can deliver it.

Start with the lead. On Thursday we told you the FCC had waived the requirement that a satellite operator lease spectrum from a terrestrial carrier before selling direct-to-device service. The same FCC action cleared SpaceX to launch up to fifteen thousand direct-to-device satellites. That loosened one of the two levers we've called decisive in direct-to-cell: the carrier alliance. This week SpaceX went after the other lever, which is spectrum itself. SpaceX agreed to buy a low-band spectrum portfolio from the investment firm Grain Management. The Wall Street Journal reports the price at roughly eight billion dollars, and neither company has confirmed it. Per SatNews, the deal covers licenses for up to fourteen megahertz of paired spectrum in the 800 megahertz band. That's a coverage band. It buys reach and in-building penetration, not raw data capacity. Musk called it "the last critical piece of the spectrum puzzle needed for SpaceX to provide complete phone coverage in America." That's his framing, not an independent read on what the finished service will look like.

Now the caveats, because they matter here. The deal still needs FCC review and customary closing conditions, and no closing date has been stated. The official filings disclosed no financial terms. So the unit economics can't yet be judged. We don't have a comparable price to weigh the eight billion against, and we can't yet say whether SpaceX overpaid or got a bargain for its coverage layer.

The market didn't wait for the paperwork. According to CBS News, T-Mobile fell eleven percent and AT&T fell seven-point-four percent in Friday morning trading. Those were intraday moves, not closing prices. Here's the detail that sharpens the irony. SatNews reports that Grain bought this same 800 megahertz portfolio from T-Mobile itself in 2025. So T-Mobile sold the coverage layer that SpaceX is now buying to compete against it. Why did T-Mobile, SpaceX's own direct-to-cell partner, fall hardest? One reading is that a partner loses most when its differentiator becomes a competitor's asset. Another reading is that this is an overreaction, and a negotiated carve-out could claw some of it back. We could not find a verified, on-record response from T-Mobile about the partnership.

Then separate the license from the capacity. Approval to launch fifteen thousand satellites is not fifteen thousand satellites. It's a permit for a constellation that doesn't exist yet. And low-band spectrum buys coverage, not carrier-grade capacity. So the incumbents repriced a threat that is real, but still years from scale.

What do the carriers do next? Spectrum has always been their moat. A satellite operator holding its own low-band licenses goes straight at it. Two responses look plausible to us, though neither has been reported. The first is lobbying against further satellite-spectrum waivers, starting with the FCC's review of this very deal. The second is leaning toward satellite partners who have no ambition to become carriers themselves.

That second path runs straight to AST SpaceMobile. AST's latest numbers come from its August 8-K. Its network has grown to thirteen satellites in orbit. Second-quarter revenue was thirty-one-and-a-half million dollars, against one-point-two million a year earlier. That's real growth, but off a near-zero base. And AST's US service is still pre-commercial, at the beta stage we described Thursday. On Thursday we also offered two readings of the waiver for AST. In one, carriers lean on AST less. In the other, they lean on it harder as a counterweight to SpaceX. A SpaceX that now competes directly with carriers tilts the evidence toward the counterweight. AST is the satellite partner that doesn't want the carriers' customers. No carrier has said so this week, so watch what they do rather than what they say. The takeaway: AST's value to carriers may rise exactly as SpaceX turns into their competitor.

Before leaving the deal, one word on SpaceX the public company. A standalone mobile-carrier business makes Starlink more separable from the launch business. That keeps the spin-out question alive. But it also adds another claim on the same capital pool. That pool already funds Starship and the Starlink buildout, and the spectrum purchase now sits on top. So the more Starlink looks like a carrier, the stronger the case for valuing it on its own, and the longer the capital queue behind it.

A license to be a carrier is only as good as the rockets that fill it. Starship still has no date for its next flight, but we now know what SpaceX wants it to do. Flight 14 put twenty-six Starlink V3 satellites in orbit, all operating nominally. Now Musk says the goal for Flight 15 is to "catch the ship, making it the first full recovery of an orbital class rocket." SpaceX has posted no official date for that attempt. The reliability problem hasn't gone away either. Flight 14 had issues with two engines on the Super Heavy booster and one on the ship. Musk says the heat shield was robust. That's his claim, not an independent finding. So a ship catch would prove full reuse is possible. It would not prove reuse is reliable or repeatable, and repeatability is what actually moves cost per kilogram.

Amazon's constellation, now branded Amazon Leo, shows the other side of the launch problem. Its last confirmed launch was July 2nd, when an Atlas V carried twenty-nine satellites, per CNBC, and the latest tracker reading we have, from mid-September, showed nothing launched after that. Amazon has the balance sheet. What it lacks is rides, and the deepest pool of rides belongs to the rival it's chasing. So launch supply, not licensing, sets the sector's pace right now, and only one company looks close to solving its own bottleneck.

And every satellite that does reach orbit adds to a bill nobody itemizes. As reported by Space.com, SpaceX disclosed that each Starlink satellite performed more than forty collision-avoidance maneuvers a year, between June 2025 and May 2026. The European Space Agency now tracks more than forty-four thousand objects in orbit. That's up about a hundred and eighty percent from roughly sixteen thousand in 2005. Nobody has published a dollar cost per maneuver. But every burn draws on propellant, and propellant is satellite lifetime.

This month brought a fresh reminder of where that debris comes from. The tracking firm LeoLabs detected a derelict Soviet-era weather satellite, Meteor 2-7, breaking apart in low Earth orbit. A debris expert quoted by Space.com says a collision is possible, but the cause will likely stay unresolved. So don't read it as an anti-satellite event. Read it as legacy junk that nobody is paying to remove.

On Saturday we said disposal is a constellation cost that never shows up in the satellite count. Avoidance is the same story. Every new megaconstellation license, including the fifteen thousand direct-to-device satellites SpaceX was just cleared for, adds to that operating tax. And the tax lands on every operator already up there. So crowding now scales with exactly the ambition in today's lead.

Pulling it together. What's genuinely inflecting is SpaceX's regulatory and spectrum position, and the market's willingness to price it as real. What's still ahead is nearly everything that turns it into a service. The direct-to-device constellation is unbuilt. Starship has yet to catch a ship, let alone do it routinely. AST's US service is still in beta. The biggest open question is whether the FCC treats a satellite company owning low-band spectrum as competition to welcome or a precedent to limit. Five catalysts to watch. The FCC's review of the Grain deal. An actual Flight 15 date and catch attempt. Any on-record word from T-Mobile about its Starlink partnership. Amazon resuming launches. And AST shipping its next three BlueBirds, numbers fourteen through sixteen.
