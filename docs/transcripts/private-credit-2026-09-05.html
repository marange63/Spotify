Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Credit & the Corporate Credit Cycle

Transcript · September 5, 2026

Good morning. It's Saturday, September 5th, 2026, and this is your private credit briefing.

Yesterday we told you the payment layer of private credit still looked benign. Defaults and non-accruals were the one metric everyone quotes as clean, and we said the real test would be November's Q3 numbers from the business development companies. That test arrived two months early. A cross-filing analysis of second-quarter 10-Qs from forty-four BDCs shows the payment layer is already moving. It is moving in one specific place, and that concentration is the whole story.

Start with the aggregate. Across those forty-four lenders, the ratio of fair value to cost on the loan books has now fallen three quarters running. It was a shade over ninety-nine percent at the end of 2025. It dropped to about ninety-seven point eight in the first quarter, and it eased again in the second.

In dollars, that is roughly ninety-three billion of fair value against ninety-five billion of cost as of June 30th. Call the cumulative move about 168 basis points of markdown, on paper that is overwhelmingly senior-secured and floating-rate. That is not a crisis number. But it is not a flat line either, and for years the loudest criticism of this asset class has been that its marks never move because they are modeled rather than traded. This quarter, they moved. One caveat on vintage before we go further. This is June 30th data being read in September. It is fresh as analysis, not as a real-time mark on today's book.

Now the number that explains why they moved. Lenders marked down eighty-one percent of their software loans this year, against just forty percent of everything outside the software book. Be precise about what that measures. It is the share of software loans now carried below cost. It is not the size of the haircut. And the gap between those two shares is the argument. This is not a broad repricing of private credit. It is a concentrated repricing of one vertical.

Ares Capital, the largest BDC, shows you how concentrated. Two software names alone accounted for more than a third of its five hundred and twenty-seven million dollars of year-to-date net unrealized losses, and that share climbs past half once you add five more software credits. So at the biggest lender in the sector, a handful of borrowers are doing most of the damage. The so-what is straightforward: this is a vertical being repriced, not a cycle turning.

There is a more uncomfortable reading underneath that, and it is the one worth sitting with. The software borrowers being marked down are legacy application companies whose revenue is being eroded by artificial intelligence. Private credit is financing both sides of that trade. The same managers writing loans against AI data-center buildout are also holding the loan book of the incumbents that buildout is disrupting. That is not a diversified position. It is a single technology thesis expressed twice, once long and once short, in the same portfolio, and only one leg is currently being marked.

On non-accruals, be careful with the samples, because two different ones are circulating. A ten-BDC subset shows non-accruals climbing to about 3.4% of portfolio cost by June 30th, up from 2.5% at the end of last year. That is a smaller, different sample from the forty-four-BDC fair-value figure. The two should not be blended into one trend line.

At Ares Capital specifically, its own second-quarter earnings release puts loans on non-accrual at 2.4% of investments at amortized cost, up from 1.8% at year-end 2025. Note the comparison carefully. That is a move since December, not year over year, and it is sixty basis points, not the "up sharply" framing some coverage has attached to it. And note where the evidence in this story is actually coming from. It is not the headline default rate. It is the filings.

Here is the skeptical turn, and it matters more than any single figure. Moving marks settle one argument and not the other. They falsify the claim that private valuations are static and unresponsive, because the valuation committees are clearly reacting to real stress in software collateral. But a markdown of that size does not tell you the marks are adequate. It only tells you they are pointed in the right direction. Direction is now evidence. Level is not. If you are underwriting a BDC allocation today, the open question is not whether the marks moved. It is whether they moved far enough relative to how hard public software comparables have already been repriced. Nothing in these filings answers that, because a mark-to-model book and a traded equity comparable are never going to reconcile on a schedule you can plan around.

Now look at what is happening to new money, because it tells a different story from the back book. New-deal direct-lending spreads have widened since late 2025, but only by roughly 25 to 50 basis points. PitchBook's second-quarter wrap puts it concretely: a loan that priced around S plus 450 late last year comes at about S plus 500 today.

That is underwriting discipline returning, but modestly. And it is meaningfully smaller than the markdown hitting the back book. Which tells you something specific. The repricing of existing software exposure is running ahead of the discipline being applied to new deals. The industry is correcting the stock faster than it is repricing the flow.

Now the handoff. If that is what is happening on the asset side, look at what lenders are paying to keep funding it.

Blue Owl Technology Finance Corporation, the technology-focused BDC in Blue Owl's stable, closed a hundred and fifty million dollar private placement of senior unsecured notes on September 4th. They priced at 7.60% and mature in 2032.

That is the third debt financing this single vehicle has done since June 30th. It follows four hundred million of 6.500% notes due 2029 in August. Total raised in roughly two months: eight hundred million dollars. And the book it is funding is fourteen point seven billion dollars of technology and software lending, which is exactly the collateral the rest of the BDC universe is marking down fastest.

Be honest about the coupon step-up, from 6.500% to 7.60%. Part of that is credit. But the tenor also stretched three years, from 2029 out to 2032, so duration is baked into that gap. Do not read the whole move as a risk premium.

The more interesting signal is the cadence. Three financings in two months is either strong originator demand for capital to deploy into an attractive lending opportunity, or a liquidity need to keep supporting a book already under pressure. The evidence does not let you pick one yet. What would settle it is net interest margin at the next earnings print. If funding costs are rising faster than yields on new software loans, that is the liquidity read. If margin holds, it is the demand read.

On payment-in-kind income, the metric we flagged yesterday as the tell to watch, one practical note. There is no single industry number for it and there will not be one to quote. It is disclosed company by company, and the dispersion across names is already wide. Palmer Square Capital BDC reported recurring PIK interest at about 1.4% of investment income last quarter, while other BDCs run at several times that. So watch it rise specifically at the technology-concentrated lenders into the third quarter, not as one aggregate statistic. PIK is where a struggling software borrower goes before it goes on non-accrual, which is why it leads the metric everyone actually quotes.

For an allocator, that reframes the decision. If this deterioration stays vertical-specific, the actionable question is sector exposure inside your direct-lending managers, not whether to hold the asset class at all. Those are different trades, and the regulators worrying aloud about private credit as a systemic risk are answering a different question than the one sitting in your portfolio.

So where does the cycle actually stand. Deterioration is real, but it is concentrated rather than systemic. One vertical, software and AI-disrupted borrowers, is being repriced by the private lenders holding the loans and by the public markets that already discounted those companies' equity. The biggest unresolved risk is not whether the marks are moving. It is whether they are moving enough. And the near-term catalysts are the third-quarter BDC filings in November, where PIK income at the technology names and Blue Owl Technology Finance's net interest margin will tell you whether this is a vertical stabilizing or a vertical still deteriorating.

That's the briefing. Have a good weekend.
