Good morning. It's Sunday, July 26th, and this is your strategic power briefing. The story this week has been the same one from three different angles: the constraints on the AI buildout and on great-power energy leverage have gone physical and legal, not financial. Capital is not the scarce input anymore. Turbines, wires, and foreign export law are.

Start with the clearest hard evidence yet. GE Vernova reported second-quarter earnings on July 22nd, and the numbers describe an equipment shortage most AI capex forecasts still don't price in. Orders rose 88 percent year over year to 24.2 billion dollars, and total backlog reached 176 billion dollars, up 13 billion in the quarter alone. But the number that matters is gas-turbine capacity under contract: it jumped from 100 gigawatts to 116 gigawatts in a single quarter, and management says the company expects to reach at least 125 gigawatts by year-end. GE Vernova describes itself as mostly sold out through 2030, with agreements already signed into 2031, and it has already sold more than half of that year's 30 gigawatts of production slots. Sit with what that means. A hyperscaler announcing a new data center today, without an existing turbine allocation, is very unlikely to get gas-fired power before 2031. Not because of permitting, not because of financing. Because the manufacturing slots don't exist. And the exits from that box are all slower or costlier than a turbine order would have been: a nuclear power-purchase agreement, a spot in a multi-year grid interconnection queue, or behind-the-meter gas and batteries built on the developer's own site. This is the physical bottleneck behind the entire AI infrastructure story, and it's more binding than the chip shortage ever was, because you can't expedite a turbine the way you can prioritize a wafer allocation.

The cash is following the backlog. GE Vernova has generated roughly 10 billion dollars in free cash flow year to date, already more than two and a half times all of 2025. That's worth a caveat, though: this is a booking, not delivered megawatts, and management's "mostly sold out" language is a qualitative characterization, not an audited percentage. It also cuts against the AI-bubble narrative in an interesting way. If AI capex decelerates before 2030, GE Vernova's multi-year backlog still gets built and paid for. The turbine scarcity that looks like a constraint on hyperscalers is simultaneously a cushion for the equipment makers, which is why power-equipment OEMs like GE Vernova and Siemens Energy are increasingly worth treating as a distinct AI-infrastructure trade, separate from chips and cloud names.

Now, even where turbines eventually arrive, you still have to connect them to load, and that's the second constraint. FERC ordered all six US grid operators, PJM, MISO, SPP, CAISO, ISO New England, and NYISO, to show cause on large-load interconnection, acting on an October 2025 directive from Energy Secretary Chris Wright. The first deadline landed July 20th: each RTO was required to file a resource-adequacy report addressing whether it can serve new and existing large loads. A second deadline, August 17th, requires each operator to justify its current large-load interconnection tariffs or propose revisions. FERC's own chairman, Swett, framed this order-by-order approach as a way, in his words, to spearhead lasting reform much more expeditiously than a single nationwide rulemaking. And it's worth naming what this is institutionally. It's a Trump-administration Energy Department steering a nominally independent regulator toward a specific industrial-policy outcome. That may be the fast route to grid reform. It's also the same independence question people ask about the Fed, pointed at FERC instead.

I want to be precise about what we actually know here, because it's tempting to round up. There is no confirmed reporting yet on whether all six RTOs filed on schedule, or on what those reports concluded. What we do have is a separate, verified number: PJM's most recent capacity auction, covering the 2027-2028 delivery year, fell 6,632 megawatts short of PJM's own 20 percent reserve-margin reliability target. That's a real hole in the one grid that serves much of the data-center corridor from Virginia to Chicago. But it comes from the auction process, not from the FERC-ordered filings, and the two shouldn't be conflated. The honest read is that FERC's exercise, so far, is procedural motion, a report and a legal filing, not new physical capacity. Whether it forces real tariff reform by August 17th is the thing to watch, not something we can claim has already happened.

That same logic, financial and physical instruments doing different jobs, shows up abroad this week too. The EU agreed its 21st sanctions package against Russia on July 23rd, its largest in four years by its own foreign policy chief's count, 218 total listings. The package designates 94 additional Russian financial institutions plus the Moscow stock exchange, pushing total sanctioned banks past 100, more than half of Russia's internationally connected lenders. It also freezes the EU's oil price cap at 44 dollars and 10 cents a barrel for twelve months, blocking a scheduled rise. Greece dropped its veto in exchange for a one-year, automatically renewing exemption letting EU firms keep moving Russian LNG to non-EU buyers under pre-invasion contracts, which tells you EU sanctions cohesion is now a negotiated, transactional thing. Other member states will want their own carve-outs next round, and Hungary and Slovakia are the obvious next askers.

The reason this package lands harder than the last several is timing. Ukraine's General Staff says drone strikes have disabled 42.7 percent of Russia's oil-refining capacity since last August, producing fuel shortages across most Russian regions. That's a combatant's estimate, worth flagging, since the IEA's independent figure is materially lower, over 20 percent offline. But even taking the more conservative number, you have kinetic damage to refining capacity landing in the same window as a frozen price cap and a hundred-plus sanctioned banks. Financial and physical pressure are compounding rather than substituting for each other, which is unusual, and it raises the cost of Russia settling energy trade just as its refining capacity is already constrained heading into winter.

Last, a brief coda that closes the thesis. Taiwan is considering a stricter export rule, called N-2, that would cap TSMC's most advanced offshore manufacturing at two process generations behind Taiwan's domestic leading edge, tighter than the current N-1 standard. This is being read explicitly as a constraint on TSMC's own Arizona expansion, not just a China-facing control. If codified, it means Washington's reshoring ambitions run into a ceiling Taipei sets by law, not by subsidy dollars or capex. It's still only a proposal, no enactment date, no confirmed TSMC position, so don't treat it as settled. But it's the cleanest illustration all week of the pattern: American industrial policy keeps hitting constraints that money cannot buy past, whether that's a turbine slot, a grid interconnection queue, or a foreign government's export statute.

Three things to watch. Whether the six RTOs' July 20th filings become public and actually quantify a shortfall, rather than just PJM's auction number standing in for the whole grid. Whether the EU's 21st package clears final Council legal adoption, not just ambassador-level agreement. And whether Taiwan's N-2 rule gets a vote.

That's the briefing for July 26th. Stay cautious, stay optimistic, and I'll talk to you next time.
