Good morning. It's October 11th, and this is your robotics and physical AI briefing.

Three times last week, this desk told you it had no fresh evidence from the robot-brain layer. That's the models, not the metal. On Friday the silence broke, but only with a CEO hire and some term sheets. Today it breaks properly, because the labs' own performance numbers are on the table. And what those numbers say is more precise than either the hype case or the bust case.

Start with MIT Technology Review's synthesis, published October 8th. It's the sharpest on-record accounting of where embodied AI actually sits right now. Its centerpiece is Physical Intelligence's model line. That line runs from pi-0 in 2024, through pi-0.5 and pi-0.6, to pi-0.7 this April. Each release improved in steps, not leaps.

The newest model, pi-0.7, attempted a task it had never been trained on: loading a sweet potato into an air fryer. Attempted is the operative word. It did not finish. Even so, cofounder Sergey Levine called it the first time they had convincingly seen that kind of compositional generalization. That's a real claim, and a narrow one. The model combined skills it already had to start a task it had never seen. It just couldn't close it out.

Set that against the loudest skeptic the piece quotes. Yann LeCun, speaking at Davos back in January, said none of the humanoid companies has any idea how to make their robots smart enough to be useful. That's an assertion, not evidence. But pi-0.7 is a small data point against it, and it also shows how far there is to go. So the honest headline isn't a breakthrough. It's a model that starts the right task and stops short of completing it. That is exactly the gap this desk has been watching for.

If that's the academic-lab view, Figure has run the most ambitious industry-scale test of the same idea. To be clear, this isn't this week's news. Figure announced Helix 2.5 on September 17th. But this desk hasn't weighed it until now, and it speaks directly to the question we've been asking. Figure ran a single model checkpoint across thirty rented homes in the Bay Area that it had never operated in. CEO Brett Adcock put it plainly: they rented the homes and are doing tasks "without any new training."

The cleanest number comes from a separate, controlled comparison, with architecture and task data held fixed. In blind evaluations, the version pretrained on Figure's large "Index" dataset succeeded on 56 percent of zero-shot trials. The same setup trained from scratch managed 9 percent. That's better than a six-fold gap. Figure says Index now generates roughly 35 minutes of new human experience every second. This is the genuinely optimistic finding of the day. Pretraining at scale, not hand-engineering, is measurably the lever that moves a robot policy forward.

Here's the tell, though. That 56 percent didn't come from the thirty homes. Figure's own blog describes the home test only in relative terms. It says Helix 2.5 "matched its success rate while using half as much adaptation data." There is no absolute number for what happened in those houses. No field completion rate, no intervention count, and no split between autonomous and teleoperated time. Figure does define failure strictly. If a human has to step in for safety, the run is aborted and counted as failed. That makes the missing field number more conspicuous, not less. And every one of these figures is Figure's own, with no independent replication.

Then there's the cost. Figure says it has committed $3.5 billion of compute to training Helix. That's frontier-language-model money being spent on a robot brain. This desk's inference: a bill that size raises the field performance Figure eventually has to show to earn it back.

And the shape isn't unique to Figure. On Friday this desk covered Skild AI's S1 model. Skild's own announcement shows the same pattern. Its in-context-learning model hit a 66 percent success rate, against 9 percent for a conventional language-prompted baseline at the same data scale of 100,000 hours. The baselines aren't identical. Figure's was a from-scratch model, while Skild's was a standard vision-language-action approach. Outside reporting also describes Skild's 66 percent as a per-step rate, not a full-task one. But the shape matches. Two labs, in two consecutive briefings, both headline uplift over a weaker baseline, and both withhold a field completion number. So the data-scaling thesis is holding up. Deployability is still unproven.

So who's actually putting robots to work in the world right now? Not the brain labs. Back to MIT Technology Review's ledger, which is blunt. Agility Robotics has hundreds of units in trials at GXO Logistics, Amazon, and Schaeffler, mostly moving bins and totes. That's useful and narrow, and it's still trials, not paid deployment at scale. Meanwhile, 1X's Neo home robot is on preorder at $20,000, due to ship later this year. It leans heavily on remote human operators. That's the same human-in-the-loop question this desk raised on Wednesday the 7th with Minerva's teleoperated humanoid. And it's a question worth putting to Figure's thirty homes as well.

On volume, China dominates outright. Per MIT Technology Review, Chinese firms built nearly 90 percent of the roughly 15,000 humanoid robots shipped worldwide in 2025. The piece doesn't name its underlying tracker, so treat that as a full-year 2025 estimate. Unitree, for its part, sells a model for under $6,000. That's less than a third of the 1X Neo's preorder price.

Into that picture steps BYD. On October 9th, China's patent office published a design patent for BYD's own humanoid. It is a design patent only. There are no specs, no price, and no production timeline. BYD hasn't even confirmed it matches the "Xiao Di" robot it has teased before. But the signal is structural. An EV and battery maker, with its own motors, packs, and volume manufacturing, is moving toward building bodies.

This desk's inference is that the race is splitting in two. Western capital is chasing the general-purpose brain, with frontier-scale training budgets. Chinese industrial capital is chasing cheap bodies in volume. If generalization arrives late, the body-volume side keeps the installed base. If it arrives early, it may land on hardware the Western labs don't build at scale. Either way, BYD's patent puts one more volume manufacturer on the body side of that split.

Credit markets are drawing the same line. On the 8th, we told you Waymo's $5 billion loan, its first-ever debt, hadn't been disclosed by Waymo or Alphabet and wasn't yet closed. On Thursday, Waymo said it has secured the money. It called the debt a step in its evolution into a "scaling commercial enterprise." Waymo runs robotaxi services in 15 US markets. The terms remain undisclosed: no maturity, no collateral, and no word on any Alphabet backing. But notice what lenders are underwriting. Not a benchmark, and not a demo. They're lending against a metered, geofenced service that is already running. That's the physical AI that needs the least general intelligence, and it's the one getting financed.

Pull the threads together, and the inflection is specific. What's real is this. Pretraining at scale measurably beats training from scratch. Cheap bodies are shipping in volume out of China. And a geofenced autonomous service can now raise institutional debt. What's still a demo is general-purpose manipulation in an uncontrolled home or factory. Every public number there measures improvement over a weaker baseline, not reliability on a paying job. The open question is who publishes the first real field completion or intervention rate. Whoever does sets the benchmark, and exposes everyone who hasn't.

Three catalysts to watch. First, any Figure disclosure of a field success rate. Second, Tesla's October earnings call, for a primary Optimus number. Third, whether BYD confirms a production humanoid.

That's your briefing for October 11th. Stay cautiously optimistic.
