Cautious Optimism Briefings

AI in Biopharma R&D

Transcript · November 13, 2026

Good morning. It's November 13th, 2026, and this is your briefing on AI across the biopharma value chain.

This week the industry got its sharpest skeptic number yet. But it tests the wrong claim. The question that matters for capital isn't whether AI can design a drug alone. It's whether spending on AI inside the wet-lab loop keeps compounding. And a key supplier's numbers this week look like a slowdown on paper, while its management insists the opposite.

Start with the claim. Vertex Pharmaceuticals' executive chairman, Jeff Leiden, told the Wall Street Journal, as summarized by Yahoo Finance, that Vertex worked with about fifty AI companies on a set of ten thousand drug compounds. None of them produced a working drug on its own. Since late October, this show has been asking AI-discovery platforms for denominators, meaning how many attempts sit behind each claimed success. Now a large-pharma insider has handed the market a concrete, falsifiable one, and it comes back zero. Fifty vendors, ten thousand compounds, no independent wins. The Journal's piece adds the plainer fact behind it. No drug designed by artificial intelligence has yet won FDA clearance.

Now the skeptical turn, in three parts. First, this is secondhand. It's the Journal's reporting as aggregated by another outlet, not the Journal's own text and not a Vertex disclosure. Second, there's no published methodology. There's no time horizon and no definition of a working drug. Nor is there a baseline for what a conventional medicinal-chemistry team would have produced from the same compounds on the same clock. A zero with nothing to compare it against can't be benchmarked. And a working drug is a bar no conventional approach clears on a vendor-evaluation timescale either. Third, look closely at the phrase "on its own." That test means AI designing a molecule with no chemist and no wet lab in the loop. That isn't how any serious AI-biopharma deal or clinical result this show has covered actually works. So the zero may be entirely true and still beside the point. There's also an incentive worth naming, and this is our inference, not Leiden's stated motive. A large drugmaker that tells the press its AI vendors failed its test also strengthens its hand when it negotiates with those vendors.

Put Leiden's number against the pattern this show has tracked. The two fresh human readouts we covered this month both came from AI acting as a selector, not a designer. Recursion's MEK inhibitor in familial adenomatous polyposis was in-licensed from Takeda. Recursion's AI picked the target and the indication, not the chemistry. In that small study, most evaluable patients saw their polyp burden fall. Evaxion's cancer-vaccine work used AI to choose which neoantigens to target, and a conventional vaccine format did the delivering. Most of those AI-picked targets triggered an immune response in patients. That's an immune-response read, not proof the vaccine works. So both signals are encouraging, and neither comes from a controlled trial. Leiden's critique lands squarely on the design-alone layer, where this show had already found the thinnest evidence. It says nothing about AI as a selector inside a human loop. Then there's the FDA point. The field's most-cited AI-designed molecule, Insilico's rentosertib, has only now reached a placebo-controlled pivotal trial. So "not yet approved" is a statement about the calendar, not a verdict on the science. The takeaway: the headline number is real, but it tests a claim the evidence never supported.

That raises a follow-on question. If insiders will say this publicly, does it change how AI-platform deals get priced? Our inference is that it tilts future licensing terms further toward small upfronts and milestone-heavy structures. That extends a pattern this show has tracked for weeks: pharma buying cheap options on AI platforms, with little paid upfront and most of the value deferred to milestones. For the AI-discovery names, it means valuations lean even harder on milestone cadence, which the companies don't control. But the more measurable test of where money is going sits one layer down the stack. It sits with the tools vendors selling into AI-driven discovery today.

That's where Twist Bioscience comes in. Twist, the DNA-synthesis and library vendor, reported fiscal 2026 results on November 12th. On the earnings call, management said 59.1 million dollars of revenue came from AI drug discovery customers. Per Benzinga, that line grew 175 percent year over year. These are real dollars moving into AI-guided screening and antibody-discovery workflows, not a platform's promise. Then the outlook. Twist's initial fiscal 2027 guidance assumes 100 million dollars for that same line. Divide the two figures, which is our arithmetic, and growth slows from 175 percent to roughly 69 percent. Benzinga reported that the shares fell on that outlook, despite a strong fourth-quarter beat.

Here's where it gets genuinely contested. Twist's own management rejects the slowdown framing. CEO Emily Leproust said on the call, quote, "indeed, we see the growth accelerating for the business." CFO Adam Laponis said the 100 million dollar figure reflects only, quote, "the customer programs, lead funnel and delivery schedules we can forecast with a high degree of confidence today." In other words, management is presenting a floor, not a ceiling.

The dollars help reconcile the two stories. By our arithmetic, this year added about 38 million dollars on this line. The new guide adds about 41 million. So the increment grows, even as the percentage falls. The rate deceleration is largely mechanical, and nothing we found on the call blames it on customer insourcing or pricing pressure. Two more points cut against reading this as a demand crack. Laponis acknowledged some customer concentration, but said the company sees it, quote, "continuing to diffuse." And Twist counts only the customers it can clearly identify as AI-focused buyers, so the line may be undercounted, not overcounted. Scale matters too. By our arithmetic, this one line is under a fifth of Twist's total revenue guide, yet it's what moved the stock. The takeaway: tools revenue remains the highest-frequency read on AI-discovery demand, ahead of any clinical readout. This quarter that read is ambiguous, a conservatively built guide rather than a clean crack in demand. And it's still one company's quarter.

Pulling the threads together, start with the map, on this show's evidence. What's showing measurable results now is AI as a selector. That means choosing targets, indications and neoantigens inside a human loop, with early human signal from Recursion and Evaxion. The spend on that loop is measurable too. It shows up in real tools dollars like Twist's line, and in clinical data bought under fixed multi-year contracts, as we covered on November 10th. What remains unproven is AI designing molecules on its own. That's the layer Leiden's zero tests, and rentosertib's pivotal trial is where it finally gets a clinical exam. Yet design is still where venture money and partnership headlines cluster, as we noted on November 8th. The near-term low-hanging fruit, in our view, is the selector move applied to molecules that already exist. Point known chemistry at a better-chosen target or disease, as Recursion did with a Takeda compound.

The biggest open question is one neither of this week's numbers measures. Does AI inside the wet-lab loop beat conventional discovery against the same baseline? Leiden's count tests design-alone. Twist's guide measures what discovery teams expect to buy, not what those purchases produce. So neither number moves the odds of clinical success up or down. Our standing view, and it is inference, is that if AI lifts success rates, the gain lands first in choosing the target and the indication. That's where much of Phase 2 attrition comes from, the wrong mechanism or the wrong disease, not the chemistry. But the evidence is two small, uncontrolled readouts, so it's a direction, not a measured improvement. What would change the view is a controlled result. On the selector side, that's Recursion's planned registrational trial. On the design side, it's rentosertib's placebo-controlled readout.

For R&D spend, our inference is a change in allocation rather than size. Budgets look set to consolidate into fewer, wet-lab-coupled AI workflows, rather than broad vendor sampling. That would mean less money spread across many AI pilots, and more concentrated in loops that generate proprietary experimental data. There was no fresh news this cycle on real-world data, regulatory, or CMC applications of AI. That's worth naming, so you know what's been quiet, not just what's been loud.

Four things to watch. First, whether Twist discloses customer concentration in more detail next quarter. Second, whether Vertex or the Journal publishes the method behind that fifty-company evaluation. Third, whether Isomorphic Labs confirms first dosing, against its own stated target of first clinical trials by the end of 2026. Fourth, whether Insilico discloses rentosertib's pivotal endpoint and readout timing. That trial is the first real clinical test of an AI-designed molecule.

That's the briefing for November 13th. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
