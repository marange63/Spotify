Good morning. It's September 17th, and this is your robotics and physical AI briefing.

Two things imposed new constraints on this sector this week, and neither of them is a capability result. One is a constraint imposed by a regulator. The other is a constraint imposed by a compute bill. Then, at the end, one small company quietly published the number this beat has been demanding for eight sessions.

Start with the regulator, because it's a correction to our own prior read. On September 9th, and again on the 14th, we graded NHTSA's audit of Tesla's Cybercab as the agency's lightest tool. Continuation, not escalation. We said the real escalation had gone to the separate Full Self-Driving fleet probe instead. That call is now wrong on its own terms. On September 10th, NHTSA escalated the certification proceeding itself. It issued a Special Order demanding a response signed under oath by a responsible Tesla officer, due September 30th. Falsifying that response carries potential criminal exposure of up to fifteen years in prison. Failing to respond fully or truthfully exposes Tesla to civil penalties of up to roughly 139 million dollars.

The substance underneath is straightforward. The Cybercab has no steering wheel, no pedals, no mirrors. NHTSA's own regulation states, verbatim, that service brakes shall be activated by means of a foot control. Tesla self-certified the vehicle as compliant anyway, rather than applying for the exemption route Amazon's Zoox used. That fork is worth pricing on both sides. Zoox took a temporary Part 555 exemption covering eight federal standards, including the foot-brake and mirror rules. In exchange it accepted a cap of 2,500 vehicles a year through 2028. Tesla self-certified and accepted no cap at all.

Be careful with the scale here, because two very different numbers are floating around. NHTSA's own audit-opening document puts the estimated population under review at 1,000 vehicles. But that is NHTSA sizing Tesla's disclosed plan to gradually expand the deployment, not the fleet on the road. The vehicles actually operating are far fewer. The most recent Texas registration data showed 420 autonomous vehicles tied to Tesla's robotaxi operation, 45 of them Cybercabs. So this is a proceeding about the legality of a rollout that is still mostly ahead of Tesla, not a penalty on a fleet already built.

Be equally precise about what a Special Order is. It's a discovery instrument, not a finding. Tesla hasn't been fined, and it hasn't been found non-compliant. NHTSA says plainly it is examining the process and technical data Tesla relied on when certifying the vehicle, not the car's on-road safety. The benign reading survives. A genuinely novel certification path is exactly what special orders exist to document, and this would look identical whether or not Tesla's determination holds up. There's also no public sign of Tesla's strategy. No filed Part 555 petition, no stated rationale, nothing on the record since the order landed. That's an absence of disclosure, not evidence either way.

But the asymmetry in outcomes is real, and it restates something we flagged on the 14th with a sharper instrument behind it now. If the no-manual-controls path gets constrained, Tesla gets pushed onto the exemption route. So the downside here isn't the penalty. The price of that route is a volume ceiling plus a human monitor back in the seat, which is exactly the labor cost the purpose-built robotaxi exists to delete. Zoox's ceiling was 2,500 vehicles a year. NHTSA has said nothing about a number for Tesla. And an adverse finding would be precedent against the whole no-pedal design category, not against one company.

That's what happens to an operator outside the existing framework. Here's what scaling inside it looks like. Waymo opened public, driverless service — not invite-only — in Denver, San Diego and Tampa on September 1st, bringing it to 14 US cities. Its stated goal is a million paid rides a week by year-end, against a spring baseline near 500,000 a week. Treat that implied doubling as a company aspiration, not a measured run-rate. Zoox is entering Houston and San Diego, but with human-driven mapping cars, not its driverless robotaxi. It already runs paid rides in Las Vegas and free rides in four other cities. The point is the stage gap. The operators working inside the regulatory framework are compounding. The operator outside it is drafting a sworn filing.

Now shift from legal constraints to physical ones. On the humanoid side, the binding constraint isn't permission, it's compute. On September 3rd, Figure AI and infrastructure provider Nscale announced a deal for up to 100,000 GPUs on Nvidia's next-generation Vera Rubin platform. The initial commitment is 3.5 billion dollars of compute, with stated intent to scale past 6 billion. First deployment is the second half of 2027, in Barstow, Texas. That's a forward commitment, not installed capacity, and it is not disclosed as cash capex — whether it's a lease, a take-or-pay, or compute swapped for equity is not in the announcement. Figure's own framing is the interesting part. It says it is now bound by the data and compute needed to train its Helix model, not by algorithms.

Interrogate that claim rather than accepting it. Bound-by-compute is the convenient story for a company raising against compute, because compute is purchasable and a capability gap isn't. The frontier-language-model analogy is also doing unearned work. Those models had a demonstrated scaling law before the buildout. No equivalent published relationship exists yet between compute and real-world task reliability for robot foundation models. Figure also says its Index platform is ingesting 35 minutes of physical video every second. That works out to roughly fifty thousand hours of footage a day, collected from a crowdsourced base the company reports at just under 70,000 weekly users. But that's a collection metric, not a usefulness one. Nobody has shown that data improves Helix's fielded success rate over teleoperation data.

Set that commitment against the demand side of this sector, which we've been measuring all week. Agility Robotics burns roughly 78 dollars of operating loss for every dollar of net sales. UBTech's largest disclosed order anywhere sits around 35 million dollars, against 10,000 units of nameplate capacity. The denominator hasn't moved. Two structural reads follow. First, robotics foundation-model training is now a second bidder for the same constrained next-generation GPU supply the frontier labs are already competing for, on a specific silicon generation, more than a year out. Second, Nscale is reported to be taking equity in Figure while selling it compute — the same supplier-as-investor structure we flagged when Foxconn led the placement into its own customer Agility. When capacity gets allocated by ownership rather than by price, the headline commitment figure becomes a poor proxy for cash actually spent.

Against all that forward commitment, one robotics company this week actually published a payback. RobotPlusPlus, a Chinese industrial robotics firm, closed a Series C. The size is disclosed only as hundreds of millions of RMB, tens of millions of dollars, so treat that range loosely. Its hull-derusting robots have logged over 2 million cumulative operating hours in the field. And here's the arithmetic: the company says repair cycles compress from six or seven days to about three, with costs down 30 to 50 percent. Discount it appropriately. It's a fundraising announcement, these are company-supplied figures, and it's an extremely narrow niche — which is exactly why the arithmetic can be stated this cleanly, and exactly why it won't generalize. But the direction of the evidence is the point. The place the numbers are legible is the place nobody is paying humanoid multiples.

Pull the threads together. Eight sessions running, and still no humanoid or robotaxi vendor on this beat has published a fielded reliability number. No uptime, no intervention rate, no cost per completed task. This week didn't close that gap. It moved the binding constraint outward, to a regulator and to a compute contract, rather than inward toward reliability. Three dates worth watching. Tesla's sworn response is due September 30th. Figure's first checkable milestone on the Nscale commitment is the Barstow deployment in the second half of 2027. And on November 10th, China's pause on rare-earth export controls expires. If those controls come back, actuator and motor costs move for everyone in this sector.
