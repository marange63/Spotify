Good morning. It's Saturday, August 29th, and this is your strategic power briefing.

We start in the Strait of Hormuz, where a second tanker was hit this week and oil fell anyway. A commercial tanker was struck by an unknown projectile on Wednesday. It caught fire, the crew put the fire out, and no one was hurt, according to the UK Maritime Trade Organization. That followed a Liberian-flagged tanker hit on Monday. Two vessels in three days. And nobody has established who is firing. "Unknown projectile" is as far as the reporting goes, on both strikes. Yet crude held near 82 dollars a barrel on Thursday, its third straight down day.

The new input is diplomatic. Oman's government put its name on a joint statement describing a proposed framework with Iran. It has two pieces: a temporary joint navigation corridor through the strait, and a joint project to clear the strait of mines. Oman's foreign minister, Badr Albusaidi, said he hopes to soon announce the corridor, which tells you where this actually stands. It is discussed and proposed. It is not signed, not ratified, and not operating. Qatar's prime minister was in Tehran on Thursday to discuss what both sides are calling a phased framework. That is still a negotiation, not an agreement. And note that nobody has established the corridor is why crude fell. The timing is coincident; the causation is not sourced.

Here's the base rate for what an announcement is worth on this lane. The United States and Iran signed a memorandum of understanding in June. Brent fell as low as 69 dollars a barrel on July 2nd, according to the EIA. Attacks resumed, and by July 23rd Brent was back near 105. The market has already paid for one diplomatic framework this summer, and un-priced it within three weeks. An unsigned corridor proposal is a thinner promise than a signed memorandum was. So if you're using 82-dollar oil as evidence the strait is functioning again, the summer's own history says be careful.

The physical evidence says more than careful. War-risk insurance for Hormuz transits is quoted at 3 to 8 percent of hull value right now. Before the conflict it ran around a quarter of one percent. Even the low end of today's range makes most transits commercially marginal, and that is with the premium already down from a wartime peak near 10 percent.

The transit data says the same thing louder. IMF PortWatch recorded exactly one commercial transit through the strait on August 16th, against a pre-crisis median of roughly 73 vessels a day. Be careful with that: it's a single published day, not a monthly trend, and no July-versus-August comparison is available. But one vessel against seventy-three is not a slowdown. That is a strait functioning as closed, while the oil price behaves as though nothing changed. Which means the pullback in crude more likely reflects barrels arriving from somewhere else — reserves, alternate routes, softer demand — than confidence that the lane itself is open.

Washington isn't betting on a quick resolution either. The USS Theodore Roosevelt carrier strike group is due out of San Diego within weeks, to relieve the USS George Washington. The deployment is publicly described as more than seven months, and the crew is reportedly being told internally to plan for eight. That is a planning assumption that runs into next spring. So watch actual transit counts, not the announcement, for the real signal.

That's a chokepoint being priced on an announcement. Here's one being priced on enforcement.

China's rare-earth controls are still, on paper, in the suspended state Beijing put them in last November. But the enforcement machinery underneath that suspension has been tightening all along. Two Japanese nationals were detained in Dalian in May, among the first known cases of foreign nationals held over an alleged rare-earth export-control violation. Separately, Shanghai Customs placed the chairman of a Chinese precision-optics firm under compulsory measures, alleging he mislabelled germanium-containing lenses as ordinary glass to dodge licensing. Customs pulled three years of that company's export records to build the case. And on July 1st, China's Commerce Ministry formalized Announcement Number 26, a whistleblower-style mechanism inviting the public to report suspected violations of strategic-mineral export rules.

Now the honest qualifier. No Chinese authority has described any of this as a single policy shift. These are discrete cases, and the pattern connecting them is inference, not an announced program. But the inference corrects something this beat argued on Monday: that Beijing was pricing the rare-earth threat without exercising it. The exercise was happening the whole time, just through customs and criminal channels rather than the headline export ban.

The price agrees with that read. The NdPr alloy benchmark — the input for the magnets in EVs, wind turbines and defense actuators — rose 21.4 percent in a single month, from about 109 dollars a kilogram in June to 133 dollars on July 1st. That comes from industry pricing data, not an official Chinese statistic, and it is a July 1st print, so treat it as two months stale as a market signal rather than as today's tape. What it captures is physical scarcity showing up in the market, not headline risk.

Beijing paired that enforcement with reciprocal targeting. It restricted dual-use exports to ten US firms including MP Materials and USA Rare Earth on June 22nd, and extended similar restrictions to 14 European firms on July 24th. So the two flagship Western rare-earth plays are being cut off from Chinese dual-use inputs at the same moment their production is being underwritten domestically. That tells you the real leverage sits in separation and refining capacity, which no offtake agreement buys. The other cost lands quietly, on people. Detaining foreign nationals over export-control allegations turns a trade dispute into a personal legal risk for Western staff working in China, and that changes staffing and travel decisions well before it changes trade policy.

From a foreign chokepoint to a domestic one. The chokepoint Americans are actually paying for this year sits on their own grid.

PJM's independent market monitor told the grid operator's members committee on August 24th that data-center load, existing and forecast, made up 9 percent of PJM's wholesale power price through July. In dollars, that 9 percent works out to 10 dollars and 48 cents per megawatt-hour. The monitor's 9 percent share is far narrower than the capacity-charge shares this beat carried earlier in the week — 38 percent on Wednesday, 40 percent on Thursday — because the monitor is measuring a different thing. The monitor's calculation is drawn from the capacity market alone, and it leaves out energy and transmission costs. That narrower 9 percent figure is also the more credible one, because it is the number that shows up in an actual bill rather than in an attribution argument.

Hold the caveat that applies to all of these figures. How much of PJM's capacity-price escalation is data centers, rather than retiring baseload generation and a clogged interconnection queue, is genuinely contested, and no counterfactual auction exists to settle it. Total PJM wholesale costs are up 46 percent year to date, to 56.7 billion dollars. That is a separate and larger figure, and it is not itself a data-center number. What makes today's version structural rather than a one-auction blip is the monitor's own words: the data-center share, they said, will continue to grow until the issues around adding large loads are addressed.

That matters because a per-megawatt-hour number is politically actionable in a way a reliability warning never was. It gives state utility commissions something concrete to allocate between customer classes. And it pushes hyperscalers further toward dedicated, behind-the-meter power, which raises the scarcity value of generation that is already interconnected.

The fix everyone points to signed another contract this month. Read the delivery date. TerraPower selected Hyundai Engineering and Construction as EPC contractor for up to eight of its Natrium reactors, 345 megawatts each, for 2.76 gigawatts of baseload capacity backing Meta's power rights. The contract carries completion, price and performance guarantees, which is more than a power-purchase memo gets you. Initial delivery is targeted for 2032.

Be precise about what has and hasn't happened there. Signing an engineering and construction contractor is not breaking ground. No site has been selected, so there is no permitting or interconnection schedule standing behind that 2032 target, and no construction start date is public. And this is the third link this week in the same chain, after a power-purchase agreement on Monday and an enrichment-supply contract yesterday. Every link lands past 2030. So whatever demand PJM is pricing today, the bridge through the rest of this decade is gas and existing grid supply, not reactors.

So: three chokepoints, three different clocks. The one being priced hardest, Hormuz, has the least evidence behind it. The one running quietly through customs offices in China is already moving prices. And the one with a real construction contract behind it doesn't deliver for six years. Watch transit counts out of the strait rather than the announcements, and watch whether federal regulators act on large-load interconnection at PJM.

That's the briefing. Have a good weekend.
