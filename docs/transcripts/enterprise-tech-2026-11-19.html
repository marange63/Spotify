Cautious Optimism Briefings

Enterprise Technology & Software

Transcript · November 19, 2026

Good morning. It's Thursday, November 19th, 2026, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

This week the big enterprise software incumbents each showed which layer of the stack they still think they can own. Salesforce is letting Microsoft own the front door while keeping the keys to its own data. Workday is buying the arithmetic underneath the agent. Palo Alto Networks is buying its way into the model layer itself. And last night, the public market put its own price on that model layer, when Anthropic priced its IPO above range.

Start with Salesforce and Microsoft, because it's the clearest structural shift of the week. On November 17th the two companies announced Copilotforce. It lets Microsoft's Copilot and Teams reach directly into Salesforce's CRM data and sales workflows. The headline sounds like Salesforce handing its front end to a rival. The companies' own language is more layered. Salesforce's announcement says it "continues to control access to CRM records, while Microsoft applies its existing enterprise compliance controls." So Microsoft gets the conversational interface, and Salesforce keeps the permission layer.

The data layer is split the same way. Salesforce's Data 360 and Microsoft's Fabric now connect in both directions. In Salesforce's words, "joint customers can query Microsoft Fabric data inside Salesforce Data 360 and share Data 360 information into Microsoft OneLake without duplicating the underlying datasets." No data moves. Each side simply reads the other's.

This continues an arc this desk has tracked all week. On the 14th, we noted Salesforce extending its own Agentforce Coworker assistant into Teams. On the 18th, we noted Salesforce still hadn't put a production-usage number on Agentforce. Copilotforce goes a step further. Now it's Microsoft's agent reading Salesforce's records, not Salesforce's agent living inside Microsoft's surface.

There are two honest readings of that. One is surrender: the vendor pushing its own agents hardest has conceded the interface. The other is a deliberate retreat to the layers that may still earn rent once agents are commoditized, which are permissions and data. Which reading wins depends on the monetization terms, and those haven't been disclosed.

Now apply the evidence ladder before calling any of this adoption. The only named deployment is Accenture. The release describes it as "the design and implementation partner and the first customer, deploying the integrations internally with about 55,000 Salesforce users." That's the integrator selling the service, deploying it on itself. It sits one step above an announcement, and well short of an arm's-length customer. A provisioned user count also tells you nothing yet about active use. No pricing or revenue split is disclosed. And outlets describe the rollout status of individual pieces inconsistently, so don't treat any of it as generally available.

Two second-order effects are worth naming, both as inference rather than fact. First, the failure domain widens. Microsoft's Teams and Copilot went down together in an outage on November 12th. If CRM workflows now run through that same surface, a Microsoft outage increasingly becomes a CRM outage too. Second, switching costs rise. On Monday this desk covered ServiceNow's Autonomous CRM Engine, a pitch to make leaving an incumbent CRM cheaper. A Salesforce embedded inside Microsoft's surface gets harder to rip out, not easier. So what? If the AI interface consolidates on Copilot, per-seat monetization of systems of record is what's at risk. Salesforce is betting the rent sits in permissions and data, not in the chat box.

Workday is making almost the identical bet, one layer further down. On November 18th, at Workday Rising in Barcelona, the company said it will acquire Honeydew, a semantic-layer company for AI and business intelligence. A semantic layer makes an AI agent compute a given business figure the same way every time, instead of reasoning it out fresh on each request. Workday's chief technology officer, Gabe Monroy, put the rationale in one line: "An AI model shouldn't be improvising the calculation behind a payroll number." Terms weren't disclosed, so there's no way to size the bet. The deal is expected to close in Workday's fiscal fourth quarter, which ends January 31st, 2027.

Be skeptical of the framing, too. Semantic layers aren't new technology. Part of this is an old business-intelligence consistency problem, relabeled as AI governance. So read it as an architectural statement, not evidence that any customer has adopted anything. A system-of-record vendor is deciding its moat is the deterministic logic under the model, the one thing an agent can't improvise.

Palo Alto Networks went the opposite direction. Instead of retreating beneath the model, it bought straight into it. Start with the quarter it reported on November 18th. Fiscal first-quarter revenue was about $3.4 billion, up 39% year over year. Next-Generation Security ARR reached about $9.7 billion, up 66%.

Backlog grew as well. Remaining performance obligations rose 35%, to $21 billion. And the company raised its full-year guidance.

On Monday, this desk called this report the next read on whether security buyers reward platforms or bundles. The headline numbers can't settle that, and here's why. Palo Alto doesn't disclose a company-wide organic growth figure in its earnings release or on the call. What the call does disclose are two sub-segment rates. Cortex revenue "grew 53% year-over-year and 40% year-over-year on a pro forma basis." Stripping out acquisitions cuts that growth rate by thirteen points. And in Idira, the identity platform built around the CyberArk acquisition, "Q1 revenue grew 23% year-over-year on a pro forma basis."

Neither figure substitutes for a total-company split. But both pro forma rates sit well below the headline growth. That's real evidence that acquisitions are doing meaningful work inside both headline numbers. So don't treat this quarter as proof of organic platformization.

Alongside earnings, Palo Alto announced it will acquire Applied Compute in an all-stock deal. Palo Alto's own release says the transaction "is valued at $4.5 billion, subject to adjustments." It describes the target only as "a leading platform for post-training and inference of open-weight AI models." Notably, that release states no revenue or customer figure for Applied Compute. Bloomberg adds that about $500 million of that $4.5 billion headline is Applied Compute's own cash and prepaid compute, already sitting on its balance sheet. So Palo Alto is effectively paying roughly $4 billion in stock for the business itself. Bloomberg also reports that co-founder and chief executive Yash Patil becomes Palo Alto's Co-Chief AI Officer once the deal closes. That close is expected in Palo Alto's fiscal third quarter.

Making the target's chief executive a company AI chief suggests a capability-and-talent purchase as much as a revenue purchase. Whether securing enterprises' own open-weight model deployments becomes a durable ARR line is genuinely unproven. If it does, other security vendors may choose to buy rather than build. That's inference, not a prediction of any deal. Either way, it's the mirror image of Salesforce and Workday. A security vendor is spending its own equity to own part of how enterprises run models.

All three of those moves are bets on where value settles around the model. Last night, the public market put its own price on the model layer itself. Standing disclosure: Anthropic makes the model behind this show. Anthropic priced its IPO at $83 per share, "above the expected price range." That range had been $70 to $78. The reported valuation is $1.5 trillion. Shares are scheduled to begin trading today on the Nasdaq under the ticker ANT. No first-day trading result exists as of this recording, so treat any debut price you hear later today as new information.

Pricing above range measures investor demand for the stock. It doesn't measure enterprise production usage, and that gap remains open. The timing is also mildly pattern-breaking. This came in a week when AI names had been selling off. An above-range price reads more as scarce demand for direct frontier-lab exposure than as a verdict on the sector. If the stock holds, Anthropic's public mark becomes the benchmark incumbent software multiples get judged against. That stays conditional until it actually trades.

Pulling the week together: Salesforce is keeping the permissions, Workday is keeping the logic, Palo Alto is buying into the models, and the lab itself just got the premium public mark. Four things to watch. First, Anthropic's first trades. Second, whether Copilotforce names an arm's-length customer beyond Accenture. Third, whether Palo Alto ever discloses an organic growth split, and whether Applied Compute closes on schedule. Fourth, the next security reads from CrowdStrike and Zscaler.

That's the briefing. Have a good one.
