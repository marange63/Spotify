Frontier AI Lab Competition

Transcript · September 9, 2026

Good morning, it's Wednesday, September 9th, 2026, and this is your Frontier AI Labs briefing.

For two years, US labs have accused Chinese competitors of copying their models rather than out-innovating them. This week that accusation got a badge. The NSA, CISA and the FBI jointly published advisory AA26-251A, naming six Chinese AI companies as running what the agencies call industrial-scale distillation campaigns against American frontier models. This is the first time the US government has put its own name behind that claim, rather than leaving it to the labs to allege it themselves.

The advisory gives a company-by-company timeline, and every line of it is the agencies' assessment rather than an adjudicated finding. DeepSeek, they say, has run an organized distillation campaign against US frontier models since at least late 2024. Moonshot AI since mid-2025. Alibaba, in the agencies' account, distilled Claude 4, Claude Opus, Claude Sonnet and GPT-5 in late 2025. MiniMax pulled chain-of-thought reasoning, reinforcement learning and software-engineering capability in the same window. StepFun took data from Claude Opus 4.1 and 4.5 between late last year and early this one. And Z.AI had distilled billions of tokens of GPT-5.5 and Claude Opus 4.8 data by the middle of this year.

Here's why the economics matter more than the espionage framing. Training a frontier model runs on the order of a hundred thousand GPUs. That is the scale OpenAI used for GPT-6 Astra at its Stargate site in Texas. Extracting that model's behavior, by contrast, costs whatever the API meter reads. Claude Fable 5.1, Anthropic's current flagship, is listed on Anthropic's own pricing page at ten dollars per million input tokens and fifty dollars per million output. The advisory describes the extraction as billions of tokens across millions of requests. Do that arithmetic. A billion tokens of frontier output, bought at the published rate, is fifty thousand dollars. That is the whole story in one comparison. One side of this race pays for a training run in gigawatts and capex, the other pays retail for the answers, and the second bill is a rounding error against the first. There's a sharper version of that point, too. The published price of the frontier is also the published price of copying it. An advisory doesn't fix an asymmetry like that. It only names it.

And that's the skeptical turn worth sitting with. This is an assessment, not evidence anyone outside three agencies can check. The advisory itself cites no telemetry sample, no forensic token log and no named intelligence source for any of the six attributions. No response from any of the named companies has surfaced in the reporting reviewed for this briefing. It's also worth noticing whose interests the document validates. The same US labs that stand to gain from tighter API terms and stricter enterprise contracts are the parties whose two years of informal complaints this now formalizes. And distillation being real does not establish the stronger claim people will want to draw from it, which is that Chinese frontier progress is nothing but extraction. Kimi, Qwen and DeepSeek have each shipped architectural and post-training work that copying alone doesn't explain.

There's also an irony in the fix. The advisory's recommended mitigations are user verification, authenticated API access, and monitoring for suspicious usage, and every one of them is aimed at the US labs' own systems. It imposes no export control, no KYC mandate, and no restriction of any kind on the accused firms. It is voluntary guidance telling OpenAI, Anthropic and Google how to harden their own front doors. And a front door hardened against high-volume scraping is slower and more expensive for the labs' best legitimate customers. An enterprise account running millions of agentic calls a day looks, on the wire, a great deal like a distillation campaign. So the cheapest available response to this threat taxes the labs' own best revenue. The clean commercial win sits somewhere else entirely. A Western company with real intellectual-property exposure now has a federal document to point at when it declines to put a Chinese open-weight model into its stack. That is worth more to OpenAI, Anthropic and Google than any benchmark result this month, and it raises the value of Mistral's sovereign-AI positioning without Mistral doing anything at all.

Washington isn't only playing prosecutor against foreign labs this week. It is also acting as gatekeeper against a domestic one. Under Secretary of Defense Emil Michael said publicly that the Pentagon's supply-chain-risk designation against Anthropic remains in effect. That contradicts Commerce Secretary Lutnick's earlier suggestion that the relationship had thawed. Two departments of the same government, speaking through named officials within days of each other, gave opposite public answers about the same company. The designation traces back to Anthropic refusing to let Claude be used for domestic surveillance or for fully autonomous weapons. A federal judge had already found the department retaliated in violation of the First Amendment. The Pentagon then invoked a separate statute to reinstate the risk designation, and Anthropic is challenging that in the D.C. Circuit, on a timeline nobody can put a date on. Now resist the clean story that Anthropic is simply locked out of defense, because the evidence doesn't support it. Reporting this week indicates the company still holds prototype work with the department, running in parallel with the fight. The plain takeaway for a buyer or a partner is that US government posture toward frontier AI is not one policy right now. It is several, contradicting each other in public, and no procurement team can plan against that.

That overhang is about to meet a disclosure document. This show has treated Anthropic's prospectus as the sector's first checkable number all week — on the fourth, again on the sixth, and yesterday, when it still had not surfaced. Today the pattern breaks. The listing has slipped from early September to mid-October, with the prospectus itself not expected until late September. At the same time, Anthropic is finalizing a revolving credit facility that has grown to fifteen billion dollars, a significant increase on what was originally planned. Some investors have floated a listing near two trillion dollars. That figure is investor chatter reported by Reuters, not a filed range and not company guidance, so hold it at arm's length rather than repeating it as a fact. The delay on its own reads as weakness. The bigger credit line cuts the other way, because it funds compute purchasing independent of the IPO proceeds, which lowers the cost of waiting. Outlets describe the slip as routine sequencing, and no Anthropic statement explains it. Here's the part to bank. Whenever that prospectus does land, a live Pentagon supply-chain-risk designation will be one of the first things a public-market investor can read about a frontier lab.

While that disclosure waits, the capital keeps moving, and it is moving through cap tables rather than balance sheets. Yesterday this show covered Samsung, a component supplier, taking an equity stake in the model lab Mistral as its lead investor. Today the same equity-for-supply trade runs in the other direction. Qualcomm issued Amazon warrants for twenty-five million shares, worth roughly four billion dollars, as part of a multi-generation deal to co-design AI inference silicon for AWS. Those warrants vest as Amazon buys up to sixty billion dollars of Qualcomm chips and related technology. Call that sixty billion what it is: a purchase ceiling tied to warrant vesting, not a signed order. Qualcomm's CFO says commercial revenue begins in the December 2026 quarter, and no architecture or performance comparison against Amazon's own Trainium chips, or against Nvidia, has been disclosed. Two directions of the same trade in two days makes this a template rather than a one-off. Compute access is being settled on cap tables now, not on purchase orders. And a credible second source of inference silicon at hyperscaler scale matters to every lab, because it puts a ceiling on the cost of serving tokens, and that cost is what decides margin for anyone whose business is inference at scale.

Add it up. Every one of today's four developments was decided in Washington or on a cap table. Not one of them was decided by a benchmark. Three things would change that read: the advisory producing actual evidence instead of assertion, a mitigation that is genuinely imposed rather than merely recommended, and Anthropic's prospectus arriving as filed text rather than as leaked chatter. Until then, access is the contested asset in this race, not capability.
