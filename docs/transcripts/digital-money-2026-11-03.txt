Good morning, it's Tuesday, November 3rd, and this is your digital money briefing. Strip away the hack headlines, and crypto's regulated 2026 ledger is close to flat. Meanwhile the on-chain dollar isn't just shrinking. It's changing shape.

Start with Bitcoin, and with the hack in a single line. Binance lost roughly six billion dollars to theft last Thursday, and this desk has covered the recovery at length. What's new is the year-to-date frame. CoinDesk reports that before last Friday, U.S. spot Bitcoin ETFs were sitting on a net inflow for the year of just over one billion dollars. Then on Friday, they recorded 643 million dollars of net outflows in a single session. By CoinDesk's own math, that one day erased nearly two-thirds of the year's net buying. Put plainly, one session undid most of a year.

And the selling didn't stop on Friday. On Monday, November 2nd, Bitcoin funds saw a further net outflow of about 399 million dollars, per SoSoValue data reported by Crypto Briefing. No outlet has yet published an updated year-to-date total, so we won't do that arithmetic for them. The direction is enough. Two straight outflow sessions now sit on top of a year that was only barely positive to begin with. One caution before reading too much into it. As this desk has noted, these outflows landed alongside a hawkish-leaning Fed hold and a broader tech selloff. So not all of this can be pinned on the hack.

Step back, and this is the widening window this desk has tracked since Thursday the 29th. That day, the two-week ETF flow was churning, not building. On Saturday, we reported the one-day outflow. On Sunday, the trailing thirty days had turned negative on at least one tracker. Now the window is the whole year. In CoinDesk's words, the funds "gave back most of what they pulled in this year in investor money."

The bull case deserves a plain statement. Total U.S. spot Bitcoin ETF assets stood at 98.14 billion dollars after Friday, per Bitfire Research. So most holders did not leave. This is a stall, not a run. But the stall is itself the story. If 2026's net new money is near zero, then this year's ETF asset base is mostly legacy allocation riding Bitcoin's price. It is not the structural institutional bid the narrative keeps selling. That has a direct consequence for issuers. With no inflow cushion underneath, a drawdown in Bitcoin now shrinks the complex's assets almost one for one. And Bitcoin traded around 77,750 dollars on Monday morning, below the roughly 80,000 it held through October. So the wrapper is holding Bitcoin in 2026. It isn't accumulating it.

If new ETF money isn't what's moving Bitcoin, what is it trading like? One fresh data point comes from Block Scholes. Its 90-day rolling correlation between Bitcoin and gold rose to 54 percent, the highest in data back to 2014. Over the same stretch, Bitcoin's correlation with U.S. equities eased. That sounds like the long-awaited arrival of digital gold. But the same source undercuts it. Over the past month, gold dropped 7 percent, while Bitcoin stayed around 80,000 dollars. A record correlation alongside such different one-month paths tells you the 90-day window is smoothing over a recent divergence. That's a statistical relationship shifting, not evidence that Bitcoin now behaves like gold. And the reading predates the hack, which hit Bitcoin with a venue-specific shock that gold never shared.

The dollar side of crypto has its own year-to-date problem, and it predates the hack entirely. Stablecoin supply hit a record 315.5 billion dollars on May 17th, and it has drifted lower since. The issuer-level data is the sharper read. Between January 1st and October 28th, USDT supply fell by 5.3 billion dollars, and USDC supply fell by 4 billion. Note the end date. That window closes the day before Binance was hit.

That forces a correction. On Sunday, this desk framed the week's stablecoin contraction as hack-window de-risking, alongside the ETFs. That framing was too narrow. The leak began months before the hack, so the hack did not start this drain. Two explanations compete, and the evidence doesn't let us pick between them. One is falling speculative and trading demand, consistent with a year of flat ETF flows. The other is a partial migration toward yield-bearing, permissioned wrappers.

That migration story has a real, if small, data point behind it. Allium counted roughly 16.17 billion dollars in tokenized Treasury and money-market funds as of October 12th. That count is about three weeks old. These are regulated cash-equivalent products living on public blockchains. Now look at where the biggest names are landing. Solana is the largest network for BlackRock's BUIDL fund, holding about 954 million dollars of it in early October. And J.P. Morgan Asset Management's MONY and JLTXX funds became available on Solana to qualified U.S. investors on October 22nd. Regulated money managers are converging on the same public chain as a settlement rail for institutional cash. That's a genuine institutionalization signal.

But keep the scale honest. Sixteen billion dollars is small next to a stablecoin float that peaked above 315 billion. JPMorgan's funds are open only to qualified investors. They are not a home for the retail and offshore trading dollars leaving USDT. And there's no flow data linking the two markets. So migration can explain, at most, part of the leak. One more caution, and it's our reasoning rather than a reported finding. A token's market cap tells you what's outstanding. It doesn't tell you how much could be sold or posted on a stressed day.

There's a Treasury-market implication too, and it's smaller than it sounds. Tokenized Treasury funds hold Treasuries by construction. So any shift out of stablecoins and into these funds mostly changes the wrapper holding the bill. It doesn't change total bill demand by much. The takeaway is shape, not size. The institutional end of the on-chain dollar is maturing, but it's far too small to explain the whole leak.

And the most regulated stablecoin in that market is about to get the heaviest supervision in it. In October, the European Banking Authority designated USDC the EU's first significant e-money token under MiCA. From November 12th, the EBA will directly oversee USDC's own funds, reserve assets, liquidity requirements, and recovery and redemption plans. France's ACPR keeps governance, fit-and-proper assessments, and conflicts of interest. The EBA has also signed a memorandum of understanding with New York's Department of Financial Services. Per The Block, it will facilitate cross-border oversight and information exchange between the two regulators. This is the first live test of MiCA's significant-token regime. And it lands on the regulated dollar stablecoin, not on USDT.

The bull case is that this builds the supervisory credibility institutions want before they underwrite a stablecoin at scale. The skeptical case is that it loads compliance cost, and possibly redemption friction, onto a token that's already shrinking. Its larger offshore competitor carries none of that EU reserve supervision. Either way, the gap between regulated and offshore dollars is now a concrete supervisory gap. Both the cost and the credibility land on USDC.

One last item, and it's a dated catalyst rather than news. Today is the midterm election, and the post-election lame duck is the near-term window for crypto's market-structure bill. The Senate blocked the CLARITY Act in September, forty-nine to fifty on a cloture vote, well short of the sixty needed. The one new wrinkle is procedural. Senator Thom Tillis filed a motion to reconsider, which preserves a path to revisit the vote. Results aren't in as we record this, so we won't guess at them.

So, three things to watch. First, whether ETF flows turn positive for the year again, or slip further negative. Second, whether stablecoin supply finds a floor. Third, how the EBA's first supervisory window over USDC opens on November 12th. Past the hack headlines, the regulated crypto ledger for 2026 is close to flat. The on-chain dollar is getting more supervised and more institutional at its edges. That's a slower, smaller story than the bull narrative sells, but it's the one the data supports.
