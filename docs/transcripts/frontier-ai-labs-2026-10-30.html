Good morning. It's Friday, October 30th, and this is your frontier AI labs briefing.

One disclosure before we start. Anthropic makes the model behind this show, and today's second story is Anthropic's own pricing.

Here's the thesis for today. The price war among the frontier labs has stopped being about the list price per token. It has moved to the price of a finished piece of work. This week, OpenAI and Anthropic fought that fight with two very different instruments. Google chose to compete on a different axis entirely. And underneath all three sits the business that sells the compute.

Let's start with OpenAI, because this closes a gap we flagged ourselves. On Wednesday, we told you to take GPT-6.1 Terra's positioning and set aside its numbers, since they weren't on the record yet. They are now, relayed by InfoWorld from OpenAI's launch materials early this week. The new Terra is priced at one dollar per million input tokens and five dollars per million output tokens. That's half the rate of the prior model, GPT-5.6 Terra. On its own, that's a straightforward list cut on a workhorse tier.

The more interesting number is the cost of actually finishing a task. On the OSWorld 2.1 computer-use benchmark, OpenAI reports that the old Terra scored 57.1 percent at four dollars seventy-six cents per task. The new Terra scores 74.2 percent at a dollar nineteen. So the cost per task fell roughly four-fold, twice the size of the headline price cut.

Notice what that gap implies. The price per token halved. The cost per task fell by three quarters. The difference is efficiency: the model reaches the answer in fewer tokens or fewer steps. That's arguably the more durable advantage, because a rival can't match it simply by cutting a list price. It has to match it in the model itself.

Now three caveats, because this is exactly the kind of number that deserves a skeptical ear. First, this is a tier move, not a flagship cut. Terra is OpenAI's cheaper workhorse line. The fifty percent cut is measured against its own predecessor. It says nothing about OpenAI's top-end rate, and nothing about a rival's price. We couldn't verify rival list prices at this tier, so the cut is proven only against the old Terra.

Second, every one of these figures is OpenAI's own. OpenAI chose the benchmarks it published. Independent evaluators have started testing Terra, but their reported scores vary from outlet to outlet, and we couldn't verify any of them. Treat the capability gain as self-reported until an outside evaluator settles it.

Third, the margin arithmetic cuts both ways. If the price per token halves and the tokens per task also fall, OpenAI's revenue per finished task is falling much faster than the sticker suggests. That only works if task volume is highly elastic, or if OpenAI's own serving costs fell to match. One possible cost-side explanation is the Google TPU capacity OpenAI was reported to be renting, which we covered Wednesday. That report is unconfirmed, so this is our inference. The alternative is plainer: OpenAI is subsidizing share in agentic workloads to own the category early. Competitor responses at the same tier, or any OpenAI disclosure of usage, would tell those stories apart.

The second-order effect lands first on agentic work like computer use and coding agents. A computer-use task at roughly a dollar pressures RPA-style automation vendors, whose pitch is cheap, scripted desktop work. It also pressures any lab whose agent revenue scales with token volume, because efficiency gains shrink that volume. So OpenAI is now competing on the price of finished work, not the price of tokens.

OpenAI cut a list price. Anthropic did something different. And again, Anthropic makes the model behind this show, so weigh this section with that in mind. This week, Anthropic and the General Services Administration announced new federal pricing. US government buyers get a forty percent discount on Claude. The discount runs for twenty-seven months, through January 31st, 2029. It replaces Anthropic's first OneGov agreement, from August of last year. Anthropic says the discounts are estimated to save the government a billion dollars. That's Anthropic's own estimate, built on usage assumptions we can't see, so treat it as marketing arithmetic.

Why a discount, rather than a list cut? On Sunday, this desk argued that with noncancelable compute commitments, Anthropic's pricing power becomes close to existential. Its IPO filing is now public. A company in that position has every reason to protect its reference list price. The cleaner move is a discount confined to one buyer class. That buys twenty-seven months of federal distribution and lock-in, in a segment that's sticky and prestigious. And it doesn't touch the number commercial customers see. That reading is our inference, not anything Anthropic has said.

The competing read is simpler. Government is a low-churn, high-trust reference customer. On that view, this is cheap customer acquisition that would happen with or without an IPO on the calendar. Either way, the move carries its own risk. A public forty percent discount is a number large enterprise buyers can now cite in their own negotiations. That erodes the very list price the segmentation was meant to protect. And deep, long-dated federal discounts can normalize low-margin public-sector AI pricing, much as the early GSA cloud price wars did. Expect OpenAI and Google to be pressed to match.

So where is Google in this price war? Mostly sitting it out, by choice. Its one pricing move, the introductory-rate playbook on Gemini that we covered yesterday, launches cheap and resets later. Our research found no new Google list-price move since. Instead, Google's newest model, Gemini 4 Carbon, launched on Wednesday and went first to a small group of cyber defenders through its Fairwind program, with wider access promised later. So Google is competing on gated access and institutional trust, not on the price of a finished task. That means Google's most capable model isn't in developers' hands while OpenAI cuts the price of agent work. In this week's price war, Google ceded the developer front rather than lost it.

Now the business on the other side of that trade, the one selling the compute rather than the model. This is context, not breaking news: a single TechCrunch report from October 20th. SpaceX's renamed Super Intelligence unit bundles Grok, its neocloud business, and the newly acquired Cursor. TechCrunch reports it generated seven and a half billion dollars of revenue for the quarter. But strip that down. About a billion dollars of that top line came from a recovery in Bitcoin prices, not from AI activity. By our own arithmetic, that leaves roughly six and a half billion from operations.

The profit line needs the opposite adjustment. The unit reported four hundred thirty-one million dollars of profit. That came after one point two billion dollars of one-time stock compensation tied to the Cursor deal. Add that charge back, and profit was roughly one point six billion, by our arithmetic. TechCrunch doesn't say whether the Bitcoin income flowed through to profit. If it did, the underlying figure is closer to six hundred million. Either way, the unit is profitable before the one-time charge.

Be honest about what we can't see. The segment reports three businesses as one, with no breakdown. So we can't tell how much revenue is model usage, how much is subscriptions, and how much is compute sold to third parties. That third bucket may include Anthropic, which on Tuesday we noted is contracted to buy compute from SpaceX.

So what does this add to Tuesday's read on this desk, that distribution is commoditizing and compute is concentrating? A consistent data point, not a proof. The model labs are pushing the price of a finished task down. Meanwhile, the one Musk-side AI business reporting a profit owns one point seven gigawatts of compute plus captive distribution. But with no segment breakdown, we can't say whether that profit comes from the gigawatts, from Grok, or from Cursor. So compute-owner margin is a hypothesis, not a finding. The first disclosure that splits out the neocloud is the test.

So here's the scorecard, dimension by dimension. On inference pricing, OpenAI is pressing hardest, by its own numbers. On government distribution, Anthropic is buying federal lock-in rather than cutting its list. On access and trust, Google is gating its newest model to cyber defenders instead of joining the price fight. On compute ownership, SpaceX is building an independent base, though we can't yet see what it earns from it. On raw capability, there's no independently verified move this week, because Terra's gains are still self-reported.

Three things to watch. First, whether Anthropic or Google answer Terra's cost per task at the same tier, or an independent evaluator publishes its own cost-to-run figure. Second, whether OpenAI or Google match Anthropic's federal terms. Third, whether Moonshot delivers its promised open weights on November 4th, adding pressure on price from below.
