Private Credit & the Corporate Credit Cycle

Transcript · August 30, 2026

Good morning, it's August 30th, and this is your private credit briefing.

Fair warning up front. This is a structural read, not a news day. The freshest thing on my desk closed three weeks ago, and I'll tell you the vintage of each piece of evidence as it arrives.

This week's running finding has been simple. Every time an outside party got to price private credit, the price came back worse than the manager's own mark. A bankruptcy court did it on First Brands. And Cox Capital's tender for non-traded BDC shares did it, offering to buy at a twenty-six percent discount to net asset value and drawing under five million dollars of orders. Today the interesting move isn't another bad outside mark. It's capital arranging itself so there's no outside price to get in the first place.

Start in structured credit, a corner of this beat we haven't touched all week. US CLO issuance, broadly syndicated and middle-market combined, ran close to $230 billion in the first half of 2026. That's half-year structural data rather than a fresh print, and the analysis underneath it comes from a law firm's market update on the CLO fund business, a source describing the market it sells into. I'm holding it to the same standard this show has applied to everyone else's self-reported numbers.

The formation itself is not stress. That's capital arriving, and a lot of it. The interesting part sits in the economics underneath. Returns to CLO equity, the first-loss tranche, have thinned enough that managers are increasingly funding that equity themselves, through captive vehicles, rather than selling it to third-party arbitrage investors. Two named closings put dollars on that, both in April. Bain Capital raised roughly one and a half billion dollars for its third captive equity fund. CVC Credit raised a billion for its fourth vehicle, a 25 percent step up from its predecessor. Those are two data points, not a market-share number. Nobody has published what fraction of this year's CLO equity is captive versus third-party. So don't hear this as "most of the market." Hear it as a direction.

Steelman the benign read first, because the source material actually supports it. The 2025 and 2026 vintages show relatively higher implied arbitrage than the 2022 to 2024 cohorts, on a loan-versus-liability basis. And the thinner equity return is described as coming from loan price stability and tighter loan spreads, which compress the spread the equity tranche captures. That is a market working normally, not one flattered by hidden risk. Do not read compressing equity returns as a distress signal.

The narrower bearish point is this. When the buyer of the riskiest tranche is an affiliate of the manager setting its valuation, you have removed the one investor with an arm's-length incentive to argue about the mark. That's the same conflict the private equity beat found on Friday in single-asset continuation vehicles, where the sponsor controls the sale and the paper prices at or above par. Two different corners of private markets, one governance shortcut. Keep the risk in the family, and there's no outside price to dispute. Neither beat can tell you the marks are wrong. Both can tell you nobody independent is checking them.

Now, how would rising loan defaults actually show up inside a CLO? Through triple-C concentration limits and overcollateralization tests, which divert cash away from equity before any tranche takes a real loss. So the first visible stress is a distribution cut, not a downgrade. That's the same mechanic this show found in BDC dividend coverage on Tuesday. The payout breaks before the losses do.

Here's the caveat that matters. That mechanism is barely running yet. On the most recent read available, an April data cut covering nearly 1,800 US reinvesting broadly syndicated CLOs, only eleven deals had failed any overcollateralization test at all in 2026. That is six-tenths of one percent of the universe. And those failing deals carry about five points more triple-C collateral than the deals that are passing. So this is a live test on a small, concentrated set of deals with identifiably worse loans inside them. It is not broad transmission of loan-market stress into structured credit. Don't let anyone tell you the CLO market is already cracking. It's the mechanism to watch, not the finding to report.

Now to where the new capital is actually going. CoreWeave closed a $2.6 billion delayed-draw term loan on August 10th, arranged by JPMorgan and MUFG. It's rated Ba2 by Moody's and BB-plus by Fitch, priced at SOFR plus 550 basis points, with roughly a five-year maturity. That's three weeks old, and it's still the freshest AI-capex financing print available. Label it correctly, too. This is bank-syndicated leveraged loan paper, not private credit direct lending, and the distinction matters because it tells you who is winning the best AI-infrastructure credits. Banks are.

That price is useful as a benchmark. On Wednesday this beat covered Eagle Point's mezzanine tranche inside an Anthropic-anchored data center project, and flagged that the coupon on the subordinated layer was never disclosed. Now there's a comparable asset with a published number. Senior-ranked, rated bank paper on CoreWeave clears at 550 over. Whatever that undisclosed subordinated layer is paying, it has to clear materially wider than that, and nobody has told us by how much. The subordination premium in AI financing is still the one number the market will not print.

The other thing worth naming in that CoreWeave facility is the term structure. Roughly five-year money is funding customer contracts that average about three years. Give that the fair reading. Delayed-draw facilities amortize, and the underlying GPUs retain some residual value, so a gap between facility maturity and contract length isn't automatically a mismatch. The bearish version depends on GPU generations turning over faster than contracts renew, and that is an assumption, not a data point. But it does push the credit question out past the current GPU generation. When the refinancing decision arrives, residual value, not contracted cash flow, is what the lender is really lending against. And CoreWeave has now raised more than $30 billion in debt and equity this year alone. The market keeps extending duration on a premise nobody has tested through a downturn.

That leads to the closing thread. Banks aren't just winning the marquee AI mandates. They're pulling business out of the existing direct-lending book too. Bloomberg's credit desk reported in the week of August 8th that highly indebted borrowers are increasingly refinancing out of private credit loans into cheaper bank-market debt, as higher-for-longer rates make private credit's premium pricing harder to justify. Be honest about how thin that evidence is. It's a single sentence, with no spread deltas, no deal names and no volumes. Treat it as a hypothesis with a test attached, not a finding.

The hypothesis runs like this. If the best borrowers can walk to the bank market, what stays inside direct-lending books skews toward credits without that option. That's adverse selection, and nothing available today sizes it. But it would mean private credit is being squeezed at both ends at once. Banks take the large rated AI-adjacent credits, and banks take back the refinancings. So the marginal deal available to a record pile of dry powder is worse than the average deal already on the book, without the reported default rate moving to show it. That is the specific way this ends badly without ever looking bad in the quarterly numbers. The benign counter is just as real. Private credit took share back the last two times the syndicated market reopened and then shut again. This could be ordinary cyclical rotation rather than a one-way bleed. The way to tell them apart is whether the paper leaving is priced better or worse than the paper staying, and no one publishes that.

So where does that leave the cycle. In all three places we looked today, CLO equity, AI capex duration, and refinancing competition, the price of the risk being taken is either set by an affiliate, not yet disclosed, or not yet earned against an actual loss. That is a different problem from default frequency, and it's the one worth watching into the fall. Three near-term tests. Cliffwater's interval fund redemption window closes on Monday, August 31st. Third-quarter BDC non-accrual and payment-in-kind disclosures land with November earnings. And watch whether a genuinely third-party CLO equity deal prices at all in the next few weeks, or whether the captive buyer shows up again.
