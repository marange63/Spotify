All week we've told you to watch whether Bitcoin's new gold-like coupling holds through today's Fed decision. That decision is still pending as we go to air, so let's be precise about what we know and what we don't. The Fed's policy statement lands at 2pm Eastern this afternoon, and going in, markets had pushed the probability of a hike to 35.8 percent, up from 25.7 percent just a week earlier — a real repricing of hawkish risk, even before anyone knows the outcome. We are not going to tell you what the Fed did, because as of this recording it hasn't happened yet. What we can tell you is the regime Bitcoin now trades in, because that's the more durable story regardless of today's vote.

Here's the shift. Bitcoin's daily correlation with the S&P 500 fell to 0.12 in the second quarter, down from 0.58 in the fourth quarter of last year. Its correlation with gold, over the same window, climbed to 0.57, and with silver to 0.63. That's not noise — that's Bitcoin behaving less like a leveraged Nasdaq proxy and more like a macro, real-rate-sensitive asset. Bitcoin opened Tuesday at $63,706, down two and a half percent on the day and down 46.7 percent year over year, with Brent crude sliding from over $100 to around $96 by July 24 — oil is now part of the same hard-asset trade that's pulling Bitcoin's correlations around.

Here's the catch, though: decoupling from tech is not automatically bullish. It just swaps one master for another. If Bitcoin now moves with gold and real yields, then a hawkish surprise this afternoon — the kind that sends gold lower — could just as easily drag Bitcoin down with it. That's the opposite of the "digital gold, inflation hedge" pitch institutional allocators have been selling. A real-rate-sensitive asset that sells off alongside gold on a hawkish Fed is not an inflation hedge. It's a rates trade wearing a different costume.

Now, don't over-read the bull case sitting underneath this. Long-term holder supply is at a record share of circulating Bitcoin, but the accumulation is concentrated in small and mid-sized wallets — not whales. Wallets holding more than ten thousand Bitcoin remain broadly neutral, and the net accumulation pace, roughly fifty to a hundred thousand Bitcoin over the past month, is about a quarter of the four-hundred-thousand-Bitcoin pace seen during the November 2024 and May 2025 upswings. And yes, the current drawdown — somewhere between 42 and 50 percent depending on which all-time high you reference — is far shallower than 2022's 77.5 percent collapse or 2018's 84 percent crash. But shallow isn't the same as bottomed. As one analysis put it, this drawdown doesn't belong in that conversation yet. Not yet. The tape itself is orderly, not distressed: funding rates sit near neutral around 7.44 percent annualized, and thirty-day liquidations on the long side have fallen from $2.2 billion to about $1 billion. This is a repricing, not a leverage flush. So the honest read is that Bitcoin's near-term path now answers to the Fed and real yields, not to AI-stock sentiment — and today's statement, whenever it lands, is the live test of whether that holds.

Now shift from price to plumbing, because beneath Bitcoin, the rulebook that's supposed to govern all of this is being rewritten in real time — and it's in trouble. The CLARITY Act, the bill that would formally split SEC and CFTC jurisdiction over digital assets, is in a knife fight before the August recess. It's passed the House and the Senate Banking Committee, but a merged draft released around July 27 dropped an ethics provision that would have barred federal officials from issuing digital assets — a provision Senate Democrats had demanded. Three senators, Murphy, Van Hollen, and Merkley, have now formally opposed the bill over that omission, an escalation from earlier in the week, when Democratic support was still merely conditioned on that provision rather than withdrawn over it. The practical deadline is August 7th, the last scheduled Senate workday before recess, and the bill needs 60 votes against a Republican caucus of roughly 53 seats. That math doesn't work without Democratic votes, and three of them just walked. So-what: the single largest pending catalyst for U.S. market structure — the thing that would clarify which regulator governs which token — may not happen this cycle, pushing genuine clarity into the fourth quarter or into 2027, and leaving derivatives, staking, and custody products in the same legal gray zone they've occupied for years.

While Congress stalls, the market is voting with its feet, and it's voting for regulated dollar rails. Circle's USDC captured about 70 percent of adjusted stablecoin transaction volume in the first half of this year, versus roughly 25 percent for Tether's USDT — even though USDT still leads on raw market cap, $184.2 billion versus $73.4 billion. Circle also just picked up final approval from the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, on July 10th, to operate as a national trust bank. And it's not going uncontested: Stripe, Visa, and more than 140 other businesses announced a rival stablecoin, called Open USD, on June 30th, designed to share reserve yield with the merchants who adopt it — Circle's stock fell 13 percent on that news alone. Meanwhile Tether is on a clock. On the prevailing legal reading of the GENIUS Act — and the implementing rules actually missed their own July finalization deadline — foreign issuers get roughly two more years before U.S. institutions must stop using non-compliant stablecoins. Tether's own disclosures show about a quarter of USDT's reserves sit in non-cash, non-Treasury assets — precious metals, lending positions, bitcoin itself — exactly the exposure that compliance clock is aimed at. Tether has already hedged by launching a U.S.-compliant token, USAT, through Anchorage Digital. So-what: volume leadership is not the same as economic leadership. Reserve income tracks market cap, where Tether still more than doubles Circle. But the direction of travel — a bank-chartered issuer, a Stripe-and-Visa-backed challenger, and a legal deadline bearing down on the incumbent — all point toward consolidation around U.S.-regulated rails, whether or not Congress ever passes a bill to formalize it.

One more data point closes the loop tomorrow. Coinbase reports second-quarter earnings on July 30th, and consensus going in is for revenue around $1.31 billion, a 12.8 percent year-over-year decline, after a first-quarter miss and a $394 million net loss. That's the cleanest public-equity tell on whether any of this institutionalization is actually showing up in revenue during a bear market, or whether it's still mostly narrative. A second straight weak quarter would corroborate the volume story we've been describing; a beat driven by USDC settlement, derivatives, or international growth would support the adoption thesis even against falling prices.

So two threads stay live past this broadcast. Does the Fed, whenever it speaks this afternoon, confirm Bitcoin's new real-rate master or scramble it again. And does Washington actually ratify the rail consolidation already happening in the market, or does the CLARITY Act die on the Senate floor with the clock running out. Both resolve within days, not months.
