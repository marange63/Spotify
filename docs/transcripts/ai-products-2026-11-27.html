Good morning. It's Friday, November 27th, and this is your AI products briefing. One standing note: Anthropic makes the model behind this show, and one of its products comes up once today, as a callback.

This week's product news comes down to one question. Who pays for agent work, and how does the bill get rationed? Two moves meter it, one vendor gives it away, and a fourth shows where the consumer end is heading.

Start with OpenAI. Over the past week, OpenAI has rationed its newest capability by tier and by surface. GPT-6.2 reached ChatGPT Work and Codex before it reached the regular chat tab. When a chat-tab version did arrive, it went to paid tiers only. Now the gate is a different kind. It's about how much budget you have left.

TechCrunch reported on Wednesday that OpenAI has begun merging the Chat and Work sides of ChatGPT. An OpenAI spokesperson confirmed the mechanism directly. Chat and Work will share a single usage meter across ChatGPT. So everyday conversations and long agentic Work tasks now draw down one pool, not two separate allowances.

The rollout is narrow so far. Product lead Thibault Sottiaux said on X that OpenAI began introducing the update to a small group of Pro subscribers. TechCrunch adds that OpenAI plans to bring shared limits more broadly in December, per its support page. So the change has started, but it hasn't reached most subscribers yet.

What happens at the cap matters as much as the merge. The lightweight Instant model sits outside the meter. Per TechCrunch, conversations using Instant remain unlimited and don't count toward the shared allowance. So hitting the cap doesn't lock you out. It drops you to the cheaper model until your allowance resets. That's a quality downgrade, not a hard stop. It also tells you what OpenAI considers worth metering. That's everything above Instant, agentic Work included.

Who feels it, and why do it? The spokesperson said most users are not expected to face more frequent usage resets. But a small fraction of OpenAI's heaviest users may hit their limits sooner. No cap size or price change was published alongside the merge, so nothing says the allowance shrank overall. Three explanations fit. The first is unit economics: agentic and reasoning work costs more to serve, so it gets rationed. The second is simplicity, since one meter is easier to explain than two. The third is steering, because an unlimited Instant pulls marginal queries toward the cheapest model. OpenAI's only gloss is that it will keep improving efficiency, so subscribers get more out of their plans. The heavy-user concession tilts the evidence toward cost, without settling it.

Here's what changes in practice. From this point on, the implications I draw are our inference, not the vendors' claims. A Pro user who runs long Work tasks now spends the same allowance their chats use. So heavy users will start budgeting inside the subscription, deciding which jobs earn the stronger model and which can run on Instant. And if the shared cap binds, predictable high-volume work has a reason to move to metered API access, where the cost is known in advance. The headline is simple. At OpenAI, the gate moved from the tier to the meter.

The same budgeting question is reaching engineering teams. GitHub has announced how it will bill Copilot code review. In GitHub's words, those reviews will be billed to your organization starting December 30th, 2026. The notice gives no price, and it doesn't say how that compares with today's arrangement. What it does fix is the payer. AI code review becomes an organizational line item. On November 22nd, we argued that the IT admin is becoming the gatekeeper of what an AI tool does inside a company. That same admin will now be forecasting a review bill.

Anthropic's Claude Ship, which we covered on Wednesday, gets to the same place by a different route. It bills on tokens, with a credit grant up front, and we framed it as a challenger to Copilot's own review features. For an engineering lead, the upshot is the same either way. Agent code review is now something you forecast by volume, the way you forecast cloud spend.

Now the other side of that trade: a vendor giving agent work away instead of metering it. Cognition, the company behind the coding agent Devin, shipped SWE-2.1 on Tuesday as Devin's new default model. Cognition's post on X says SWE-2.1, like its predecessor, is trained with pure reinforcement learning on Kimi K3. The same post says SWE-2.1 is free for all Devin Pro, Max, and Teams for the next two weeks. Cognition also claims large gains over SWE-2 across a wide range of benchmarks, with no independent evaluation behind them yet. Separate reporting describes a faster variant, and a security variant with looser safeguards for defensive work.

The structural point is one Cognition doesn't make itself. Cognition doesn't just resell someone else's frontier model. It takes an outside base model, Kimi K3, and does its own post-training on top. Owning that post-training gives a product company a pricing lever that a pure reseller lacks. Giving away two weeks of its flagship model is that lever in use. It fits a point we made on November 23rd, that coding share has been moving on price, not on benchmark rank. Still, a two-week window is a promotion, not a pricing plan. It may be buying usage data and mindshare more than it signals durable economics.

For a team trialing Devin, that suggests a practical move. Log your real usage during the free window. That gives you your own baseline for pricing the workload once the promotion ends, whatever Cognition decides to charge.

There's also a supply wrinkle. The Kimi models come from Moonshot. On Thursday, we reported that Moonshot looks compute-constrained, after it reportedly walked away from a leased chip deal. So Devin's default model traces back to a Chinese lab that is short of capacity. A product built on someone else's base model inherits that base's supply risk. The takeaway on Cognition: owning the model lets a product company use price as a weapon, at least for now.

At the consumer end, the bet is distribution, not metering. Meta has put its Muse agent inside Instagram Direct Messages, rather than keeping it in a separate app, as MacRumors and others report. In a chat, you can share a post and ask Muse to act on it, such as adding a restaurant to an itinerary. Muse can also turn your saved posts into grocery lists or itineraries. And reportedly, it won't take an action without your explicit permission. The first wave reaches five countries, with the United Kingdom and Australia, Muse's two newest markets, left out for now.

That's a concrete workflow, not open-ended chat. The design choice is the point. The agent lives where people already send each other posts, so there is no new app to adopt. Meta hasn't published its own post on the integration, and a same-week rollout has no usage numbers yet. So file Muse as a distribution bet. Lower friction is the setup, and habitual use is the test still to come.

Three things to watch from here. First, whether OpenAI publishes a cap size, or widens the shared meter beyond that small group of Pro subscribers. Second, what Cognition charges once its two-week free window ends. Third, whether GitHub publishes per-review pricing before its December 30th billing date.

Across all of it, none of these vendors has published a stable list price for agent work. Procurement teams are budgeting for a resource whose unit cost is still being discovered. The practical response is to meter your own agent usage now, so you know the workload before the vendors name the price.
