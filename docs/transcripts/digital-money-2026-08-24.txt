Good morning. It's Monday, August 24th, and this is your digital money briefing.

Bitcoin rose 15.2% last week. It's still down 36.1% from where it traded a year ago, and the all-time high it set on October 6th, 2025 — $126,198 — remains far out of reach. This show has spent five straight days on the tape underneath that number: the Treasury buyback, the ETF inflow days, the leverage getting flushed out and back in. We're not doing that again today. The more interesting move isn't in the price at all.

Start with the collateral layer, because it's the one part of digital money compounding independent of what bitcoin does. We flagged this bridge yesterday and told you to hold the number loosely, because it came from a single secondary write-up. Today there's a fuller picture. Tokenized Treasury funds have grown 107% year over year, and the sector now holds somewhere around $15 billion — the figure that first circulated was $15.2 billion, and a primary industry dashboard pulled for this briefing puts it closer to $15.6 billion. Treat the exact number as approximate and moving. The growth rate is the part that isn't in question. Put that next to the cold open and you have the whole argument of this briefing: this asset class compounded at 107% a year over a stretch in which bitcoin fell 36%.

The bigger change is at the top of that market. Circle's USYC has overtaken BlackRock's BUIDL as the largest tokenized Treasury fund, at roughly $2.9 billion against roughly $2.6 billion, and that leadership swap now shows up on a primary dashboard, not just on the outlet that first reported it. Stay skeptical about what the swap actually means, though. Circle is the issuer of USDC, and USYC is the yield-bearing leg of its own settlement stack. Its growth may say more about Circle vertically integrating its own ecosystem than about outside institutions choosing a neutral rail.

The harder question is whether any of this is genuinely used as collateral, or just parked as a cash wrapper earning yield. There's now a real, if thinly sourced, answer. BlackRock's BUIDL can serve as collateral in decentralized lending, and the same analysis has it accepted as off-exchange derivatives collateral at major trading venues. Circle's USYC has been wired into Binance as off-exchange collateral, backing institutional derivatives positions rather than sitting idle. One secondary write-up carries all of that, and nobody has put a dollar figure on how much is actually pledged at any given moment, so don't overstate it. But a tokenized Treasury share functioning as posted margin is a categorically different claim than a fund simply growing its assets.

That upgrade matters for a risk we could only infer yesterday. If money-market fund shares are posted as crypto margin, the contagion path runs both ways. Stress in short-dated Treasury funds or in their redemption mechanics can now reach crypto margin calls, and a crypto liquidation cascade can reach back into fund redemptions. Unquantified, but no longer hypothetical.

One correction to the record, because it matters for how confident to be. The claim that Treasuries make up more than half of all tokenized real-world-asset value doesn't survive a second look. Two independent tallies put the share somewhere between roughly 42 and 47 percent, depending on which base you count against — closing in on half, not already past it. It's still the largest single category by a wide margin, so size it honestly in both directions. It dominates its own segment, and the segment itself is still small. Two funds hold about $5.5 billion of that $15 billion market. That's the same concentration pattern this show has flagged in ETF flows and in the stablecoin duopoly, one layer further down. Every part of digital money that institutionalizes seems to concentrate as it does it.

So what does the collateral layer actually buy the broader market? Not what the Treasury needs. From September 9th, the buyback operations run at a maximum of at least $4 billion apiece, double the old cap, aimed squarely at the long end. The on-chain bid quietly building underneath stablecoin reserves and tokenized Treasury funds doesn't reach that part of the curve at all. It buys bills, not thirty-years. It's real demand, and it's growing fast, but it sits at the wrong maturity to be the tailwind the long end actually needs. File it as a genuine but marginal offset, not a rescue.

If the collateral layer is growing, the float that funds speculation is moving the other way — not collapsing, but changing what it's for. Total stablecoin supply is up 14.3% year over year, at $308 billion as of the middle of this month. That same figure is 4.5% below where supply peaked back in May.

Meanwhile the venue where that float actually gets used as trading margin has thinned out hard. Binance's monthly futures volume fell from $2.55 trillion in July of last year to $1.40 trillion this July, nearly cut in half in twelve months. Some of that gets read as capital rotating out of crypto entirely. That's CryptoQuant's framing, not an established fact, and it deserves real skepticism, because supply also moves on routine redemption and reissuance. The more defensible read is compositional rather than directional. The dollar float backing this market is bigger than it was a year ago. The share of that float being used to lever up trading is shrinking fast. Stablecoins are drifting from margin collateral toward settlement infrastructure — a bullish signal if you think of them as payment rails, a worse one if you were using them as your liquidity gauge for everything else.

Growth in the instruments raises the obvious next question: who is actually licensed to run the rails they sit on? On August 11th, Abu Dhabi's Financial Services Regulatory Authority granted Coinbase a Financial Services Permission to arrange deals in investments and provide custody, specifically to launch tokenized securities fully backed by underlying shares, under that regulator's own supervision. That's not a narrative. It's a dated authorization in a primary regulatory announcement. No revenue, no timeline and no scale has been disclosed for the business it's meant to enable, so don't get ahead of the announcement. Coinbase stock has been rallying on exactly this kind of forward-looking optionality, even though the company's most recently reported quarter missed badly on both revenue and earnings — a valuation gap worth noting once and moving past, not re-litigating.

The part that should actually bother a US listener is where this happened. A US-listed exchange needed a Gulf regulator's permission to launch tokenized equities, in the same season the US framework for that activity is still a bill that hasn't passed. If that Abu Dhabi hub scales before Washington finishes anything, the US doesn't just lose a few months of lead time. It loses first claim on what the eventual standard for tokenized-equity settlement looks like, and inherits whatever gets built somewhere else instead.

Now the counterweight, at the other end of digital money's balance sheet. We told you back on the 19th that Strategy had gone eight straight weeks without buying a single bitcoin, redirecting cash toward defending its STRC preferred stock instead. There's now a number attached to how it did that defending: 1,690 bitcoin sold between August 3rd and August 9th, at an average price of $64,262 a coin, funding the repurchase of just over 1.15 million STRC preferred shares. Two caveats before you use that. Those figures come from secondary reporting rather than Strategy's own filing, and nobody has confirmed whether further sales happened after August 9th, so don't read it as an established cadence. And resist the easy "the flywheel is breaking" line: Strategy's equity was still trading above the value of its holdings at the time, so this was a choice about which liability to prioritize, not a forced margin call. It's still an uncomfortable data point. An instrument built to give investors levered upside in bitcoin now generates bitcoin supply the moment its own preferred trades below par. That's reflexivity running in a direction nobody who bought the pitch actually underwrote.

Put the two ends of this briefing next to each other. The unlevered collateral layer grew without selling a single asset. The levered treasury-company layer sold bitcoin to defend a liability. That's the honest shape of institutionalization in digital money right now: real, but concentrated; unlevered where it's growing, reflexive where it isn't; and still small enough that most of the numbers in this briefing are rounding errors against the markets they're supposed to be transforming. Which makes today's material evidence about where to look for institutionalization, not evidence that it has already arrived. The test worth actually watching isn't this week's price. It's whether tokenized Treasury assets keep compounding through a stretch where bitcoin itself is down 36% year over year. If they do, this is infrastructure. If they start tracking the tape instead, it was always just another beta wrapper with better branding.

That's the briefing for today. Talk to you tomorrow.
