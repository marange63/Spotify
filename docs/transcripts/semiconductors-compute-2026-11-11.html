Good morning. It's Wednesday, November 11th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

Nvidia's sell-off deepened on Tuesday, and today a congressional deadline lands on top of it. The trigger is the reporting that Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang had an affair with a woman now described as a suspected Chinese spy. What began as a story about one executive's private life is now a story about Nvidia's sales channel and its standing in Washington.

Start with the price. Per The Motley Fool, Nvidia closed Tuesday down 5.52 percent, at $168.80. Decrypt reports that at the afternoon low, the stock was down 18.42 percent for November and had given back all of its 2026 gains.

The peers moved the other way. AMD gained 1.3 percent, and Broadcom climbed nearly 4 percent. That makes two straight sessions in which Nvidia's closest challengers rose while Nvidia fell. We don't have a confirmed close for the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index. But on the names that matter most, the market is pricing something specific to Nvidia, not a derating of AI compute. Two up days against a falling leader also make rotation harder to dismiss. Part of Nvidia's cost may be showing up as its rivals' gains.

Then size the damage. Decrypt reports the two-day decline was on track to erase more than $470 billion in market value. That's an intraday estimate, not a closing figure. We don't have a China revenue number to set against it. But a loss of nearly half a trillion dollars is hard to square with a haircut to a China business that export rules had already capped. In our reading, the market is pricing something broader. It's pricing key-person risk. It's pricing an earnings call consumed by compliance questions. And it's pricing a Washington response that could reach beyond China policy.

The price moved because the substance moved toward the sales channel. The woman at the center of the reporting is identified as Alice Huang. Despite the shared surname, she is a different person from Nvidia's chief executive. Alice Huang has been affiliated with Megaspeed, a Singapore-based chip reseller. Megaspeed has been accused of helping restricted Chinese firms acquire Nvidia chips. On Tuesday, a Yahoo Finance headline said the alleged spy linked to Jensen Huang, quote, "aided $7.5 billion in China chip shipments." Treat that as reported, not confirmed. No filing or primary source backs it.

Jensen Huang and Nvidia also face a shareholder lawsuit. Per StockAnalysis, it claims investors were misled about his affair with a suspected spy. Its court and filing details aren't confirmed in today's reporting. Whatever its merits, securities litigation is a multi-year overhang. It runs regardless of how the board's inquiry ends.

The channel angle also reframes moves Nvidia made earlier. On Saturday, we reported Nvidia imposing resale conditions on cloud resellers and stepping up chip tracking in Southeast Asia. One reading was defensive: Nvidia protecting its export licences ahead of scrutiny. The darker reading is remediation, a company tightening a channel it already knew was under a microscope. The evidence in hand can't separate the two. Either way, that channel policing is now a record that investigators and plaintiffs will read.

Hold one caveat underneath all of this. Nvidia's board has announced an independent investigation into whether Jensen Huang was targeted by a foreign intelligence operation. The board says it has no reason to believe national security or company secrets were compromised. Nothing reported so far is a government finding that he or Nvidia broke export rules. So the board's line is the company's starting position, not a verdict. It's also the line a subpoena would test first.

And Washington has now put dates on this. House Foreign Affairs Chairman Brian Mast told ABC News he was prepared to subpoena Jensen Huang unless he responded by today, November 11th, to a request for a closed-door briefing. At research time, it was unresolved whether Huang had responded or a subpoena had been issued. Then there's the sequencing. Nvidia reports quarterly earnings on November 17th. The requested congressional briefing falls one day later, on November 18th.

That calendar is the real analytical story. It turns next Tuesday's earnings call into something close to a pre-hearing compliance statement. Management's language on governance and export controls will be read as testimony, whatever the operating numbers say. The second-order effect is on how the stock reacts. A strong print may not clear the discount, because the question actually being priced doesn't resolve until the following day.

It also weakens a counter-reading we offered on Monday. The argument was that if China chip policy is set by executive-level trade bargaining rather than by Congress, a governance scandal might not change a single licensing decision. A subpoena threat from the Foreign Affairs chairman, plus a briefing request timed for the day after earnings, is Congress trying to build exactly that lever for itself. That's our inference. If it works, the reach wouldn't stop at China. It would extend to how every US accelerator vendor sells through resellers across Asia, raising compliance costs on a track separate from the trade talks.

So where does that leave Nvidia competitively? Nothing reported touches the product. What's exposed is channel access, customer relationships across Asia, and political standing in Washington. All three now sit inside a legal and regulatory process with dates attached. That sharpens an incentive hyperscalers and labs already have, which is to qualify AMD and Broadcom as second sources.

But wanting a second source and getting one are different problems, and this is where Nvidia still holds ground. None of what follows is new this week. It's the constraint the governance story runs into. Accelerator supply is gated by advanced packaging and memory, and Nvidia has been buying position in both. Reuters reported that Nvidia is providing, quote, "billions of dollars in financing to expand CoWoS packaging capacity" at SPIL, the ASE Technology unit. TrendForce reports that packaging customers now prioritize securing deliveries over negotiating prices. In a seller's market like that, the incumbent with prepaid capacity holds the slots.

AMD is fighting for the same capacity. It has unveiled more than $10 billion of investment across Taiwan's chip industry. Lisa Su told Reuters that CoWoS and advanced packaging are, in her words, super important for AMD's high-performance computing. Memory is tighter still. Micron says it has already signed agreements covering the vast majority of its 2027 high-bandwidth memory supply. And in the reporting we have, AMD's rack-scale Helios system has no confirmed customer shipments yet, so qualification is still ahead of it, not behind it.

So the competitive ledger splits by dimension. Nvidia is losing ground on permission and process, and both run on a calendar. It is holding ground on supply, where its lead is prepaid packaging capacity. A buyer can sign a second-source hedge this quarter. That hedge only becomes deployed racks when packaging and memory slots open up.

The same reseller channel Washington is squeezing from one side is getting squeezed from Beijing's side too. China's commerce and science ministries have a draft export-control list covering 40 technologies, more than half of them in AI. Fourteen of those 40 would be banned from export outright, and the rest would be restricted. Comments close November 15th, so the final text isn't settled. So far, only a pre-export registration requirement is in force, and no enforcement case has been reported. The split tells you how hard the draft aims, not how hard it will bite.

Now look at where that lands. A reseller like Megaspeed sits in the gap between the two ecosystems. Washington is tightening what moves into China through intermediaries. Beijing is tightening what Chinese AI and chip-design know-how can move out. Any firm brokering between the two now faces paperwork and scrutiny on both legs of the trip. In our judgment, that intermediary layer is where the cost of doing business is rising fastest.

So here's the calendar that matters this week. Today, Mast's subpoena deadline. November 15th, Beijing's comment period closes. November 17th, Nvidia reports, and the market will be listening for compliance language as much as revenue. November 18th, the requested congressional briefing. The first and last dates tell us whether Congress actually builds its export-control lever. The fifteenth tells us whether Beijing's draft hardens or softens. And the seventeenth tells us whether Nvidia's numbers can cut through a governance overhang that the fundamentals alone haven't cleared.

That's the briefing for November 11th. Stay cautiously optimistic.
