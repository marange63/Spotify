Good morning, it's Thursday, August 27th, and this is your Digital Money briefing.

Start with a callback, because today this show gets to close a question it has spent four days failing to answer. On Sunday we named a specific, falsifiable test on Bitcoin's rally. Had the crowded short book simply been replaced by a crowded long one? We said we could not source the number that would settle it. Yesterday we called that a fourth straight day of failure. Today half of it is answered, and the answer cuts against the bullish read.

Bitcoin futures open interest fell to a five-month low of 587,584 BTC, down from 645,760 BTC on August 14th, while price ran from around $62,000 to over $80,000. That is the CoinDesk reading as of August 25th. Falling open interest into a rising price is the textbook signature of short-covering. It is not the signature of fresh leveraged conviction, and it is not the signature of spot accumulation. So the mechanism of August's rally was positioning unwinding rather than money arriving. That is a materially weaker story than "institutions are piling in," and a more fragile one, because a squeeze is finite by construction. It ends when the shorts are gone.

Two honesty notes before moving on. The first is provenance. A different provider, measuring a narrower window over the initial squeeze, reports a completely different absolute level of open interest. Both series fall, and both fall hard. But they are two separate measurements, not one number, and this show is not going to average them into a false precision. That is the same discipline we applied yesterday to three competing figures for a single liquidation session. The measurement problem on this beat is now a persistent feature, not a one-off.

The second note is what stayed open. Has open interest started rebuilding since the 25th, and where do funding rates sit right now? We commissioned a targeted follow-up search specifically for those two numbers. It came back empty. No dated, quotable figure from a named source. So we are dropping the claim, circulating elsewhere, that funding has stayed comfortably below double digits in annualized terms. We cannot stand behind it. The leverage-rebuild question stays open, and nobody should tell you otherwise on the strength of an unsourced number. Keep the trigger in view as well. This move started with a Treasury bond-buyback announcement, a bond-market policy event, not a crypto-native catalyst.

So if new longs did not buy this rally, who sold into it? There is a real, if imperfect, answer. Bitcoin's long-term holder cohort, meaning coins that have not moved in over a year, fell by roughly 356,000 BTC, about 2.9 percent, to 11.84 million BTC over a trailing 30-day window through mid-August. That is the first meaningful monthly drawdown in that cohort in months, against an average monthly change near zero over the prior year. Pair it with the falling open interest and the lead converts from a negative into a positive claim. The mechanical bid was short-covering, and the supply that met it was old coins moving.

Now be honest about the timing, because it is the weak point. That 30-day window largely predates the rally's final leg up through $80,000. It measures distribution before the move, not necessarily into it. And a Glassnode read from early July pointed the opposite way, toward long-term holders returning to net accumulation. Two snapshots in the same summer, opposite signs, and they cannot both describe the present. Treat that as genuine ambiguity rather than a settled trend. The second-order point survives either way. If old coins were already leaving before the squeeze, the float available to absorb the next leg is thinner than it looks, and that cuts in both directions.

Now the second gap this show has been chasing. Spot Bitcoin ETFs extended their inflow streak to seven trading days, adding $314.37 million on August 25th, pushing August's total to $3.03 billion. That is about $390 million short of October 2025's full-month total, with four trading sessions still to run. So the record framing is a projection, not a result. Note also that the comparison base keeps moving. Yesterday this show measured August against April's 2026 high; today's source measures it against October 2025. And these are aggregator figures, not issuer or Farside disclosures.

Here is the number we could not source until today. Year to date, this category is still net negative, but that deficit has more than halved, down to $2.26 billion. Put the two figures side by side and you have the real story. A fund category still $2.26 billion in the red for the year, even after its strongest month since last October, is repairing a drawdown. It is not compounding an accumulation regime. The record month and the negative year are the same fact seen from two different distances, and only one of them gets quoted. Category assets near $99 billion do not change that, because most of that total is price marking up units already outstanding.

One thing the flow data cannot do is establish sequencing. Whether creations chased the squeeze or helped drive it is not answerable from flow alone, and we are not going to assert an order of operations we cannot demonstrate.

There is also a correction to make, to our own correction. Yesterday this show retired its thesis that BlackRock's IBIT was steadily eating a larger share of ETF flows, because IBIT's share had fallen to 62 percent. On August 25th, IBIT took $284.4 million of that day's $314.37 million total. Call it ninety percent. Track the series across a single week: the mid-fifties, up into the eighties, back to the high seventies, down to 62 percent, and now near ninety. The honest read is not an escalating trend, and it is not mean reversion either. It is that one fund's share of daily flow carries very high day-to-day variance, and anyone reporting a single session's share as a trend is reading noise as signal. What does survive is the dominance itself, and the concentration risk that travels with it. A single redemption day at one wrapper will get reported as an institutional reversal.

So if the flow data cannot carry an institutionalization story on its own, what actually changed in digital money this month? It is regulatory, and it came from an agency rather than from Congress. On August 18th the SEC proposed Regulation Crypto Assets, its first bespoke federal offering regime for crypto investment contracts. It creates a startup exemption allowing up to $5 million raised over a rolling four-year period, with no financial statements required. Above that sits a two-tier fundraising exemption. Up to $20 million over twelve months with no audit assurance, and up to $75 million over twelve months requiring audited financials. Chairman Atkins framed it as giving issuers "bespoke pathways to raise capital in the U.S., while providing appropriate investor protections."

Two caveats, and then the argument. Our research could not retrieve the SEC's own release directly, so this rests on a law firm's summary plus the Atkins quote. Some other accounts describe a single $75 million threshold rather than the two tiers, so the fine structure is not settled from the primary text. And this is a proposal, not a final rule. The comment period runs 60 days from Federal Register publication, and nothing here governs issuance today.

The argument is what it does to the legislative calendar. This substantially anticipates the offering framework Congress has been trying to write into the CLARITY Act, whose first Senate procedural vote has already slipped to September 15th, with ethics language, law-enforcement provisions and stablecoin yield treatment all still unresolved. That cloture vote, even if it succeeds, is a first hurdle rather than passage. So a CLARITY delay in September is now worth less to issuers than it would have been a month ago, because the agency has already built a working alternative.

The trade-off is durability, and the market is currently pricing this as pure good news. A framework built by agency rulemaking stands up fast. It is also unwound just as fast by the next chair, in a way a statute is not. The locus of crypto market-structure rulemaking has shifted from Congress to an agency, and that is a weaker foundation than the headline suggests.

Put it together and August's digital-money story is thinner than the price chart. The rally's mechanism was short-covering, not new conviction. The visible institutional channel had a record month while remaining underwater on the year, and on the most recent session nine of every ten new dollars ran through a single fund. And the one durable structural change came not from flows and not from a bill, but from a regulator writing its own rules while Congress waits. Two things worth watching from here. Whether open interest and funding actually rebuild. And whether ETF creations survive the end of the covering. Neither is answered yet.
