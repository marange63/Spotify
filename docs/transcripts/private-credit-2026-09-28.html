Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Credit & the Corporate Credit Cycle

Transcript · September 28, 2026

Good morning, it's September 28th. This week's AI-credit news is a delay, not a default, and delay is exactly how this credit cycle keeps taking its losses.

Start with Project Jupiter. Oracle sent a force-majeure notice to Blue Owl's Stack Infrastructure unit over the New Mexico data-center campus being built for OpenAI. The notice cites delays in securing power to the site. Under the contract, securing that power is Oracle's job, not Blue Owl's or Stack's. Now the capital structure, because it matters. Roughly twenty banks lent about eighteen billion dollars to build this campus. That's bank syndicate debt, not private credit. Blue Owl's exposure is roughly three billion dollars of equity. That equity earns a lower rent during construction and steps up to a higher rent only after completion. So the notice extends the period in which Oracle pays the lower development-stage rent. Blue Owl still gets the higher rent for the full planned term, but its start slips. No principal is written down by the notice itself. The loss, if there is one, shows up as deferred time, not a missing dollar. That matters for marks. A model-based valuation is built to catch a missed payment, not a stretched timeline. So a loss like this can sit inside a fund's internal rate of return for quarters before it surfaces in a headline mark.

Now test the two competing readings, because neither fits everything that's known. The alarmist read says this is the AI-credit bubble cracking. That overstates it. No payment has been missed. Blue Owl says the notice does not change the financial commitments to the project. And Oracle's own statement says Project Jupiter "remains on our planned schedule." Hold that next to the notice itself. A company says nothing has changed, while filing paperwork to protect itself if something does. That looks less like a confirmed delay and more like a pre-emptive contractual hedge. There's a less charitable version, too. The clause is excusing a power obligation that is Oracle's own. So this could be a genuine physical constraint, or a tenant using the clause to pace commitments it now wants to slow. The reporting can't tell those apart, and neither can we.

But the complacent read doesn't survive the tape either. Oracle shares fell three and a half percent the day the reports hit, and Blue Owl closed down three point six. The debt tells the cleaner story. Last Sunday we reported that eighteen-billion-dollar Jupiter loan quoted at eighty-nine to ninety-one cents, with syndication stalled. Reporting on the notice now puts it below ninety cents on the dollar. We also noted then that the project had lost its gas pipeline request. Power was the soft spot before anyone filed a notice. So the market-priced stress here sits with the banks. Blue Owl's equity takes its hit through a deferred return that a model-based mark may not register at all.

That revises a call this show made last Sunday. We said unsold Oracle-linked paper on syndicate balance sheets would push AI lending toward private capital. This week shows the risk didn't migrate cleanly. The banks are still holding the Jupiter debt, now at a discount. And the private capital in the deal, Blue Owl's construction-phase equity, took the first contractual delay event. The risk is sitting at both layers at once. The practical consequence lands in the documents. On the next vintage of data-center deals, expect lenders and equity to ask much harder who carries power-availability risk. That's an implication, not repricing we've seen yet.

The same week, public equity investors ran their own test of AI-infrastructure risk. SB Energy, the SoftBank-backed developer building a large Ohio data-center campus for OpenAI, pushed its IPO from September to at least mid-to-late October. As the New York Times reported, it struggled to find enough buyers at its targeted valuation of fifty billion dollars or more. Be fair to the deal. A one-month slip is a pricing disagreement, not a financing failure, and the ask may simply have been rich. The press links it to Jupiter, but that's co-movement attributed to one cause, not proven contagion. Still, it carries a credit implication. If public equity won't fund AI infrastructure at the asked price, the funding gap doesn't close. It has to be filled by debt and private structures instead. So in one week, a bank loan behind one OpenAI-linked project traded down, and public buyers balked at another's equity. The implication to watch is that more of the marginal dollar in this buildout lands on lenders, including private credit. Less of it lands on public equity holders, who can simply walk away from a mispriced deal.

That time-for-loss trade isn't new to AI infrastructure. It's how the broader private credit book has been getting through this cycle all along. We've reported Fitch's record default rate, six point three percent on a trailing twelve-month basis, several times since last Sunday. The new detail is the mechanism. Across Fitch's trailing twelve months, payment deferrals and switches from cash interest to payment-in-kind made up forty-seven percent of the eighty-nine defaults it recorded. Extension and deferral aren't the exception in this market. They're close to half the machinery.

Proskauer's default index tells a similar story, with a wrinkle. Its second-quarter default rate came in at two and a half percent, a slight decline from the first quarter. A Proskauer partner said that decline reinforces the market's "resilience." Now look inside the headline. The rate fell for larger borrowers, and it fell for the smallest ones. But it rose for the middle bucket, companies with twenty-five to fifty million dollars of EBITDA, to three point four percent from three point one. That's one quarter and a small move, so it could be noise. And Proskauer and Fitch measure different loan universes over different windows, so don't read one as validating the other's level. But the direction rhymes. Last Sunday we flagged Fitch's mid-size cohort as the one where defaults jumped. That's now two gauges pointing at the same part of the market, borrowers too small to refinance publicly and too large to restructure quietly. A "resilience" headline sitting on a deteriorating middle is exactly the gap between marketing and mechanism this beat exists to flag.

Briefly, on sizing. Registered BDC portfolios have more than doubled over three years, to roughly five hundred sixteen billion dollars of debt at cost as of the first quarter. On that bigger base, non-accrual tranches at the ten largest BDCs reached three point nine five percent of debt at cost in the second quarter, up twenty basis points from the first. That's second-quarter data. It's also a different Morningstar cut from the sector-wide non-accrual share we cited yesterday, so don't read the two as one series. Third-quarter filings arrive in November.

So where does the cycle actually stand? There's no break. But losses across this market, in the corporate book and now in AI infrastructure, are increasingly being converted into delay rather than booked as realized loss. The biggest unresolved risk is that this duration risk doesn't show up in a model-based mark until something forces a test. And more of it now sits in AI-infrastructure exposure whose binding constraint, power, is one the lenders don't control. That's a different kind of risk than the corporate book usually carries. A sponsor can amend and extend a loan's maturity. But nobody at Blue Owl or in a bank syndicate can build a power substation faster by rewriting a covenant.

Watch three catalysts, without assuming their outcome. Blue Owl's Credit Income Corp tender closes September 30th, days after the Jupiter notice. It's the first live read on that manager's retail queue since the news. SB Energy's rescheduled IPO window lands in mid-to-late October, and it will show whether public equity comes back at a lower price or stays away. And alternative-manager earnings land in late October and November, when any commentary on Project Jupiter would first surface from Blue Owl itself.

That's the state of the credit machine. Talk soon.
