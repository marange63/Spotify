Good morning. It's Saturday, August 22nd, and this is your AI products briefing.

For the last week, this show has tracked capability and cost: what a model can do, and what it costs to run it. Today the variable that moves is different. It's who pays, and who distributes. Every item in today's briefing is a decision about money and access, not a demonstration of new capability.

Start with the lead, and a callback. On Friday, we told you OpenAI had uncapped free-tier text chat in ChatGPT, defaulting free users to a cheaper model, and we called it a bet that cheap inference could subsidize free usage without a subscription wall. Three days later, the revenue side of that bet is scheduled to arrive. On Monday, August 24th, OpenAI is due to begin showing ads inside ChatGPT across 31 European markets, including Germany, France, Spain, Italy, Poland and Scandinavia. That is a scheduled launch, not a shipped one, and nothing about how those ads actually perform in Europe can be known today. The market list and the date come from press reporting of OpenAI's announcement, not from a filed disclosure.

The interesting part isn't that OpenAI sells ads. It already does, in the US since February and in a string of other markets since. The interesting part is how European ads are allowed to target. Under GDPR, personalized targeting normally requires explicit consent, which is hard to get and easy to refuse. So OpenAI is targeting only on what's actually happening in the moment: the current conversation topic, approximate location, device type, time of day, and language. No persistent behavioral profile follows you between sessions. That's a live test of whether stated intent inside a conversation is a good enough substitute for the profile-based targeting the entire ad-tech industry was built on. It might be. Someone asking ChatGPT to plan a kitchen renovation is showing more real purchase intent in that sentence than most inferred ad profiles ever capture. If that clears at ChatGPT's scale, the read-across is to every assistant operating in the EU, and it undercuts the ad industry's founding claim that persistent profiles are what make targeting work at all.

Two things temper this. First, on the legal mechanism. Reporting indicates OpenAI is leaning on GDPR's legitimate-interest basis for this contextual targeting rather than an opt-in consent prompt, meaning European users aren't asked permission before these ads run. Hold that loosely: it comes from secondary reporting, not from OpenAI or a regulator directly. But if it holds, it's a more aggressive posture than it sounds, and it leaves OpenAI less room to retreat if a regulator tests it. Second, on the money. OpenAI did disclose pilot economics, just not recent ones. Press reporting in the spring put the US pilot above $100 million in annualized revenue within six weeks of its February launch, on OpenAI's own claim, and coverage of OpenAI's May self-serve Ads Manager launch put the advertiser count above 600 with an initial default maximum bid around $60 CPM. Those are US figures from March and May. Nothing has been disclosed about European performance, and nothing has updated the US numbers since. So the honest read is that OpenAI has shown its ad economics work somewhere, once, months ago. Expansion into Europe is a decision built on that, not new evidence it works there too. And if the European ads underperform, the pressure lands right back on free-tier quality or usage limits, days after OpenAI removed them.

One more detail worth banking. The ads land on the free and Go tiers; the ad-free lane in Europe is ChatGPT Plus, at roughly 23 euros a month, or Pro at roughly 229. Those prices, again, come from reporting rather than a price filing. With ads on the free tier, that subscription increasingly functions as a toll to avoid ads, not just an upgrade for more capability. That reframes what OpenAI is actually selling, and it quietly puts a ceiling on how intrusive the ads can get before they start cannibalizing the subscription.

Consumers pay with attention. Enterprises pay through a different channel entirely, and that's the other structural deal of this month. On August 13th, IBM and OpenAI announced a partnership embedding GPT-5.6 and Codex directly into IBM Consulting Advantage, IBM's client-facing consulting platform. IBM joins OpenAI's top partner tier and will train thousands of consultants to sell and deploy OpenAI's models. IBM's Andy Baldwin framed it this way: the challenge organizations face isn't access to AI technology, it's integrating it securely at scale. That's a striking thing for a partner to say, because it's also IBM describing exactly where it, not OpenAI, captures the margin. Read plainly, this deal tells you OpenAI can't close large, regulated-industry deployments on its own sales motion, so it's renting IBM's. It's the same trade this show covered on Tuesday when Stripe reportedly bought OpenRouter: the premium is being paid for access to buyers and billing rails, not for models. Bound it, though. No financial terms were disclosed, and no named client deployments exist yet. This is a framework agreement and a training commitment, not proof of shipped work.

A consulting engagement like that only becomes a running deployment if the agent can actually be governed inside a regulated workflow. That's where Google's enterprise agent stack has something the rest of this category has been missing. Google's Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform went generally available in late July, taking the whole governance layer live at once: an agent runtime that can run a task continuously for up to seven days, a memory bank, an agent identity system, a gateway, a registry, evaluation and observability. That's the standing state of Google's stack rather than breaking news. What makes it worth a slot today is the sourcing. For most of this past week, this show has closed by flagging that the load-bearing claims in AI products are vendor-reported, with no independent audit trail and no named customer attached. Google's launch post breaks that pattern twice. AT&T says its AI sales agents use the Agent Memory Bank to resume customer conversations by recalling prior interactions, in AT&T's own words moving from guessing to remembering, which is a quiet admission of how weak the prior generation of sales bots actually was. Best Buy describes using Agent Identity, a native permissions system, to apply least-privilege access and accountability to autonomous agents running inside its operations.

Bound that immediately. These are two curated quotes in Google's own launch post, not an independent survey, and neither one comes with a cost saving, a volume, or a deployment count. But two named enterprises describing production use of agent memory and agent identity is still more than this category has offered all week. So here's the so-what: the plumbing needed to let an agent run unsupervised inside a regulated business now has named users. That doesn't mean the category has broad adoption. It means the specific bottleneck holding pilots back from production finally has evidence it can be crossed. And it sets a bar the other clouds have to match, because whoever standardizes agent identity holds the same lock-in position the audit surface holds.

One brief item connects both halves. Anthropic's Cowork, previously a desktop-only tool for running multi-step agent tasks, finished rolling out to mobile and web for all paid subscribers on August 18th. The architectural change is that tasks now run on Anthropic's servers, so a session started on your laptop in the morning continues if you check it from your phone at lunch without losing progress. That's the same bet AT&T is making with agent memory: an agent as a running job, not a single session. Keep the limitation plain, though. Local file access and browser control stay desktop-only, so what actually travels to your phone is the ability to approve and review, not to do the underlying work. It's a notification surface with a good story attached, not yet a mobile agent. No usage figures have been disclosed since the rollout finished, and the sourcing is secondary reporting rather than Anthropic's own release notes. The open question underneath it is economic: always-on server-side sessions turn bursty compute into sustained compute, which is a different margin profile for a flat-price subscription.

So step back. For a week, the constraint on this show was capability, then cost, then consent. This week it's monetization and channel. OpenAI is closing its consumer loop with ads behind the free tier it just uncapped, and buying enterprise reach it can't generate on its own through IBM. Google is answering with the governance plumbing that turns a sales pitch into something a regulated business will actually run. None of it is a new capability. All of it is about who ends up paying, and who controls the door.

That's the briefing. Thanks for listening, and I'll see you next time.
