Strategic Power, Energy, and Institutional Change

Transcript · November 22, 2026

Good morning. It's Sunday, November 22nd, 2026, and this is Strategic Power.

Washington just pushed its chip chokepoint down into the foundry, and then a senior official called the job finished. At home, the AI build is finding money faster than it can find megawatts. Capital and controls are the easy inputs. Electrons are the binding one.

Start with the chokepoint. Commerce's remote-access rule goes after Chinese buyers who rent advanced compute in overseas data centers, rather than buying the chips outright. It took effect on publication, on November 19th. One sourcing note: we're working from law-firm and trade-press summaries, not the Federal Register text. What's new is that the same rule also reaches into the foundry.

The rule strengthens an existing foundry due-diligence requirement. Foundries like TSMC and Samsung must presume that any advanced logic chip is a controlled item, unless they can show otherwise. They also lose the ability to rely on a customer's own attestation that a chip falls below the transistor-count threshold. And the rule removes an exemption that used to cover non-AI chips, including smartphone processors. That last change is why ordinary consumer silicon is now caught in a fight that began over AI accelerators.

Per FT and Reuters reporting, TSMC has told Chinese chip designers to halt orders on 16-nanometer and more advanced processes. The exception is customers who obtain a US export license. The fallback is generally limited to older 28 and 40-nanometer nodes. Even those qualify only if the chips skip high-bandwidth memory and advanced packaging. Named customers include Xiaomi, Oppo and Vivo at TSMC. At Samsung, they include Baidu's chip unit Kunlunxin, and ByteDance. Neither foundry has confirmed the pause on the record.

The market didn't wait for confirmation. TSMC shares fell more than 4 percent on the day the tightened foundry checks landed. That's a one-day move, but it's the first price anyone has put on this channel. Taiwan's own legislature, meanwhile, is reviewing tougher criminal penalties for illegal exports of critical chip materials and equipment to China. That's a committee review, not law. But it means TSMC may soon answer to two export regimes at once.

So US-China tension now has a transmission channel into semiconductor equities that runs through Taiwan and Korea, not just through Nvidia. Those foundries are no longer only suppliers. They're compliance nodes.

Now the part that actually breaks a pattern. For the past month, every move on chips has been a step up. First came the remote-access rule. Then came chip amendments attached to the defense bill. Then came House committee records showing Commerce had approved hundreds of thousands of AI chips for Chinese companies. On our read, that record made legislation likelier. On November 21st, Treasury Secretary Bessent gave the first official signal of a ceiling. Speaking to CNBC after returning from Asia with President Trump, he said, quote, "The proper chip restrictions are in place." He added, quote, "the remote access has been curtailed." That's Treasury calling the current rule the stopping point, not a first step.

Treat that ceiling skeptically. It's a statement of view, not a policy action. And the administration is split on the record. Axios reported that President Trump backed tougher Nvidia-related China controls three times, each time stalled by Commerce Secretary Lutnick. That report dates from November 11th, ten days before Bessent spoke, so the president's view may have moved. Either way, Congress isn't bound by it. The chip amendments riding on the December defense bill are the legislature's own vehicle.

The easy market read is that escalation risk is now capped, which is good news for AI capex. That's only half right. If Treasury's ceiling holds, the near-term policy tail from Commerce narrows. But the risk moves to Congress in December, and to Beijing in January. So the ceiling belongs to Treasury, not to Washington. The legislative branch is the likelier source of the next move.

A ceiling on chips has a rough counterpart in minerals. China has already extended its suspension of its expanded rare-earth export controls into January. What's new is flow data on how the truce is actually working. China's rare-earth exports fell almost 10 percent year on year from January through September.

September alone tells a different story. Exports that month rose 6.7 percent year on year. One month is not a trend, and the year-to-date number is still down. But it's the first hint that the truce is producing marginal relief rather than a frozen standoff. There's a bigger limit, though. Aggregate tonnage doesn't isolate dysprosium and terbium. Those are the heavy rare earths that defense magnets depend on. They remain under China's licensing regime, which was never suspended.

Here's our inference. A capped chip regime in Washington and a modestly loosening mineral regime in Beijing look like two halves of one managed detente. But it's reversible on Beijing's schedule, because the heavy rare-earth lever is fully intact. For markets, the names that move most on January are ex-China producers like MP Materials and USA Rare Earth. Our read is that they would rally if Beijing re-tightens, because they are the alternative supply. A clean renewal of the truce would likely take some of that premium back out.

Those are the levers abroad. At home, the constraint is power, and first the money meant to buy it. Oracle filed an 8-K on November 20th that caps its additional capital-markets fundraising at 20 billion dollars, for its investment cycle through fiscal 2030.

That cap is expected to split evenly between equity and gross debt. It sits on top of a separate 20 billion dollar equity raise completed earlier this fiscal year. That's a ceiling on new raises, not a commitment to spend.

The more revealing line is about who else pays. The filing says, quote, "Vendor financing, customer-provided hardware and customer prepayments are expected to reduce upfront cash requirements." Whatever the public messaging about being fully funded, the filing itself is narrower. Oracle isn't carrying the build alone. It's leaning on suppliers and customers to carry part of it. That relocates risk onto balance sheets that aren't Oracle's.

That relocation has a cost. The Fed's October minutes, released this week, warned that AI-related debt exposures were "becoming increasingly concentrated among a small number of firms." Vendor financing and customer prepayments don't disperse that exposure. They bind the supplier, the builder and the tenant more tightly together. So the funding problem is being solved by deepening the very concentration the Fed flagged.

Money, though, is the easy input. Megawatts are the hard one. On power, the latest hard data point is still PJM's capacity auction, with results released in July. It cleared at the price cap of 325 dollars per megawatt-day. Even at the cap, it landed about 6.8 gigawatts short of PJM's reserve-margin target.

The cap also hides how severe that shortage is. PJM ran its own simulation of the auction without the cap. On our arithmetic from that simulation, the cap held the auction's cost about 13 billion dollars below where it would otherwise have cleared. That's 13 billion dollars of scarcity signal the price never sent.

The cap protects ratepayers, which is a fair political choice. But it mutes exactly the investment signal that would close the gap. And the queue won't close it soon. PJM's fast-energization filing, its plan to connect large loads faster, says the first cycle wouldn't finish until 2029.

The second-order effect is our inference. A capacity price held at the cap keeps revenue firm for independent power producers already in the footprint, names like Constellation, Vistra and Talen. But a capped price mutes the case for building new plants. That pushes large loads toward self-supply and behind-the-meter generation. It also keeps political pressure on ratepayer bills. So when a hyperscaler says its next campus is fully financed, remember that financing and interconnection are separate problems. Only one of them is solved.

The contrast that lands the point is Palisades, Holtec's nuclear plant in Michigan. Holtec's own securities filing says nuclear fuel loading has, quote, "commenced," while restart preparations continue. That's a physical step toward an operating asset, not a press release. But the same filing says final power operations are subject to compliance with technical specifications. So there's no confirmed restart date. Announcements and funding caps are abundant this month. Operating capacity is scarce, and that scarcity sets the pace of the AI build.

Pulling the thread together. Chip controls are capped, at least by Treasury's account. Rare-earth flows are in a managed, reversible truce. Capital is abundant, and increasingly shared across the supply chain. Electrons are not. Three dates to watch. First, the defense-bill chip amendments as they head to the floor in December. Second, PJM's next capacity auction, whose window opens December 9th. Third, January 10th, when Beijing's suspension of those rare-earth export controls is reported to come up for renewal.
