Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Credit & the Corporate Credit Cycle

Transcript · July 25, 2026

Good morning. It's Saturday, July 25th, and this is your private credit and corporate credit cycle briefing.

For most of this week, this show has asked whether private credit's marks are honest — secondary discounts, distressed exchanges, the question of whether reported values reflect reality. Today the tell isn't in the marks. It's in the money. Investors are trying to leave these funds, and for the second quarter running, the funds won't fully let them out.

Start with the numbers. Blue Owl's flagship non-traded BDC, Credit Income Corp, holds roughly 34 billion dollars in assets. Investors asked to pull 3.6 billion dollars of it in the second quarter — 18.8% of shares. That's actually down from 4.2 billion in the first quarter, but it's still the second straight quarter the fund has hit its cap. A sister fund, Blue Owl Technology Income, took requests for 38.1% of its shares. Apollo's Debt Solutions BDC saw 15% of shares tendered. Ares Strategic Income jumped to 14%, up from 11.6% the quarter before. Blackstone's BCRED climbed to 12%, up from 7.9%. Every one of these funds — Blue Owl, Apollo, Ares, Blackstone, and BlackRock's vehicles alongside them — hit the same standard 5% quarterly redemption cap.

That cap isn't new; these vehicles always carried it. What's new is that requests keep blowing through it, quarter after quarter, against a backdrop of defaults grinding higher across the asset class. Blue Owl says its flagship fund doesn't need to sell a single private loan to fund the tender. Take that as management's claim, not an audited fact — it's exactly what a stressed vehicle would say, and the only real test of it is a forced sale that hasn't happened yet. Bank of America expects this quarter to mark the peak in redemption pressure. Hold that loosely too; it's a forecast, not a turn you can see in the data. So the retail and evergreen private-credit growth engine, the one built on promising periodic liquidity to a widening pool of investors, is now routinely turning people away. Twice running. That's the structural liquidity mismatch critics warned about, showing up in the data instead of the theory.

And it has a second-order cost the headlines miss. The whole bull case for these vehicles was distribution — the wealth-channel and 401(k) push that was supposed to widen the buyer base for years and compound assets under management along with it. If gating runs into a third quarter, the wirehouses and advisor platforms that sell these funds start throttling new allocations. The AUM-growth story that underwrites the managers' own equity valuations slows with it. That's the mechanism by which a fund-level liquidity problem becomes a franchise-level growth problem.

If this were only about bad loans, it would stay contained to credit funds. It didn't. Partners Group, a major private markets manager, just gated its flagship evergreen private equity fund — 8.6 billion dollars in assets — after redemption requests hit 9.8% of net asset value against the same 5% cap. A second fund from the same manager, 15.8 billion dollars in size, is facing requests near 6%. Three more funds worth a combined 9.7 billion dollars are tracking between three and a half and five percent — all in or near cap territory. Partners Group's CEO said it plainly: redemption pressure that had been concentrated in private credit for several quarters is now migrating into private equity.

Those figures come from secondary reporting on a Bloomberg account, not a primary filing, so hold them loosely on precision. And there's a less alarming reading worth keeping on the table. This could be the same investors rotating out of Partners Group's own illiquid credit book, not fresh liquidity stress in private equity broadly. The migration story and the rebalancing story both fit what we can see, and this pass couldn't cleanly separate them. But either way, the direction matters more than the decimal. It's not purely a private-credit underwriting problem. It's a redeemable-vehicle structure problem, and investor behavior is the common thread running through both asset classes.

Now flip to the other side of the ledger, because while investors are trying to get out, lenders are raising more money than ever and putting less of it to work. US direct-lending deal volume fell by roughly 55% quarter over quarter in the second quarter — down to somewhere around 33 billion dollars from about 75 billion in the first quarter, the weakest quarter for lending activity since 2023. Two independent secondary sources agree on that direction and rough magnitude, though the exact decimal varies between them, so take it as roughly 33 billion, not a precise print. Deal-backed lending tied to private equity buyouts and LBOs fell sharply too.

At the very same time, fundraising for North America-focused direct-lending funds jumped to roughly 16 billion dollars in the second quarter, up from just 1.3 billion in the first — the strongest fundraising quarter in two years. That's a genuine fork, not a verdict. It could mean lenders are being prudently selective given the redemption and default stress building elsewhere. Or it could mean deployable capital is now outrunning bankable deal flow — a classic late-cycle imbalance. If it's the latter, that dry powder eventually chases scarcer deals, which means tighter spreads and looser covenants, the opposite of the discipline this moment calls for. And covenant erosion is exactly the kind of damage you only see later, once the credits go bad. Which read wins depends on where that capital goes next.

The allocators funding this machine are already voting. A recent survey of institutional limited partners found 70% expect diversification away from core direct lending to be the dominant private-credit trend over the next year, and only 6% plan to add to mid and upper-mid-market direct lending specifically — even as private credit overall has grown to 18 to 20% of these investors' private-markets portfolios, up from under 10% in 2022. The same survey names what's pushing them out. Seventy percent point to competition compressing spreads and terms in direct lending. Fifty-eight percent flag portfolio problems or outright fraud from weak underwriting. That's the asset class's own backers saying the compensation no longer matches the risk.

Treat it as a single manager-commissioned survey, not a hard flow number. But it lines up with everything else this week: the money isn't leaving the asset class, it's leaving the strategy that built it, headed instead toward asset-based finance and special situations. So the growth doesn't stop — it migrates, and that's a different risk profile than a direct-lending book quietly winding down.

One thing to watch. The parent companies behind these funds — Blue Owl, Ares, Blackstone — haven't yet reported second-quarter earnings. When they do, that's the first chance to hear management address the gating directly. It's also the setting in which sustained redemptions could force a manager toward secondary loan sales or a GP-led liquidity solution at a discount to net asset value. That is the moment that would test whether the self-reported "no loans need to be sold" claim, and the marks behind it, survive contact with a real clearing price. That's still the question this whole week has circled: whether the marks hold up under real pressure. This week, the pressure just found a second front.

That's the briefing for today. Thanks for listening, and I'll see you next time.
