Good morning, it's Friday, August 14th, and this is Frontier AI Lab Competition.

Start this week with Google, because the same company just handed us evidence on both sides of the "who's winning" question in the space of a few days. On August 5th, Google announced the most senior leadership turnover any frontier lab has seen this year. Demis Hassabis is stepping back from running Google DeepMind day to day, moving up to Chair of GDM and Chief Scientist of Alphabet, while continuing to lead Isomorphic Labs. Koray Kavukcuoglu, previously GDM's CTO, steps into the operating seat as SVP of Google DeepMind, now reporting straight to Sundar Pichai and running Gemini model development, frontier research, and the app and developer teams. That's the promotion side of the story, and it's confirmed directly by Google's own blog post.

The other side is an exit. Jeff Dean, Google's chief scientist for 27 years, is leaving to co-found an independent public-benefit company called Discovery Loop, aimed at automating machine-learning and scientific research. He's not going alone. Google's own blog names Sanjay Ghemawat as leaving alongside him, and Discovery Loop's lead investor, Radical Ventures, names all four co-founders in its own investment announcement: Dean, Ghemawat, and also Oriol Vinyals and Quoc Le, two more senior DeepMind research figures, Vinyals a VP of Research and deep-learning team lead, Le a founding member of Google Brain. That's a confirmed four-person senior-researcher exodus, not just Bloomberg color. Google itself is staying close to it, calling itself a founding investor and cloud partner. What isn't confirmed is any number attached to broader attrition. Reporting points to other named departures to Anthropic and OpenAI, but no verbatim, citable figure exists for how many researchers have actually left, so treat the "wave of departures" framing as directional, not counted. And whether this reshuffle is Google getting ahead of a talent problem or reacting to one is genuinely a matter of interpretation across outlets, not something Google has characterized itself. Bloomberg separately reports Google is physically consolidating AI leadership in Mountain View, which reads as urgency either way. So what this tells you: on the research-talent and velocity axis, competitive pressure from Anthropic and OpenAI is now visibly reshaping Google's org chart, whatever the motive.

Now the counterpoint, from the same company, the same week. Sundar Pichai announced the standalone Gemini app has crossed 1 billion monthly active users, the 14th Google product to hit that scale. Gemini went from a few hundred million users a year ago to over 950 million by July, then through a billion in August, according to Google. Google also says sixty-three percent of usage is now voice-based. That puts Gemini about two months behind ChatGPT, which Google says reached 1 billion in June. Two caveats worth holding onto: this is a Google-disclosed number via Pichai, not an audited figure, and MAU definitions across companies aren't necessarily comparable. It's also just the standalone app, so it understates Google's total AI reach through Search and Workspace, but don't conflate it with those either. Still, the takeaway is real: research-bench strength and consumer distribution are not the same competitive axis, and Google can win the second even while visibly straining on the first. Android, Search, and Workspace bundling are doing distribution work that doesn't depend on who's running the research org this month.

Shift now from the talent front to the pricing front, where the news cuts against the running assumption that Chinese labs just keep getting cheaper. DeepSeek moved its V4-Pro model out of preview into general availability on August 13th as V4-Pro-0813, built for agentic tool use with a million-token context window. Independent evaluator Artificial Analysis scored it 53 on its Intelligence Index, roughly ten points behind the frontier cluster: Claude Opus 5 at 63, Claude Fable 5 at 62, GPT-5.6 Sol and Grok 4.6 tied at 61. So DeepSeek is still trailing at the top of the leaderboard. But the real story is what happens three days later. Effective midnight Beijing time on August 17th, DeepSeek is raising its peak-hour cache-hit input pricing from 0.025 to 0.30 renminbi per million tokens, a twelve-fold increase, with smaller but still substantial hikes across other token types off-peak. DeepSeek hasn't explained why. The most plausible read, and it is inference, not a company statement, is that compute costs or margin pressure are catching up with even the most aggressive low-cost lab. That doesn't mean DeepSeek is retreating from the open, cheap strategy. It means the floor under how cheap "cheap" can stay just moved. So what: if this holds, the calculus of routing workloads to Chinese open models purely on cost starts to weaken as the price gap to Western frontier APIs narrows.

The same pricing axis got a second entrant this week. SpaceXAI, the merged xAI-SpaceX entity, shipped Grok 4.6 on August 13th, an iteration on the same underlying model rather than a new scale-up. Artificial Analysis scored it 61, tying GPT-5.6 Sol, at roughly two dollars and six dollars per million input and output tokens, more than sixty percent cheaper than Claude Opus 5's five and twenty-five. Musk called it objectively number one on intelligence, speed, and cost combined; that's a vendor claim, not an independent finding. What is notable is where the reaction showed up: SpaceX's now-public stock jumped as much as 11% intraday on the release before giving some of that back, meaning the net move isn't settled. But a model release moving a launch company's share price at all tells you AI is now a market-priced revenue line for SpaceX, not a side project, whatever Musk's separate and unverified claim that AI revenue could rival core space revenue by year-end. Put together with DeepSeek, Grok 4.6 means Anthropic and OpenAI now face convergent margin pressure from two directions at once, even as the top of the leaderboard stays remarkably clustered between 61 and 63.

Close on the resource question underneath all of this: power, not just chips. Riot Platforms, a bitcoin miner pivoting to AI infrastructure, disclosed a 20-year, 9.1-billion-dollar lease, extendable to 16.1 billion, for 191 megawatts of capacity at its Rockdale, Texas site, running through June 2048. Riot named the tenant only as "a leading frontier AI lab," but Bloomberg identified it as Anthropic. Capacity comes online in stages, 96 megawatts by December 2027, the full 191 by June 2028. This is a new financing channel layered onto the coalition-and-lease structures this show has already tracked this week, Nvidia's capital coalition, the Theseus infrastructure deal, and Broadcom-backstopped chip-lease debt. What's genuinely unresolved is whether this 9.1 billion sits inside or on top of Anthropic's previously reported compute-commitment estimates; nobody has published a reconciled total, so don't assume you can just add it up. What is clear is the pattern: labs are now locking up grid-connected power a decade or more in advance, the same way they lock up chip supply, and every new fixed, long-duration commitment raises the stakes if AI revenue growth ever decelerates faster than the payments keep coming due.

That's the state of play: Google trading research depth for distribution scale in real time, the China price war hitting its first real ceiling, and power becoming as contested and as pre-committed as compute itself. That's Frontier AI Lab Competition for August 14th. Talk to you next time.
