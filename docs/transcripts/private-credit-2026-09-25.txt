Good morning, it's Friday, September twenty-fifth, and this is your private credit briefing.

The theme today is a split screen. The retail queue for private credit is shrinking, not clearing. Institutional money, meanwhile, keeps arriving with no queue at all. That divergence is the story.

Start with Apollo Debt Solutions, Apollo's flagship retail business development company, with about twenty-six billion dollars in total assets. On Tuesday, Apollo told investors it will cap redemptions at the standard five percent of shares for a third straight quarter. This time, fourteen point seven percent of shares sought to exit. That is down from sixteen point eight percent the quarter before. Apollo's letter says it estimates that the vast majority of this quarter's requests are investors re-tendering unfulfilled requests from prior quarters. In other words, Apollo says this is mostly old money still trying to leave, not new money heading for the door. That is Apollo's own estimate, not an independently verified count. Treat it as the interested party's read of a number that looks bad at face value.

The underlying SEC filing shows how Apollo built its other headline number. The letter says investors who sought liquidity this year will have received an estimated seventy-five percent of their requested capital. The filing states that the figure assumes first-quarter requesters re-tendered the unfulfilled balance in both the second and third quarters. If some of those first-quarter holders gave up, or came back for different amounts, the denominator moves. Apollo doesn't say by how much. We also still can't pin down the first-quarter request level from a primary source. That means we can't back out how large the trapped cohort really is. So the seventy-five percent is what a model says a re-tendering holder would receive, not a count of what real investors got back.

The filing also gives us the first hard flow number for the quarter. Net of about two hundred million dollars of new subscriptions, Apollo expects roughly five hundred million dollars of outflows this quarter, about three percent of net asset value. Note the yardstick. Net asset value is smaller than the twenty-six billion of total assets, so the percentages don't map onto that headline size. That three percent is almost exactly the net outflow Blackstone disclosed for BCRED earlier this month. So two of the largest vehicles in the channel are draining at the same slow rate. That's a real drain, but it isn't a run. Sustained, though, three percent of NAV a quarter eventually forces a manager to sell its most liquid, best-performing loans first. That quietly dilutes the yield left for everyone who stays.

Credit quality inside Apollo's book is not the trigger. As of June thirtieth, non-accruals were just eight-tenths of a percent of cost. The cap reflects a liquidity mismatch. An evergreen fund offers quarterly windows, and the loans inside it can't be sold on demand.

Now the callback. On Sunday we argued that request rates at North Haven, Cliffwater and BCRED had flattened because they were converging on a stock of previously gated holders, not a fresh wave of fear. Apollo's letter is the first time a manager has said exactly that in its own words. So the falling request rate, from sixteen point eight to fourteen point seven percent, means the queue is draining at the pace the cap allows. It does not mean sentiment has turned.

That re-tender framing suits Apollo, but it cuts both ways. If it's true, new exit demand is contained, and a binding five percent cap is simply the structure working as documented. A capped tender is not a default, and it is not a run. But the same framing means the same holders have now been told no for three straight quarters. And roughly a seventh of the fund's shares are still asking to leave. That is not what a healthy vehicle looks like, even when nothing is technically broken.

The second-order effect lands on distribution. These semi-liquid wrappers are becoming a two-speed system. Capital enters at NAV but exits in five percent slices. That makes the wealth-channel and 401(k) push harder to scale on current terms. It also strengthens the case for closed-end or listed structures when managers sell private credit to individuals. One caution before extrapolating. We could not confirm whether Ares, HPS, KKR or other large non-traded BDCs capped this quarter. So Apollo is a data point, not yet a sector-wide print.

So what are those trapped holders actually sitting on while they wait? That takes us back to the other open question from Sunday's briefing. Bloomberg reported that private credit defaults are one percent, six percent or nineteen percent, depending on who's counting. On Sunday we couldn't say which measure produces the nineteen. Now we can. It's PIMCO, and PIMCO's methodology includes debt serviced by payment-in-kind, where interest is paid with more debt rather than cash. This is not a new default print. Fitch's six point three percent sits in the middle. That is a record for Fitch's trailing twelve-month default rate across about thirteen hundred private borrowers. Houlihan Lokey sits below one percent. Their universes and time windows differ, so the three numbers are not strictly comparable. And don't assume the highest number is the honest one. Some PIK is negotiated at origination, not toggled under stress, so counting all of it as default overstates distress. The honest reading is that the one-to-nineteen spread mostly measures how much stress is being deferred through extensions and PIK. It does not measure realized losses. Here's the connection to Apollo. The holders stuck in these redemption queues own a slice of that deferral, at a net asset value no real downturn has tested.

Now the other side of the ledger, the money that can't leave even if it wanted to. About two weeks ago, on September tenth and eleventh, two European direct-lending raises showed capital still arriving at scale. ICG is targeting fifteen billion euros for its sixth senior direct-lending fund. That roughly matches the fifteen point two billion euros its predecessor raised in 2024. That predecessor itself blew past an original target of ten to twelve billion. Separately, Arini has secured more than three billion dollars toward a roughly four-billion-dollar debut fund, aiming to close before year-end. Remember that one is a target and the other is capital secured toward a target. Neither is a final close. These are closed-end vehicles. There's no quarterly redemption window, no cap, and no queue.

That's a real counterweight to a simple "private credit is cracking" narrative. Locked-up capital keeps flowing to direct lending regardless of stress in the semi-liquid wrappers. But don't read it as pure good news. ICG matching its predecessor rather than beating it could just as easily mean demand is plateauing. And a target is not deployed, performing capital. More dry powder chasing a finite pool of private-equity-backed borrowers, in a slow exit market, is the classic setup for spread compression and looser documents. So the complacency risk sits on the institutional side, in future underwriting. On the retail side, the redemption caps are at least forcing some discipline.

So here's where this leaves the cycle. On the data, nothing has moved since Sunday. That goes for public leveraged credit too. We found no fresh signal this week in leveraged loans, high yield or CLOs, so we've left them out rather than recycle stale numbers. What has changed is the read on who holds the risk. Liquidity risk in private credit is splitting by wrapper, not by loan book. Semi-liquid retail vehicles are gating as designed, draining slowly, and holding marks nobody has stress-tested. That is the biggest unresolved risk. Closed-end capital keeps arriving without that constraint. That stabilizes liquidity, but it concentrates future underwriting pressure where the dry powder is biggest.

The watchlist is unchanged. Blue Owl's Credit Income Corp tender closes September thirtieth, against last quarter's eighteen point eight percent request rate. A similar figure would say that queue is not draining. Fitch's September default print lands in mid-October, and there's been no new Fitch data since our last read. Third-quarter BDC filings arrive in November, and they will test whether Apollo's re-tender story holds across the sector. And watch for ICG's first close, to see whether that fifteen-billion-euro target converts into committed money.

That's the briefing. Talk to you next time.
