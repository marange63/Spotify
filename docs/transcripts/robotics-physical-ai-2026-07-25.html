Good morning, it's July 25th, and this is your Robotics and Physical AI briefing. The biggest new number in robotics this week is a valuation, and it isn't attached to a humanoid.

Reports emerged yesterday that Anduril, the defense-autonomy company behind the Lattice and Hivemind software platforms, is in talks to raise a new funding round that would push its valuation to roughly one hundred billion dollars. TechCrunch put it plainly: the round "may push its valuation up by a whopping forty billion dollars to about a hundred billion dollars." Sit with the escalation for a second. Anduril priced its Series G at thirty and a half billion dollars in June of last year. Eight weeks ago, in May, it raised five billion dollars at sixty-one billion, which was itself roughly double that Series G mark. Now, in under two months, the reported target is up another sixty-four percent. So the mark has more than tripled in about thirteen months: a clean doubling first, then another sixty-four percent on top, and that second leg is compressing into weeks instead of a year.

The company says its 2025 revenue more than doubled too, to two point two billion dollars. So this isn't a no-revenue story getting marked up on vibes. It's a story about the mark moving faster than even fast revenue growth can justify, and that's the tension worth naming: is this valuation now front-running the business rather than tracking it? Two caveats matter here. Anduril itself was noncommittal, saying no financing decisions have been made. And reporting suggests the deal may be structured in two tranches, with the richer, hundred-billion-dollar price only unlocking if Anduril hits financial targets. That structure is itself a signal — even the investors writing the check aren't paying full price for certainty; they're pricing in execution risk.

Here's why the number travels beyond Anduril. A hundred-billion reported mark on a defense-autonomy software business resets the pricing anchor for the whole cohort. Shield AI and the other mission-autonomy vendors chasing the same government dollars now get benchmarked against it, and so does every humanoid round looking for a comp. So what this means: Anduril's number becomes the reference point the rest of the sector gets priced against, whether or not the round ever actually closes at that level.

Capital is sprinting. Now look at where the actual, disclosed operational proof is showing up, because it's not where the capital is concentrated. At the World AI Conference in Shanghai last week, the Chinese humanoid and industrial-robotics maker AGIBOT unveiled four new products. Headlining them was the A3 Ultra, a full-size humanoid standing one point seven four meters and weighing sixty kilos, with fifty-one degrees of freedom. But the part that matters wasn't the spec sheet. It was a real deployment. AGIBOT disclosed that its G2 mobile robots have been integrated into a tablet quality-inspection line at electronics manufacturer Longcheer, and the company's own account says the line is now "processing around 3,000 units per shift after installation was completed within 36 hours."

Thirty-six hours from install to production throughput, on a named customer, with a stated unit count. Compare that with where Tesla's Optimus program stands after this week's earnings call: still zero confirmed units shipped, with Tesla saying explicitly that its first production runs, starting in the third quarter, are earmarked for internal training-data collection, not customers. One caveat on AGIBOT's number: it's the company's own disclosed figure, verified by nobody outside the deal, with no independent read on throughput or defect rates, and we don't know if this is one pilot line or the start of a broader rollout. No pricing was disclosed for any of the four products either, so this is deployment evidence, not margin evidence. But even with that discount, the pattern holds. So what this means: the company with the most disclosed capital and the company with the most disclosed operational proof are, right now, two different companies, and neither of them is America's flagship general-purpose humanoid program.

That same pattern — show the actual hardware, not just the roadmap — runs down to the component level too. Earlier this month, the home-robot maker 1X unveiled a new hand for its NEO humanoid, and it's a genuinely concrete manipulation-layer data point. The hand has twenty-five degrees of freedom, described as "22 fully actuated joints in the fingers and palm and three at the wrist," with tactile sensing that measures "pressure, contact location and shear forces across the fingers" — enough, 1X says, to detect a slipping object and adjust grip in real time. It's rated IP68, so it's waterproof and dust-resistant, and 1X says it has already stood up a dedicated production line, targeting as many as ten thousand hand units this year.

Why single out a hand? Because manipulation, not walking, is the harder unsolved problem in humanoid robotics. Locomotion is largely a solved demo now; dexterous, tactile grasping is where general-purpose robots still fail. So a maker committing real manufacturing capacity to it is a signal that dexterity is becoming a componentized, scalable product rather than a one-off lab trick for each robot maker. And 1X isn't alone in treating it that way — AGIBOT showed its own twenty-degree-of-freedom dexterous hand at that same Shanghai conference this month. Two makers, one American and one Chinese, both industrializing the hand as a standalone product. If that holds, it sets a template that Figure, Apptronik, and the rest may have to match or license rather than build from scratch.

The caveats are real. Ten thousand units of hand capacity is not ten thousand robots delivered, and no customer has yet received a finished NEO — Early Access is still a twenty-thousand-dollar pre-order, not a shipment. And the tactile and slip-detection claims are 1X's own spec sheet, untested by any independent party. So what this means: real engineering progress is landing at the component level even where full-robot delivery timelines keep slipping.

Put it together and the spine of the week is a split screen. The money is piling into defense autonomy, at a velocity that's starting to outrun even doubling revenue. The proof of working hardware is piling into Chinese industrial deployment and componentized hands, not the marquee humanoid brand. Those two facts point at different companies, and that gap — between where capital is most confident and where operational proof is most concrete — is the thing to keep watching. Over the next few weeks: whether Anduril's round actually closes anywhere near that hundred-billion mark, whether AGIBOT's Longcheer line stays a single pilot or expands, and whether Unitree, still unpriced on the Shanghai exchange as of this taping, finally sets a number the rest of the sector can measure itself against.
