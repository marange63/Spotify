Good morning, this is Cautious Optimism Briefings, the digital money desk, for Sunday, September 20th, 2026.

Yesterday we closed on one open question: would the spot Bitcoin ETF wrapper confirm the squeeze? The answer arrived Friday, and it is a technical yes and a substantive no.

The Block reports that investors added 433 million dollars to the US spot Bitcoin funds on Friday. That left a net inflow of 6.2 million dollars for the week ending September 18th. So the week finished barely positive, after a negative stretch that had run through Thursday.

Now put that against scale. The Block puts ETF trading volume for the week at 16.17 billion dollars, roughly double the week before. Turnover doubled while net flow was six million dollars. The complex holds about 102.5 billion dollars in net assets, and it is still down about 1.45 billion dollars in net flows for the year.

The so-what is that the wrapper was traded, not accumulated. On the 15th, the 17th and the 19th this show tried to read a direction out of the flow prints, and the next print reversed it each time. The daily series is noise. The year-to-date level, still negative, is the demand signal, and it has not turned.

Friday's burst was also narrow. Crypto Briefing reports that Fidelity's FBTC took in about 311 million dollars that day. BlackRock's IBIT added about 108 million. Those two funds were roughly 97 percent of the day's inflow, and Fidelity, not BlackRock, was the bigger buyer. So the headline print is really a read on two distribution channels, not a market-wide bid. Those issuer figures come from press reporting, not a primary flow table.

Now the price action those flows sat beside. Bitcoin climbed as high as 81,702 dollars on September 18th, after trading near 76,400 the day before. Roughly 192 million dollars of leveraged positions were liquidated within a single hour, and the overwhelming majority of that was shorts. That is aggregator data, and other trackers give larger totals.

That is the signature of forced covering, not of repricing. And I want to be careful about what it says on rates. Bitcoin rallied into a Fed that had just hiked. The tempting read is decoupling. But a short squeeze through a macro event is not a correlation reading. One week cannot grade the rates-versus-Bitcoin question either way.

One more macro note, because it sits underneath the positioning. The Bank of Japan also moved this week, to 1.25 percent, a 31-year high, and the yen still weakened against the dollar on the decision. Carry conditions are intact. That is the tail risk beneath the leveraged book I just described.

So if that was covering, the question becomes what the next bid has to climb through. This part is new, and it is structural. A caveat first. The Glassnode notes behind it are dated September 9th and 16th, so they predate Friday's move. Treat what follows as position levels, not as current relationships to spot.

Glassnode shows roughly 1.07 million Bitcoin acquired between 83,000 and 86,000 dollars, mostly by long-term holders. It also puts the US spot ETF complex's own breakeven near 86,000. The biggest call strike above spot is 85,000 dollars, and the largest options expiry of the quarter lands this coming Friday.

Put the week's net ETF inflow next to that. Six million dollars of net demand is walking toward a million coins held by people now within a few percent of getting their money back, after sitting through a full drawdown. This is the densest sell-into-recovery zone in the cycle, and the wrapper is bringing six million dollars a week to it. That argument does not require a bearish macro view. It only requires the supply to behave the way supply at cost basis usually behaves.

In fairness, the bull case has evidence too. Glassnode says the long-term holder share of realized profit fell to 47 percent, from 88 percent at the August peak. Those holders sat out the rally. That is supply withheld, not supply distributed. The trouble is that withheld supply sitting near its cost basis is exactly the supply that tends to come out when price finally reaches it.

So who else could bring a check? The answer, increasingly, is nobody new. Earlier this week I said a single preferred buyback by the Bitcoin treasury company Strategy was not a regime change. Then two straight weeks with no Bitcoin purchases at all did look like a regime change. Glassnode now shows it was never about one company. Net purchases by listed companies total about 5.9 thousand Bitcoin over the past three months. In July of 2025 alone, the same cohort bought 89 thousand.

Strategy is only the visible example. It held its Bitcoin unchanged for a second straight week and spent about 139 million dollars buying back its own STRC preferred instead. That is as of its September 14th filing; Monday's is not out yet. The cohort's average entry is 80,500 dollars, against a Friday high of 81,702. These buyers are back at cost.

That matters for a second-order reason. This cohort was a price-insensitive bid, funded by a premium to net asset value. The same reflexive mechanism can run in reverse. The moment one of these companies needs balance-sheet flexibility, a holder sitting at its cost basis becomes potential supply. The marginal corporate bid did not slow. It stopped.

If the domestic institutional bid has stalled, where did the activity go? For that we need the August exchange data. This is monthly data published in early September, not a read on last week.

CoinDesk Data reports that centralized exchange volume rose 12.7 percent in August, to 4.29 trillion dollars, rebounding from July's multi-year lows.

Spot led that rebound, up almost 19 percent. And Binance's derivatives share climbed to 46.9 percent, the highest since November 2023. The recovery concentrated on the largest offshore venue.

Treat the level with care. A second tracker puts August spot volume roughly forty percent below CoinDesk's figure and explicitly flags possible wash and bot activity. So the direction is more reliable than the size. And the rebound is off a multi-year low, not a breakout.

The same CoinDesk Data review shows perpetuals on tokenized assets hitting a record 602 billion dollars of volume on centralized exchanges. That is notional trading volume, not tokenized-asset holdings. Even so, it says tokenized-asset trading is scaling offshore, ahead of the onshore permission for it.

That is the measurable cost of the crypto market-structure bill, the Clarity Act, failing in the Senate on the 15th. With no statute, the perimeter is now drawn by agency rule instead of by Congress. Meanwhile the leverage that set last week's price sits on venues no US regulator supervises, and its concentration got worse, not better. So the onshore-venue debate is really a market-structure question about where Bitcoin's price is discovered. At these volumes, it is not yet a US revenue story.

Now the last leg, the dollar float that was supposed to be the structural demand. Glassnode has the stablecoin market cap near 301 billion dollars. That is flat on the week and about 4 percent below its April peak. Tether says USDT has extended its share to over 60 percent of the total, so the float gaining share is also the float furthest from US supervision.

Here is the consensus error. Stablecoins are widely treated as the marginal buyer of Treasury bills. That only works if the float keeps growing, and it has not grown since April. A stable float is a stable buyer of bills, not a growing one. Anyone modelling incremental bill demand off stablecoin adoption is extrapolating a line that has been flat for five months.

Yet the issuers are more profitable than ever. Tether reported roughly 1.5 billion dollars of operating profit in its second-quarter attestation. That is an August 4th disclosure, not news. Circle, for its part, estimates that 100 basis points on rates adds about 737 million dollars to annual reserve income. That is a static company estimate, and it assumes the float does not shrink.

So profitability and adoption have decoupled. More margin on the same dollars raises the opportunity cost for anyone holding a coin that cannot pay them, and that pushes dollars toward yield-bearing on-chain instruments without expanding the pool at all. This is margin, not adoption.

Let me pull it together. Every demand channel in this stack is flat, offshore, or underwater. The corporate bid stopped. The stablecoin float stopped compounding in April. The volume that did come back came back on unsupervised venues. And the one channel that grew last week grew by six million dollars, into a million coins of overhead supply.

Two things to watch. First, whether ETF flows hold through Friday's quarterly expiry without another two-issuer burst. Second, whether the corporate cohort defends its 80,500 dollar cost basis or starts to distribute.
