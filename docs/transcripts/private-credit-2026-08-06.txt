Good morning, it's Thursday, August 6th, and this is your private credit briefing. Today's story is credit starting to price the gap between what borrowers claim and what lenders will actually underwrite.

Start with the leveraged loan market, because for the first time this AI capex cycle, investors said no to the terms on offer. CoreWeave went out to raise a $2.6 billion delayed-draw term loan to fund GPU purchases for Anthropic, Jane Street and Hudson River Trading compute capacity. Initial price talk was four and a quarter to four and a half percentage points over the benchmark. It cleared at five and a half points over, a 97-cent discount, yielding 10.44 percent to a September 2031 maturity. That's a real repricing, not a rounding error. CoreWeave also had to accept a 1.35-times debt-service coverage covenant and full amortization until it reaches investment grade. Underwriters are now pricing actual cash-flow risk into an AI compute borrower. They were not doing that just weeks earlier, when Apollo led the $35 billion Broadcom-Anthropic financing on far friendlier terms. Bloomberg's reporting says at least four AI-linked borrowers had to sweeten terms that same week.

One of those four was Proofpoint, and it's worth being precise about why, because the two stories are not the same trade. CoreWeave is an AI infrastructure company; the risk lenders were pricing was AI capex financing risk, whether the compute buildout it's borrowing against pays off. Proofpoint is Thoma Bravo's cybersecurity business, with no AI-compute exposure at all. Its $5 billion amend-and-extend refinancing got reworked with roughly two dozen document changes before it cleared: Proofpoint gave up its right to strip collateral away from existing creditors, accepted a bar on privately negotiated debt buybacks, and took tighter covenant protections, landing near a 9.3 percent yield with maturities pushed to 2030. The reason lenders gave wasn't compute economics. It was that AI itself might disrupt Proofpoint's software business and erode its long-term competitiveness. So what's actually happening is broader than an AI-infrastructure story: lenders are starting to price AI disruption risk into software credit generally, not just AI-capex risk into companies building the infrastructure. That's a bigger claim than a single repriced GPU loan, and it deserves to be treated as such.

But don't read this as a uniform market turn against anything AI-adjacent. Two weeks before the CoreWeave and Proofpoint week, Blackstone's QTS — an investment-grade data-center loan, rated Baa3 by Moody's — got upsized from $3 billion to $3.25 billion and priced at two and a quarter points over benchmark, with no pushback at all. Same data-center-driven demand, same AI buildout story, completely different reception. The discipline being applied right now looks concentrated in non-investment-grade, AI-exposed credits, not spread evenly across the AI and data-center financing complex. That bifurcation matters more than the headline: rated, asset-backed data-center paper is still getting bid aggressively, while unrated or lower-rated AI-linked and AI-exposed names are the ones facing real friction. Whether that's the start of a durable repricing or one week of new-issue indigestion from a crowded AI-debt calendar is genuinely unresolved. But the competitive read cuts both ways too. If the syndicated loan market keeps demanding covenants, private credit lenders who can commit full size — the Apollo-Broadcom playbook — may simply absorb more of that AI-infrastructure financing, which would blunt the discipline rather than confirm it market-wide.

Now to what's happening on paper already outstanding, because that's where the market's own benign narrative gets tested. S&P's LCD data through July 31 shows the conventional leveraged loan default rate holding flat at 1.11 percent by amount, 1.25 percent by count. On its face, that's a calm credit environment. But once you count distressed liability management exercises that trigger a downgrade to D or SD, the combined default rate climbs to 4.56 percent by issuer count in July. That's up from 4.46 percent in June and 4.36 percent in May — three straight months of a widening gap between the headline number and the real one. July alone brought one bankruptcy filing and two separate liability management exercises among constituents of the Morningstar LSTA Leveraged Loan Index. The mechanism here is straightforward: a growing share of what would once have been payment defaults are instead getting resolved through amend-and-extend deals and creditor-on-creditor maneuvering that keep an issuer technically current on cash interest while still representing a real credit loss for lenders who get primed or excluded. The headline 1.11 percent is measuring something narrower than actual credit stress, and that gap is not shrinking, it's growing month over month. The so-what here is recovery assumptions: loans still counted as current in that quiet 1.11 percent bucket may recover far worse than historical averages once they're actually tested, because the mechanism keeping them out of the default count is precisely the one that primes and dilutes existing lenders.

One more data point on where mismatch is showing up, this time in liquidity rather than credit quality. Cliffwater's Corporate Lending Fund, the largest U.S. private-credit interval fund at roughly $31 to $33 billion, took redemption requests on about 17 percent of its shares in the second quarter, up from 14 percent in the first. Management responded by cutting the repurchase cap from 7 percent to 5 percent of shares, roughly $1.6 billion, meaning investors got back only a fraction of what they asked to withdraw. This is a structural story, not necessarily a credit-quality one — it's not clear whether the surge reflects genuine worry about portfolio risk or simply investors wanting liquidity elsewhere. But at the largest vehicle in the retail private-credit channel, two straight quarters of redemption demand outrunning the repurchase cap is exactly the liquidity mismatch this asset class's retailization push, including the 401(k) conversation, has to prove it can absorb without gating.

Pulling this together: the credit cycle is not turning, but it is starting to show its seams. Fundraising, fee income and headline default rates all still look benign. Underneath that, loan investors are now pricing AI-related risk for the first time this cycle, though selectively — punishing non-investment-grade AI-exposed names while investment-grade data-center paper sails through — and the true stock of impaired leveraged loans is climbing steadily once you strip out the accounting trick of liability management exercises. The biggest open question is whether the CoreWeave and Proofpoint repricing is the start of durable AI-credit discipline or gets absorbed by private credit stepping in with full-size commitments instead. Watch the next few weeks of AI-linked loan pricings for whether the wider terms hold, and watch August's LME-inclusive default print for whether that climb from 4.36 to 4.56 percent keeps going. Both would tell you whether today's discipline is real or a single loud week.

That's the briefing. Have a good one.
