Good morning, it's Sunday, July 26th, 2026, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

This week the market stopped treating AI capital spending as a uniform tailwind. The bill for that spending is now showing up in three different places: on a credit rating, on a cash flow statement, and inside an operating margin. Oracle, Alphabet, and SAP each handed us one face of the same story, and together they tell you where enterprise software money is actually flowing versus where it's just being promised.

Start with Oracle, because it's the clearest case. On July 23rd, the Department of Defense awarded Oracle a nearly seven billion dollar, ten-year contract to supply on-premise software across the military branches, the Coast Guard, and the intelligence community. The structure matters: it's a base of three point three one billion dollars over an initial five-year period, rising to six point nine nine billion only if a five-year option gets exercised. The Pentagon says the deal saves taxpayers at least four hundred forty-one million dollars versus prior procurement. That's real, durable government revenue. But it landed against a much larger backdrop: Oracle's stock fell about thirty-three point seven percent between June 8th and July 8th, including a nine-session losing streak. Fiscal 2026 capital expenditure rose to fifty-five point seven billion dollars, producing a twenty-three point seven billion dollar free-cash-flow shortfall. S&P responded by cutting Oracle's credit rating to triple-B-minus, as net debt ended the fiscal year at ninety-seven point six billion dollars, up roughly twenty percent year over year.

Now, you might expect the counter-argument here to be that Oracle lacks the contracted backlog to justify that spending. It doesn't. Oracle's remaining performance obligations hit six hundred thirty-eight billion dollars at the end of its fiscal fourth quarter, up three hundred sixty-three percent year over year — a backlog larger than the five hundred fourteen billion dollars Alphabet reported for Google Cloud this quarter. So the problem isn't a shortage of contracted demand. The problem is what that backlog is made of and when it turns into cash. Oracle itself has said only about twelve percent of that RPO converts to recognized revenue within the next twelve months, with another third landing somewhere between one and three years out. And a meaningful slice of the increase came from AI contracts where the customer prepaid Oracle for GPUs or supplied the hardware directly — arrangements that behave more like financed capacity deals than conventional subscription revenue. So the seven billion dollar Pentagon award is a genuine offset to sentiment, but it's the wrong category of business, on-premise licensing rather than an AI cloud win, and it's nowhere near large enough to change the financing math driving the credit downgrade. Oracle's real vulnerability was never the size of its backlog. It's the gap between debt taken on today and cash that won't arrive for years.

And that gap sets up the trap Oracle is in. A cut to triple-B-minus leaves the company one notch above high-yield. That rating isn't just a scorecard, it's a price. Every further downgrade raises what Oracle pays to borrow the money it still needs to build the AI cloud capacity its backlog is promised against. So the downgrade and the capex are locked in a feedback loop: the cost of financing goes up at exactly the moment the financing need is largest, and the cash to close the gap is still years out in that slow-converting backlog. That's why a government licensing deal, welcome as it is, doesn't move the needle. It's the right direction and the wrong order of magnitude.

Now compare that to a company with the opposite financing profile. Alphabet's Google Cloud backlog is what Oracle's gets measured against, and this quarter Alphabet showed that even a cash-generative hyperscaler is starting to feel capex scrutiny. Second-quarter capital expenditure came in at forty-four point nine two billion dollars, nearly double what Alphabet spent a year earlier, and full-year guidance was raised again to a range of one hundred ninety-five to two hundred five billion dollars. The capex surge pushed quarterly free cash flow negative, to a loss of five point eight six billion dollars. That's a real inflection: the market is now discounting capex increases even when the underlying growth beats expectations. The distinction from Oracle isn't the direction of the pressure, it's the source of the money. Alphabet is burning cash it generates elsewhere in its business. Oracle is burning cash it borrowed. Same instinct from investors, different verdict, because financing quality is now the discriminator, not AI exposure itself.

That brings us to the third face of this story, and it's the one that should worry enterprise software buyers most, because it shows the cost of AI migrating from the balance sheet to the income statement. SAP reported cloud revenue up twenty-four percent to six point three billion euros, with current cloud backlog reaching twenty-two point nine billion euros, up twenty-seven percent year over year, driven by on-premise-to-cloud ERP migrations. Strong numbers. But SAP simultaneously cut its 2026 operating profit outlook to a range of eleven point eight to twelve point two billion euros at constant currencies, citing more than one hundred million euros of dilution from acquisitions closed in July, alongside margin pressure from research and development spending and, explicitly, token costs. That last item is the news. SAP is, as far as this dossier shows, the first major enterprise incumbent to name inference spend directly as a line item dragging down profit guidance. The company hasn't broken out how much of the margin cut comes from tokens versus acquisition dilution versus R&D, so don't take this as a precise number, but the disclosure itself is the signal. If generative AI features are being bundled into existing per-seat subscription pricing, and the inference cost of running them is now visible enough to move guidance, that's evidence incumbents may not be able to absorb AI costs within current pricing forever. Watch for that to force a move toward usage-based pricing rather than the all-you-can-eat seat model.

So what does this add up to. Three companies, three balance sheets, three different verdicts from the market on essentially the same underlying behavior, which is spending heavily to build and deliver AI capability. Oracle is punished because its capex is debt-financed against a smaller-margin legacy business, and its backlog, however large, converts to cash too slowly to matter this year. Alphabet is scrutinized but tolerated because its capex is funded by a cash-generative core business, even as free cash flow itself has now gone negative. And SAP shows that even companies not building data centers are absorbing AI's cost, just further down the income statement, in the price of running the models their customers actually use. The discriminator across all three isn't whether a vendor is spending on AI. Nearly all of them are. It's whether they can pay for it out of what the business already earns, or whether they're borrowing against a future that hasn't arrived yet. Watch the next round of earnings for which incumbents start naming token costs the way SAP just did, and watch Oracle's credit spreads for whether that gap between capex and cash keeps widening.
