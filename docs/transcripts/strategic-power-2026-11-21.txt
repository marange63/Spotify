Good morning. It's Saturday, November 21st, 2026, and this is Strategic Power.

This week the gap between declared policy and physical reality ran in both directions. Washington's chip chokepoint on China turned out to be far looser in practice than in its rhetoric. The AI build at home, meanwhile, is tighter in wires and transformers than its gigawatt announcements suggest. And in between, the Federal Reserve put on the record how concentrated the financing of that build has become.

Start with the chips. On Thursday, CNBC reported on records released by the House Foreign Affairs Committee and its chairman, Brian Mast. They show the Commerce Department approved the sale of more than eight hundred seventy-nine thousand Nvidia and AMD AI chips to Chinese companies, as of May. That came against roughly three point four million chips requested between December and May. Approved buyers include Alibaba and Tencent. License requests also named ByteDance, Tesla's Shanghai operation, and Baidu.

All month this desk has tracked Washington tightening the chip perimeter. On Monday we reported that Beijing, not Washington, was restricting how many H200s Nvidia could actually sell into China. Today we learn how much Washington had licensed in the first place.

Two details sharpen it. First, CNBC reports that some of those approvals went to companies the Defense Department designates as "Chinese Military Companies." The reporting does not say which ones. Second, Nvidia has confirmed through a spokesperson that it has since stopped shipping H200s to China, and it has given no reason. Its last disclosed figure was two point four billion dollars of Hopper-generation shipments to China, in the quarter ended October 25th.

Now the case for skepticism. These are license approvals, not shipments. No source has quantified how many of the eight hundred seventy-nine thousand chips actually reached China. And this desk's own reporting suggests the binding limit on real flows was Beijing's purchase restrictions, and now Nvidia's halt, rather than Washington's paperwork.

The approval rate also cuts two ways. A sell-side analyst quoted by CNBC put it at "about one out of every four," and said "there was a process in place." On that reading, a seventy-five percent denial rate is a working screen. On the other reading, it is still a very large absolute volume, licensed to China's biggest platforms. Our judgment is that the volume matters more for strategic power than the ratio. A screen that denies three requests and approves the fourth still put hundreds of thousands of chips within reach of China's largest AI buyers. But the real damage depends on deliveries, and nobody has counted them.

The records also surfaced two suspected smuggling fronts, Maginfra and New Matrix. Mast's verdict was blunt: "Both are fake companies." Reporters who visited their listed Chinese offices found them empty. Commerce's only on-record reply was general. Licensing decisions, it said, are "the product of deliberation and consultation among multiple U.S. Government agencies." It did not address the two firms.

The timing is what changes the politics, and this part is our inference. The revelation lands after a month of tightening, from Commerce's remote-access rule to chip amendments now attached to the defense bill. It also lands before that bill reaches the floor in December. A record of generous past licensing makes legislative tightening likelier. It also shifts scrutiny off Nvidia's management and onto Commerce's own practice.

Taiwan, meanwhile, is reaching for statute. Its legislature is reviewing amendments on illegal exports of critical chip materials and equipment to China. They would raise the maximum penalty to ten years in prison, with fines up to ten million New Taiwan dollars. It is a committee review with no effective date, not law. But the contrast is the point. In the same week Washington's licensing record looks permissive, the island most exposed is legislating criminal liability. On our read, that raises a real question about which jurisdiction actually anchors the chip chokepoint.

Markets are already leaning into that question. AMD closed down more than seven percent on Thursday, after what CNBC described as a short-seller report alleging China exposure. That report is an allegation, not a finding. But our read is that the market now prices China exposure as a liability, not an option. The lesson from the chip story is simple. The export control was measured in licenses, and the licenses were generous.

Those chips are the demand side of the AI race. The supply side is electrons, and this week the International Energy Agency said the wires are the problem. Its World Energy Outlook was published on November 19th, and we have its figures through CNBC's reporting. The agency projects that global data-center electricity use could double by 2030, to around a thousand terawatt-hours a year. The doubling isn't the news, because a steep power path is already consensus. The news is the agency's blunter line: supplies of copper, cables and transformers are already tight.

The IEA sizes the fix at twenty-six million kilometers of new power lines by 2035. Without those upgrades, the agency warns, networks could become more congested and less reliable.

This generalizes a story we've tracked regionally. On Wednesday we reported PJM's fast-energization filing, whose first cycle wouldn't finish until 2029. The IEA is now saying that isn't a PJM quirk. It is the global condition.

Keep the discipline, though. A thousand terawatt-hours is an outlook projection, not permitted or built capacity. Likewise, the IEA's projected seventy-five percent rise in grid spending over the next decade is a scenario, not appropriated money or wire in the ground. The market implication is our inference. Tight equipment favors transformer and cable suppliers and transmission builders over the pace of announced compute timelines. Data-center pipelines will likely energize more slowly than their press releases imply. America's chip ceiling abroad turned out to be administrative. Its compute ceiling at home is physical.

Building those wires, and the data centers behind them, takes debt. And on Wednesday the Fed said where that debt sits. Minutes from the Fed's October meeting show that "most participants observed that AI-related debt had grown rapidly." Those exposures, the minutes add, were "becoming increasingly concentrated among a small number of firms."

Some context on that meeting. The Fed held its target range at three point seventy-five to four percent, on a ten-to-two vote. Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack dissented in favor of a hike. The inflation backdrop is October CPI, which ran hot, at zero point four percent headline and zero point three core, each a tenth above expectations. But be clear about what the minutes don't say. They do not make AI financing a driver of the rate path. The concentration language is a flagged risk, not a stated trigger.

Still, the concentration point matters on its own terms, because it is visible in real time. On Thursday the Nasdaq Composite closed down two and a quarter percent, and CoreWeave fell more than six percent. Our read is that this is the correlated repricing that concentration implies. If a handful of balance sheets carry the exposure, an equity derating can pass into credit through the same names. This is also the second Fed document this week to flag that exposure. Our sister desk on private credit reported that the Fed's Financial Stability Report added its first-ever section on data-center leverage. So the grid build the IEA calls for will be financed against that backdrop. The build is concentrated in capital, just as it is constrained in copper and transformers.

Pull the thread together. Chips leaked abroad by licensing choice. The binding limit on compute at home is physical. And the money behind the build sits with a small number of firms the Fed is now watching. Three dates to watch. First, the defense-bill chip amendments as they head toward December floor action. Second, the Fed's December 9th decision, which hasn't happened yet. Third, January 10th, when China's current rare-earth export suspension is set to expire.

That's Strategic Power for November 21st. Stay cautiously optimistic, and have a good weekend.
