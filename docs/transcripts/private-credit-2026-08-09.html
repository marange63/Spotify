Private Credit & the Corporate Credit Cycle

Transcript · August 9, 2026

Good morning, it's Sunday, August 9th. This is Cautious Optimism Briefings.

Today's read on private credit is not about the cycle turning through defaults or spreads. It's about stress showing up in the plumbing: who's funding the funders, whether retail investors can actually get their money out, and whether the diligence behind asset-based lending holds up when someone lies to you. The headline numbers stay benign. The wiring underneath them doesn't.

Start with the Federal Reserve. On Friday the Fed's Board published a FEDS Note using confidential Y-14 supervisory data, and it's the first time anyone has put hard numbers on a channel we've only been able to describe qualitatively: how dependent business development companies have become on a small number of banks. Bank loans now make up about 40 percent of average BDC debt, roughly double the share a decade earlier. Total bank commitments to BDCs in the sample exceeded 60 billion dollars. And the top three banks lending to BDCs account for nearly half that market. The Fed also found that during the 2022 tightening cycle, banks charged BDCs an extra 0.9 percentage point in premium simply for the privilege of the credit line. That premium is the number to watch going forward. It's a real-time gauge of how the regulated banking system prices non-bank counterparty risk, and a rising premium in the next tightening episode would be an early warning that banks themselves are losing confidence in the private-credit complex. None of this means banks are exposed to First Brands-style losses directly — these are typically senior secured claims, a small share of large-bank capital. The systemic case here is concentration, not direct credit risk: if one or two of those top three lenders pull back for their own regulatory-capital reasons, BDC funding could tighten broadly, independent of how the underlying loans are actually performing.

If the banks are the funding backbone, the retail channel is the other pressure point, and this week gave us a live read on it. Blackstone's BCRED, its non-traded direct-lending vehicle, took Q2 redemption requests equal to about 10 percent of shares outstanding, up from 7.9 percent in the first quarter. It again fulfilled only 5 percent, the fund's standard cap, holding net outflow at roughly 3 percent of NAV — essentially unchanged from Q1. Cliffwater's direct-lending interval fund saw requests around 17 percent of shares and also held its 5 percent cap. So far, nobody has broken the gate; the structure is doing exactly what it was designed to do. But the gap between what investors are asking for and what they're actually getting is widening, and that gap is precisely the non-bank vulnerability indicator the Fed and the Financial Stability Board have been watching for. There are two competing explanations, and the data can't settle which is right: Bank of America argues wealth advisors are over-requesting in anticipation of proration, a mechanical artifact rather than genuine flight. The alternative is a real shift in sentiment away from perpetual-life vehicles. Either way, persistent unmet demand creates pressure on managers to raise caps using sponsor capital, the way BCRED already has once — and that quietly blurs the line between investor liquidity and balance-sheet support.

The stress in the wiring shows up as an actual credit event in First Brands, the auto-parts supplier whose Chapter 11 was triggered by fabricated and double-pledged receivables. Roughly 2.5 billion dollars in factored invoices could not be matched to real inventory sales, and customers froze about 250 million dollars in payments that were critical to the debtor-in-possession budget. The creditor fight is getting uglier, not cleaner: Onset Financial, a short-duration inventory lender, collected about 2.9 billion dollars in repayments against 2.5 billion it financed, and is still claiming another 1.9 billion owed. The creditors' committee calls Onset a net winner; Onset calls itself a fraud victim. On the private-credit exposure side, the numbers are smaller and more specific than they first looked. Jefferies' Point Bonita Capital fund has disclosed roughly 715 million dollars of exposure. UBS has counted more than 500 million. Separately — and this is not additive to those two — a joint venture between Japan's Norinchukin Bank and Mitsui reportedly faces 1.75 billion dollars of its own exposure to First Brands, per Bloomberg. That's a distinct, standalone claim, not a combined tally of western-bank losses. This is a diligence failure, not proof the broader asset class is unsound, but expect it to force market-wide tightening of borrowing-base and factoring diligence, which raises origination costs across asset-based lending. And with hearings continuing this month, expect recoveries to print below the marks private funds had been carrying — a live test of whether valuations get written down before or after the market forces the issue.

So how are the biggest managers actually deploying into this environment? Ares reported record Q2 fundraising of about 36 billion dollars and dry powder of 170 billion, up 13 percent year over year — both already known. What's new is the composition: 75 percent of its 12.4 billion dollars in U.S. direct-lending commitments went to existing portfolio companies rather than new deals. Management blames slow M&A, and that's a plausible read, but the same data is equally consistent with extend-and-support inside portfolios that are quietly under strain. The falsifiable signal to watch is Ares' shadow pipeline — NDAs signed up about 35 percent quarter over quarter, new deals logged up about 30 percent. If that pipeline converts into closed new-money deals in the second half, it's a genuine normalization signal. If it doesn't, record dry powder is really just capital waiting to prop up the existing book.

One more tell before we close. Sector-wide PIK income across BDCs fell to 8.2 percent of total interest income in the first quarter, down from 8.6 percent and the lowest reading in two years — on its face, reassuring. But the more useful lens is the migration path from performing to PIK to non-accrual, and if the improvement in aggregate PIK is concentrated among healthy borrowers while a smaller cohort deteriorates straight through to non-accrual, the headline ratio is masking exactly the kind of tail risk that matters here. A falling average can sit on top of a fattening tail. And that is the same shape as the redemption gates and the funding concentration: the aggregate looks calmer while the distribution underneath gets more skewed. Treat it as a hypothesis to watch in the next round of BDC prints, not a confirmed pattern.

Put it together and the credit cycle looks calm on the surface and stressed in the plumbing. Bank funding to BDCs is concentrated in a handful of lenders and now has a price on the risk premium attached to it. Retail redemption gates are holding, but the gap between requests and fulfillment is growing. And First Brands has put a real dollar figure on how badly asset-based-lending diligence can fail when someone wants it to. None of that is a default cycle turning over. All of it is where the losses show up first if one does. The single unresolved risk is that none of this wiring — the funding concentration, the liquidity gates, the private marks — has been stress-tested by an actual downturn, only by a benign one. Watch the First Brands hearings later this month, the rest of BDC earnings season for non-accrual migration, and whether Ares' pipeline actually converts in the second half.
