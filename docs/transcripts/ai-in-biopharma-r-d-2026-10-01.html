Cautious Optimism Briefings

AI in Biopharma R&D

Transcript · October 1, 2026

Good morning, it's Thursday, October 1st, 2026, and this is your briefing on AI in biopharma R&D.

The claim has shifted. For months, the industry has sold AI as a speed story: faster discovery, cheaper screening, shorter timelines. This week, Roche put AI in the same breath as the one metric that determines pharma's economics, the clinical success rate. The evidence hasn't caught up to that juxtaposition, and walking through why is today's most useful exercise.

Start with what Roche said at its pharma investor day on Monday, September 28th. Genentech's R&D head, Aviv Regev, framed the company's direction as what she called AI independence. Roche says it has started constructing autonomous AI-driven laboratories, where models, experiments, and automation run as one connected system. The mechanism on the decision side is an internal tool called Target Nexus. According to Reuters coverage of the event, forty percent of pipeline decisions made between the fourth quarter of 2025 and the second quarter of this year already carry a tracked AI contribution. Roche expects that tool to contribute to eighty percent of research portfolio decisions by the end of this year. A tracked AI contribution is a company-defined metric, and Roche has not disclosed how it is measured. So AI touches a large share of decisions by Roche's own yardstick, and nobody outside can audit that yardstick yet.

Now the number that made headlines. Roche's chief medical officer, Levi Garraway, told investors, quote, our numbers thus far this year of phase III successes are tracking above eighty percent, end quote. Reuters coverage puts last year's comparable rate at sixty-five percent. Put those disclosures side by side and the implied story writes itself. AI picked better bets, and the bets are paying off. Until now, no source offered a clean comparison of AI-shaped versus historical success rates, and on September 25th we said to treat any headline uplift with suspicion. This is the first such figure, so let's test it.

The skepticism comes in three layers. First, timing. Target Nexus's tracked contribution covers decisions made from the fourth quarter of 2025 onward. Phase III trials reading out in 2026 were not selected last winter. They were selected years earlier, long before Target Nexus reached anything like its current reach. So whatever is driving this year's success rate, it cannot plausibly be this year's AI tool. At best, the eighty percent figure is a baseline for a future, genuinely AI-shaped cohort.

Second, the denominator. Neither Roche's own transcript nor the wire coverage discloses how many phase III readouts sit behind either figure. A handful of trials can swing a year-to-date rate by double digits. Pipeline mix could also explain the number. A year heavy in validated mechanisms or line extensions looks better than one heavy in novel biology. Survivorship could explain it too, if weaker programs were culled before phase III. Treat this as a fragile, small-sample statistic until Roche publishes the count.

Third, and most tellingly, Roche's own chief medical officer gave the credit to something else. Garraway said, quote, a lot of it resulted directly from the bar assessment, end quote. He pointed to, quote, the bar and these other governance reconfigurations and other measures, end quote. The bar is Roche's portfolio-governance framework, introduced at the end of 2022. It screens candidate programs for patient impact, revenue potential, and what the company calls optimized risk before they advance. By Roche's own account, portfolio value has risen ninety-three percent since the bar arrived. That came with a reallocation of roughly one point six billion Swiss francs in R&D resources. This is a governance and portfolio-pruning story that predates Target Nexus by years. And it is Roche's own executive, not an outside critic, offering the alternative explanation. So AI independence and the success-rate jump were things Roche discussed at the same event, not things Roche says are causally linked.

What AI independence means is narrower than the phrase suggests. Regev defined it as owning proprietary models and the infrastructure underneath them. In her words, quote, our commitment to AI independence in R&D, you have to own the learnings and the improvements that AI can develop, end quote. That's a claim about in-house architectures, in-house agents, and dedicated compute for training and inference, fed by Roche's own lab and clinical data. It also speaks to the IP question this show keeps tracking. In effect, Roche wants the model weights, and whatever they learn from its proprietary data, to stay inside the company rather than accrue to a vendor. It is not a claim that Roche has replaced its outside partners. Roche and Genentech are a key paying partner for Recursion on AI-derived targets, and Regev's framing never mentioned Recursion. That is not evidence the partnership is at risk. But if Roche moves target selection in-house, Recursion is the exposure to watch.

What does land is the capital story. Roche is redirecting roughly two billion Swiss francs of R&D savings into programs and productivity initiatives by this year. It is also targeting up to twenty new molecular entities to market by 2030. Redirecting savings means AI and automation are being funded largely from inside the existing R&D envelope. That fits this show's standing judgment of roughly flat total budgets and a falling cost per shot on goal.

Three second-order effects follow, and all three are inference rather than reported fact. One, platform partners get squeezed. If a top pharma decides target and portfolio selection is a capability to own rather than rent, leverage shifts toward proprietary data and in-house wet-lab automation. That is bad news for AI-native platforms whose pitch is exactly that selection layer. Two, expect a disclosure race. Novartis, AstraZeneca, and Lilly will face investor pressure to publish their own AI-contributed-to-X-percent-of-decisions numbers. Each will be self-defined and none will be comparable. Investors should ask for denominators and cohort tracking instead. Three, the stakes. If a late-stage success-rate lift proved both durable and attributable to AI, it would be the biggest R&D-productivity re-rating story in pharma. That is why the evidentiary bar sits this high.

If better clinical odds come from anywhere, the honest mechanism is better target selection upstream. The best public evidence for that right now is a Stanford paper published in Science on September 17th, describing a system called the Virtual Biotech. According to Stanford's release, more than thirty-seven thousand AI agents annotated outcomes across fifty-five thousand, nine hundred and eighty-four historical clinical trials. The finding was that drugs targeting genes with cell-type-specific expression were forty-eight percent more likely to reach market. They were also associated with thirty-two percent fewer adverse events.

That is rigorous and peer-reviewed. It's also retrospective and associational. Cell-type specificity could be a proxy for safer target classes or better-understood biology, rather than the causal driver of success. And the paper's forward-looking lung-cancer strategy is a proposal, not a trial result. The real contribution here isn't a new theory of biology. It's scale. A human genetics team could in principle derive the same heuristic. What thirty-seven thousand agents add is the ability to annotate fragmented, messy historical trial records at a speed and coverage no team could match. Our standing view on real-world data is that the binding constraint is linking records across fragmented, post-HITECH formats, not HIPAA and not model capability. Agents that can read messy records at scale go straight at that bottleneck. So the value is data wrangling, not new biology.

Pull the two threads together for an honest map of the stack. Both of this week's stories are large language model stories. One is decision support across a portfolio. The other is agents annotating trial records. Neither is a specialized structure or molecule-design model, and this week brought nothing new from that layer. What is new, at least on this show, is the workhorse layer being pointed at the outcome metric itself, rather than at labor productivity in documents and code. But neither Roche nor Stanford has produced a prospective result.

So what would make Roche's claim falsifiable? First, disclosure of the readout count behind both years. Second, the share of this year's phase III wins that sit on novel versus already-validated mechanisms. Third, a prospective cohort of Target Nexus-shaped programs, tracked to their own readouts.

Those are the open questions, plus one dated test at the other end of the stack. Isomorphic Labs is running against its own end-of-2026 clock for a first IND, with about three months left. That is the test of whether a company built on structure and design models can reach the clinic on its own timeline. On success rates, our view hasn't moved. Expect a modest improvement, concentrated before phase II, unless prospective cohort data says otherwise. On spend, Roche just showed the pattern. AI is being paid for out of reallocated savings, not new money, so total R&D budgets look roughly flat. Right now, Roche's eighty percent is a marker, not a verdict.
