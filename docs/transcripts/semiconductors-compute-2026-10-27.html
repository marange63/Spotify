Good morning. It's Tuesday, October 27th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

Over the past week this desk has tracked a bottleneck migrating up the AI supply chain. Nvidia was the allocator. Then Nvidia started paying six suppliers to build back-end capacity. Then the strain showed up at the toolmakers. Today it shows up somewhere new: Nvidia's own published spec sheet.

Here's what happened. Nvidia's Vera Rubin NVL72 product page now lists 19.2 terabytes per second of HBM4 bandwidth per GPU. An archived August version of that same page showed 22 terabytes per second. That's roughly a 13 percent cut. NVLink bandwidth per GPU dropped too, from 3.6 down to 3 terabytes per second. Third-party trackers spotted the change by comparing the live page with the archive. Nvidia has not explained it, and no spokesperson has gone on record.

It gets messier under the hood. Nvidia's separate eight-GPU HGX page still shows the old per-GPU numbers, the original 22 and 3.6 terabytes, sitting right next to revised system totals. And those totals don't reconcile. Eight GPUs at the new 19.2 figure would add up to about 154 terabytes per second. The page now shows 130. So this isn't a single, tidy re-spec. It's an internally inconsistent set of numbers that Nvidia has quietly changed and not cleaned up. That also means the 19.2 figure itself could still move before volume shipment.

What wasn't cut matters just as much. HBM4 capacity per GPU is reportedly unchanged. The headline 50 petaflops of NVFP4 compute is unchanged too, though it's now spelled out as a sparse-math number. So bandwidth fell while capacity and compute held. That means Rubin's ratio of bytes delivered to flops of compute got worse. This next part is our inference from those figures. For training, that ratio matters less. For memory-bound inference, the decode step that dominates serving large models, bandwidth is close to the binding constraint. So the cut likely bites harder on inference economics than the 13 percent headline suggests.

Follow that one step further. A hyperscaler or lab that built its serving-cost model on the original spec sheet now gets less bandwidth per GPU. All else equal, it needs more GPUs for the same memory-bound throughput. That's more demand for HBM and packaging, not less. A weaker part can still be a bigger order.

Be precise about which competitive dimension this touches. This is a manufacturing and supply-chain datapoint: what HBM4 can actually be qualified and shipped at. It is not an architecture-leadership datapoint, and it does nothing to Nvidia's software and ecosystem position. CUDA didn't get slower this week.

So why would bandwidth fall at all? There are several competing explanations. Nvidia may be setting conservative launch clocks it can raise later. The original 22 may always have been an aspirational target rather than a qualified spec. Or it may be the memory itself. HWBusters reports that the implied HBM4 data rate fell from 11.7 to 9.6 gigabits per second per pin, citing reporting that suppliers are struggling to hit the original speed bins. That would make this a memory-supply problem, not an Nvidia design choice.

But there's a contradicting account. TrendForce's SK hynix earnings preview, published yesterday and citing Chosun Biz, says HBM4 shipments may have fallen short for a second straight quarter. It blames delays in Nvidia's own Rubin rollout and longer-than-expected performance tuning for mass-produced chips. That points the finger back at Nvidia's schedule, not supplier capability. Those two accounts conflict, and nothing on the record resolves which one dominates.

Keep the status straight, too. Rubin isn't shipping in volume yet. This is a pre-launch spec revision, not a field performance shortfall, and it's evidence about roadmap optimism, not a broken product. Now set it beside Friday the 23rd, when this desk covered Nvidia paying six back-end suppliers to expand substrate, test, and optics capacity. Jensen Huang's line then was, "Capacity is the constraint." Put the two stories together and, as inference, the lesson is this. Volume can be bought with prepayments. Per-stack memory performance, on this timeline, apparently cannot.

If HBM4 is where the spec gave way, the leverage sits with the people who make it. And the first of them reports tomorrow.

SK hynix is due to publish third-quarter results Wednesday. They haven't reported yet, so treat every number here as an expectation, not a result. TrendForce's preview headline calls for a record operating margin around 78 percent. Separately, the Seoul Economic Daily reports that HBM prices are expected to more than double next year as AI demand keeps supply tight. Both are forward-looking estimates, not confirmed outcomes.

Here's what a doubling would mean for everyone else. Memory would take a larger share of the accelerator bill of materials. That pressures gross margins at Nvidia, at AMD, and in the custom-ASIC programs, all of which buy the same stacks. SK hynix's print is the first test of how much of that is already showing up in its numbers.

This is also the compute-constrained versus economics-constrained split playing out in real time. Accelerator buyers are compute-constrained. They need the memory, and they'll pay up for it. PC and device buyers are economics-constrained. They absorb the spillover through pricier conventional DRAM as capacity shifts toward HBM. One side pays a premium for scarcity. The other eats the cost of it.

Now be skeptical of the "memory has escaped the cycle" story building ahead of tomorrow's print. A 78 percent margin is a peak-cycle number. Record margins are exactly what the top of an ordinary cyclical upswing looks like, and all three HBM makers are adding capacity. The Rubin cut complicates the bull case as well. If suppliers can't yet hit the pin speeds Nvidia wanted, their pricing power at that top tier weakens. Or Nvidia may simply have taken whatever bins were available. Both readings are live. So don't let tomorrow's margin settle a structural argument that one quarter can't settle.

Scarcity also decides who goes around the rules. Digitimes reported on the 23rd that the Israeli security firm Lava identified 32 Nvidia Blackwell Ultra B300 GPUs through monitoring endpoints linked to China Telecom. That's network telemetry, not physical evidence. The report cannot establish where those chips actually sit. They could be outside China entirely, for example in Southeast Asian data centers serving Chinese customers remotely. That would be a remote-access loophole, not smuggling, and it calls for a different policy fix. And 32 GPUs is a rounding error against a market measured in millions of cards. Its value is as an enforcement signal, not a volume signal.

Keep legal market share separate from physical access. An IDC report accessed by Reuters put Nvidia's share of China's data-center AI chip market at 34 percent in the first half of this year, down sharply as Huawei and other domestic suppliers fill the gap. That's months-old structural data, not a fresh print. But it frames the gray-channel story. Recall this desk's Thursday the 22nd report on Chinese hyperscalers over-ordering the export-compliant RTX PRO 5500. And on Saturday our Strategic Power desk reported that Taiwan plans to criminalize unauthorized shipments of advanced chips and AI servers to China, with no effective date yet set. Put together, and this is inference, enforcement looks set to shift from policing which chip is sold toward policing who gets remote access to compute. The next restriction is more likely to target cloud access than a new chip threshold.

One brief item for the watch list. Intel is reported to have signed Tesla as a customer for its 14A manufacturing node, chip unspecified. That speaks directly to the question this desk raised on Saturday: can Intel Foundry name an outside leading-edge customer at all. It's a reported engagement, not a wafer commitment. Intel's third-quarter earnings land Thursday, and that's the test of whether a chip, a volume, or any revenue sits behind the name.

So, the dates. SK hynix tomorrow. Intel and Samsung's divisional numbers Thursday. AMD next Tuesday, November 3rd. Also watch for any Nvidia statement on the Rubin spec, and keep half an eye on Wednesday's Fed decision as the financing backdrop for this capital-intensive buildout.

That's the briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
