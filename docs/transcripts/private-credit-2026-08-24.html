Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Credit & the Corporate Credit Cycle

Transcript · August 24, 2026

Good morning. It's Monday, August 24th, and this is your private credit briefing.

Start with Delaware Life. Three sessions ago, this briefing closed on the argument that in an asset class this opaque, repricing happens through disclosure before default. Here is that mechanism firing, three days later, and it didn't arrive as a missed payment. Delaware Life and Clear Spring, the annuity insurers inside Mark Walter's Group 1001, have restated their related-party investment exposure. The number previously filed with regulators was roughly one point four billion dollars, about three percent of investments. The corrected figure, as of the most recent year-end, is more than seventeen billion dollars, at least thirty-nine percent of total invested assets. That restatement follows grand-jury subpoenas the two companies received in February. Manhattan prosecutors are examining whether the insurers failed to disclose that their private-credit holdings were backing other Walter-controlled ventures, in a probe running in parallel with the SEC. S&P Global Ratings responded by cutting Delaware Life's outlook to negative while affirming the A-minus financial strength rating.

Be precise about what this is and isn't. No charges have been filed against Walter or against the companies. This is an investigation, not an adjudicated finding of wrongdoing. And it is one insurer's book, not evidence of a sector-wide practice. But the size of the error is the story, independent of whatever the investigation concludes. A related-party figure that was wrong by roughly twelve times is a controls failure on its own terms. And the S&P action is worth sitting with, because it admits two readings at once. Either the agency believes the underlying assets are sound and only the disclosure was wrong. Or a rating affirmed on a balance sheet that was misdescribed by a factor of twelve tells you how much independent verification actually sits behind these affirmations. I'm not picking one of those on no evidence. Hold both.

On the read-across question, the best available data point comes from a peer with no similar problem. Athene, the Apollo-backed annuity giant, formally classifies only seventeen percent of its assets as affiliated, and thirteen percent as related party, under insurance regulatory and accounting definitions. Athene also revealed this month that seventy-five percent of its assets are Apollo-originated. That second figure surfaced through an independent analysis of Athene's own disclosures, not through a regulator, so weigh it accordingly. The gap between the formal category and the economic reality is the real finding here. It does not prove any other insurer has Delaware Life's specific problem, which is a previously misstated figure now corrected. It shows that affiliated and related party are narrow regulatory boxes that can understate true sponsor concentration industry-wide, including at insurers under no federal scrutiny at all. So we cannot say from public filings whether Delaware Life's thirty-nine percent is an outlier or close to the norm, once you measure sponsor exposure the way Athene's own disclosure does. That we can't answer that is itself the finding.

On remediation, Walter's holding company, TWG Global, has entered a definitive agreement to exchange up to six and a half billion dollars of Delaware Life's affiliated investments for assets that aren't affiliated. No completion timeline has been confirmed. Treat that as agreed, not done.

So why does an insurance-book story matter to the rest of the credit machine? Because the funder is changing. You already know the retail wrapper for this asset class, the non-traded BDC, cratered eighty-two percent year over year last quarter. What's fresher is the shape of the institutional money replacing it. Ares raised more than thirty-six billion dollars in the second quarter, a record quarter, and ended it with a record hundred and seventy billion dollars of dry powder. Hold that number correctly. Dry powder is committed capital, not deployed capital and not performing capital, and it earns nothing until it is lent. Separately, Bloomberg reported on Thursday the twentieth that allocators are broadening away from concentrated corporate direct lending toward asset-based finance, meaning collateral-backed, cash-flow-visible structures rather than pure sponsor-backed corporate risk. The marker for that is KKR's Asset-Based Finance Partners Two, which pulled in six and a half billion dollars. Flag the timing, though: that fund closed in 2025, so it is being used as evidence for a 2026 rotation rather than as a 2026 data point. And nothing in the reporting establishes that Ares' own inflows are being steered away from the strategies showing stress. Record dry powder is undeployed by definition. That stays an open question.

Take the direction at face value, though, and it breaks a framing this show has been running since Wednesday. The argument was that retail's retreat merely changes the identity of the lender while the book itself stays the same. If new institutional allocations are genuinely tilting toward asset-based finance, then the corporate direct-lending book is losing its retail funder and its share of new commitments at the same time. It isn't the same book with a new funder. It's a different book. And the capital still arriving sits increasingly on balance sheets, insurance and asset-based vehicles, that don't mark to market daily. Which loops straight back to Delaware Life. The pool absorbing more of this risk is the pool least able to show you, in real time, what it is actually holding. Its stress surfaces in restatements, reserve assumptions and ratings actions rather than in a redemption queue, which means the warning arrives later and with less granularity than the one the wealth channel just gave us.

Now the asset side, because if that's who is funding the book, this is what they are being handed. Bloomberg's Credit Weekly on August the eighth is the most recent quantified read on this flow, so treat it as the freshest number rather than as this week's news. It reports companies with private debt refinancing into the syndicated bank market about three times more often than borrowers move the other way. Catalent is the showcase. It shifted four point one billion dollars of Ares and Blue Owl private loans into syndicated term loans, and cut its borrowing cost by about two hundred twenty-five basis points. Banks have the balance sheet to keep doing that: commercial and industrial loan growth ran at a fourteen point two percent annualized rate in the second quarter, against four point four percent a year earlier. And JPMorgan's roughly fifty-billion-dollar direct-lending allocation, reported back in April, says this is a build-out of permanent origination capacity rather than an opportunistic pricing window. An allocation is capacity, not loans written. But capacity is what changes who wins the next refinancing.

Here's the correction that matters, though. That three-times figure is a count of deals, not a count of dollars. The dollar flows are close to parity. Roughly nine point two billion dollars moved from private credit to banks through mid-July, against about nine billion moving the other way. So this is not a rout out of private credit. It's many small, clean credits leaving one at a time, while a few large deals still arrive in the opposite direction. That is the analytically uncomfortable part, because a count-weighted exodus of the better, more refinanceable names is exactly the shape adverse selection takes. And the Catalent saving measures it. Two hundred twenty-five basis points is a direct read on an illiquidity premium that borrower was paying and evidently never needed to.

Follow that through the portfolio arithmetic, because it cuts two ways at once. When the strongest credits prepay fastest, the residual book gets worse without a single new default, and the reported non-accrual ratio rises on the denominator alone. That residual is the same book we covered last week as running non-accruals at a median two point eight percent of loan cost across the twenty largest BDCs, the highest reading since 2017. Meanwhile whatever replaces the credits that left comes on at a lower spread, so net investment income and dividend coverage compress before any credit loss is recognized at all. Then set record dry powder against a shrinking pool of borrowers the banks don't want, and you have deployment pressure at exactly the moment underwriting discipline matters most. Deployment pressure is how lenders end up in newer collateral types and thinner structures.

So pull it together. Two migrations are running at once. On the asset side, the better credits are leaving for banks, leaving a thinner, more cheaply compensated residual with direct lenders. On the funding side, the wealth channel has retreated, new institutional money appears to be tilting toward asset-based structures, and insurance capital, the pool with the weakest and now visibly unreliable disclosure, becomes more of the marginal lender by default. The cycle isn't cracked. It's grinding, and the composition of what's left is deteriorating faster than the headline default numbers show. The biggest unresolved risk in this asset class right now is not a default rate. It's disclosure. Watch three things into quarter-end. Whether any other insurer-manager combination draws a similar restatement or ratings action. The completion of Delaware Life's asset swap. And Cliffwater's third-quarter repurchase window, which we've flagged all week and which closes August 31st, followed by the third-quarter non-accrual prints, which tell you whether the residual book direct lenders are left holding is deteriorating as fast as the flow data implies.
