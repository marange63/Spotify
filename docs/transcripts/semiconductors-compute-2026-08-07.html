Cautious Optimism Briefings

Semiconductors & Compute Infrastructure

Transcript · August 7, 2026

Good morning, it's Friday, August 7th, 2026, and this is your semiconductors and compute infrastructure briefing.

July was the worst month for chip stocks since October 2008. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell roughly 21 percent, erasing about 2.2 trillion dollars in market value and leaving the index 23 percent below its June 22nd record high. On nearly half the month's trading days the index moved at least 4 percent, and every single session had an intraday swing of at least 2 percent, a level of volatility the sector hasn't seen since 2020. TSMC fell 15 percent, Micron dropped 29 percent, and Intel sank 35 percent, its steepest monthly decline since September 2000. And yet retail investors treated the rout as a buying opportunity, pouring a record 12 billion dollars into semiconductor ETFs in a single week, even as institutions were selling.

Here's the paradox that matters: the fundamentals kept beating all month. TSMC guided revenue above consensus. AMD's data-center business grew over 100 percent year over year. Memory prices kept rising. So this was not a demand collapse. It was a repricing of AI capex economics — investors deciding that revenue growth and backlog are no longer sufficient on their own, and that capital intensity and margin durability now have to clear their own bar. Nothing shows that shift more clearly than what happened inside a single earnings report last Tuesday.

AMD beat on both lines. Revenue came in at 11.5 billion dollars, ahead of consensus, with data-center revenue up 107 percent year over year to 6.7 billion dollars. The company guided next quarter to roughly 13 billion dollars in revenue. And the stock still fell — roughly 8 to 9 percent after hours, according to multiple outlets tracking the after-hours tape. The headline explanation making the rounds is a gross-margin miss: AMD's GAAP gross margin came in at 54 percent, against a sell-side consensus estimate of 56 percent. But that framing needs a correction, because on the earnings call itself, CFO Jean Hu described AMD's own non-GAAP gross margin — the metric the company actually guides to and the one Wall Street models track — as having expanded to 56 percent, up over 200 basis points year over year and 80 basis points sequentially, on favorable product mix and growing data-center contribution. So the "miss" that's being reported is really a GAAP-versus-non-GAAP framing gap, not a clean shortfall on a single consistent metric. Secondary analysis has tied near-term margin pressure to the cost and yield-maturation curve of AMD's Helios AI rack system, which uses high-bandwidth memory intensively, though that link comes from analyst commentary rather than anything management said on the call.

What's not in dispute is capital expenditure: it roughly tripled, from 282 million dollars a year ago to 808 million dollars this quarter. That's the number investors actually punished. Even where AMD's preferred margin metric expanded and the top line beat, the market sold the capex acceleration. Growth plus an expanding margin still wasn't enough — that's the new bar. And note who that new bar points at next: Nvidia reports soon, and if the market is now grading capital discipline even on a triple-digit-growth quarter, the accelerator leader will face the same scrutiny AMD just drew, not a free pass on its own buildout.

Push that same test to its extreme and you get Intel, whose stock fell 35 percent in July for the worst month since September 2000. The technical story here is genuinely good: 18A output ran about 25 percent above internal targets and up more than 50 percent quarter over quarter, and Panther Lake chip costs are down roughly 50 percent year to date. None of that moved the stock, because Intel's Foundry division still posted a 2.1 billion dollar operating loss, and external — meaning paying, non-Intel — foundry revenue was only about 293 million dollars, roughly 5 percent of total foundry revenue. Management guided capex above 20 billion dollars for this year and said 2027 spending would be significantly higher still. Intel also took a 12.5 billion dollar mark-to-market accounting loss on shares held in escrow for the U.S. government's stake under the CHIPS Act, though that's an optics event, not operating deterioration. The real story is that capex without a proven external customer base is getting punished regardless of how good the yield numbers look. Intel's technology is working. Its commercial proof point isn't there yet. And that punishment isn't just cosmetic: a sustained selloff tightens the capital-markets valve for exactly the most capex-heavy names, the ones that most need external financing to fund the buildout in the first place.

But here's what the tape is missing: the physical demand signal is still red hot. TrendForce reported this week that TSMC's 3-nanometer monthly wafer starts will hit 180,000 by early in the fourth quarter, two to three months ahead of prior expectations, on strong AI and high-performance-computing demand. That's on top of TSMC's July capex raise to 60 to 64 billion dollars for the year and 2-nanometer capacity that's already booked out into 2028. And Samsung's foundry business, after a long stretch of underutilization, is reportedly nearing full utilization in the second half of this year, with 2-nanometer design wins expected to more than double, driven by Broadcom and cloud AI-HPC customers. Yields there remain an open question, so call this a potential turning point rather than a confirmed one. But taken together, capacity is being pulled forward, not cut. That tells you the sector's problem right now is economics, not availability — and a genuine Samsung recovery would be the first real evidence of a viable second source at the leading edge, which matters for pricing leverage against TSMC.

That capacity story runs straight into pricing power, the one competitive dimension where the durability question is genuinely live — and there's a twist. If AMD's HBM cost pressure is real, the flip side is pricing power for the memory makers who supply that high-bandwidth memory, SK hynix and Samsung. And on memory pricing overall, contract prices are still rising: DRAM up 13 to 18 percent quarter over quarter in the third quarter, NAND up 10 to 15 percent. But that's a sharp deceleration from the second quarter's 58 to 63 percent DRAM surge. The supercycle is cresting, not collapsing. Meanwhile China's CXMT debuted on Shanghai's STAR Market in late July in Asia's largest IPO of the year, raising about 8.6 billion dollars, with shares briefly surging as much as 466 percent. CXMT already holds nearly 8 percent of global DRAM share, ranking it fourth worldwide, and the IPO proceeds are earmarked for capacity expansion. That's a real structural entrant. But it's a multi-year threat, not a 2026 one — CXMT remains well behind on leading-edge process and has no meaningful HBM capability, so Samsung, SK hynix, and Micron keep their near-term pricing power intact.

Put it together and the thesis holds across all four stories: July's crash reprices what the market will pay for AI capex, not how much AI compute the world wants. The chips that get punished going forward will be the ones burning cash ahead of proven customers and defensible margins. The ones that hold up will be the ones with capacity, contracted demand, and pricing power already in hand.
