Good morning. It's Sunday, August 9th, 2026.

All week this show has been tracking a single pattern: announced power outrunning delivered power. Texas froze its data center interconnection queue on paper. Washington slapped a polysilicon tariff on paper, effective four months out. The Fed chair talked tough on paper while the thirty-year yield did the opposite. Today that pattern breaks, and it breaks on the other side of the ledger. The delivered capacity this week isn't American — it's the capacity an adversary just lost.

Start with Russia. Ukraine's General Staff estimated on July 4th that just under 43 percent of Russian oil refining capacity had been disabled by drone strikes — that's Kyiv's own battle-damage assessment, not an independently verified figure, so treat the precise number with some caution. But it isn't an outlier. Independent Western analytics corroborate the scale, if not the exact figure: Bloomberg, citing EA Analytics, reported that Russian refineries processed an estimated 3.6 million barrels a day in July — the lowest throughput since May of 2002, roughly a third below the normal seasonal run rate. Two different methodologies, capacity disabled versus throughput lost, landing in the same 33-to-40 percent band. That convergence is what makes this credible: strikes on Russian energy targets ran 194 times in the first half of 2026, an eleven-fold jump from a year earlier, and something in that range is actually offline.

The knock-on effect showed up at home in Russia, not just at the export terminal. Russian diesel exports reportedly collapsed to around 187,000 barrels a day in the first week of July, before Moscow redirected what supply it had to the domestic market — and by July, fuel restrictions were affecting roughly 50 million Russians, about 35 percent of the population. That's a wartime economy rationing fuel to its own citizens because the refineries that would otherwise export it are on fire. And that domestic strain matters beyond the export math: fuel rationing raises the political cost of the war for Moscow directly, independent of whether Western sanctions ever bite — a pressure point that could weigh more at a future negotiating table than anything in Brussels' package.

Compare that to the West's parallel track: the EU agreed its 21st sanctions package on July 23rd. The headline framing floating around described it as a full phase-out of Russian oil, gas, and nuclear energy — but the actual measures adopted are narrower and more conditional than that: a temporary suspension of the oil price-cap mechanism, a continued exemption letting Russian LNG keep flowing to third countries for roughly another year, an exemption carved out for one nuclear project, and a transaction ban limited to a single refinery. So the honest read is: Ukrainian drones have done more measurable, near-term damage to Russian productive capacity in months than the EU's latest sanctions round will do by its own more modest, partially-exempted terms. Kinetic action is outperforming the paper instrument built to achieve the same goal. The so-what: if you're pricing European diesel cracks this winter, the input that matters is Ukrainian strike tempo, not Brussels' sanctions calendar.

The same delivered-versus-paper gap runs the other way on critical minerals. China can de-escalate its headline tariff fight with Washington while still landing a precision strike underneath it. On June 22nd, Beijing's Commerce Ministry added ten US firms to its export control list — including MP Materials and USA Rare Earth, which happen to be the two largest recipients of US federal rare-earth independence funding, at 550 million and 1.6 billion dollars respectively. That's not a random retaliation list. Those are the exact two companies the Pentagon is counting on to break dependence on Chinese refining. And the leverage behind that move is structural, not improvised: China accounted for 61 percent of global mined rare-earth supply and 91 percent of global refining and processing capacity in 2024. The broader truce — China's suspension of its October 2025 export controls — holds until November 10th, 2026. So Beijing is proving it can keep the big trade détente intact while still squeezing the specific firms that threaten its refining monopoly.

Whether the entity listing actually cuts inputs those two companies need, or is mostly symbolic pressure, isn't confirmed yet. What is confirmed is the calendar: November 10th is now a dated flashpoint, the moment the market finds out whether this truce gets extended or lapses. And there's a second-order effect already in motion: every month Beijing demonstrates that leverage, the investment case for non-China refining gets stronger — MP's own Fort Worth magnet plant, Lynas, the Pentagon's offtake deals — truce or no truce, which also makes November 10th a natural volatility date for defense and critical-minerals equities. The so-what: rare-earth independence funding is real money, but 91 percent refining share is real leverage, and right now leverage is winning.

That contest over physical inputs has a domestic mirror in the grid. We walked through Texas's own interconnection freeze earlier this week — Governor Abbott's pause on new data center grid connections against a queue of more than 474 gigawatts, roughly five times ERCOT's own peak demand record. The federal counterpart to that lands this month. FERC issued show-cause orders back in June against the six major grid operators — PJM, MISO, SPP, CAISO, ISO-New England, and NYISO — preliminarily finding their tariffs unjust for failing to properly handle large-load interconnection requests. Those operators had roughly 30 days to file reliability data, and they have roughly 60 days, landing around mid-August, to file revised tariffs or justify why their existing rules already meet the bar. This is the structural version of what Texas did state by state, done at the federal level across the entire eastern and central grid.

But notice what it isn't yet: a deadline to file is not a resolved rule. Operators can defend the status quo. The outcome that would actually reprice AI capex — forcing data centers to bear the full cost of the substations and transmission upgrades they trigger — is still undecided. And if it does land on the data centers, the effect isn't just higher hyperscaler power bills; it's geographic. Capacity migrates to whichever jurisdiction offers the most predictable, fastest interconnect, so a patchwork of divergent state and federal rules quietly decides where the next wave of AI buildout physically goes. So the state-to-federal escalation is real, but like most of this week's domestic story, it's authority moving before capacity does.

One place this week where the West's own capacity is genuinely ahead of schedule: NATO defense spending. European Allies and Canada increased defense spending 20 percent versus 2024, and the alliance's own July 7th update shows five members already meeting the new 3.5 percent core-defense guideline and seventeen meeting the broader 1.5 percent security guideline — both years ahead of the 2035 deadline. Germany alone is on a path from 86 billion euros to a planned 152 billion by 2029. Budget guidelines met on schedule are still commitments rather than fielded weapons systems, so the gap between appropriated money and delivered hardware remains the open question for European primes' order books. But relative to everything else on today's ledger, this is the one item that's structural, funded, and running ahead of plan rather than behind it — the exception that proves the pattern.

That's the state of play: kinetic force beating sanctions in Russia, targeted leverage beating trade truce optics in rare earths, federal authority still chasing grid capacity at home, and European rearmament as the lone case of delivery outrunning the announcement. That's your briefing for August 9th. Have a good day.
