Good morning. It's Tuesday, October 13th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

Here's the thesis for today. Last week the chip equity market and the memory contract market handed down opposite verdicts on whether AI demand actually lasts. This week's prints from ASML and TSMC start to settle which one had it right.

Start with the break. For weeks, the evidence on chip demand pointed one way. Scarcity pricing was spreading through the stack, in memory contracts, in foundry pricing, and in packaging capacity. Thursday, October 8th, delivered the first real challenge to that picture. And it came from the customer side, not the supply side. The Financial Times reported that OpenAI's annualized revenue stood at approximately fifty billion dollars as of September. That's far short of the roughly seventy billion dollars that multiple outlets had reported late last month. Chip and AI-infrastructure stocks sold off. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index finished the week lower, while the broader market proved more resilient.

Be careful about what that number does and doesn't prove. The reporting we have doesn't establish why the two figures sit twenty billion dollars apart, so we won't guess at it. What we do have is OpenAI's response. Bloomberg reported on October 9th that the company expects its annualized revenue to reach at least seventy billion dollars by year-end. Take that at face value, and OpenAI needs to add about twenty billion dollars of annualized revenue in a single quarter. By our arithmetic, that's roughly forty percent on top of September's base. It's an aggressive target, and it's a company forecast, not a result. And here's the telling absence. We found no chip supplier or named analyst on record cutting a capex or HBM demand forecast in response. The selloff repriced sentiment and concentration risk. It did not visibly change anyone's model of demand.

There's also a positioning story. Nvidia set a record high on October 5th, up roughly twenty-five percent year to date. By October 8th it had retreated from above two hundred forty-three dollars toward two hundred thirty. TSMC had hit its own record that same October 5th. Micron fell alongside the other memory producers. When a whole complex has run that hard, an ambiguous headline doesn't need to be true to move the tape. It just needs to give traders a reason to take profit. So what this really exposed is how narrow the evidence is under multi-year AI-infrastructure valuations. One lab's revenue number was enough to move the whole chip complex in a single week.

That points to the distinction that matters most for chip demand. AI data-center demand has been constrained by availability, with buyers ready to take more if they could get it. Last week's selloff asks a different question. Can the end customers' economics keep funding that demand? Those are separate questions. Compute can be scarce today while the economics stay unproven. The OpenAI episode didn't answer the economic question. It only reminded the market that the question exists.

If the equity market blinked, the contract market didn't. Micron's fiscal fourth quarter, reported at the end of September, brought revenue of about fifty-four billion dollars in the single quarter. That's nearly five times the eleven billion of a year earlier. Micron's 2027 contract book isn't new. Its timing against the selloff is. In its prepared remarks, management said it has completed agreements for the vast majority of its calendar 2027 HBM bit supply. It said those agreements carry significant price increases year over year. And it said HBM revenue grew faster than total company revenue in the quarter, though it gave no dollar figure for HBM itself. That's the contract market's verdict on the same durability question. And it was locked in at the end of September, before the OpenAI headline existed.

Here's the tension, though. A pre-sold contract is only as durable as the buyer's funding. Look at how OpenAI's compute is being paid for. SoftBank has turned to junk bonds to fund its commitments to OpenAI. So a locked HBM contract and a nervous equity market aren't necessarily contradicting each other. The contracts show committed intent. The equities may be starting to price the counterparty and funding risk behind that intent. On this narrow point, our lean is that the equity market carries more information. Contracts record what buyers promised. Prices reflect whether investors believe those buyers can keep paying. That's a judgment call, not a settled fact.

The other threat to memory pricing comes from supply, not demand. A DIGITIMES roundup published October 12th reported that Samsung's twelve-layer HBM4E passed customer quality validation at Nvidia and major hyperscale data-center operators. Samsung said it could not confirm the report. No production volume or timing was disclosed. Treat it as rumor-grade. And keep the dimensions separate. Customer qualification is not volume production, and volume production is not allocation share. The incumbent with the most to lose is SK hynix. Its SEC filings, citing IDC, put it at a fifty-six point four percent share of the HBM market by revenue in the first quarter of this year. Even if Samsung's validation is real, it mostly presses on 2028 HBM pricing rather than 2027. That's because Micron has already said most of next year's bit supply is spoken for.

That matters for how long memory's pricing power lasts, which is openly disputed. Micron's CEO, Sanjay Mehrotra, says memory supply won't balance until 2028. Acer's CEO, Jason Chen, expects prices to start easing in late 2027. Chen's case rests on new Chinese DRAM and NAND capacity, not on high-bandwidth memory. A third qualified HBM supplier is what would make Acer's earlier timeline right for HBM as well. So 2028 is where memory's two exposures meet. A capex slowdown would hit the next contracting round from the demand side. A qualified Samsung would hit that same round from the supply side. So the real test isn't whether Samsung gets qualified. It's whether a third supplier shows up in time for that next round.

Beyond memory, the past week was light on hard industrial news. We found no fresh primary-source developments on packaging capacity, equipment orders, or export controls. The quiet is itself informative. No supplier rushed out a guidance change after the OpenAI headline. That puts extra weight on this week's two earnings reports as the industrial evidence.

ASML reports Wednesday, October 14th. It guided the quarter to total net sales of eleven to twelve billion euros, with a gross margin of fifty-five to fifty-seven percent. TSMC reports full results on Thursday, October 15th. Its quarterly revenue is already known from monthly disclosures, at about one point four nine trillion New Taiwan dollars. That's roughly fifty-one percent above a year ago. Neither company has reported margins or outlook yet, so treat every other number as guidance, not a result.

Both businesses were largely booked and contracted before the OpenAI headline. So a clean beat on either won't tell you much on its own. The forward language will. Listen first for ASML's bookings commentary on 2027 and 2028 lithography demand. Equipment sits furthest from the end customer. Fab-equipment names, ASML among them, had already sold off in mid-September on worry that AI spending could decelerate faster than guided. If ASML's bookings language holds, the buildout's planning horizon hasn't moved. Then listen for TSMC's tone on 2027 capex and advanced-packaging capacity. Those are the parts of either release that carry new information into this debate.

So here's where that leaves us. The equity market just found out how narrow the evidence is under its own valuations. The contract market has already made its bet. This week's two prints won't close that gap. But the forward commentary inside each one will show which side is reading the room correctly.
