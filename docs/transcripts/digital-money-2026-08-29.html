Cautious Optimism Briefings

Digital Money, Crypto, and Market Structure

Transcript · August 29, 2026

Good morning, it's Saturday, August 29th, and this is your digital money briefing.

Start with the boldest claim on the beat this week. Grayscale published research on August 27th on Bitcoin's correlations. Bitcoin's 90-day correlation with gold has climbed above 50 percent, up from near zero at the start of this year. Over the same stretch, its correlation with the Nasdaq 100 fell from above 60 percent to roughly 33 percent. Layer August's price action on top and the story writes itself. Bitcoin is up 26 percent this month, against gold's 13.8 percent and the Nasdaq's 4.8. Read straight, that is Bitcoin graduating from high-beta tech proxy to gold-adjacent debasement hedge. It's the strongest bullish interpretation available this week, and it deserves to be stated plainly before we pull it apart.

Now the turn. The window that produces this correlation shift is the same window this show spent five straight days establishing was a short squeeze. In the 24 hours ending early on August 20th, short liquidations hit 1.74 billion dollars. That was the second-largest such total on record. Forced covering, not fresh conviction buying, is what kicked the month's rally into gear. A 90-day correlation whose entire change sits inside one month is a statement about that month. And that month contained a record liquidation cascade. Any correlation computed across a forced unwind will look decoupled from equities almost by construction. The move simply wasn't driven by the flows that push tech stocks around.

There is also a publisher problem worth naming. Grayscale is an ETF issuer. A research note concluding that its own asset class has become a gold-adjacent macro hedge is a seller's claim about a seller's product. That doesn't make it wrong. It makes it a claim that needs an outside check, and no outside check has arrived. We could not retrieve the Grayscale note itself either. The coefficients here are as reported by Yahoo Finance and CoinDesk, not verified against the primary publication. So here is the fact, stated narrowly. Bitcoin's equity beta roughly halved over one measurement window. Whether that's a mandate change or a squeeze artifact is not yet knowable. And 33 percent is still a real, positive correlation. This is not decoupling. It's diminished coupling.

There is one flow check available, and it cuts both ways. US spot Bitcoin ETFs have taken in roughly 3.5 billion dollars net this month, across nineteen trading days, fifteen of them positive. That is real allocator money arriving, and it is the kind of buying a genuine reclassification would produce. But it is also exactly what a squeeze-driven price move pulls in behind it. Flows follow price here as readily as they lead it. So the money is showing up, and it does not settle the mandate question.

If it is real, the consequence is an allocation question rather than a price call. Bitcoin priced as a gold-adjacent macro hedge sits in a different sleeve than Bitcoin priced as high-beta Nasdaq. Different sizing, different drawdown tolerance, different mandate language. The mirror of that is the risk. A durable break from equity correlation is exactly what removes the assumption that Bitcoin sells off automatically in an equity drawdown. That assumption is an input into how multi-asset and risk-parity desks size the position. One month of data cannot support changing it, and sizing up on this evidence is the specific error the data invites. What has to hold for the reclassification to survive is simple. The correlation has to persist through a month with no squeeze behind it.

That leaves an obvious next question. If the correlation shift rode on top of a leverage event, what does leverage look like right now? That's the number this show has chased for five days running. Until now nobody had published current funding and open-interest readings we could stand behind. Today we have a partial answer. It arrives in a shape that reframes the question rather than settling it.

VanEck's mid-August ChainCheck put Bitcoin open interest around 51.4 billion dollars, up roughly 7.3 percent over the trailing 30 days. Funding had flipped decisively positive, running at an annualized 8 to 15 percent, and positive in 88 of the prior 90 eight-hour windows. Read alone, that looks like a long book being rebuilt fast.

Now set it against the coin-denominated figure this show quoted on the 27th, which sat at a five-month low over almost the same stretch. A fresh, independently sourced reading obtained this week points the same way. One aggregator has coin-denominated Bitcoin futures open interest contracting by roughly 11 percent into late August, even as price climbed sharply. Remember that Bitcoin was up 26 percent on the month. A dollar notional that rose 7.3 percent while the underlying asset rose 26 percent is exposure being marked up by price. It is not leverage being added. The number of contracts outstanding was shrinking the whole time. So leverage is rebuilding in dollars and shrinking in coins, and the coin count is the measure that tracks positioning rather than price. Our five-day question resolves into a denominator dispute, not an answer.

Two limits, stated directly. Sources disagree sharply on the absolute coin open-interest level, so I won't hand you a precise number. The direction, contraction in coin terms, is the part that holds up across both readings. And on funding, VanEck's positive reading is a mid-August snapshot. Nothing confirms it is still the state of play today. Treat funding as unconfirmed since, not as continuing on the same track.

What we can say cleanly is about the level, not the trend. Funding positive at 8 to 15 percent annualized is ordinary carry. It is not the kind of extreme reading that has historically preceded a forced unwind of longs. Positive funding tells you longs are paying to stay long. It does not tell you a liquidation cascade is coming.

If the leverage layer's signal depends on which denominator you pick, the liquidity layer's depends on composition rather than quantity. Total stablecoin market cap hit a record 308 billion dollars, up 14.3 percent year over year. That headline hides a real divergence underneath it.

Tether's USDT has shed 4 billion dollars in market cap over two months, as of mid-August. Nearly 870 million of that went in the most recent eleven-day window alone. That reading is roughly two and a half weeks stale at this point, and it may have partly reversed since.

USDC carries the more interesting number. Its annualized on-chain velocity runs at 741 times a year, against USDT's 74. That's a tenfold gap, on a float more than 100 billion dollars smaller. It's the cleanest evidence available on which dollars in this market are actually working, versus parked on an issuer's balance sheet. The caveat is that velocity partly measures function, not victory. USDC is disproportionately the exchange and trading-venue dollar. USDT is more the offshore savings dollar. Some of that gap is simply what each token is for.

CryptoQuant reads the USDT contraction more bullishly. Its view is that the deepest USDT contraction phases have historically marked points where selling pressure was closer to exhaustion than to further acceleration. That's the firm's interpretation, not a fact. And the firm hedges it itself, conceding that redemptions and spot selling likely respond to the same risk-off conditions rather than one leading the other. Don't mistake it for a bottom call.

The second-order piece is worth sizing honestly. A structurally shrinking USDT float is a Treasury-bill demand story as much as a crypto story. Sustained redemptions mean bills sold, or not rolled, at the front end of the curve. No figure for Tether's bill holdings is in evidence today. So the direction is fair to state and the magnitude is not. Treat it as small against the broader bill market, not as a market-moving flow. And if USDC keeps gaining velocity share, the winners are the distribution partners who earn on turnover rather than on float. Fee-and-network monetization is replacing rate rent as reserve yields compress.

Zoom out and the pattern across all three items is the same. A correlation regime, a leverage rebuild, and a liquidity signal. Three claimed developments this week, and each one turns on how it was measured rather than on what actually happened. The gold correlation is real for one month. Whether it survives a month without a squeeze behind it is the test. The leverage rebuild is real in dollars and false in coins. Watch whether coin-denominated open interest starts rising, not just dollar notional. Keep both tests in mind before you treat any of this as a settled regime change.

That's the briefing for August 29th. Have a good weekend.
