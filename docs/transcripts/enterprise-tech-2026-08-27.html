Cautious Optimism Briefings

Enterprise Technology & Software

Good morning, it's Thursday, August 27th, 2026, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

Yesterday we flagged that CrowdStrike and Salesforce were reporting after the close. We called them the first real test of the AI-displacement fear that hit software stocks on August 20th. That fear has a specific mechanism, so state it plainly before anything else. On August 20th, Bloomberg reported that Anthropic was preparing to file publicly for a share sale, and four widely held enterprise software names sold off together that afternoon. The reasoning relayed from trading desks was that a public filing would show, for the first time, how much enterprise money Anthropic already collects directly from the same customers these vendors bill by the seat. If the lab is already being paid for the work, the per-seat subscription is the line item at risk. That is the fear. Nobody has seen the filing.

The prints are in, and Okta reported alongside CrowdStrike and Salesforce. All three beat. All three rallied hard. But the composition of those beats is not what the displacement story predicted, and that composition is the real news.

Start with Salesforce. Revenue for the second quarter of fiscal 2027 came in at eleven point three five billion dollars, up eleven percent year over year. That beat a consensus near eleven point three two billion. Shares jumped roughly fourteen percent in after-hours trading. On the headline, that reads as vindication for the AI-enterprise story. The composition is stranger. Net income jumped eighty-seven percent to three point five three billion dollars. Salesforce disclosed a two point six billion dollar gain on strategic investments tied to its stake in Anthropic. On our own arithmetic, that single mark is larger than the entire year-over-year increase in net income. It is a valuation mark on a private stake, not cash from customers, and it reverses if Anthropic's next round prices flat or lower.

Sit with the irony there. The prospect of an Anthropic filing is what knocked this whole software complex down six days earlier. Now Anthropic's private valuation is the largest single line in one of those same companies' earnings beat. Same entity, opposite sign, one week apart.

Strip that mark out and the operating story is more modest. Current remaining performance obligation grew fourteen percent to thirty-three point five billion dollars. Free cash flow rose eighty-one percent to one point one billion dollars, comfortably above consensus. Salesforce raised full-year revenue guidance to a range of forty-six point one to forty-six point four billion dollars, about eleven percent growth at the midpoint. That is solid and unremarkable for a company this size. It is not evidence of an AI-driven acceleration.

Now the number everyone was waiting for. Agentforce annualized revenue passed one point five billion dollars, up more than two hundred forty percent year over year. Combined with Data Cloud, that AI-product run-rate reached nearly three point nine billion. Hold two things about those figures at once. They are annualized run-rates, not recognized revenue, so they do not line up against the reported revenue line. And set the one point five billion against the forty-six billion dollar full-year guide, and on our own arithmetic — not a Salesforce disclosure — Agentforce is still a low single-digit share of the company. Even the combined figure is only high single digits on that same math. Name the tension rather than resolving it. This is real, it is compounding quickly, and it is not yet big enough to move the corporate growth rate. Salesforce also disclosed seven billion cumulative Agentic Work Units delivered across Agentforce and Slack, with three point two billion in the quarter, up ninety-seven percent sequentially. Treat that one carefully. The company has never published what one Agentic Work Unit equals in dollars, and no other vendor reports anything like it. Ninety-seven percent growth in a unit only the vendor can define benchmarks against nothing.

There is a deeper wrinkle underneath. Salesforce has restructured how it discloses revenue. It no longer breaks out Sales Cloud and Service Cloud at all. It now reports one bucket called Agentforce Apps, which bundles Sales, Service, Marketing, Commerce and Slack together. A second bucket is called Data 360, Headless Platform, and Other. We could not confirm this quarter's figures for those two buckets from a primary source, so take the most recent quarter where they are confirmed. There, the bucket carrying the Agentforce name grew only about nine percent. The Data 360 bucket grew about twenty-five percent.

In other words, the revenue line branded with the AI product name is mostly older CRM subscription revenue growing slowly. The faster-growing data infrastructure revenue sits in a bucket with a different name. So the question everyone wants answered — whether core subscription growth excluding AI is decelerating — is one Salesforce's own disclosure no longer lets you answer. That is a disclosure-quality problem as much as a growth problem. The honest read is this: the operating business is fine, the AI-specific run-rate is real but small, and the label and the substance point in opposite directions.

Salesforce showed you its fastest-growing number. CrowdStrike showed you its mechanism.

CrowdStrike's quarter is the clean counterexample. Revenue hit one point four seven billion dollars, up twenty-six percent, a modest beat against consensus. The number that matters is net new ARR. A record three hundred thirty-three million dollars, growing fifty-one percent while the installed base grew twenty-five. Be careful what that gap is. Net new ARR is a bookings-style metric off a much smaller base, not recognized revenue. But the direction is real. New commitments are accelerating while the installed base compounds steadily.

The disclosed driver is Falcon Flex, CrowdStrike's platform-consolidation contract vehicle. Ending ARR from Flex accounts now exceeds two point two nine billion dollars, up one hundred one percent year over year. Module adoption is deepening alongside it. More than half of subscription customers now run six or more Falcon modules, and about a quarter run eight or more. Non-GAAP operating margin rose to twenty-five percent from twenty-two. Shares rose more than eleven percent. Press coverage framed all of this as AI-threat demand. CrowdStrike disclosed no AI revenue attribution whatsoever. The driver it did disclose is customers collapsing point-product security budgets into one larger contract. So where a vendor actually shows you the mechanism, the mechanism is bundling. And the real test of Falcon Flex is not this quarter's bookings. It is whether those consolidated contracts hold at renewal, because a vehicle like this front-loads commitment well ahead of consumption.

That is the vendor that was never part of the displacement trade. Now the one that was.

Okta was one of the four software names sold off on August 20th, and it fell about five percent — purely on that report about an Anthropic filing nobody has actually seen. This week gave us the first hard number since then, and it went the other way. Revenue came in at eight hundred five million dollars, beating Okta's own guidance range of seven hundred ninety to seven hundred ninety-four million. Current remaining performance obligation grew fourteen percent to two point five eight five billion, ahead of the company's own eleven percent guide. Full-year guidance was raised to ten to eleven percent revenue growth. Shares surged about nineteen percent.

That is a very large move for a company guiding to low double-digit growth, and the honest interpretation is narrower than the headlines. This is a positioning unwind, not proof of agent-driven demand. Okta disclosed no metric for non-human or agentic identity revenue, so none of that framing comes from the company. And the raised full-year guide is still low double digits, not a step up to a new growth tier. There is a second thing hanging over Okta that this print does not touch. The bull case for identity was that it is one of the few layers a model vendor cannot absorb — deciding which agent may act as whom, and logging what it did. Anthropic shipped enterprise-managed authorization as a platform feature this week, which puts that bet in question. A beat-and-raise quarter does not settle that. What the quarter does prove is narrower. The August 20th selloff was priced on a document nobody has read, and the first actual result since then did not support the fear.

Pull back and look at all three prints together. Three companies beat, three rallied, and not one of them disclosed what share of its growth came from AI. Salesforce gave you a self-defined work-unit count. CrowdStrike and Okta gave you nothing at all on attribution. The market spent last week repricing software down on a forecast about AI displacement, and this week repriced it back up on results that do not settle that question either way. What would settle it is a disclosure none of these vendors made: AI revenue in a unit that is comparable across companies. Until that shows up, watch the buckets, not the branding.
