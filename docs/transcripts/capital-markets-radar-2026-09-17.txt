Good morning, it's Thursday, September 17th, and this is your capital markets radar.

On Monday this desk argued that the painful outcome was not a hike. It was a hike paired with a signal of more to come. The funds that configuration hurts are trend-following CTAs and vol-target funds, who came into this week long equities and carrying a record short book in government bonds. A hawkish hike breaks their equity leg while doing nothing to relieve that bond short. That is the path the Fed took on Wednesday afternoon. We were right about the setup. We were not right about what breaks, and the tape is already arguing with us on that point.

Start with the decision, because the mechanics matter for everything downstream. The FOMC voted unanimously to raise the federal funds target range by a quarter point, to 3.75 to 4.00 percent. That is reported as the first increase since 2023, and it closes a cycle the market had spent two years assuming was the regime. The projections did the rest of the work. Sixteen of eighteen participants now see at least one more quarter-point hike before the end of this year. And by the close, traders were pricing three hikes through June of next year. Keep those two claims separate: the dot plot is the committee's projection, the three-hike path is the market's own pricing on top of it.

The quarter point itself was not the news. It was roughly 92 percent priced going in, on CME FedWatch. The surprise came through a different door. Kevin Warsh, at his first press conference as chair, said he would be, quote, hard-pressed to describe broad financial conditions as restrictive. That framing did more damage than the 25 basis points. It tells you the bar for a pause just moved higher, and it takes away the anchor investors had been using to guess where the terminal rate sits. When you cannot locate the ceiling, you widen the distribution around every discount rate in the book.

Look at where the repricing actually landed on the curve, because it answers a question this desk left open yesterday morning, before the statement. The two-year Treasury yield jumped more than 7 basis points, to 4.738 percent. The ten-year rose only 2 basis points, to 5.016 percent. That is a front-end-led bear flattening. Yesterday morning we could not say whether the move toward 5 percent on the long end was real rates and term premium or inflation expectations. This session says it is mostly neither. It is the market pricing an actual policy path, in the part of the curve where policy lives. The practical consequence is that the long-duration discount-rate damage from this repricing is smaller than a ten-year at 5 percent, taken alone, would suggest.

Equities still did not like it. The S&P 500 fell 1 percent, to its lowest close since July. The Dow dropped 1.7 percent, with financial shares leading the decline. The Nasdaq's fall was the mildest of the three major indexes, which is itself a clue: if this were a pure long-duration discount-rate shock, the tech-heavy index is the one that should have been worst.

So sit with the financials detail, because it is the most informative sector signal of the day. Banks are the sector that normally welcomes higher policy rates, because higher short rates on floating assets are a net interest margin story. When banks underperform into a flattening curve instead, the market is not repricing their cash flows. It is questioning their earnings power and the quality of their loan book. And note the obvious tension: a 1 percent index decline is a very small response to what is genuinely a regime change. Either the market truly had it priced, or the rules-based, price-insensitive selling by those same systematic funds, the selling we described on Monday, simply has not been triggered yet. Do not read a quiet tape as an absorbed shock.

Here is where our own framing takes a hit. On Monday we named small caps as the observable that would separate a coherent systematic book from an incoherent one, because Russell constituents carry more floating-rate debt and a hike reaches them through earnings, not just through the discount rate. The Russell 2000 fell about seven-tenths of a percent on the day — less than the S&P, not more. Outlets differ slightly on the exact decimal, so treat it as a magnitude rather than a precise print. The floating-rate-stress leg of that thesis did not show up in the tape. The broader claim that the equity leg is the one that gave way still holds. The narrower one about where it gives way does not.

Credit is the test that has not run yet. Going into the decision, investment-grade spreads sat at 81 basis points at the start of September, not far from the series low touched in January, which was the tightest reading since 1998. High-yield spreads were near 265 basis points as of September 11, also close to the tightest levels of the year. Those are pre-decision benchmarks and we have no confirmed, dated print since, so the only defensible sentence is that credit has not been shown to re-mark. From 81 basis points there is almost nothing left to tighten into and a great deal of room to widen. Watch new-issue concessions first. Primary market pricing tells you a spread regime has changed days before the index does.

Now the other side of this tightening, which is arriving from two directions at once. The dollar had its best day in three months, with the Bloomberg Dollar Spot Index up half a percent, its biggest jump since June. Note the timing across the session: even Bitcoin only softened once the presser turned hawkish, so the path signal, not the rate, did the work across every asset on the tape. That dollar strength is landing at the same moment as a genuine physical supply shock in crude. A drone attack on September 10 knocked out Saudi Aramco's East-West pipeline to Yanbu. Aramco has since canceled or delayed European cargoes, telling some customers it cannot deliver through mid-November. That is the seller itself dating the shortfall, which is what converts a headline outage into a confirmed physical gap. Rystad Energy estimates that a month-long disruption would keep as many as 120 million barrels of exports off the market — a scenario figure, not a measured loss. Brent was trading near 108 dollars a barrel on Wednesday morning.

The market may be under-reading the duration of that and over-reading the price. WTI slipped on rising US inventories the same day Brent firmed. That divergence says this is a European and seaborne logistics problem, not a global shortage of barrels, which caps the pass-through to US headline inflation relative to what the Brent print implies. But the sell-side reflex that supply shocks are transitory does not sit well against a deliverability gap that the producer has already dated into November.

Put the dollar and the barrel together and you get a double tightening on everyone outside the United States. Dollar funding gets more expensive because rate differentials widen. Imported energy gets more expensive at the same time. Neither of those shows up in the S&P print. They show up in emerging-market funding stress and in levered speculative positions, off screen, and they compound mechanically without requiring another Fed statement.

Gold is the piece that broke pattern, and it broke ours. This desk spent two briefings burying the fiscal-hedge thesis, on the grounds that gold was losing ground with its best tailwind still blowing. Wednesday was the test that thesis was designed to fail: a hawkish hike plus the dollar's strongest session in three months. Gold spiked intraday to around 4,365 dollars an ounce, then reversed to close near 4,240, down barely more than a percent. Commerzbank called the limited damage somewhat surprising, and traced it to fiscal concerns in long-end yields plus rising US political risk.

Take that carefully. Commerzbank is inferring a cause from price, not observing a flow, and we have no positioning data to separate a re-rating from the partial unwind of a crowded long. What the session does support is narrower and still useful: gold's correlation to real rates is weaker right now than standard portfolio construction assumes. If you are sizing gold as a duration substitute in a macro book, you are mis-specifying it. That is an allocation implication, not a price call.

One slower channel keeps running underneath all of this. A Fed chair who says financial conditions are not restrictive, while the ten-year sits at 5 percent, is telling corporate borrowers and mortgage markets that the refinancing repricing has further to go. That door opens on a lag the barrel does not have, and nothing about Wednesday closed it.

So here is where it leaves you. The Fed removed the market's anchor for how high rates go. The curve says that is a policy repricing rather than a term-premium shock, which limits the duration damage. The equity leg gave way, but not where we said it would. And the one part of the capital structure with no cushion left, credit, still has not shown its hand. The next dated spread print and the concessions on the next big new issue are what settle it.

That's the radar for today. Trade carefully out there, and I'll see you tomorrow.
