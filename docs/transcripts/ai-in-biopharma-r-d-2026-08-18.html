Cautious Optimism Briefings

AI in Biopharma R&D

Transcript · August 18, 2026

Good morning. It's Tuesday, August 18th, 2026, and this is your briefing on artificial intelligence in biopharma R&D.

Six days ago this beat led with Novo Nordisk naming Amazon Web Services its strategic cloud and AI partner. At the time it looked like a single top-five pharma making an infrastructure bet. It no longer looks like one deal. This week the same move shows up as a pattern, and it comes with an on-record admission from the people running R&D at the biggest drugmakers in the world about exactly where AI is and isn't paying off. So today the story isn't the Novo deal itself. It's the shape of the wave it belongs to, and the fork pharma now faces over who actually owns the AI it's buying.

Start with what we already knew, because it sets the frame. On August 10th, Novo and AWS opened a co-innovation hub in London, with the stated aim, in AWS's own words, to compress the path from drug target to first human dose. That's a discovery-scale ambition. But the only quantified proof point either company has offered is not a discovery number. It's an adoption number. And the deep dive on the primary AWS blog sharpens exactly what that number is. AWS says more than 25,000 Novo employees now use a generative-AI chatbot built on Amazon Bedrock to build chatbots, retrieve information, draft documents, and act as, quote, "virtual colleagues for nonregulated processes." That's a headcount of people using a tool for informal, non-regulated work. It is not a measured productivity gain. Neither company has published a baseline or a percentage against one, and no deal value or minimum cloud spend was disclosed anywhere. So the honest read hasn't changed since the 12th: Novo has bought a discovery-stage ambition sitting on top of an operations-stage metric, and this deal by itself says nothing about clinical success odds.

Here's what is new. Novo isn't the only one. Back on April 22nd, Merck signed its own agentic-AI mega-deal, this one with Google Cloud, and unlike Novo, Merck put a number on it. Merck's own release calls it a multi-year investment valued at up to one billion dollars, deploying an agentic platform across research and development, manufacturing, commercial, and corporate functions. That matters for two reasons. First, because Merck disclosed a dollar figure and Novo disclosed nothing, you cannot rank the two by size — anyone calling the Novo deal "one of the largest" is asserting something no source supports. But second, and more important: two separate top-ten pharma companies signing hyperscaler mega-partnerships within four months of each other starts to look like a direction, not a coincidence. The compute providers want to be pharma's operating system, not just its vendor. Now the discipline on that read: two data points is thin. It's a forming pattern to watch, not a confirmed trend, and I'd want a third before calling it one.

That infrastructure story has an industry-wide echo, and this is the genuinely fresh material this week. A survey of R&D leaders at Pfizer, Lilly, AstraZeneca, Novartis, and Bristol Myers Squibb draws almost exactly the line Novo is testing. Pfizer's CEO attributes the company's multi-billion-dollar cut to R&D and SG&A spend directly to AI, saying, quote, "the main lever was the successful deployment of AI," and the company is adding more than 1,200 GPUs across discovery, legal, manufacturing, and marketing. At Lilly, predictive models for small-molecule and antibody binding and ADMET properties now run on every prospective program before a single compound is synthesized. A Lilly executive put it plainly: "we live in a predict-first era already. That's no longer aspirational; that's operational." AstraZeneca claims its early-discovery target design and validation is now more than 50 percent faster with AI in the loop.

That's the real, bankable side of the ledger — cost discipline, workflow speed, predictive triage before wet-lab work even starts. Now set it against what Novartis and BMS leadership are saying on the same record. One R&D head called AI "not a magic panacea," adding, "it's not like suddenly AI is gonna come up with a drug tomorrow." Another said flatly there's no "magic unlock" coming in the next twelve to twenty-four months. And Lilly's own team supplied the sharpest caveat of all: by the time an AI-designed asset actually reaches the clinic, the model that designed it is, in their words, "probably obsolete." That's a genuinely uncomfortable admission. It means the AI-discovered drugs entering trials today may not even be a fair test of what today's models can do, because the models have already moved on. So the operational layer is real and monetized now. The molecule-design layer is still a promise, and even the people building it say don't expect a near-term unlock.

That split shapes how pharma is choosing to own its AI, and here's the fork. Novo is renting hyperscaler infrastructure. Merck is doing the same with Google. But Sanofi is doing something different. In June, it signed a five-year exclusive license for Owkin's K Pro "AI Scientist," with Owkin leading end-to-end development of proprietary AI agents built specifically for Sanofi. No deal value was disclosed, so treat this as a structural signal, not a sized commitment. But the structure is the point. Sanofi wants exclusivity over the underlying agent and its outputs, not a generic API relationship it shares with every other customer on the platform. As more sponsors decide agentic AI is core infrastructure rather than a commodity tool, expect more of this build-and-license posture, and more scrutiny of who actually controls the model weights and the data that trained them.

Pull the threads together. Every one of this week's "real" wins — Novo's adoption count, Merck's billion-dollar platform, Pfizer's "main lever," AstraZeneca's 50 percent figure — is a company-reported number with no outside baseline behind it. That's the same test this beat keeps running, day after day: a marketed number sitting on top of a much harder one underneath. And the value keeps sliding down the stack, away from the molecule and toward operations — the further you get from biology and the closer you get to workflow, the more auditable the payoff becomes. None of that resolves the question that actually matters for pipeline economics: whether any of this moves the probability that a drug survives the clinic. Insilico's rentosertib remains the trial to watch on that question, still enrolling toward a Phase III readout that won't land before well into next year. And as of this month, still no AI-discovered drug holds full FDA approval. Until one does, the infrastructure story and the clinical-success story are separate conversations — and only one of them is what actually pays for the R&D budget.

That's the briefing for today. Thanks for listening, and I'll see you next time.
