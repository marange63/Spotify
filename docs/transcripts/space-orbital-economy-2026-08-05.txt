Good morning, and welcome to Space and the Orbital Economy. It's Wednesday, August 5th, 2026.

SpaceX reported its first-ever public earnings yesterday, and the print answers the question this show has been tracking since the IPO: is SpaceX a space company or an AI infrastructure company. Increasingly, it looks like both, and the market did not like the mix. Q2 revenue came in at $7.8 billion, up 92% year over year, beating Bloomberg's consensus of $6.81 billion. Adjusted EBITDA hit $3.5 billion against a $2 billion estimate. That is a clean double beat. And yet SPCX shares fell roughly 7% in postmarket trading. The reason is capital allocation, not results.

Quarterly capex exploded to $18.4 billion, more than six times the year-ago figure, and SpaceX now discloses AI as its own formal reporting segment, alongside Space and Connectivity. That segment posted $2.6 billion of revenue, up 213% sequentially, and roughly $15.8 billion of the quarter's capex went into AI compute infrastructure specifically. A big piece of that money built two data centers in and near Memphis, Tennessee, originally intended to train xAI's models. SpaceX has since repurposed much of that capacity to rent out to Anthropic and Google under compute deals struck just before the IPO. So the market's read is straightforward: investors underwrote a space and satellite thesis, and instead they're funding a terrestrial AI cloud buildout that happens to share a balance sheet with Falcon and Starlink.

CFO Bret Johnsen gave the clearest version of where this is going. On the earnings call, he said the company is on a trajectory, including contribution from Cursor, to reach $100 billion of annualized recurring revenue by the end of this year. That's the precise guidance to hold onto, because it comes with a real condition attached: it explicitly bakes in SpaceX's pending acquisition of Cursor's parent company, Anysphere, a $60 billion all-stock deal announced in June. That deal was still open, not closed, as of this earnings call — SpaceX expects it to close sometime in the third quarter.

Musk offered looser language on the same call, calling the $100 billion figure "not a question mark" and saying it's what SpaceX would hit even if it did nothing further. Treat Johnsen's math as the operative guidance and Musk's framing as color, because the CFO's own arithmetic requires Cursor to close. If that deal slips, the December target gets harder to hit. SpaceX also disclosed $6.7 billion of additional cloud-services revenue already under contract, ramping over six months starting in October — real backlog, but still a ramp, not booked revenue today. And despite the AI story dominating the narrative, SpaceX is still unprofitable: the quarter's net loss was $541 million, narrower than a year ago, but a loss nonetheless. The AI segment itself lost $1.3 billion, though that loss is narrowing.

There's a real signal buried in this print, too. Starlink disclosed standalone economics for the first time: 12 million subscribers at quarter-end, with connectivity-segment adjusted EBITDA of $2.6 billion, ahead of the $2.41 billion estimate. That's the first hard benchmark analysts have for valuing the entire low-Earth-orbit broadband category — Kuiper, Eutelsat OneWeb, AST SpaceMobile — against something real rather than guesswork. So what does all this mean. SpaceX's consolidated capex can no longer be read cleanly as orbital infrastructure spend. It's now cross-subsidized with, and increasingly driven by, an AI cloud business, which means anyone trying to value Starlink or launch in isolation has to first strip out the AI overlay — and anyone worried about AI capex overbuild now has to worry about it inside a space name too.

But strip the AI story out entirely, and the actual space stack is genuinely inflecting. Starship's Flight 13 flew on July 25th, and for the first time, an operational payload went up on the vehicle: twenty third-generation Starlink satellites, deployed successfully from the ship's payload dispenser. The upper stage then executed a controlled splashdown in the Indian Ocean roughly 65 minutes after launch and, notably, remained fully intact — a first. Musk said the landing was precise enough that it would have been caught by the launch tower's arms had SpaceX attempted it, and that success is now driving the plan for Flight 14: the first-ever attempt to catch the Starship upper stage on the tower, contingent on data review clearing no issues. That flight is targeted for as soon as this month, though treat that as Musk's aspiration rather than a scheduled date.

The booster side of the story is less clean. Only 10 of 13 planned relight engines fired for the landing burn, and only about five were still running at splashdown, producing a hard, higher-velocity impact. So the progress this flight is real, but it's asymmetric — the ship is closing in on full reusability while the booster still has reliability work to do. And full-stack reusability, both stages working every time, is the entire cost-per-kilogram thesis Starship's business case depends on. It isn't there yet.

And someone is paying for all of this buildout, on the defense side in particular. Three national-security space actions landed inside a single three-week window in July. On July 14th, the Space Development Agency awarded $1.75 billion in prototype contracts — roughly $955 million to L3Harris and $798 million to Sierra Space — for 36 additional Golden Dome missile-tracking satellites, targeted to be launch-ready by the end of 2028. On July 21st, Space Systems Command tripled the ordering ceiling on the NSSL Phase 3 Lane 1 launch contract, from $5.6 billion to $17 billion, pushing the program's total ceiling to $30 billion. And on July 29th, the Space Force gave SpaceX a further $1.6 billion for 18 Falcon 9 launches through the end of 2027, the largest publicly disclosed Lane 1 order to date, procured in roughly two months from requirement to award.

The tripled Lane 1 ceiling is the load-bearing evidence here — you don't triple a contract cap within months of setting it unless demand for proliferated satellite and launch capacity is running ahead of the Pentagon's own prior planning. That widens the pool for smaller launch entrants like Firefly, Stoke, and Relativity trying to break into national-security work beyond SpaceX and ULA. Meanwhile, this morning SpaceX launched three next-generation AST SpaceMobile direct-to-cell satellites, BlueBird 11 through 13, which nearly double peak download speeds versus AST's earlier units. That's a genuine technical improvement in the race to compete with Starlink on direct-to-cell, though no subscriber or carrier-revenue numbers exist yet to say whether it's translating commercially.

Now the counterweights. China's Qianfan constellation has passed roughly 200 satellites in orbit, targeting 324 launched this year alone on the way to 15,000 approved satellites by 2030, and Chinese state broadcaster CGTN claims per-satellite manufacturing costs have fallen more than 96% through mass production. Treat that cost figure as an unverified state claim rather than a confirmed number, but even directionally, it's the kind of unit-economics pressure that could eventually squeeze Starlink and Kuiper on replacement costs. And the debris commons keeps tightening under all of this proliferation. The European Space Agency's CRASH Clock — a measure of how long until a collision if all maneuvering stopped — has fallen from 164 days in 2018, before megaconstellations began launching, to just 2.5 days as of May 2026. That's not breaking news, but it's the sharpest available quantification of the externality every constellation described in this briefing is contributing to.

A few near-term catalysts worth watching. Blue Origin targets New Glenn's second flight no earlier than August 15th, its first launch since the May 28th pad explosion destroyed the vehicle and damaged its Cape Canaveral launch site — the clearest live test of whether the US gets a credible second reusable heavy-lift provider. Starship's Flight 14 tower-catch attempt could come this month. And Rocket Lab reports earnings on August 10th.
