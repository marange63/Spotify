Good morning, it's Thursday, September 17th, 2026, and this is Strategic Power.

On Tuesday this beat said the Federal Reserve's decision was coming the next afternoon, and that nothing about the outcome was settled. It is settled now. It went the other way.

The Federal Open Market Committee raised the target range by a quarter point, to three and three-quarters to four percent. That is the Fed's own language, and it is a hike, not the cut the President has been demanding. The Committee approved the statement by a twelve to nothing vote, with no dissents reported. That tally comes from the statement itself, though I could not get the named roll-call line.

This is the first rate move under Kevin Warsh. The Senate confirmed him in May, fifty-four to forty-five. That was the closest confirmation vote for a Fed chair in the modern era, and it carried largely on the expectation that he would cut. He did the opposite in his first outing.

Now let me be careful with you about why. The Fed's own statement cites "geopolitical developments" as one source of elevated uncertainty. That language is generic. It does not name the Iran war, and it draws no causal line from that war to this decision. The statement says only that the move will "support a timelier return to the Committee's 2 percent goal." So treat the war-driven framing you are about to hear as my analysis, not as something the Fed said.

What is on the record is the reaction. On Truth Social, the President wrote, quote, "LOWER THE INTEREST RATES FOR THE UNITED STATES OF AMERICA, AND FAST!" Speaking to reporters the same day, he said of Warsh, quote, "I do, I mean I'm relying on Kevin." That is public frustration with the decision, and no break with the man he appointed.

So the honest read is narrower than "Fed independence vindicated." One hike is not a regime. With diesel at a record, a cut would have been hard to defend on any framework anyone at that table uses. The decision may reveal less about institutional courage than the headline suggests, and more about how little room the Committee actually had.

What makes a cut hard to defend right now is a supply constraint nobody can negotiate with. That takes us to the Iran war, now in its seventh month.

On Tuesday this beat carried the only public comment on Saudi Arabia's damaged export pipeline. The U.S. Energy Secretary, Chris Wright, said, quote, "I think you'll see that pipeline running back soon." We told you to treat that as a hope, not a schedule. There is now an estimate on the table for the suspended loadings at Yanbu. Five to six weeks. That figure comes from a secondary military tracker, not from Aramco and not from the Saudi energy ministry, so hold it loosely. But five to six weeks is not "soon." It is an engineering schedule contradicting an official's expectation, and that gap is the cleanest evidence this episode has that policy hope and repair time are different objects.

Then the blockade arithmetic, from that same tracker rather than a Pentagon or CENTCOM release, so take it as directional. The cumulative count of commercial vessels redirected around the Strait of Hormuz reached a hundred and three as of September 15th. Only four vessels transited that day. None of them carried crude oil or liquefied natural gas. Separately, U.S. diesel hit a record six dollars and twenty-seven cents a gallon around this period. That is the price that reaches the Fed's desk.

So the so-what. Deliverable energy supply right now is being set by repair crews and by a blockade, not by any lever in Washington. That is why the rate decision looked forced rather than chosen.

It also sorts winners cleanly. Producers and exporters whose barrels never cross a contested chokepoint earn a security premium they did not have to build anything to get. That is non-Gulf crude, U.S. LNG, and refiners long distillate. Import-dependent economies take the hit twice, on inflation and on the current account at once, and they take it while the Fed is raising the cost of the dollars they need to pay for the cargo.

The one thing that can move faster than a repair schedule is a political decision to stop. Washington took two votes on that question this week.

The House passed a War Powers resolution, two hundred and twenty to two hundred and four, directing the President to remove U.S. forces from hostilities with Iran. Seven Republicans crossed over. That is the largest defection from the administration's Iran policy so far. Read it as a leading indicator of political capital, not as an instrument. A concurrent resolution has no force of law without the Senate and a presidential signature, so no troop posture changes today.

But track it anyway, because it is an oil variable. Part of what is holding barrels off the water is an American enforcement decision. Enforcement decisions reverse with a phone call. Damaged steel does not. If Republican support for this campaign keeps thinning, the fastest path to cheaper crude is not a repair crew in Saudi Arabia. It is a political reversal in Washington.

Now the second vote, which resolves a question I asked you to watch on Monday. Whether the House would actually move on the Graham-named Russia and Iran sanctions bill. It did, two hundred and sixty-two to one hundred and fifty-nine, and the bill is now on the President's desk. It reportedly cleared the Senate eighty-six to eleven last month, though that tally is secondary reporting rather than a roll call I can verify.

The substance is narrow and sharp. The bill authorizes tariffs of up to a hundred percent on countries that rank among the top five buyers of Russian crude or gas. China and India are named. It adds sanctions on Russian officials and financial institutions.

The vote's internal split does real analytical work. Two hundred and three Republicans and fifty-eight Democrats voted yes. A hundred and fifty-two Democrats voted no, and their objection was about handing the President discretionary tariff power, not about sanctioning Russia. Seven Republicans also voted no. That is the same count that crossed on the war resolution, though I can't confirm they were the same members.

Here is the skeptical read. The President has not signed this bill as of this recording. Even signed, it is a "may impose" power, not a trigger. No tariff rate applies to any country today. My instinct is to call this escalation against Beijing and New Delhi. The more defensible read is the opposite. Leverage like this is often most valuable left unused, and it arrives roughly a week before an expected Xi-Trump meeting. Authorization is not imposition.

And if it were invoked, notice which way the first-order effect runs. Pushing China and India off discounted Russian crude sends both buyers toward Gulf and U.S. barrels, into a market already short from the blockade and the pipeline. The sanction's first move on price runs opposite to its intent.

So line up the three instruments Washington produced this week on one scale. The Fed's hike is in force today. The sanctions bill is authority awaiting a signature, and then a decision to use it. The War Powers resolution has no legal force at all. Only one of the three binds anything right now, and it is the one that never needed a floor vote.

All of this raises the cost of building things, at the exact moment the thing being built has a constraint that isn't financial.

At the SEMICON Taiwan trade show early this month, TSMC executives said the company is constructing roughly twenty fabrication and advanced-packaging facilities at once. That is well above its historical build rate. The equipment-demand surge behind it is not new, and we covered it yesterday. What is new is the constraint those executives named. Not capital. Not even tool availability. A shortage of specialized construction workers in Taiwan, which they do not expect to ease before 2027.

Weigh the source before you bank it. These are trade-show remarks relayed through the press, not a filing and not an earnings call. And a company that blames labor is a company arguing for looser visa policy, and against the assumption that more subsidy money buys more capacity. That framing is self-interested, even if it happens to be true.

The collision is real regardless of motive. The Fed just repriced the cost of capital upward for a data-center and chip buildout in which capital was never the binding constraint. The binding constraint is people who can pour concrete and pull cable. No rate decision touches that, in either direction.

So here's the through-line for the week. The instruments that actually bound something were a rate decision and a repair schedule. Both take effect without anyone's consent. Everything else Washington produced is still paper, with a signature or a decision to use it still pending. Watch two things from here. Whether the President signs the sanctions bill, and whether Yanbu comes back inside that five-to-six week window.
