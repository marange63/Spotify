Good morning. It's Friday, October 30th, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

Yesterday this show ended on one question. Would Amazon's AWS print confirm the backlog pattern at a third hyperscaler? It printed, and it did. So here's today's thesis. At the infrastructure layer, the demand question is settled for now. What actually sets the pace, on both the revenue line and the capex line, is GPU supply. And the market has stopped paying for growth on its own. It's paying for cash conversion, even when that conversion was forced by a supplier's delivery schedule rather than chosen by the company.

Start with AWS. Per Amazon's earnings release, AWS sales grew 39.3 percent, its fastest growth in nearly five years. That puts the business on a 184 billion dollar annualized run rate. The profitability is just as striking. On the earnings call, Amazon put AWS's own operating margin at 38.7 percent. That's nearly three times Amazon's company-wide margin, which was itself the best in the company's history. The gap is the point. AWS is carrying the consolidated profit line, and the rest of Amazon is not.

Then comes the number that answers yesterday's question. On the same call, Amazon put the AWS backlog at 578 billion dollars, growing at a triple-digit rate year over year. Set that against revenue growing at 39 percent. Contracted demand is compounding far faster than AWS can deliver it. That's the same gap this show drew on Thursday for Microsoft's commercial backlog and for Google Cloud's. So it's now three hyperscalers out of three. This is a pattern, not an anecdote. And it raises the obvious next question. If demand isn't the constraint, what is?

The answer is supply. On Microsoft's earnings call, CFO Amy Hood said third-party GPU providers had delays. Those delays push some capacity Microsoft expected out to the first half of 2027. Microsoft also trimmed its capex outlook, to about 170 billion dollars from about 175 billion, per TheFly. Outlets disagree on which fiscal period that figure covers, so read it as a modest trim, not a precise annual number. The direction is what matters. Microsoft isn't spending less because it wants less capacity. It's spending less because the capacity hasn't arrived.

Alphabet tells a similar story from a different angle. Its third-quarter capex came in about 3.7 billion dollars below what analysts expected, per CNBC. Yet in the same report, management signaled a significant increase in 2027 spending, with no dollar figure attached. Alphabet didn't attribute the shortfall to supply. But on Thursday this show noted Google's own admission that it is compute-constrained and has started renting third-party capacity. My inference is that this year's underspend is deferral, not discipline. The money hasn't disappeared. It's landing later than planned, because the hardware is late.

Here's what that means for enterprise buyers. If hyperscaler capacity is slipping into the first half of 2027, the binding constraint on enterprise AI rollouts is no longer budget approval. It's capacity reservations. That's inference, but it has practical consequences. Enterprises that locked in committed capacity early are better placed than those still waiting to scale out of pilots. And cloud AI pricing should stay firm, because the vendors have no surplus capacity to discount.

Now to the market's verdict, which landed in a very particular way. Alphabet's Class A shares fell 12.6 percent at the close, per Fortune. That was the stock's worst day as a public company. The trigger was cash, not growth. Alphabet posted negative free cash flow of about 9.1 billion dollars for the quarter, alongside that unsized 2027 increase. And it happened despite cloud revenue roughly doubling, which we covered yesterday. A doubling in cloud didn't save the stock. Visible cash burn sank it.

Amazon got a milder version of the same treatment. It reaffirmed roughly 220 billion dollars of capital spending for this year, per its release. It also missed on earnings per share. Shares then fell about 6 percent further in after-hours trading, per Yahoo Finance. There's also a caution on that record margin. Depreciation on a build that size lags the spending itself. So today's peak margin may flatter Amazon's run-rate economics once those assets start hitting the income statement. That's inference, but it's the right lens for reading Amazon's margin over the next several quarters.

Microsoft, by contrast, was rewarded. Its stock jumped about 4 percent on the beat, per CNBC. That was the early reaction, and at least one outlet reported the gain fading during the session. Either way, there's an irony worth sitting with. If part of what investors liked was a lower capex number that looked disciplined, some of that number was forced by suppliers falling behind. The market may be rewarding timing as much as restraint.

Alphabet isn't alone in the cash squeeze, and the next example comes from last week. Per reporting on Meta's results, Meta's capex exceeded its operating cash flow for the quarter. That left free cash flow at around negative 2.1 billion dollars. Meta also narrowed its full-year capex range to 135 to 145 billion dollars. That raised the floor, not the ceiling, so it's a marginal signal rather than a step-up. But it turns capex outrunning cash into a two-company fact rather than a one-company anecdote.

Why does that matter? Because when capex exceeds operating cash flow, the marginal dollar of the AI buildout has to come from the balance sheet or from outside financing. For weeks this show tracked that financing strain at one remove, in supplier bonds and lender backstops. Now it sits directly on hyperscaler cash flow statements. The debt-funding step is my inference, since none of these companies announced new borrowing this week. But it sets up the next test. If 2027 guidance brings a large step-up, it will collide head-on with the market's new cash-conversion lens. So the scorecard has changed. Doubling cloud revenue no longer earns the multiple by itself. Converting that growth into cash does.

That concentration of money shows up one layer up the stack, too. Per a Yahoo Finance writeup of ServiceNow's earnings call, the company signed 19 deals above 5 million dollars in net-new annual contract value last quarter. That's up 135 percent year over year. But the percentage flatters a small base, of roughly eight such deals a year earlier. So the count matters more than the growth rate. ServiceNow also now has 680 customers above that 5 million dollar line. One check on Thursday's upbeat margin read for ServiceNow: adjusted earnings per share came in at a dollar even, against a dollar-two consensus, per one earnings recap. AI-attached bookings haven't yet translated cleanly into earnings.

Now put ServiceNow next to two data points from Thursday. Microsoft said its customers with very large Copilot deployments rose eightfold. And secondhand reports put roughly 250 Anthropic customers in the eight-figure spending tier. ServiceNow's large-deal cohort fits the same shape. At every layer of the stack, enterprise AI revenue is pooling in the biggest accounts. That's a sign of real, organization-wide deployment inside those accounts. But it also means each vendor's growth hinges on a relatively small number of procurement decisions, not broad-based adoption. A single large customer pulling back at that tier would show up in the numbers fast.

So, pulling it together. Demand for AI infrastructure is real, now confirmed by the backlogs at all three hyperscalers. GPU supply is setting the pace for both revenue and spending. The market has shifted from rewarding growth to rewarding cash conversion, sometimes for the wrong reason behind a lower number. And the dollars flowing through enterprise software are concentrated in a small set of very large accounts.

Watch list for next week. Palantir reports on November 2nd. The first hard 2027 capex numbers, especially Alphabet's, will test whether this year's underspend really was deferral. And Anthropic's Claude Enterprise agentic features are due to switch on by default around November 16th.

That's your briefing. Stay cautiously optimistic, and have a good Friday.
