Good morning. It's Sunday, November 8th, and this is Capital Markets Radar.

Yesterday's briefing left three verdicts open. Where would Friday's equity close land after Thursday's rout? Where would the front end of the curve settle? And was credit actually under stress? All three are in now, and they tell one story. The stress didn't clear. It moved down the capital structure, out of equities and into rates and credit.

Start with credit, because that's the lead. CNBC reports that one of CoreWeave's bonds, carrying a 9.625 percent coupon, now yields above 16 percent. And Nvidia's credit default swap spread has risen 76 percent in a month, to about 139 basis points. That second number is the surprise. Nvidia has the strongest balance sheet in the AI complex, and it now costs meaningfully more to insure against its default than it did a month ago. One caution. That's a one-month change from a starting level CNBC doesn't give, so the percentage can overstate the alarm.

Read that against the frame this desk used on Wednesday, when we called AI credit steeply tiered by quality. In levels, the tiers still hold. CNBC puts Oracle's spread near 298 basis points, roughly double Nvidia's, and CoreWeave's near 1,100. What's changed is the direction. Protection is getting more expensive for the strongest name, not just the weakest. There's a competing read of the Nvidia move, and it's our inference rather than reported fact. Swaps on a cash-rich mega-cap make a convenient, liquid proxy hedge for the whole AI book. So some of that widening may be traders hedging AI exposure broadly, not taking a view on Nvidia's solvency. Either way, the conclusion is the same. The cost of insuring AI exposure is rising at the top of the stack.

Now the other side of the ledger. The same CNBC reporting says the lending window is still open. Goldman Sachs tallies 67 billion dollars of AI bond deals since late October. Jefferies strategist Christopher Wood says the most plausible way the AI capex arms race ends is that, in his words, "the credit card will be taken away." That's a test of availability, not price. On the evidence, the card hasn't been taken away. What's rising is the cost of carrying it, at least in the secondary market. We can't say whether any of those new deals paid a concession, because no reporting breaks them out. So price is deteriorating while availability holds.

The qualification that matters most is breadth, and this time we can measure it. We pulled the ICE BofA option-adjusted spread indexes from the St. Louis Fed's FRED database. On Thursday, the day of the rout, broad US high-yield spreads widened about 16 basis points, to 3.48 percent. That's a modest move for the worst equity session since the tariff shock. Investment-grade spreads closed Thursday at 0.93 percent, exactly where they stood on Tuesday. So while protection on CoreWeave and Nvidia gets pricier, the broad indexes aren't confirming a wider credit event. One limit worth noting. That series runs only through Thursday, so Friday's credit tape isn't in it yet.

Here's what that means for the buildout. CoreWeave's bond now yields more than six percentage points above its own coupon, which is simple arithmetic from the two figures. On our read, that's roughly where new unsecured paper from a levered neocloud would have to price today. That raises the hurdle rate on exactly the capex that's supposed to keep absorbing GPU supply. The window is open, but the toll is rising.

The price of that money starts at the front end, which is where Friday's jobs report comes in. We covered the 165,000 payrolls headline yesterday. What's new is the wage print and the short end. Average hourly earnings rose 0.4 percent on the month and 3.1 percent on the year. Hawks will lean on that ahead of Tuesday's CPI. But it's one month, and that annual pace isn't obviously inconsistent with a cooling labor market. So the hawkish reading of wages is an argument, not a fact.

The front end took the hawkish side anyway. The two-year Treasury yield rose 5 basis points on Friday, to 4.51 percent. That closes yesterday's open question. The long end tells a different story. The ten-year sits at 4.86 percent, down 45 basis points from its early-October peak. That's why the gap between twos and tens has compressed to 35 basis points. Be careful with the attribution, though. That flattening is a month-long rally at the long end, not something the jobs report did. On Friday itself, the two-year rose while the ten-year barely moved.

Fed funds futures ran the same round trip. Market-implied odds of a December hike fell to about 70 percent after Thursday's rout, from above 80 earlier in the week. After Friday's print, they're back near 77. The FOMC hasn't met, so this is pricing, not a decision. But the Fed put got priced in and taken back out within two sessions. The long end has given the market a month of relief. The short end just took some of it back. And the short end is where floating-rate and short-dated AI borrowing gets priced.

So did equities believe the print? Here's the test we set yesterday. A bounce straight through a hawkish jobs number would say the market reads Thursday as positioning, not fundamentals. The bounce happened, but it was thin. The S&P 500 rose just 0.31 percent on Friday, after falling 4.4 percent on Thursday. That retraces well under a tenth of the loss, and the index still lost ground on the week. The Nasdaq Composite bounced harder, but it still posted its fourth straight weekly decline. Thursday's trigger is also contested. Some coverage points to the election and data-center politics. NBC points instead to weak earnings from Qualcomm and Arm. We'd pick neither. The positioning read survives, but barely. One green Friday isn't a bottom, and that losing streak says the derating trend is intact. The real tell is where the stress went. It's showing up in rates and credit, rather than resolving in equities.

The slower-moving version of the credit story is private. On Friday's show we called private credit the pressure point, and said stress would show up there late and all at once. KBRA, as reported by Bloomberg, now counts a record 112 private-debt borrower defaults, up 22 percent from the prior quarter. Be clear about what that is. It's a quarterly, lagging count that almost certainly predates Thursday's rout. So it confirms a deterioration already under way, not the equity shock landing in private books. The forward risk is refinancing. Davidson Kempner's chief investment officer is flagging a wall of private-credit maturities coming due in 2027 and 2028. That wall now meets a front end pricing a hike, not a cut. For sponsors, the refinancing math just got harder, before the wave even arrives.

The fastest gauge of liquidity is the on-chain dollar, and it's retreating too. Spot bitcoin ETFs lost 1.28 billion dollars over the week, per SoSoValue data relayed by Crypto Times. That's the largest weekly outflow since late June. BlackRock's IBIT accounted for about 94 percent of it, according to 24/7 Wall Street. That's the same concentration in the flagship wrapper we flagged on Wednesday, now stretched across a full week. Part of that share simply reflects IBIT's size, so it isn't proof of unique institutional selling on its own.

The stablecoin data is the more telling signal. Total stablecoin supply fell about 6.7 billion dollars in the week to Saturday, the steepest weekly drop of the year. BlackRock's tokenized Treasury fund, BUIDL, shrank by about a quarter of a billion dollars over the same stretch. Both major stablecoins held their dollar pegs, so this is contraction, not a stress event. And as our Digital Money desk has reported, supply has been drifting lower since May. So this accelerates an existing leak rather than marking a new regime. BUIDL is also small enough that one or two holders can move it. Linking the drain to the hawkish repricing is our inference, not an established cause. Still, this is liquidity leaving the system, not just prices marking down. The on-chain dollars that could re-lever into a dip are shrinking in the same week the front end turned hawkish.

Three things to watch. First, October CPI, due Tuesday and not yet released, against hike odds in the high seventies. Second, broad high-yield and investment-grade spreads. They barely moved through Thursday, and if that changes, the single-name AI stress has gone systemic. Third, whether the next AI-linked bond deal clears clean or needs a concession. A deal that fails or gets pulled would be the moment the credit card actually gets taken away.

That's Capital Markets Radar for November 8th. Trade safe, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
