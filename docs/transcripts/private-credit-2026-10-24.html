Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Credit & the Corporate Credit Cycle

Transcript · October 24, 2026

Good morning, it's Saturday, October 24th, 2026, and this is your private credit and corporate credit briefing.

The industry's own trade body just sized private credit at four point one trillion dollars. In the same release, it admitted that one in six of the funds it surveyed is carrying non-accruals above five percent. That tension is today's story: the money is growing faster than the proof that the lending is sound.

Start with the headline. The Alternative Credit Council, the private-credit arm of AIMA, reported that global private credit assets under management rose seventeen percent year-on-year to four point one trillion dollars in 2025. That comes from the ACC's research as carried by Bloomberg and Private Equity Wire. The ACC also said the average share of loans subject to significant amendments eased to ten point six percent, from eleven point seven percent in 2024. Those amendments cover payment holidays, maturity extensions, covenant changes, and PIK. Both of those are 2025 figures, so read them as structure, not this quarter's strain. And together they are the half of the release built for a press quote.

Here's the half that matters more. Roughly one-sixth of the surveyed funds reported non-accrual rates above five percent. That is a share of funds, not an industry default rate, and it describes a tail rather than an average. A sector-wide mean can look perfectly healthy while a sixth of the vehicles underneath it are deep into impairment. That fits the ACC's own framing that deterioration is, in its words, concentrated in specific sectors rather than across the wider market. Fair enough. But a concentrated tail covering a sixth of funds is still a lot of funds.

The improving amendment share deserves the same skepticism. Amendments only happen when a loan actually hits a trigger. A lower share can just as easily mean fewer loans reached that trigger this year, not that the book got safer. Fitch's numbers show why the definition matters. Its private credit default rate hit a record six point three percent for the twelve months through August. That measure counts interest deferrals and extensions, which narrower trackers and BDC non-accrual rates leave out. In this asset class, stress shows up in the definitions before it shows up in the losses.

Notice the language, too. The ACC said negative investor sentiment has become, quote, disconnected from underlying market conditions. Blue Owl reached for the same word with its own shareholders this month. Its letter said its results underscore, quote, the disconnect between the market's fears of AI disintermediating software and what its book is actually doing. So the sponsor's talking point has become the trade body's talking point, within a few weeks. Neither one is an independent assessment, and neither has been tested by a real downturn. When the industry and the firms inside it reach for the same defense, that earns more skepticism, not less.

So the asset base is growing fast, and the tail of its loss record is unproven. If the money is growing that fast, the next question is where it can go.

Bloomberg reported this week that direct lenders are fighting for every dollar in smaller deals. The latest hard number comes from PitchBook LCD. Its last volume read had direct lenders providing about forty-five billion dollars of new loans in the three months through May. That was the lowest since the second quarter of 2023. It's spring data, not the third quarter, but it fits the pattern we flagged yesterday at Blackstone, where money came in far faster than it went out the door.

The more current signal is where the good borrowers are going. In the third quarter, PitchBook LCD found that a refinancing exit into the syndicated loan market was available only for the higher-quality tier of direct-lending names. And pricing in that refinancing wave split by quality. B-plus deals averaged about two hundred eighty-nine basis points over SOFR this quarter. The B-minus bucket, by contrast, saw its spreads widen by more than half a point, to four hundred eleven over. So the weakest names now pay more than a hundred and twenty basis points above the B-plus tier.

That cuts against the easy story, that a glut of capital is compressing spreads across the board. For the weakest credits, compensation is actually rising. The sharper point is selection, and this is our inference. If the best borrowers can refinance out into the liquid market, what stays behind in direct-lending books tilts toward the weaker tier. So the binding risk isn't uniformly loose pricing. It's that private books keep the credits the public market won't take, just as the asset base grows seventeen percent.

That opacity about who is holding what has a price, and this week someone paid it.

The London Stock Exchange Group agreed on October 22nd to buy Octus, the credit-intelligence platform formerly known as Reorg, from Permira for three point five three five billion dollars in cash. LSEG expects Octus to generate more than three hundred million dollars of revenue in 2026, with an adjusted EBITDA margin of around forty percent. It says that revenue has compounded at double-digit rates since 2022. Those are LSEG's own expectations, not audited results. Off those figures, the price works out to roughly twelve times expected revenue and about twenty-nine times EBITDA. That's a rich multiple for a data business.

LSEG is stretching to pay it. It expects leverage to reach the upper end of its one-and-a-half to two-and-a-half times target range by the end of 2027. It expects the deal to be neutral to adjusted earnings per share in the first full year after completion, and accretive after that. And this is signed, not closed, so the terms still have to survive to completion.

What is LSEG actually buying? LSEG's own framing is that it is strong in liquid credit, and Octus brings expertise in private and distressed credit. Bloomberg cast the deal as the group fighting back against fears that AI will erode its information business. That sets up an asymmetry worth noticing. The AI-disintermediation worry that has Blue Owl's software borrowers under pressure is being explicitly rejected here. Proprietary, document-level credit intelligence is being priced as the thing AI can't easily replicate. The information about private-credit risk is being bid up faster than private credit is proving that risk is benign.

Now to the retail channel, where untested marks meet investors who can't simply wait them out. Blue Owl's gates are now three quarters running, and the peers are gated too. But request volumes behind those gates are easing at the margin. At Apollo Debt Solutions BDC, redemption requests fell to fourteen point seven percent of shares, from sixteen point eight. At Ares Strategic Income Fund, they eased to thirteen point one percent, from fourteen point four.

Blue Owl's tech-focused vehicle, OTIC, is still the outlier, and its backlog is clearing in slow motion. After its latest payment, OTIC will have provided about four hundred forty-six million dollars of liquidity. That is thirty-five percent of original tender requests, delivered over six months. Two things are true together. Easing requests look like relief. Yet Apollo's and Ares's requests still run between two and three times the five percent cap, and OTIC's at nearly eight times. That's attrition wearing down a backlog, not resolution of the underlying mismatch.

So where does that leave the cycle? It still reads as a grind, not a turn. Today's data earns that read: the industry's averages look fine, while its tail, its weakest borrowers, and its retail exits all show strain. The biggest unresolved question is that one-sixth of funds above five percent non-accrual. Is it a stable, sector-specific tail, as the ACC insists? Or is it the leading edge of marks that simply haven't met a real downturn yet?

The tests we've flagged all week are now days away. Ares Capital reports October 27th, and Ares Management on the 29th. Apollo discloses asset-level marks on October 30th, ahead of full earnings on November 3rd. Watch the non-accrual and PIK lines in those prints for whether the tail the ACC just disclosed is holding steady or widening.
