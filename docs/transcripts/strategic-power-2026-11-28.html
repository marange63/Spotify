The most important thing this week is not a rule or a permit. It is a strait that no license can open. The Strait of Hormuz remains effectively shut in the US-Iran war, and that is now the binding constraint on the energy system.

The headline is subtler than it looks, though, because two things are true at once. Supply is adapting at the margin. And a large piece of capacity is gone for years.

Start with the adaptation. The National reports 41 Qatari LNG loadings so far in November. That compares with 35 in October and 21 in September. So loadings have nearly doubled since September, and they are at their highest since the war began.

The reported workaround is a mix of dark-fleet tankers and rerouted cargoes. That also means the data is soft. Only a minority of Qatari cargoes broadcast their position, so the loading count rests on estimates. Treat it as the direction of travel, not an audited number.

Now the structural side. Iranian strikes damaged Ras Laffan Trains 4 and 6, knocking out 12.8 million tonnes a year. The National puts that at about 17 percent of Qatar's LNG exports. QatarEnergy has estimated that repairs would take three to five years.

A caveat on timing. The strike itself dates to March, and the lost-revenue figure dates to then as well. QatarEnergy's chief executive, Saad Al Kaabi, put it at about 20 billion dollars a year. That number is not new this week. What is new is the picture around it. Eight months on, the market has had time to adapt, and the adaptation still cannot replace 17 percent of one of the world's largest LNG exporters.

So the point is this. Dark-fleet cargoes can recover volume that is stranded. They cannot rebuild trains that are physically broken. The recovery is real, and the hole is durable.

That hole is already pushing buyers toward coal. BRS Shipbrokers research, reported by Splash247, shows Pakistan's seaborne coal imports surged 51.2 percent year on year to 11.52 million tonnes in the first ten months of 2026. I want to be careful here. That is one country, over ten months, and the figure does not isolate the war. It is a proxy for what price-sensitive South Asian buyers do when gas disappears. It is not an Asia-wide number, and I would not stretch it into one.

Still, the direction makes sense. A buyer that cannot afford or cannot find LNG burns coal. That runs against the decarbonization story, and it adds demand for a commodity markets had written off.

The mirror image sits in the United States. If 17 percent of Qatari export capacity is out for years, American LNG developers hold pricing leverage they did not have a year ago. Long-term contracts get repriced, and final investment decisions look easier to finance. That is the energy-abundance argument in its sharpest form. The country with surplus gas and working export terminals gains strategic weight when a rival supplier loses trains.

Now the price. Brent settled Friday at 95.44 dollars a barrel. That was down 1.13 dollars, or 1.2 percent on the day. West Texas Intermediate settled at 83.08. Those are settlement prices. Some intraday quotes ran higher, so be wary of headline numbers that mix the two.

OPEC Plus meets Sunday. Reporting says the group is set to keep first-quarter 2027 output targets unchanged. That is an expectation. The meeting has not happened.

So the lesson for oil is that a modest weekly dip does not mean the shock is fading. Brent near 95 is still a price that keeps energy inflation embedded in the data.

That price is what the Federal Reserve is reacting to. It is the bridge to the monetary side of this story.

FXStreet cites market pricing at a 99 percent chance that the Fed raises rates by 25 basis points at the December meeting. That meeting is December 9th. It has not happened, and neither has the November CPI report the following day. So this is market-implied odds, not a decision.

The point is the cause. This is not a hiking cycle born of a booming economy. It is an energy-shock hiking cycle. Oil at this level keeps disinflation stalled, and the central bank is leaning against it. That is a supply problem being met with demand-side medicine. It works by hurting growth, and the people who feel it first are the capital-intensive builders.

The 10-year Treasury yield rose nearly 6.5 basis points to 4.928 percent. Other outlets show different readings for the 10-year, so I am using the quoted one. The dollar index is up about a quarter percent at 104. Higher yields and a firmer dollar are the textbook combination that should hurt gold.

Instead, gold is holding up. FXStreet has it trading at 3,633 dollars, having rebounded from a low earlier this autumn. That is the non-consensus signal. Gold is rising against higher real-rate pressure and a stronger dollar. The most natural reading is a monetary-credibility bid. Buyers are not worried about this month's rate. They are worried about whether the fiscal and monetary authorities can absorb an energy shock without losing control of the long end.

I would not over-read one session. A single day does not make a regime. But the divergence is worth tracking, because it tells you who is buying gold and why.

Bitcoin is a different story. Low-tier crypto outlets report it near 61,000 dollars after a sharp drop. That is unverified, so treat it as color. If true, it would mean Bitcoin trades as a risk asset in this tightening, and not as the hard-money hedge that gold is acting like. That distinction matters for the narrative, and it is a reminder that hard money is not one trade.

The second-order point for infrastructure is straightforward. Higher-for-longer raises financing costs for the exact things this show tracks. Utilities, independent power producers, data center developers and private credit funds all borrow against long-lived assets. A 10-year yield near 5 percent makes every gigawatt more expensive to build.

Is there anything that cuts the other way? One force. Peace in Ukraine.

Jared Kushner said, in comments carried by Radio Free Europe, that, quote, we have been able to convince the Russians of this concept that any security guarantees for Ukraine would be based on Article 5. The Wall Street Journal separately reports that Washington has floated two land-for-peace options, including ceding the Donetsk fortress belt in return for NATO membership. And in a statement this week, Trump and NATO chief Mark Rutte cautioned Russia against widening the war.

Be skeptical here. There is no confirmed breakthrough. The Journal's account is single-source reporting. The Kushner quote comes from a summary, not a full transcript. And Russia has not signaled acceptance. It still demands a NATO bar, and you cannot trade territory for membership while also honoring that demand.

The market relevance is narrow but real. A real settlement would lower the risk premium on European energy and on Russian flows, and it would offset some of the Hormuz upside in oil. So peace is the one plausible downside for crude. But I would not underwrite it on this evidence.

Let me close with a parallel chokepoint, because it shows the limit of one kind of power. For weeks this desk has described control exercised through permits. Minerals, compute and grid connection all run through licenses. That is power you can grant or withhold from a desk.

The chip remote-access rule, which we covered earlier in the week, is moving from rulebook to political fight. I will not repeat the mechanics. What is new is the politics. The Senate Banking Committee is reported to hold a hearing on December 3rd with Nvidia's Jensen Huang, and Commerce Secretary Lutnick is reported to follow on December 10th. Those dates come from summaries, and neither hearing has been held. Beijing has called the rule economic coercion. And Treasury Secretary Bessent has said no further restrictions are needed. That sets up a Treasury versus Commerce split inside the administration.

That split matters for one reason. It means the compute chokepoint is not settled policy. It is contested, and it could ride into year-end legislation.

So here is where this leaves us. In compute, power is a permission, and permissions can be argued over in a hearing room. In energy, power is physical. You cannot negotiate a broken LNG train back into service. The market is adapting around Hormuz, but it is adapting around a loss measured in years.

That is the thing to watch. Not the next headline, but whether replacement supply, in American LNG and in everything else, gets built faster than the demand for it gets destroyed.
