Good morning, and welcome to Capital Markets Radar for Saturday, September 19th.

The number that moved most this week was not the funds rate. It was the probability attached to a meeting that has not happened yet. The Fed hiked on Wednesday, unanimously. By Thursday, a relay of the CME's FedWatch gauge had an October hike at a coin flip. By Friday morning, CNBC put it near 58 percent. The decision is three days old, and October 28 is already the live meeting.

Start with why the odds kept climbing. Sixteen of the eighteen participants who submitted projections expect at least one more increase this year. The committee median puts the funds rate at 4.1 percent this year and leaves it there next year, with cuts only after that. That is a one-more-and-hold path, not a tightening cycle. And it rests on core inflation falling by almost a full point next year, which is another way of saying it rests on the energy shock fading.

Now the complication. Warsh submits no dot at all. Press accounts have him disowning the projections, and he said he would be hard pressed to describe broad financial conditions as restrictive. He also said this summer's inflation readings do not tell him that underlying trends have meaningfully improved. Those are reported quotes relayed through secondary outlets, not transcript-verified, so take the direction as reliable and the wording as the gist.

In our last briefing we argued that the policy path was never in the price, and that the repricing risk sat ahead of us rather than behind us. The October curve spent Thursday and Friday making that argument for us. A market that never discounted the tightening has to discount it late. Late is where the violent moves live.

There are two worlds here. If the committee median is the reaction function, the peak is one hike away and the front end is roughly fair. If Warsh's own view is the swing vote, terminal is unanchored and the front end is still too low. Nothing this week distinguished them. Every data print between now and the October meeting carries an event premium, and a VIX at 14.88 is not charging for it.

That front end is what shapes the Treasury curve, so go there. Friday left the 2-year near 4.74 percent and the 10-year right at 5 percent. That is roughly 26 basis points of curve between them. The shape matters more than the headline. The front end led, which makes this a bear flattener driven by policy, not a term-premium blowout.

So the market is pricing a policy path, not a fiscal accident. That distinction limits the discount-rate damage to long-duration equities relative to what a 5 percent headline implies. Tuesday's intraday spike, the highest 10-year level since 2007, was driven by oil above 100 dollars and no timeline for reopening the Strait of Hormuz.

But the long end is a global problem, and this week one central bank blinked. On Thursday the Bank of England paused gilt sales for six months and halted sales of long-dated gilts entirely. That came days after UK long yields hit their highest since 1998.

Do not oversell it as a pivot. The same announcement set out a multi-year plan to offload most of the remaining 488 billion pounds of gilts by the middle of the next decade. It changes the pace and the composition at the long end. It does not abandon quantitative tightening.

The precedent is the point. A central bank has just treated term premium as an explicit policy variable, and the tool it reached for was the balance sheet. If US long-end stress persists, the same question lands on Treasury issuance and on the pace of Fed runoff. A chair widely seen as a balance-sheet skeptic, who will not call conditions restrictive, is the last person who would welcome that conversation.

Staying with central banks, the Bank of Japan raised its policy rate to 1.25 percent on Friday. The vote was 7 to 2, and both dissenters were appointees of the sitting prime minister. Governor Ueda did not rule out back-to-back hikes.

The market's answer was perverse. The yen weakened past 157 to the dollar, and the Nikkei rallied. My read is that you trade the dissent, not the decision. Two government appointees voting no reads as a political ceiling on the hiking path, and the currency sold that ceiling. That is a different claim from saying the Bank of Japan is simply behind the curve.

There is a tail here worth naming. Japanese long yields recently hit their highest in three decades. If Japanese institutions start repatriating money out of Treasuries and gilts, global term premium feels it immediately. Nothing I have seen shows that happening. It is a risk channel, not an observed flow.

The dollar closes that loop. The Dollar Index rose 1.18 percent on the week, its best week since June. A dollar that strong on top of an oil shock is a double tightening for every energy importer outside the United States. It compounds mechanically without another word from the committee. And it shows up in emerging-market funding and in levered books long before it shows up in the S&P.

Oil deserves one beat, because it is the assumption underneath the Fed's hawkishness. WTI settled Friday at 95.71 dollars. Reporting has Saudi Arabia restoring about half of the East-West pipeline within days, with full capacity a matter of weeks away. Yesterday we flagged that timeline as an intention being priced as a fact.

Nothing named has confirmed it since, and the strait is still effectively shut. Summarized IMF PortWatch data had transits running near a tenth of the pre-crisis norm earlier this month. Both directions are being over-read. Ship-to-ship transfers are moving some barrels, so this is not a total shortfall. But a repair schedule is not a reopening. This is a short-gamma position on headlines, and if the repair slips, the committee's disinflation assumption for next year is the first thing to break.

Now to the asset that should have hated all of this. Crypto had a strange week. On Tuesday the CLARITY Act fell short of the 60 votes it needed to advance, and every Democrat who negotiated the text voted against it.

On Thursday the SEC answered with what it calls an Innovation Exemption for tokenized stock. It runs five years and carries limits on symbols and on volume. Read that sequence again. Congress failed to build a legal on-chain equity venue by statute, and the executive branch built one anyway, by order, two days later.

Bitcoin then rose about 5 percent on Friday to roughly 81,000 dollars. Reporting put about 470 million dollars of crypto shorts liquidated over a single day as the price broke through resistance.

We need to grade ourselves here. In our last briefing we said the bid under Bitcoin was fragile because the ETF allocator was leaving. Fragility describes the quality of a bid, not its direction, and we conflated the two. Fragile support produced an up-move, not a down one.

Do not read it as an allocation event either. The prior week saw more than 460 million dollars leave US spot Bitcoin ETFs, ending the strongest inflow streak of the year. A squeeze is the absence of sellers plus forced buyers. The durable item is the exemption, and the exemption expires, and a future commission can reverse it. Capital committed against a temporary carve-out prices differently from capital committed against a law.

That brings me to what still has not been tested. High-yield spreads stood at 2.76 percent on September 15. That mark comes from a FRED mirror, and it predates the hike. We have named credit as the untested leg in five straight sessions now, and no post-decision print has arrived. The absence of a print does not mean spreads held. It means nobody has shown us. And genuinely tight spreads may reflect pre-funded supply rather than confidence, in which case the new-issue concession widens before the index does.

The rest of the tape says the same thing. Gold set a weekly high in the same week the dollar had its best run since June. The Dow lost 1.5 percent on the week while the S&P finished roughly flat. Gold rising against a stronger dollar is a bid that is not the rates channel at all. This desk has been wrong about gold in both directions, so I will not offer a third thesis.

A week that contained three central bank decisions did not close the gap between what has repriced and what has been stress-tested. October 28 is where that gap most likely closes.
