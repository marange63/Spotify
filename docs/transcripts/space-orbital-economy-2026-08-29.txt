Good morning. It's Saturday, August 29th, and this is your space and orbital economy briefing.

Blue Origin now knows why New Glenn exploded at the Cape in May, and knowing the cause has not saved the schedule. Chief executive Dave Limp said on August 6th that the failure traced to the main oxygen valve on one of the BE-4 engines, confirmed by hardware recovery and inspection. The company says it is retrofitting a modified valve to existing engines. That is a hardware-availability claim, not a licensed return-to-flight date, and none has been committed publicly beyond "before year-end." The number that actually moves the story sits later in that same disclosure. The first Blue Moon Mark 1 lunar lander, the vehicle called Endurance, is now not expected to fly until the first quarter of 2027. Limp's own words were that Blue Origin expects that launch "to occur in Q1 of 2027," with the next three landers following only after that one flies.

Be precise about what that slip does and does not touch. It is tempting to read a lunar-lander delay straight into NASA's crewed Artemis timeline. That link is not there. NASA restructured Artemis in the spring, months before this slip was disclosed. Artemis III is no longer a full lunar-surface landing. It is now an Orion mission that docks in Earth orbit with prototypes of two landers, Blue Origin's crewed Blue Moon Mark 2 and SpaceX's Starship. The first actual crewed landing has been pushed out to Artemis IV, targeted for 2028. Blue Moon Mark 1, the vehicle that just slipped, is a separate uncrewed cargo pathfinder. So the real cost of this slip is not a quotable Artemis milestone. It is Blue Origin's credibility as a lunar cargo operator, and it lands in the same quarter as another flagship program.

That other program is Vast's Haven-1, the commercial station billed as a first bridge to a post-ISS American presence in low Earth orbit. It has moved from a 2026 target to no earlier than the first quarter of 2027 as well. And Rocket Lab has flagged that the window for a 2026 debut of its Neutron rocket is narrowing, with the company itself raising the prospect of a slip into 2027. That is three flagship programs, from three unrelated companies, in three different segments of this industry, converging on roughly the same quarter. Landers, stations, medium-lift rockets. None of them share hardware or a supply chain, which makes the correlation more interesting than any one of the delays. The most plausible read is a common bottleneck in test capacity and qualification, not a run of isolated company stumbles. That read is inference rather than disclosure, so hold it loosely. But the practical takeaway is firm either way. If you are underwriting anything in this sector against a company-guided date, the base rate on that date is one to three quarters late.

Here is the turn worth making. Engineering dates move. Licence dates, mostly, do not. Amazon's Kuiper constellation, now rebranded Leo, has just supplied the cleanest test of that this year. Amazon's FCC licence required 1,618 satellites in orbit by July 30th — half of its first-generation constellation. Reporting puts the actual deployed count somewhere in a range of roughly 354 to 392 satellites as of August, so treat it as approximate. Either way it is under a quarter of what the licence demanded. Amazon has requested a deadline extension rather than face the consequence of missing it outright. Whether the FCC has granted, denied or even docketed that request has not been confirmed.

That is a legal deadline that has already passed, not a soft target sliding in a press release. The interesting question is not whether Amazon is embarrassed. It is what the FCC does next. If a deadline missed by roughly a factor of four produces nothing more than a routine extension, the milestone regime stops functioning as a use-it-or-lose-it mechanism on scarce orbital slots and spectrum. And that matters well beyond Amazon. It is the same regulatory tool the West would lean on to contest the slot filings behind China's Guowang and Qianfan constellations. A milestone regime that does not bind an American licensee is not going to bind a Chinese one either. So watch what the FCC actually does here. It sets the price of a missed deadline for every constellation operator queued up behind Amazon.

Now move from a regulator writing dates that may or may not stick to a customer writing cheques against dates that are contractual. The Space Development Agency awarded roughly 1.75 billion dollars combined to L3Harris and Sierra Space in July, to build 36 satellites for the Golden Dome missile-tracking and missile-defence architecture. L3Harris took about 955 million dollars for eighteen satellites. Sierra Space took about 798 million for the other eighteen. Both sets are due to be available for launch by the end of 2028. Note the wording: available for launch is a delivery condition, not an on-orbit date. Splitting that money across two contractors, rather than concentrating it in one or in the usual SpaceX and Anduril cohort, buys industrial-base breadth. It costs integration risk and schedule variance. That is an odd trade for a programme branded as accelerated, and it tells you the government has chosen supplier resilience over speed.

The clocks inside Golden Dome do not line up either. Bloomberg reported this month that the Pentagon is targeting 2027 for the first flight tests of prototype orbital interceptors, with a full intercept demonstration not until 2029 and potential procurement starting in 2030. That is paywalled reporting captured secondhand, so hold the dates loosely. But take them at face value for a moment. The tracking satellites are due in 2028. The interceptors are supposed to begin flight testing a year before that. The whole thing is meant to intercept something in 2029. A sensing layer and a kill layer running on different clocks is not an architecture yet. It is two programmes that happen to share a name. And this is the same Golden Dome whose Space Force programme chief warned this month that development could halt in October without new money from Congress. The government is contracting more than two years out on a programme it has told Congress might stop funding in about five weeks. For L3Harris and Sierra Space that is an award you can book into backlog but cannot yet treat as a funded run-rate.

Not everything in this sector is sliding. Arianespace flew Ariane 6 to geostationary transfer orbit for the first time on August 27th, carrying the MTG-I2 weather satellite for the European Space Agency and EUMETSAT. That was the rocket's fourth launch of the year and its first mission to that orbit. It is a real capability milestone for Europe's sovereign launch programme, and it is the rare data point this week where a vehicle did something new roughly on schedule rather than late. But be honest about what it is and is not. Four launches a year is a capability demonstration, not capacity. It does not relieve Amazon's Leo backlog, and it does not meaningfully change the manifest arithmetic for anyone waiting on medium or heavy lift this year. Capability and capacity are different goods, and only one of them clears a bottleneck.

Pull the threads together and the picture is this. What is genuinely inflecting in the orbital economy right now is contracted demand — the Space Development Agency's cheques, the FCC's licence conditions on Amazon, Ariane's actual customer manifest. What is still a demo, or at best a guidance target, is almost every schedule attached to that demand. The binding constraint has moved from launch capacity to schedule credibility. Hardware that was supposed to fly in 2026 is stacking into the first quarter of 2027, while regulators and procurement officers keep writing deadlines this sector's recent record does not earn.

So watch three things from here. Whether the FCC enforces or quietly waves through Amazon's missed milestone. Whether Blue Origin's return-to-flight guidance becomes a licensed date rather than a target. And what the Pentagon decides on Golden Dome funding in October.
