Good morning. It's Thursday, November 5th, and this week the money and the deployments in enterprise AI are moving at two different speeds.

Start with the money. Menlo Ventures published its 2026 State of Enterprise AI report this week. The headline is enterprise AI spending of roughly 177 billion dollars this year, up 5.4 times in a single year. Treat that as Menlo's modeled estimate, not a measured market size. Menlo built it from a bottoms-up model informed by a survey of nearly 400 U.S. enterprise decision-makers. And a venture firm with portfolio companies in this market has every reason to print a big number.

The sharper figure sits inside the report. Per Menlo's survey, enterprises spent roughly two and a half times what they had planned for AI this year. Buyers didn't just spend more. They blew through their own budgets. So where did the money go? Menlo puts about 99.5 billion dollars of the total in infrastructure, which it describes as the stack that runs models and the agents built on them. On the application side, the single biggest line is coding tools, at 34 billion dollars. Menlo doesn't draw a clean boundary around what infrastructure includes, so hold that split loosely. But here's my inference. Spending this heavy in infrastructure and coding looks like consumption, developer tooling, and pilots. It doesn't yet look like agents running business processes in production. Spend is an input, not an outcome. The budgets have arrived. The question is whether the agents have.

They mostly haven't, according to Okta. Its Customer Identity Trends Report, as reported by Techzine, finds organizations missing their own AI agent rollout schedules 80 percent of the time. Only 20.3 percent of respondents say they're shipping AI features at their target pace. And 59 percent of senior leaders blame compliance, security review, or governance as the main obstacle.

Two cautions before taking that at face value. First, Okta sells identity management. A survey concluding that governance is the bottleneck favors Okta's own product category by construction. Second, missing a self-set schedule measures how aggressive the schedule was as much as how hard deployment is. An 80 percent miss rate may say as much about planning optimism as about execution.

Still, one number in the survey is concrete enough to stand on its own. Only 22.4 percent of organizations building agentic systems give each agent a unique identity. That's the mechanical reason governance can't sign off. An agent running on a shared or borrowed credential can't be audited, scoped, or revoked on its own. No security team can approve that, however good the model is. So the bottleneck Okta is describing is control, not capability.

That carries a budget-sequencing implication, and it's inference. If fewer than a quarter of agent builders issue per-agent identities, the next dollars likely go to identity, observability, and shutdown controls before more agents ship. That's a tailwind for identity and security vendors. It's a timing headwind for agent-platform revenue.

The supply side looks very different. SAP says it expects 400 AI agents in its Autonomous Suite by the end of 2026. It's backing that with a 100-million-euro partner-led fund for the Asia-Pacific region. Note what that 400 counts. It's agents available in SAP's suite, not agents running at customers. So vendors are counting what they've built, while customers are missing the dates they set for running it. The fund is telling too, and this is my inference. SAP is paying integrators to deliver, which suggests implementation capacity, not software, is the scarce input. If that's right, systems-integrator demand rises with the rollout gap. And if governance is the bottleneck, someone is going to sell the fix.

That someone, this week, is CrowdStrike. It agreed to acquire two startups, Fiddler AI and Dreadnode, in deals reportedly worth 495 million dollars combined. That figure is reported, not disclosed. CrowdStrike didn't give a price when it announced Fiddler, though it separately confirmed the Dreadnode deal. Alongside the deals, CrowdStrike launched what it calls an AI kill switch inside its Falcon Guardian product. It lets enterprises halt a misbehaving agent.

Tie that back to Okta's identity number. If fewer than a quarter of organizations give each agent its own identity, revoking one agent surgically is mostly off the table. A kill switch is the blunt instrument you reach for instead. Be clear on the evidence level, though. This is a product announcement. No customer counts or revenue came with it.

There are two honest readings of these deals. The offensive one says agent security is becoming its own budget category, and CrowdStrike is buying the lead before rivals catch up. The defensive one says agent control could just as easily settle inside identity platforms like Okta, or inside data platforms. Snowflake's hard AI spending caps, which this show covered yesterday, are exactly that kind of rival control point. On that view, an endpoint vendor is buying a seat before the control point gets decided elsewhere. Nothing in these deals settles which reading wins. A pair of sub-500-million-dollar tuck-ins buys capability and a narrative, not disclosed revenue. Only agent-security revenue or attach rates would decide it.

The timing raises the stakes. As this show noted on Monday, CrowdStrike's own print is estimated for around December 1st. That same Monday, we covered Fortinet's stock selling off after it cut one line of guidance, service revenue. My inference is that the market will want a number behind CrowdStrike's agent-security story, not just the story. So what: CrowdStrike just bought a narrative, and December is when it has to start showing the numbers.

Buyers are hedging in a second way, and it shows up in the contracts themselves. On Tuesday, this show asked whether Palantir's revenue running ahead of its bookings was healthy burn-down or a sign the pipeline wasn't refilling. A quick re-ground. Palantir's third-quarter revenue grew 96 percent year over year, and U.S. commercial revenue grew 148 percent. The new piece is the sequential bookings line. Per TechCrunch's reporting, U.S. commercial total contract value booked in the quarter was 1.46 billion dollars. That's down from a record 2.13 billion the quarter before.

Palantir CEO Alex Karp offers a third reading, neither burn-down nor refill failure. Customers, he says, are moving "much quicker to deployment, shorter duration of the contract... but then an ability to expand that contract dramatically." Call it duration compression. If contracts are shorter, contract value per deal falls mechanically, even when demand is healthy. On that reading, the drop from a record is ambiguous rather than bearish. It also fits the rest of today's evidence, and this link is my inference. Buyers who can't yet govern their agents have every reason to avoid multi-year lock-in on technology that's still moving fast.

Now the skeptic's view. Karp's account is management's framing, not a disclosed metric. Palantir published no contract-duration figure that would let anyone verify durations actually shortened. It's also the most flattering available explanation of a sequential decline. One quarter isn't a trend, and a record prior quarter may simply have been lumpy. The test is mechanical. If shorter contracts expand the way Karp promises, next quarter's contract value and remaining deal value should reaccelerate. U.S. commercial remaining deal value grew just 4 percent quarter over quarter this time. That's the number to watch.

The wider implication is inference, but it matters for how these companies get valued. This show has spent the week on bookings quality, from hyperscaler backlog to ServiceNow's pulled-forward renewals. If short, expandable contracts become the norm for AI deals, the issue isn't only that the metrics can be flattered. Bookings, contract value, and remaining performance obligations lose predictive power altogether. Investors would have to underwrite expansion rates and net retention instead of contracted backlog. So what: shorter contracts are buyers pricing in their own uncertainty, and vendors' forward metrics will get noisier because of it.

Pull it together and you get one caution expressed four ways. Enterprises are committing money fast, per Menlo. They're deploying agents slowly, per Okta. Vendors are racing to sell them control, from CrowdStrike's deals to SAP paying its integrators. And buyers are refusing long lock-in, per Palantir. Three things to watch. First, whether Palantir's contract value and remaining deal value reaccelerate next quarter. Second, whether CrowdStrike discloses any agent-security revenue in its December print. Third, whether SAP's 400-agent year-end mark comes with agents customers are actually running. Cloudflare is also due to report around now, and its numbers weren't in hand for this briefing.

That's the briefing. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
