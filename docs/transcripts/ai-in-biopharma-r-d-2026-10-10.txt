Good morning. It's Saturday, October 10th, and this is your briefing on artificial intelligence in biopharma R&D.

The theme today is that the evidence bar in AI drug design is rising, from logos on a press release to falsifiable preclinical numbers. But both of today's developments stop at the same wall. They validate starting points, not drugs.

Start with Chai Discovery. The AI protein-design startup announced a collaboration with GSK yesterday, and this one is different from the deals we've been tracking all week. GSK put Chai's models to a wet-lab test before signing anything. Chai's models generated designs against GSK's own targets zero-shot, meaning with no target-specific training. GSK then tested those designs in its own lab. According to the release, GSK found binders to all of the tested targets. GSK's own statement says it evaluated the models across a panel of diverse targets and saw designs with strong binding affinities.

Here's why that matters to this show. All week we have called Chai's deals logos, not commitments, because the terms were undisclosed and nobody outside could see what had been proven. GSK is the first partner we can point to that tested the product in its own lab before renting it. The deal count is also climbing. By this show's own tally, Chai had four pharma deployments when we covered it on October 3rd. Bristol Myers Squibb made five. GSK makes six. Chai itself only calls GSK the latest in a string of collaborations since the start of the year. So the buyer checked the product, and the buyers keep coming. That narrows our caveat. It does not retire it.

Now the skeptical turn. Binding in a screen is the lowest rung of the biologics ladder. It says nothing about specificity or function. It says nothing about developability, meaning aggregation, viscosity, or immunogenicity. And it says nothing about activity in a living system. "Binders to all tested targets" also sounds like a perfect score, but we don't know the denominator. GSK didn't disclose how many targets were in the panel or which ones they were. It gave no hit rate and no affinity numbers. Those details aren't in the release or in any of the trade coverage. That absence matters, because a vendor-favorable selection effect is possible. GSK picked the panel, Chai wrote the announcement, and a failed evaluation would not have produced a press release.

There's also a competing read. Pharma may keep signing with several design vendors precisely because none of them is decisive yet, and a sixth logo is consistent with that hedge. So the honest read is that GSK's test says Chai's tool is worth renting. It does not say the tool makes better drugs.

On money, GSK's terms remain fully undisclosed. One figure keeps circulating for Chai's separate deal with Eli Lilly, a mid-eight-figure annual access fee. That comes from an Endpoints News reporter citing unnamed sources, and he notes the terms were never publicly disclosed. Treat it as reporting, not fact, and don't extend it to GSK by analogy.

So what does this mean beyond Chai and GSK? Start with discovery services. If large biologics pharma routinely rent zero-shot design models after testing them in-house, early hit-finding spend shifts. It moves away from immunization and display-library labor and toward validation capacity. That's our inference, but it points to pressure on conventional antibody-discovery service providers, and a tailwind for high-throughput validation vendors. Inside pharma, the same logic could slow the growth of in-house computational biology teams, with budget moving toward platform license fees instead.

Then there's the rent-versus-own debate. On Friday we covered Isomorphic Labs, where private capital is reportedly paying frontier prices to own the model layer outright. Chai's path is the opposite. Pharma evaluates first, then rents, on undisclosed terms. If a buyer can test a design model in its own lab and then simply rent it, model access starts to look like a purchased input. The premium for owning a model then has to be earned through the clinical assets it produces, not through access to the model itself.

That's the biologics side, and it's showing binders. Small molecules are showing something harder to fake, which is specific, falsifiable drug-like properties. Insilico Medicine nominated ISM1354 on September 30th. It's an AI-designed small-molecule antagonist of the GIPR receptor for obesity, succeeding an earlier candidate called ISM0676, nominated back in January. Insilico disclosed oral bioavailability of 75 to 104 percent across the species tested. It also reports at least 18-fold greater plasma exposure than a clinical-stage benchmark compound at the same dose. And it estimates a roughly 45-fold safety margin from monkey toxicology. Those are real, checkable numbers, which is a higher disclosure bar than the typical "AI-designed" marketing claim.

But every one of those numbers is preclinical. There's no IND and no human dosing. The safety margin comes from non-GLP toxicology, and Insilico itself calls it an estimate. The benchmark compound is unnamed, so the 18-fold exposure edge can't be independently checked. And higher exposure only helps if potency and dose line up, so it isn't, on its own, proof of a better drug.

The bigger risk is the biology. GIPR antagonism as an obesity mechanism is a contested bet. It sits next to the approved agonist incretins from Lilly and Novo Nordisk, which are already generating outcomes data. The bull case is that antagonists could someday show a differentiated profile, such as better muscle preservation, but nobody has shown that in people. AI design can optimize a molecule's properties. It cannot validate the underlying biology.

One more thing worth noting. ISM1354 is a successor to January's ISM0676, and it arrives nine months later under a release headlined "Breaking the Safety Barrier." That suggests the first candidate ran into trouble, plausibly on safety. That's an inference on my part, not a disclosed fact.

Now the number Insilico led with. The company says ISM1354 is its tenth preclinical candidate nomination in the first nine months of the year, a company record. That's a throughput metric, and it's the one AI-discovery companies love to publicize. The metric that would actually move pharma economics is conversion: candidates to IND, IND to the clinic, and the clinic to Phase Two success. No AI-native company has shown that conversion rate at scale yet. For investors, that's the right way to underwrite these names. A high nomination cadence with low conversion would mean AI makes cheap candidates, not better drugs.

That brings Chai and Insilico back together. Chai's binders and Insilico's candidate count both measure output at the very front of the funnel. Neither speaks to conversion.

A quick note on what didn't move this week. There were no fresh developments on real-world-data interoperability, clinical operations, digital pathology, or regulatory strategy. And large pharma's third-quarter earnings commentary on AI R&D productivity is still ahead of us.

So where does that leave things? There are three questions to watch. Do GSK or Chai ever disclose panel data or deal terms? Does any Chai partner nominate an actual development candidate from a Chai design? And does ISM1354, or its predecessor, reach an IND?

On clinical success rates, today's evidence sits entirely at the front of the funnel, so it doesn't move the odds on Phase Two at all. On R&D spend, rented design tools look like a variable cost that pharma can turn on and off. The spending that's hard to avoid is the cost of validating what these models generate. Renting the model is the easy part. Proving what it made is still the expensive part.

That's the briefing. Have a great weekend.
