Good morning, it's Monday, November 2nd. This is your private equity briefing. The theme today: with arm's-length exits still scarce, the industry is buying itself time and liquidity rather than finding it in the market. The question for everyone on the other side of the table is whether what comes back is cash, or just time.

Start with the pressure that drives all of it. Limited partners want cash back, not paper. McKinsey's survey of three hundred LPs, as reported this year, ranks DPI tied with multiple on invested capital for second place, behind IRR. Fifty-four percent of those LPs call DPI critical or most critical. DPI hasn't overtaken IRR. But it's now a gating metric for re-ups, and a general partner heading into a fundraise with thin distributions has a problem to solve. Everything else today is a variation on how managers are answering that problem.

One tool for solving it is the NAV loan, and that's where the structural risk sits. A distribution funded by borrowing against the fund's remaining assets returns cash today. But the loan still has to be repaid, typically from future exits. So it's a lien on tomorrow's distributions. It stacks fund-level leverage on top of the leverage already inside the portfolio companies. And it lifts reported DPI without testing a single mark against a buyer. If exits stay slow, that tail gets worse, not better. That's why the line LPs draw matters. Older LP polling by Rede Partners pointed one way: comfortable with NAV loans that fund add-ons or refinance debt, opposed to the version that sends money out. LPs aren't objecting to leverage. They're objecting to borrowed distributions.

The pricing of that leverage is sorting itself out, but not dramatically. Corpay Private Markets data, reported by Alternative Credit Investor on October 29th, shows non-bank NAV margins widening by about thirty-eight basis points since February of last year, to 794, while bank margins narrowed to 435. Corpay's own explanation is mostly mix and rates. Non-bank lenders have moved higher up the risk curve, and they need wider spreads to hold an absolute return as base rates fall. So read it as riskier facilities migrating to pricier lenders, not as lenders sounding an alarm. The practical point for sponsors is simple. The riskiest fund-level borrowing, the kind most tempting when exits are slowest, is also the most expensive.

Now, a borrowed distribution is ultimately a fundraising tool. So look at how fundraising is going, even at the top of the industry. On Blue Owl's October 29th earnings call, chief financial officer Alan Kirshenbaum said GP Stakes the Sixth has gathered about eleven billion dollars, including co-investment. He said the final close may slip into the first quarter of next year. That vehicle buys minority stakes in other alternative managers. It's supposed to be the defensive corner of the industry, a bet on managers' recurring fees rather than on any one fund's exits. If even that strategy is raising slowly at a top platform, LPs may be rationing commitments across the whole alternatives complex. That's broader than just punishing buyout funds with weak cash returns. And if a leading GP-stakes platform is running long on its own flagship, smaller rivals in the strategy are unlikely to find the road easier.

Two honest caveats on Blue Owl. The eleven billion includes co-investment, which flatters the fund total. And we don't know the original target, so we can't measure how far behind plan this is. A one-quarter slide could be ordinary late-raise friction. Still, a later close pushes fee-earning assets, and the fee-related earnings attached to them, into next year. So even the annuity side of manager economics isn't fully insulated from the exit cycle.

The same constraint shows up at the asset owners. Preqin reports CalPERS's private equity allocation at 19.3 percent of the fund as of May, against a 17 percent policy target. That's above target but inside CalPERS's policy band, so it isn't a breach. Its overall private-markets allocation reached 36.1 percent, up from under 34 percent in February of last year. A bellwether pension running over target has less room for fresh commitments. The likely reason is that marks are growing faster than cash is coming back. That's an inference, not something CalPERS has said. But if it's right, dollars stay locked in rather than freed for redeployment. An overweight pension has two levers. It can slow the pace of new commitments, or it can sell existing fund stakes into the secondaries market. Both work against a manager trying to close a flagship. Either way, the limit on new money looks increasingly like capacity at the allocator, not lack of appetite.

Now to the deal side, where the capital that is moving often moves through insiders. The Financial Times reported on October 30th that Clayton, Dubilier & Rice is working with GTCR on a joint take-private of Global Payments, near forty billion dollars in enterprise value. GTCR is already Global Payments' largest outside shareholder. No offer terms have been disclosed. The shares closed Friday at $96.84. With no offer on the table, there's no premium to judge yet. And the forty billion is enterprise value, which includes the company's debt, so it can't be read straight against the share price as a premium.

What can be judged is the financing signal. A take-private anywhere near that size needs lenders willing to underwrite megadeal leverage again. If this one clears, it says the deal-feasibility constraint has loosened at the very top of the market. If it stalls on financing, it says sponsor appetite is running ahead of what lenders will bear. Either way, these are reported talks, not a deal, and it may not happen.

The more interesting piece is GTCR's position. It came out of the Worldpay deal. In that transaction GTCR received more than fourteen billion dollars in cash and roughly ten billion dollars of Global Payments shares. That ten billion is what the shares were worth then, not a current mark. Whether GTCR would roll or sell hasn't been reported. But if it rolls, a take-private turns one of the few listed, sellable sponsor stakes back into private paper. That's the reverse of an exit, at the very moment the industry says exits are the problem. It also puts a fairness question to Global Payments' board. When one bidder is already the largest outside holder, the board has to show the price was tested against someone who isn't. Otherwise price discovery happens inside a small circle of interested parties, not at arm's length.

So where does the cycle stand? Liquidity is being engineered, through fund-level borrowing, longer fundraises, and insider-led deals, rather than found in arm's-length sales. The binding risk is still the exit and distribution logjam, and fund-level leverage is now a live way to paper over it. The near-term tests are close together. Apollo reports on Tuesday. KKR reports on November 9th, the same day final bids are due in GFL Environmental's take-private process. If GFL's winner is a strategic buyer rather than a sponsor, that's a genuine sale to an outside owner. If it's a sponsor, it's more capital going in, not cash coming out. And Blackstone has promised a fourth-quarter jump in realizations, flagged repeatedly and still unproven. Here's the threshold. If Blackstone's realizations and KKR's monetizations show cash exits to outside buyers, rather than recaps, continuation deals, and borrowing, the thaw is real. If not, the engineering continues, and DPI stays a number managers manage rather than earn.

That's the briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
