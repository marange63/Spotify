Good morning, it's Friday, August 14th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

All week we've been hunting the AI buildout's real chokepoint. Monday it was wafers. Then it was CoWoS packaging. Then it was memory. Today TSMC itself answered the question, and in the same breath moved the goalposts.

Speaking at the OCP APAC Summit on August 11th, TSMC's VP of Advanced Packaging, Jun He, disclosed that the company's 5.5x-reticle CoWoS packaging — the interconnect that lets a GPU sit next to up to twelve HBM4 stacks — is now yielding, in his words, "consistently topping 98% across multiple AI customer products and reaching as high as 99% in some cases." That's a real engineering result. CoWoS capacity has nearly doubled every year for three straight years across TSMC's ten advanced-packaging sites, and TSMC is targeting 140,000 wafers a month by late this year, 220,000 by 2027. These figures come from conference remarks relayed through trade press, not a TSMC press release, so treat them as reported rather than officially disclosed. Still, the direction is clear: the packaging bottleneck that constrained GPU shipments in 2024 and 2025 is being engineered away.

But don't hear "yield solved" as "supply solved." He was explicit: "we haven't fully met demand yet." And he named where the constraint is actually going next — not CoWoS, but memory, and for the first time on this show, ABF substrates, the build-up film that carries signals between the chip and the board. That's a genuine shift in the bottleneck's address. It moves from something TSMC owns and controls to inputs made by other companies entirely — Micron, SK hynix, and Samsung on memory; Ibiden, Shinko, Unimicron, and Nan Ya on substrates.

Follow-up reporting on the ABF leg puts real numbers behind that warning, though the numbers themselves come from analyst models relayed through trade coverage, not from the substrate makers directly. On the demand side, ABF consumption for AI systems grew 63% in the past year alone, and more than 70% of all ABF demand now comes from high-density server, AI, and HPC processors. Against that, analysts modeling the leg see substrate demand compounding around 30% a year while capacity grows only about 7%. Morgan Stanley models a shortage building at roughly a 16 percent annual clip through 2027; Goldman Sachs sees a steeper gap, about 33 percent a year through 2028, driven by AI accelerator and ASIC substrates simply growing two-and-a-half to four times larger over two years. Ajinomoto, the Japanese food company that holds the sole license on the base film, is investing 25 billion yen by 2030 to lift its own output by half — a multi-year build that can't close a gap analysts expect to bite within two years. So here's the sharp version of the second-order effect: if those growth curves hold, ABF allocation, not CoWoS yield and maybe not even HBM supply, becomes the thing that decides which customers TSMC can package first. The packaging chokepoint didn't disappear. It migrated one layer down the bill of materials, into a component this show hasn't named before, and into a supplier base concentrated in a handful of Taiwanese and Japanese firms sitting on top of a single Japanese material monopoly.

That hands off cleanly to memory, the leg of TSMC's warning we can already quantify. Fresh pricing data out this week shows HBM4 contracts for 2027 are effectively sold out, at roughly double HBM3e pricing — around $32 a gigabyte to Nvidia, $36 to Broadcom, and $40 to AMD, according to trade reporting. Worth pausing on that spread. AMD is reportedly paying about a quarter more per gigabyte than Nvidia, which matters because AMD's newest accelerator, the memory-heavy MI455X, is not cost-neutral against Nvidia's platform the way raw GPU comparisons suggest — the bill-of-materials math is different, and it runs against AMD on the single scarcest input. It's also worth revisiting what we said on Tuesday, when Samsung's HBM4 yield gains looked like the first real crack in SK hynix's pricing power. A sold-out 2027 order book at double the prior generation's price says that premium is holding, not cracking — qualifying a second source hasn't yet loosened what buyers actually pay. And none of this frees up ordinary memory: the same wafers being pulled onto HBM lines are the ones not making commodity DRAM, so the consumer and enterprise memory tax we've tracked all week keeps running underneath the AI story regardless of who wins the HBM4 fight. So what: memory remains a genuine structural profit story for the chipmakers that control it, not just a cyclical spike investors should expect to fade.

Now, the demand side isn't just absorbing that scarcity — some of it is trying to route around it. AMD announced on August 6th it's acquiring Taalas, a Toronto startup that hardwires specific AI models directly into silicon, eliminating the memory-bandwidth bottleneck that general-purpose GPUs carry for inference. AMD's SVP Vamsi Boppana called it a way to deliver "differentiated inference performance and efficiency," and the plan is to fold the technology into AMD's Instinct, EPYC, and Helios rack-scale roadmap. Think of this as the architectural answer to the exact constraint TSMC just flagged: if a chip is wired for one model, it doesn't need to move as much data through scarce, expensive HBM. It's a bet, not a shipped product — deal value wasn't disclosed, and there's no public timeline for how the technology actually ships. It only pays off for high-volume, stable workloads, since hardwiring trades away flexibility. Worth separating this from AMD's headline quarter — record revenue of $11.5 billion, up 50% year over year, with data-center revenue up 107% to $6.7 billion — which is strong execution but a different story from the inference bet itself. The Taalas deal, alongside AMD's Cerebras partnership, signals that inference economics, not training-scale compute, is becoming the next place chipmakers actually compete. And if model-hardwired silicon proves out at volume, it does something to the whole thesis: it fragments the "general-purpose GPU for everything" assumption underneath most AI-infrastructure capex models, and it becomes a demand-side release valve on exactly the memory bottleneck the lead described — pressure that would land not just on Nvidia's inference roadmap but on every hyperscaler ASIC program chasing the same cost gap.

So step back and look at the whole week. The AI buildout's binding constraint keeps sliding down the bill of materials — from wafers, to packaging, and now to memory and, newly, to a substrate most listeners had never heard named until today. TSMC solved the piece it controls. What it doesn't control — HBM supply and ABF capacity — is where the next eighteen months of accelerator availability will actually get decided. Watch whether that sold-out HBM4 premium survives the next contract cycle, and whether ABF becomes 2027's real chokepoint.

That's the briefing. Have a great weekend.
