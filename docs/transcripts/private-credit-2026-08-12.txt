Good morning. It's August 12th, and here's what's moving underneath corporate credit and private markets.

The story we've been telling you to watch since the ninth is still not resolved, and that non-resolution is itself the news. First Brands, the auto-parts LBO that collapsed into an alleged two-point-three-billion-dollar factoring fraud, has a reformulated liquidating plan on the table in front of a Houston bankruptcy judge. Under that plan, at an assumed three billion dollars of eventual litigation-trust proceeds against eleven-and-a-half billion dollars of allowed claims, Class 3 litigation-trust interests work out to roughly 8.4 cents on the dollar. That's the debtors' own model. It is not a confirmed outcome. As of the most recent evidence, First Brands' lawyers were still urgently pressing the judge to approve the plan, and the debtors themselves warned that failure to win approval could force a disorderly Chapter 7 liquidation instead — one that would likely produce a different, and plausibly worse, recovery than the negotiated 8.4-cent scenario. So park any instinct to call this a printed loss. It's a debtor-modeled illustrative number inside an unconfirmed plan, with a real liquidation tail still live. The debtors' own filings put the stakes in stark terms: absent this settlement, administrative, priority and general unsecured creditors could see zero, because roughly seven-and-a-half billion dollars of senior claims sit ahead of them in the waterfall. And one more caveat worth banking — that waterfall is built by claim class, not by lender type, so there is no disclosed breakdown of what private-credit funds specifically recover versus what banks and factoring counterparties get. The same receivables were pledged to both. So the honest read today is narrower than the headline: the plan on the table implies a recovery near eight cents, confirmation is still pending, and Chapter 7 remains an explicit, debtor-acknowledged risk.

Why does that matter beyond one bankrupt auto-parts company? Because whenever this does resolve — confirmed plan or forced liquidation — it will be the first real loss-given-default print this cycle on a name that both banks and private credit funds were exposed to through the same collateral. Compare that against last week's continuation-vehicle sale in a distressed BDC book that cleared near ninety-five cents on the fund's own mark. Same credit cycle, two dramatically different outcomes, and the difference isn't default frequency — it's collateral integrity. First Brands is a fraud-and-factoring failure, not a clean first-lien workout, so don't generalize eight cents to the asset class. But it does tell you that when a recovery is genuinely tested rather than modeled, the range can be enormous, and private credit's marketing pitch has always rested on assumptions about recovery, not just about who defaults.

That brings us to the book that's still being marked to model rather than tested by a transaction: the Q2 BDC earnings season. The sector narrative is "stabilization" — non-accruals spiked forty percent quarter over quarter in Q1, to just under ten billion dollars of debt at cost across a hundred and seventy-four BDCs, and the story now is that Q2 calmed down. That's true in aggregate. It's not true underneath. Ares Capital's non-accruals rose again, a third straight quarterly increase, to two-point-four percent of the portfolio at cost, with dollar non-accruals up fifteen percent from the prior quarter to seven hundred and eight million. Blue Owl Capital Corp's non-accruals rose to two-point-eight percent from two percent, and its net asset value slipped a point, with repayments running well ahead of new commitments — capital coming back faster than it's going out. Goldman Sachs BDC's non-accruals climbed to five percent from four-point-seven. Meanwhile the distressed-heavy books improved, but off a much worse base — Oaktree Specialty Lending cut its non-accrual share to four-point-two percent from nearly six, FS KKR Capital came down to three-point-eight from four-point-two, and Apollo-backed MidCap Financial fell to four-point-six from five-point-three. Put those side by side and the picture inverts the "stabilization" headline: the highest-quality, most closely watched books keep creeping worse, while the already-troubled books improve off a low bar. That is not what healing looks like. That's the signature of stress broadening into names the market had assumed were clean, even as the average smooths it over. One footnote worth naming precisely, because two different Ares entities get conflated in the reporting: Ares Management, the manager, describes its platform-level U.S. direct-lending non-accruals as flat and under two percent — a different measure from Ares Capital Corp, the publicly traded BDC, at two-point-four percent at cost. They are not the same number, and treating them as interchangeable is exactly the kind of averaging that lets a marketing narrative outrun the underlying book.

So if the present book is quietly deteriorating at the top of the quality stack, where is new risk actually being written? Apollo's record quarter offers the forward-looking answer. Beyond the headline origination numbers you've already heard, the fresh detail is composition: seventy-five percent of this quarter's origination was investment-grade rated, averaging triple-B-plus, at spreads of three hundred forty basis points over Treasuries — and that's before you count the roughly fifty billion dollars of signed deals still to flow into future quarters, including financing tied to the AI-infrastructure buildout. Private credit is moving up the credit-quality stack, but into asset-backed, correlated exposure to the same hyperscaler capex thesis that Apollo, Blackstone, Blue Owl and KKR are all financing simultaneously. Three-forty over Treasuries on triple-B-plus risk is either fair compensation for genuine complexity, or it's a crowded trade getting priced like commodity credit. Worth noting too: Apollo's adjusted earnings per share missed Street consensus even with record fee-related and spread-related earnings — a headline-versus-bottom-line gap that's easy to miss under the origination number.

Pull the threads together and the cycle looks like this. The realized-loss side is still unresolved — First Brands hasn't printed a confirmed number, only a debtor-modeled scenario with a Chapter 7 tail attached. The present book is not stabilizing evenly; it's diverging, with the blue-chip names creeping worse under an averaging headline. And new risk is being written further up the credit-quality stack but into concentrated, correlated AI-capex exposure across nearly every major manager. The unresolved question underneath all three: whether it's loss-given-default, not probability of default, that private credit has systematically under-marked. Watch three things from here — whether First Brands actually gets confirmed or converts to Chapter 7, whether the rest of BDC earnings season extends the blue-chip non-accrual creep, and whether that triple-B-plus, three-forty spread on AI-infrastructure financing holds up once more of it starts trading rather than just closing.
