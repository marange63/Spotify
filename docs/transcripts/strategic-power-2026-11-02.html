Strategic Power, Energy, and Institutional Change

Good morning, it's Monday, November 2nd, and this is your Strategic Power briefing.

Two chokepoints, one clock. The oil chokepoint in the Strait of Hormuz just saw a sharp escalation in a war now in its ninth month, and the next move there is being timed to tomorrow's midterm vote. The magnet chokepoint, China's rare-earth export controls, hits its own deadline exactly one week after that. Let's start with oil.

On Thursday and Friday, October 29th and 30th, Iran's Revolutionary Guard said three tankers caught fire after hitting mines on what it called an unauthorized southern route near Oman. Iran's army said a fourth tanker burned east of the strait. That's Tehran's claim, and only one attack is independently confirmed. The UK's maritime trade agency verified that a small boat attached two explosive devices to a tanker northeast of Khasab, Oman. The blasts started an engine-room fire that disabled the ship, and the crew got out safely. US Central Command says American forces have cleared mines from the main transit routes. The IRGC's spokesman, Hossein Mohebi, answered by demanding proof, quote, "with independent evidence, not a statement." Nobody outside Iran has verified the full count. That gap is the real story. Insurers and shipowners have to price the risk that a mine might be there, whether or not it is. So Tehran can tax the strait's traffic without ever proving a single device exists. Ambiguity itself is the weapon.

Here's the scale underneath that. CNBC notes the strait normally carries about one-fifth of the world's energy supplies. Since the war began in late February, daily transits have fallen to single digits. That's a nine-month structural wound, not a one-week flare-up. So when December Brent settled at $117.64 a barrel on Friday, after touching $118, it was a new leg on an already-damaged base. That was Brent's highest close in six months. Our Throughline last Thursday had Brent near $115, the hawks' inflation alibi. Friday's close sat roughly three dollars above that. OPEC+ met on Sunday and held December output steady, its third straight monthly pause, so that group isn't adding a relief valve. Prices have reportedly eased back since Friday's peak, but outlets disagree on the level. Take the direction, a modest retreat, rather than any single Monday number.

Now the binary everyone's actually watching. The Wall Street Journal reported that Admiral Brad Cooper, the top US commander in the region, briefed President Trump on military options for Iran. The briefing also covered the ongoing US blockade. Per the Journal, the administration may resume military action after tomorrow's vote. That's sourced reporting about options, not a confirmed decision. But it means US energy-security policy is effectively being sequenced to the electoral calendar, not to conditions on the ground. There is a parallel, quieter track. Vice President Vance sent a letter through Qatari mediators to Mohsen Rezaei, who heads Iran's Supreme National Security Council, proposing a US-Iran meeting. Asked whether he had directed that outreach, Trump said simply, "No." Iran's Tasnim news agency said Tehran has received the letter but has not yet responded. So a real off-ramp is on the table, but the administration is declining to own it politically. That cuts against any clean story that de-escalation is imminent. Treat it as a live option, not a resolution.

There's also a cost showing up away from the headline price. CNBC, citing a tanker analyst at the broker Braemar, reports that transportation cost per barrel of Hormuz crude rose to about $21 by late October. In September it was about $6.50, so that's roughly a tripling. Asian importers could now be facing 20 to 30 percent of their delivered crude cost going to freight alone. That's striking, because volumes barely moved. Kpler data cited by CNBC puts October's Hormuz crude exports at nearly 11.1 million barrels a day, only about 2 percent below September. So the war isn't shutting the strait. It's imposing a toll on it. That toll is a structural tax on global oil flows, layered on top of whatever Brent prints. And it can persist even if the mine claims turn out to be exaggerated.

What does this mean beyond the Gulf? A six-month oil high feeds straight into the inflation data the Fed watches. This desk reported last Thursday that the Fed held with two dissents in favor of a hike. Sustained oil in the one-teens strengthens the hawks' hand going into December, without needing a single new data point. On relative positioning, non-Gulf producers and US shale and LNG exporters gain ground simply by not routing through the strait. Import-dependent buyers in Asia and Europe absorb both the higher headline price and that freight tax. Energy abundance at home isn't an abstraction here. It's the hedge, and this war is the live test of whether that hedge pays off.

Oil isn't the only chokepoint with a post-election clock. China's one-year suspension of its expanded rare-earth export controls lapses November 10th, now eight days out. There's still no published renewal instrument from Beijing. The new data point worth banking comes from Bloomberg Intelligence. It forecasts a 4.4-fold rise in non-Chinese output of neodymium and praseodymium, the core magnet materials, from 2024 to 2030. Even so, it still projects a 36 percent global shortfall by 2030, with demand growing about 7 percent a year. So the diversification effort that's actually underway doesn't close Beijing's leverage this decade, even on a forecast that assumes it succeeds. And the outcome isn't just a binary of clean extension or clean lapse. Analysts flag a third path, selective reinstatement aimed at specific elements or end-uses. That would preserve uncertainty, and therefore leverage, without technically breaking a commitment. One more thing holds regardless of November 10th. The April 2025 licensing regime on seven medium and heavy rare earths stays in force either way. So even a full extension is only partial relief for defense and magnet-dependent manufacturers.

Leverage over chokepoints runs both ways, and it isn't only Beijing's to use. Taiwan is preparing to add AI servers and advanced chips to its controlled-goods list, which would make unauthorized shipments to China a criminal offense. Taiwan's economy minister, Kung Ming-hsin, ties it to aligning some of Taiwan's export controls with US measures. This follows our item last Friday on Nvidia B300 chips turning up through exposed telemetry linked to China Telecom. We said then that accidental discoveries like that tend to get turned into policy. This looks like that arc, though we can't establish a direct causal line from that finding to this plan. It's still a proposal, not law, with no enactment date. And a criminal statute only bites if you can detect the smuggling in the first place. The B300 case showed detection currently depends on luck more than enforcement infrastructure. So a bigger penalty without better verification may not move much at the margin.

So here's the watch list for this week. Tomorrow's midterm vote is the trigger date for any US decision on Iran, and we won't guess the result. Watch whether Washington resumes strikes or lets the Vance channel develop. Watch November 10th for whatever Beijing does or doesn't publish on rare earths. And watch whether Taiwan's chip plan moves from proposal to statute. All three sit on the same post-election calendar. And all three test the same question: whether policy announcements become real, operating constraints, or stay on paper.

That's Strategic Power for November 2nd. Stay cautiously optimistic.
