Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Credit & the Corporate Credit Cycle

Transcript · October 29, 2026

Good morning. It's Thursday, October 29th, and this is your private credit briefing.

The public discount on private marks has stopped being just a number on a screen. Now it is forcing actual transactions. And the stress sitting underneath that discount is increasingly resolving somewhere nobody can see it.

Start with the deal. On Tuesday, Ares Capital agreed to buy Apollo's MidCap Financial, the BDC that trades as MFIC, for two point six billion dollars in cash and stock. Shareholders get ten dollars eighty-eight cents a share. That splits into roughly a dollar twenty-one in cash and nine dollars sixty-seven in Ares Capital stock. The companies called it a premium of more than thirty percent to MFIC's prior closing price. Wells Fargo is providing seven hundred fifty million dollars of financing. The deal is signed, not closed, with closing expected in the first half of 2027. Once it closes, Ares Capital's direct-lending portfolio grows to more than eight hundred companies.

This is the arc we've been tracking for a week. On Saturday, the asset base at the big platforms was growing faster than they could lend it out. Yesterday, record fundraising met a sharp pullback in origination volume, and the outlet for that excess capital was a forty-billion-dollar GPU lease. Today the outlet is different. Instead of originating new loans, Ares Capital is buying an existing book outright. When you can't deploy fast enough through origination, you deploy through acquisition.

Now, the headline premium invites a bullish read: private marks getting validated in the market. Don't take it. The merger press release discloses MFIC's own preliminary net asset value, as of September 30th, at approximately twelve dollars eighty-six cents a share. That estimate hasn't been approved by MFIC's board yet, so treat it as preliminary. But against it, the ten-eighty-eight purchase price is roughly fifteen percent below book, not above it. So the thirty-percent premium is entirely a function of how far MFIC's stock had already fallen. By my arithmetic, MFIC had been trading below about two-thirds of its own estimated NAV. This is a take-under of book dressed as a take-out.

There's a second wrinkle. Only the cash sliver, about a dollar twenty-one, is fixed. The stock piece floats. Per the release, the exchange ratio uses the greater of Ares Capital's NAV per share or its share price just before closing. MFIC holders also share in half of any premium at which Ares Capital trades above its own NAV. That sharing is capped, so the stock piece tops out at about one hundred two percent of Ares Capital's NAV. In plain terms, nearly nine-tenths of the consideration is a bet on Ares Capital's stock holding above its own book through next year's closing. MFIC holders aren't cashing out at a tested value. They're swapping one set of private marks for another. The companies themselves describe the deal only as "modestly accretive" to Ares Capital's core earnings. So this is a deal that moves scale, not one that proves private marks are sound.

For context on the acquirer's own book, Ares Capital's third-quarter release, out the same day, put loans on non-accrual at two point four percent of investments at amortized cost. At fair value, the figure is one point four percent. That cost-versus-fair-value gap is the marking question in miniature.

So why would Apollo, a top-tier sponsor, sell a captive vehicle to its direct rival? There are two live readings. One is that sub-scale public BDCs simply aren't worth defending when they trade below book and can't raise equity to grow. The other is that Apollo's capital increasingly prefers insurance-funded and non-traded channels over a listed vehicle that keeps marking against a market that doesn't believe it. Either way, scale is becoming the moat, and the discount has become a transaction instead of a quote.

Why would a stock trade that far below its marks in the first place? Because of where the whole listed sector trades. KBW data, reported by Reuters, put listed BDCs at roughly point seven two times net asset value. KBW reads that discount as implying about nine percent of further portfolio losses that NAVs haven't recognized. On Monday we named valuation as the biggest unresolved risk. Yesterday we said the gap between public prices and private marks would itself measure the lag. Now it has a rough size.

That figure is KBW's inference from price, not a disclosed loss, so weigh both sides. A discount also prices in illiquidity, fee drag, and falling base-rate income. Those would make nine percent an overstatement of real credit loss. Income quality cuts the other way. Allianz Research puts PIK interest at about eleven percent of direct-lending income, so a meaningful slice of reported yield isn't cash in the door. Add the foreclosure data I'll get to, and my lean is that the market is closer to right than the NAVs are. That isn't proven yet.

The discount also explains the consolidation mechanically. A BDC at point seven two times book cannot issue equity without diluting its holders, so it either shrinks or sells. And every sale removes another independently priced public BDC, leaving fewer market data points to check private marks against.

The same gap shows up on the non-traded side, just with a different exit. Across nineteen NAV BDCs, Robert A. Stanger and Company data show third-quarter tender requests easing to eleven and a half percent of NAV, down from twelve point seven. Fidelity's BDC filled every request for a fourth straight quarter, but it is the exception, not the rule. Here's my reading of the plumbing. Listed holders exit at roughly seventy-two cents on the dollar. Non-traded holders queue to redeem at full NAV behind five-percent caps. Of course the queue persists. Same gap, different plumbing.

If the market is pricing in losses the NAVs haven't shown, where exactly are those losses going? Lincoln International data, reported by Debtwire, put the covenant default rate in private credit around three percent. The shadow rate, once you count bad PIK loans, is closer to five. And foreclosures, lenders simply taking the keys instead of filing for bankruptcy, ran above twenty-four billion dollars in 2025. This year is on track to beat that by a substantial margin. That rhymes with the Moody's numbers we covered yesterday, though Moody's uses a different sample, so don't chain the two into one series.

The mechanism matters more than any restated rate. Private credit is increasingly resolving its worst credits through quiet foreclosure rather than public Chapter 11. That can be a feature. Fast key-turning often preserves more recovery value than a contested bankruptcy or a creditor fight. But it's also a bug. A foreclosure generates no public docket and no independent recovery print. And the asset that comes out the other side gets marked by the same manager who made the original loan. It also means public default and recovery statistics will understate private losses heading into the 2027 and 2028 maturity wall. Opacity, not the default rate itself, is the risk that compounds here.

One more piece belongs in this chain. CLO formation is the engine that buys leveraged loans, and it keeps the syndicated refinancing door open for the strongest private-credit borrowers. That engine is cooling. Egan-Jones data show U.S. CLO new issuance at a hundred twenty-six point eight billion dollars through September. That's down seventeen point six percent by volume against last year's record pace. It's a cooling from a peak, not a seizure. September alone still priced thirty-eight billion dollars of deals. But at the margin it narrows the exit the best credits use to leave private books. That tilts what's left behind toward weaker paper.

So where does the cycle actually stand? Still a grind, not a crisis. But the market has stopped merely pricing the gap between public skepticism and private marks, and started trading on it. Ares Capital buying MidCap Financial is the first priced deal built on that gap, and it resolved against the bullish case, not for it. The biggest unresolved risk remains marks on foreclosed and PIK-heavy assets that no public process has tested. Watch two things from here. First, the wave of BDC earnings in the first two weeks of November, where non-accruals and NAV marks either confirm or refute KBW's implied gap. Second, whether another manager follows Ares Capital with a signed BDC deal of its own.

That's the briefing. Talk tomorrow.
