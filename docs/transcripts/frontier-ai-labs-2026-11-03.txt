Good morning. It's Tuesday, November 3rd, and this is your frontier AI labs briefing.

One disclosure up front. Anthropic makes the model behind this show, and today's lead benchmark has an Anthropic model on top. Weigh the framing accordingly.

For most of the past week, this desk's scorecards have ended the same way: no independently verified capability move. That streak broke on November 2nd. SemiAnalysis, the chip-and-compute research shop, published a new agentic benchmark called GDPx. It's the first outside, cross-lab read of frontier capability since Artificial Analysis's revised index put Gemini 4 Carbon first, which we covered on October 29th.

Here are the headline numbers, all from SemiAnalysis. Anthropic's Claude Opus 5.5 scored 12 percent. Google's Gemini 4 Carbon scored 10.9. OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra scored 9.6. In SemiAnalysis's own words, "even the best model scores just 12%." The score is pass-at-one across three attempts. It covers eleven domains, weighted equally. Grading mixes strict pass-fail and partial credit, depending on the domain. So the headline is a score, not a count of finished tasks.

Two things to hold at once. First, the spread at the top is thin. Opus 5.5 leads Carbon by just over a point. Carbon leads Astra by a similar margin. No error bars are reported, and nobody has replicated the result yet. On October 29th, this desk declined to treat Carbon's one-point lead on Artificial Analysis as decisive. The same standard applies to Opus 5.5 today.

Second, the ranking splinters domain by domain. Opus 5.5 is the best all-around model, and wins quant trading, hardware design, and chip design. Carbon is the strongest biology model. SemiAnalysis says that's likely due to weaker safety classifiers. Our read: Carbon may be refusing less, not knowing more. Carbon also edges out the win in software engineering. Astra wins recursive self-improvement convincingly, and is the best model for materials science. Then there's Meta. SemiAnalysis puts Meta meaningfully behind in the aggregate. Yet it calls Meta's Muse Wonder Full "the best available cyber model today by a meaningful margin." And finance and legal tasks were so hard that essentially every model scored exactly zero.

This also closes a loop. On October 29th, we asked whether any outside evaluator would get to test Google's gated Carbon model. One did, and it placed second. SemiAnalysis doesn't say how it got access. Now notice the contrast in provenance. Carbon's cybersecurity scores at launch were Google's own, and Google measured them with the safety guardrails switched off. GDPx is somebody else's ruler, on different tasks, so the two sets of numbers don't compare. The point is who holds the ruler, not the size of the score. Our inference: if GDPx becomes a recurring reference, buyers and investors get a cross-lab yardstick the labs don't control. That erodes the marketing value of benchmark suites a lab picks for itself.

Now the caveat on GDPx's own independence. SemiAnalysis built its tasks with fourteen specialized data and RL-environment partners. At least two have standing ties to the labs being ranked. Arithmetic supplied the cybersecurity tasks. SemiAnalysis says it "focuses exclusively on selling cybersecurity data to American frontier labs." Fleet was the sole source of the alignment tasks. SemiAnalysis says they "maintain research partnerships with every major frontier lab."

SemiAnalysis discloses no commercial relationship of its own with Anthropic, OpenAI, or Google. But silence isn't the same thing as independence. There's also a structural incentive, and this is our inference. Firms that sell training data and environments to labs benefit from a benchmark that shows a big remaining gap. None of that proves the scores are skewed. It does mean the exam writers do business with the students, specifically in cyber and alignment. Treat it as a caveat on independence, not a reason to dismiss the result.

Now widen the lens, because the crown moves with the ruler. Artificial Analysis, a different outside evaluator, scored Meta's Wonder Full at 56 on its Intelligence Index. That's up from 48 for Meta's prior model. It puts Wonder Full level with Claude Sonnet 5.5, and ahead of Opus 5.5 on coding-agent tasks. That score reaches us through Meta's own launch post, dated October 12th. So treat it as three-week-old context, not news. Meta priced the base Wonder model at four dollars per million input tokens and twenty per million output. That's the same band this desk said the US frontier tier had converged on, back on October 29th.

So line the rulers up. Opus 5.5 leads GDPx overall. Carbon topped Artificial Analysis's revised index last week. Wonder Full wins GDPx's cyber domain, and beats Opus 5.5 on coding agents by Artificial Analysis's measure. And the margins at the top keep landing around a point. On this evidence, no lab holds a capability lead you could call a moat.

Ranking aside, the absolute level may matter more. So what does 12 percent actually mean? There are two readings. The bearish one says frontier marketing has run well ahead of demonstrated capability on long-horizon, economically valuable work. On that view, capex is being deployed well ahead of demonstrated breadth. The bullish one says an unsaturated benchmark aimed at real economic tasks is a map of untapped revenue. Every point gained from here is plausibly billable work, not a diminishing return on a saturated leaderboard.

One data point complicates both stories. Anthropic's IPO filing shows first-half revenue of 16.3 billion dollars, against 1.16 billion a year earlier. Same disclosure applies, since it's Anthropic's own filing. That revenue comes from the lab whose best model scores 12 percent on GDPx. Our inference is that current revenue is being earned on a narrower band of work than GDPx tests. So low breadth scores don't contradict the revenue growth. They bound how far it can run without real capability gains. What would settle the bull-bear question is the next version of GDPx, and whether scores move faster than revenue.

So if capability isn't separating the labs this week, distribution and capital have to. Prices point the same way. With the frontier converging on one price band, our inference is that pure model-serving margins come under pressure. That brings us to OpenAI, which placed third on GDPx and spent the week buying anchor customers instead.

On November 2nd, at the ADIPEC conference in Abu Dhabi, OpenAI signed a set of agreements. Two of them carry the story. The first is with ADNOC, the state oil company, and G42, to deploy OpenAI's technology across ADNOC's operations. The second is a five-year agreement with Inception42, covering frontier AI adoption across government, enterprise, and education. The coverage we have attaches no dollar value to either.

The backdrop is the round we covered on Sunday. OpenAI cut its target to about 20 billion dollars at a 1.25 trillion dollar valuation. That's down from about 30 billion at a higher valuation. The Wall Street Journal puts commitments so far at about 11 billion.

Hold two readings here too. The bull case is durable lock-in. State-linked anchor customers, one of them on a five-year term, are distribution a benchmark score can't buy. The bear case is fundraising optics. On Sunday we noted the reporting describes the round as led by investors from the United Arab Emirates. Signing visible deals in that same region, mid-raise, looks like signaling as much as substance. What tells the two apart is whether either agreement ever acquires a dollar value or a compute commitment. The other tell is where the round finally closes.

Either way, there's a second-order cost. These deals tie OpenAI's growth story more tightly to one region's capital and one region's customers. Lock-in and concentration risk are the same fact, read from two sides. So what: OpenAI is buying distribution it can't win on the leaderboard, just as Anthropic goes public on its own disclosed revenue.

Quick scorecard, by dimension. Capability: compressed and ruler-dependent, with the first outside cross-lab read since October 29th. Benchmark credibility: Google's self-reported, guardrails-off cyber scores on one side, outside rulers on the other, and GDPx carrying its own partner caveat. Distribution: OpenAI moving into sovereign channels. Capital access: OpenAI's round still unfilled. Open-weight pressure: one catalyst due tomorrow.

Three things to watch. One, whether Moonshot's Kimi K3.1 Pro actually ships open weights tomorrow, November 4th, as promised. Two, whether anyone replicates GDPx, or SemiAnalysis publishes a second version, and whether any lab disputes the methodology given those task-partner ties. Three, whether either Abu Dhabi agreement acquires a dollar value, and where OpenAI's round finally settles.
