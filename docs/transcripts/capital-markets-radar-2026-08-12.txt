Good morning, it's Wednesday, August 12th, and this is Capital Markets Radar.

We left the Fed's three-way fork unresolved on Tuesday, and this morning's July CPI print was supposed to be the tiebreaker. It wasn't clean. Headline CPI rose 0.20% month over month, holding the annual rate steady at 2.7%, which reads as roughly in line. Core CPI did not cooperate. Core rose 0.32% on the month, the strongest core reading since January, and that pushed core year over year up to 3.1%, the hottest core print since February and well above the 2.5% consensus economists had penciled in. So this is not the cooling number the market wanted going into a Fed meeting that's already split.

Look at where the heat concentrated, because it's not noise. Medical care services rose 0.8% on the month, airfares jumped 4.0%, both running well above expectations. And tariff-exposed goods kept firming too: footwear prices jumped 1.4% in July, the largest monthly move since April 2021. Strip out the volatile pieces and you still get firmness in the categories the Fed watches most closely for persistence. That's a combination of sticky core services and tariff pass-through showing up in the same print, which is a harder inflation problem for a central bank to talk down than a single volatile category.

Remember the setup here. On July 29th, the FOMC held rates at 3.50 to 3.75%, but three regional presidents, Cleveland's Beth Hammack, Minneapolis's Neel Kashkari, and Dallas's Lorie Logan, dissented in favor of a hike. That's the first time since September 2016 that three FOMC members dissented with a unified directional view. Chair Kevin Warsh called it "a good family fight" and explicitly declined to rule out a September hike. Eight days later, July payrolls fell 23,000 against a consensus for plus 83,000, with May and June revised down a combined 103,000 jobs, and that flipped the conversation toward a September cut. Today's CPI was meant to settle which side wins. Instead it handed the hawks new evidence. The honest read is that markets still lean toward a September cut on the growth story, but I can't give you a clean post-CPI odds number this morning, because a verified, dated repricing figure isn't available yet, and that gap is itself worth noting: the market hasn't finished digesting this print. What we do know is that a committee with three sitting hawkish dissenters just got a core inflation number that validates their concern, which raises the odds of continued dispersion inside the FOMC rather than a tidy consensus landing on a cut.

And there's a second-order cost to that dispersion. A committee that splits three ways under a brand-new chair can't offer clean forward guidance, because it doesn't have a settled view to guide toward. So expect the rate path to stay contested and realized rate volatility to run hot around every print between now and the September 16th meeting. Each data release becomes a re-vote, not a confirmation, which is exactly the regime where a single number can whip the curve around.

Now, the inflation side that CPI alone can never settle. The Strait of Hormuz remains effectively closed to normal traffic. Shipping through the strait has collapsed to somewhere between eight and fifteen vessels a day, versus roughly 130 transits before the US-Israel war on Iran began in February. Brent crude, as of August 10th, sat around $87.72 a barrel and WTI around $82.13, both up about 5% that day, with Brent carrying roughly a 16% premium versus pre-war levels. Iran says the blockade stays until sanctions ease and reparations are paid. That's a persistent, supply-side inflation impulse, and it's the kind of shock rate policy can't touch. So even before today's hot core print, Warsh's room to cut without looking like he's tolerating inflation was already narrow. Now it's narrower.

The same supply shock is showing up in industrial metals. COMEX copper hit a fresh all-time high of $6.77 a pound on August 7th, breaking the previous record set in May. Part of that is speculative buying tied to data-center and power-infrastructure demand, so it's corroborating evidence that the AI buildout is showing up in hard-commodity prices, not just capex guidance. But part of it is also Hormuz-linked: the strait disruption has cut into sulphuric acid supply, an input to copper processing, tightening physical availability on top of the demand story. So the same geopolitical shock that's keeping oil elevated is now raising input costs for the exact grid and data-center buildout that's driving copper demand in the first place. Copper doesn't usually carry an inflation signal, a geopolitical signal, and an AI-capex signal all at once; right now it carries all three. That's a margin and timeline risk for the AI-infrastructure thesis worth watching, not just a commodity headline.

Gold is the cleanest tell for how this whole complex is trading. It jumped over $100 an ounce on August 7th to $4,353, its best week since January, on the back of that weak jobs report. By this morning it was quoted around $4,406, still grinding higher. Gold right now is a dual hedge: one leg is the rate-cut trade, rallying on falling real-yield expectations, the other leg is Hormuz safe-haven demand. That's an unusual combination, and it means gold is exposed on the rate leg specifically to a print like today's. A genuinely hot CPI read would tend to knock down the rate-cut leg of that rally even as the geopolitical leg holds firm, so watch for gold and long-duration Treasuries to start diverging rather than moving together, the way they have for the past week. The dollar sits on the same fault line. It's been drifting lower as cut odds firmed, but a core print this sticky argues the other way, so the easy dollar-down trade is no longer one-directional either.

Which brings us to the part of the market that isn't pricing any of this. High-yield credit spreads sit at 270 to 271 basis points as of August 10th, in the richest decile of their entire history against a long-run median near 450 basis points. Spreads below 350 basis points have historically signaled late-cycle complacency. So you've got a war-driven oil shock, a core inflation print that just handed ammunition to the Fed's hawks, and a labor market that's rolling over, three live and independent shock vectors, and credit is priced as if none of them exist. That's the asymmetry to watch. Spreads this tight give almost no cushion, so if any one of those threads breaks, the widening won't be gradual.

Put it together: the CPI print you were waiting for to resolve the Fed's dilemma instead confirmed it. The hawks have real data now, the doves still have a weakening labor market, and the geopolitical inflation impulse from Hormuz isn't going anywhere. Every asset that rallied on the rate-cut trade this past week, gold, copper, credit, is exposed to the same repricing risk if September doesn't deliver the cut markets have been counting on. The one clean thing that would break the tension is a Hormuz reopening, and that's a headline, not a forecast. That's it for today.
