Private Credit & the Corporate Credit Cycle

Transcript · November 15, 2026

Good morning, it's Sunday, November 15th, 2026, and this is your private credit briefing.

All week this desk has said the first real price on AI data-center debt would come from ING's loan auction, with bids due November 19th. A signal arrived early instead. It came from the buyer's side, and it showed up as a smaller deal rather than a lower price.

Here's the lead. QTS, the Blackstone-backed data-center operator, cut a planned private debt sale by about sixty percent. Bloomberg reported on November 13th that it struggled to attract enough demand and refused to pay much higher yields. The target was about eight hundred million dollars. What's left is about three hundred million.

The structural detail comes from a secondary summary of Bloomberg's reporting, so treat it as reported, not final. The reduced raise is two instruments, not one. A hundred million dollars is a seven-year private placement. It priced on Friday at two hundred forty-five basis points over benchmarks. That's a market dominated by long-term holders like life insurers and asset managers. The other two hundred million is a five-year bank loan, expected to price in the coming weeks.

The use of proceeds is the tell. QTS had planned to use the full eight hundred million to refinance existing five-year bank debt. With the smaller deal, that bank financing simply stays in place.

So what actually happened? QTS didn't cut its price to clear the market. It cut its size. Buyers wanted more yield than QTS would pay on the rest, so the rest never got sold. That's a bid-ask gap between an issuer and the private capital it's counting on.

There are two honest readings. The first is issuer discipline. A Blackstone-backed operator, with its existing bank financing as a fallback, simply declined to overpay. On that reading, the size cut signals strength, not distress. The second reading is buyer fatigue. QTS has already tapped the private placement market several times. Some long-term buyers may simply be full on the name. That's this desk's inference, not something the reporting states. One deal from one issuer can't settle which reading is right.

That matters for the insurance channel. Life insurers are the natural long-dated buyer of paper like this. If they're rationing a repeat issuer, the annuity-funded bid for data-center credit has a ceiling.

One more detail cuts against complacency. People with knowledge of the deal warned that wider pricing here could set a more expensive reference point for other Blackstone-related debt. So bank this. The non-bank bid for data-center credit exists, but at a price above where this paper was meant to clear.

Now place that in the arc this desk has tracked since the end of October. First, banks sought credit insurance on AI debt. Then ING moved to sell data-center loans outright. Then bank and private credit committees hesitated on new deals. Now a sponsor-backed issuer sizes down rather than meet the buyers' price. Each step moves this risk closer to a real non-bank price.

The second-order effect lands on the banks. ING's sale tests whether banks can lend and then distribute this risk to non-banks. If non-bank buyers demand a premium that issuers won't pay, that exit narrows. The exposure either stays on bank balance sheets longer, as QTS's old bank debt now will, or it gets sold at a discount.

That's why the ING auction cuts both ways. As a reminder, ING is seeking bids on about five hundred million dollars spread across twelve data-center loans. One slice is tied to the Stargate project. Bids are due November 19th. No price talk has surfaced since the sale was first reported, so there's nothing to react to yet. A clean clear near par would push back on today's read. Discounted bids, or lots pulled, would extend it.

Here's the subtler point. Resizing hides the price. When a deal shrinks instead of repricing, no clearing spread prints for the part that didn't sell. Holders of similar paper get nothing to check their model marks against. QTS's two forty-five on the small slice is the exception, and it's a real comparable. But it prices the paper buyers were willing to take, not the paper they turned down. So the first hard mark on data-center loans may still come from the ING bids, not from private deals.

The same pattern, clearing the good paper and leaving the rest, shows up at the vehicle level. BlackRock's TCP Capital is one of the BDCs reviewing strategic options. Yesterday this desk asked whether it would actually transact, and at what fraction of net asset value. Part of the answer is in. Per an Octus BDC roundup, TCP sold assets to Pantheon Ventures at 98.9 percent of their reported fair value.

On its face, that's a near-mark sale, and good news for anyone worried TCP's book is overvalued. But the detail matters. The assets sold had, on average, later maturities and lower non-accrual rates than the rest of the book. TCP sold its better paper. A near-mark price on the best loans tells you little about the marks on what remains. By construction, the residual is more concentrated in troubled credit. And each sale like this makes the leftover vehicle harder to sell at a similar price. So TCP's final strategic outcome is a tougher test of its NAV than the Pantheon price implies.

Now TCP's quarter. Per earnings-call transcript coverage, not the 10-Q, non-accruals jumped to 14.2 percent of portfolio cost. At fair value they were 3.4 percent. Both ratios roughly doubled from the prior quarter. We don't know when the Pantheon sale happened relative to the September 30th quarter end, so don't pin the jump on it.

What stands out is the gap between cost and fair value. It's the widest this desk has logged all season. Earlier this month we flagged about a point at BCRED, about two at Blue Owl's listed BDC, and about three at Goldman's BDC. TCP's gap is more than ten points. The two ratios use different denominators, so you can't read a recovery rate off the gap. But you can read it two ways. One is that TCP has marked its problem names down hard, which is conservative. The other is that the embedded loss is larger than the fair-value headline suggests. Both stay live until more of the book actually trades.

Not every lender looks like that. Chicago Atlantic BDC reported zero non-accruals, against a reported industry average of 3.7 percent of cost. That average is undated, and its peer set isn't specified, so hold the comparison loosely. But the point stands. This is a dispersion story, and manager selection is doing more work than asset-class beta.

So pull the threads together. Across the system this week, stress is surfacing through rationing and selection, not markdowns. QTS kept less than four-tenths of its planned deal. TCP sold its better paper. Both echo the redemption queues this desk covered on Thursday. In semi-liquid funds, sponsors paid out only part of what investors asked for. In each case, the system rations volume instead of printing a lower price. That can keep headline marks and default rates calm for longer than a cycle that clears by price. Volume that never trades doesn't enter anyone's fair-value hierarchy.

Where does that leave the cycle? There's no broad default-rate turn to report today. The stress is real, but it's showing up in what doesn't get done, not in lower prices. The biggest unresolved risk is the paper nobody has priced. That's TCP's residual book, and the data-center debt still sitting with banks. It stays marked to model, with no trading comparable to test it against.

Watch three things. First, ING's bid deadline on November 19th. Second, whether QTS's two hundred million dollar bank loan prices, and at what level. Third, how TCP's strategic review resolves now that the easy sale is done.

That's the lending machine for November 15th. Stay cautiously optimistic.
