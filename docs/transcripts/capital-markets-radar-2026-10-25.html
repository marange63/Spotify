Good morning, it's Sunday, October 25th, and this is Capital Markets Radar. The AI financing scare is now sorting capital by how fast it can leave.

All week, the worry in this market has been that AI borrowers were running out of exits at the same time. Private lenders were hesitating at the AI door, and Meta's five-year default swaps hit a record. Over the past few days, the largest alternatives manager on the planet has been reported as stepping through that door. The headlines say Blackstone sees opportunity in wider AI spreads. The transcript says something narrower, and the gap between the two is today's story.

Start with what Blackstone actually disclosed. On the third-quarter earnings call, Jon Gray said that across roughly 1.4 trillion dollars of assets, about 7 percent sits in AI infrastructure, split between debt and equity. Seven percent sounds modest, but on a base that size it is a very large book.

Now the framing. Bloomberg and Private Equity Wire reported the call as Blackstone leaning into widening AI credit spreads. Read the transcript itself and Gray is more guarded. By the transcript's account, he treated the rate and spread move mainly as a valuation headwind on assets Blackstone already owns, the stabilized, leased ones. The upside he described is narrower. On new assets that are not yet leased, he said, Blackstone should be able to command a higher yield. And he added a caveat the easy version skips. He said it is getting harder to deliver, particularly at scale.

Then the composition of the book. A summary of the same call reports that the vast majority of that 7 percent sits in data centers with fifteen-to-twenty-year leases. So the higher-yield opportunity Gray pointed to is a small, newer slice. It is not a blanket bid on the AI-credit complex. Here's the uncomfortable part. That extra yield on unleased projects is compensation for exactly what's missing, a signed tenant. And tenant cash flow is the risk that wobbled when CoreWeave's debt sold off earlier this month. So the exposure to a CoreWeave-style shock is concentrated in the newest vintage, not spread across the whole book. The takeaway: the private valve is open, but at a price, and only for the riskiest new projects.

So is private credit actually being paid for the risk it's taking? Credit Benchmark reviewed twenty major U.S. private-credit funds. That's a sample, not the whole market. It found that median loan spreads have compressed to roughly 4 to 5 percent, down from 5 to 6 percent a year earlier. Over the same stretch, the share of loans priced above 8 percent has grown, and default risk is drifting higher. Put those together and the compensation isn't spread across the book. It's concentrated in a riskier tail. The typical loan pays less than it did a year ago, while a growing slice pays a lot more for carrying the risk everyone is worried about.

That has a second-order consequence, and this part is our inference. Headline private-credit spread data can look calm while the tail fattens underneath it. As AI financing migrates from public bonds and default swaps into private, asset-specific loans, it leaves the instruments that reprice every day. Stress then tends to surface later and in lumps, as concentrated blowups rather than steady index widening. The market watching public CDS is watching the half of the problem that marks daily.

Now layer on liquidity. On the same call, Blackstone's CFO, Michael Chae, said net realizations were down 20 percent quarter over quarter. That's the firm's gauge of value crystallized from exits. Meanwhile, Blackstone took in 63.3 billion dollars of new inflows in the quarter, per InvestmentNews. So money keeps coming in while exits slow. Our inference: the capital being offered the newest, highest-yielding AI projects comes from investors who are waiting longer to get cash back. A manager raising that much each quarter also has to deploy it. Reaching for higher yields on new projects is partly about putting fee-earning capital to work, not only about value. The bottom line: AI credit risk isn't leaving the system. It's migrating into the part that marks slowest and discloses least, and that part has its own liquidity strain.

Now the other side of that trade, the money that can leave daily. Per Bloomberg reporting of Goldman's prime-brokerage data, relayed by Hedgeweek, hedge funds cut technology exposure in the five sessions to October 15th by the largest proportion since Goldman's records began in 2016. That lands on top of short sales outpacing long buys by roughly five to one this month, per the same Goldman data. But tech still makes up about 23.5 percent of Goldman's global prime book. That's around the 90th percentile of the past five years. These funds trimmed. They didn't exit.

Here's the twist. Goldman's global fundamental long-short funds were down 2.5 percent for October as of Monday, cutting year-to-date gains to 10.1 percent. Funds are cutting their most crowded exposure while losing money doing it. That points to part of this selling being forced de-grossing, not high-conviction calls. Two limits apply. This is Goldman's client base, not the whole hedge-fund universe. And every figure is relayed second-hand. For positioning, the point is this. A book still that crowded in tech can squeeze just as easily as it can keep liquidating. A squeeze, if it comes, would be a positioning event, not a verdict on AI financing.

That book now walks straight into Fed week. The FOMC decision lands Wednesday, October 28th, and it hasn't happened, so no outcome and no odds here. The remaining hyperscaler earnings are expected the same week. A reassuring signal would meet a short base primed to cover. A disappointment would meet a long book with plenty of room left to de-gross. That's the setup, not a forecast.

The rest of the cross-asset tape goes into that meeting looking, to us, more like a growth scare than an inflation scare. On the Treasury's own par curve, the 10-year yield fell 17 basis points on Thursday the 22nd, to 4.96 percent. Gold didn't share in that haven bid. It has slipped from its October 9th close of 4,194 dollars, per goldsilver.com. And the dollar eased too, touching a three-week low on October 20th as the yen firmed, per Vantage Markets. So the flight to safety went into duration, not into bullion and not into the dollar. Our read: bonds now hold a crowded haven trade of their own, and a reassuring Fed or earnings catalyst would test it at the same moment as the equity shorts.

The same liquid-money logic shows up in crypto. On Thursday, October 22nd, U.S. spot bitcoin ETFs saw 635.6 million dollars of net outflows, per SoSoValue data reported by The Block. That was the largest single-day outflow since late June. It more than wiped out the previous day's inflows. BlackRock's IBIT led the exits. By Saturday evening, bitcoin was trading around 80,380 dollars, per CoinDesk. The most liquid institutional crypto wrapper is behaving like the hedge-fund book, with fast money leaving at the first sign of stress. One caveat: one heavy outflow day right after a heavy inflow day is a choppy wrapper, not yet a regime change.

So here's where this leaves us. The AI financing scare is sorting capital by its liquidity, not settling who ultimately eats the risk. Daily-liquid money, the hedge-fund books and the bitcoin ETF wrapper, is running. Locked-up capital, led by Blackstone, is stepping toward the newest and narrowest slice of that risk. And across private credit, the extra pay sits in the tail while the median loan got cheaper. Watch three things into the Fed decision and the rest of earnings season. First, whether new, unleased AI-infrastructure deals keep pricing at those higher yields. Second, whether the hedge-fund book squeezes or keeps liquidating. Third, whether the bitcoin ETF wrapper keeps bleeding, or whether this proves to be one choppy week.

That's Capital Markets Radar for this Sunday. Have a good week ahead.
