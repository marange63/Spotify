Cautious Optimism Briefings

Strategic Power, Energy, and Institutional Change

Transcript · October 10, 2026

Good morning, it's Saturday, October 10th, 2026.

Today's theme: both capitals are holding their hardest levers in reserve rather than pulling them. Meanwhile the one input neither side can negotiate, physical power, still runs on its own slow clock.

Start with a correction, and then a complication. Twice this week, on Wednesday and Thursday, we treated November 10 as the single cliff for US-China trade. On Wednesday we also said nothing showed renewal talks underway. That missed a big piece. Seeking Alpha reports that Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent announced on September 23 that the trade truce, originally due to expire November 10, now runs through January 10, 2027. China's Commerce Ministry later said its trade arrangements with the United States are extended to the same date. That covers the tariff and non-tariff measures from last October's Kuala Lumpur deal. We missed an extension that was already two weeks old, and you should know that.

Now the complication, which matters more. The Commerce Ministry statement does not mention rare earths. The suspension of China's October 2025 rare-earth controls sits in a separate instrument, Announcement Number 70. According to the Minor Metals Trade Association, Beijing announced it as a twelve-month suspension on November 10, 2025. On its own terms, that suspension runs out in November. And per trade-press analysis, no numbered Commerce Ministry announcement has amended it. Reading the January extension as covering rare earths is therefore an inference, not a published text. So what: the tariff cliff moved to January, but the rare-earth date we warned you about may still be standing, a month from today, under its own name.

The price tape agrees with the cautious reading. Argus's European index for dysprosium oxide sits at twenty-five hundred to three thousand dollars a kilogram. That is up a hundred and ninety-four percent since January, and nearly tenfold since China's controls began. Erbium oxide is up eighty-seven percent since the start of April alone. Those are not de-escalation prices. Part of the reason is simple. China's April 2025 controls on seven elements, including dysprosium and yttrium, were never suspended at all. Beijing can tighten that layer without breaking any truce. For magnet makers and defense-electronics supply chains, the extension changes the timing of the risk, not its structure. Western refining capacity remains the slow-moving hedge. A calmer-sounding truce plausibly takes some political urgency out of funding it. So what: the deadline moved, the leverage did not, and ex-China buyers are already paying as though scarcity is structural.

Beijing is holding its lever in reserve. So is Washington, and the clearest example is Taiwan. Today is Taiwan's National Day. In his address, as reported overnight, President Lai Ching-te called on Indo-Pacific democracies to help maintain peace and the status quo. Beyond that headline, the speech hasn't been verified, so I'll leave it there. The substance sits in Washington. A record fourteen-billion-dollar arms package for Taiwan was approved by Congress in January. It still needs President Trump's sign-off, and it has been stalled since May. According to Fortune, Trump has said he is holding it "in abeyance and it depends on China." He has also called it "a very good negotiating chip."

That is the obvious reading, and there is a competing one. In May, Acting Navy Secretary Hung Cao told a Senate appropriations subcommittee the pause was to make sure the US had the munitions it needed for Epic Fury, the Iran operation. In the same breath, he added that the US has plenty. So the munitions rationale is on the record, and so is the official denial of a shortage. Read together, the pause looks like a diplomatic chip resting on a real question about defense-production depth. That testimony is five months old. Nothing in the record lets us cleanly separate the two motives. Nothing reports a decision to cancel or release the package either. One inference follows. The longer big-ticket deliveries hinge on US-China bargaining, the stronger Taipei's case for cheaper systems it can build at home, like drones and mines. So what: congressional approval is not delivered capability. Five months on, the gap is being filled with leverage, not hardware.

Washington's third lever is the one that comes due soonest. On Thursday this briefing said the Graham sanctions bill sat on the president's desk with no confirmed signature. The Center for Strategic and International Studies now describes it as law. Per CSIS, most of its provisions take effect October 18, eight days from now. It allows tariffs of up to five hundred percent on Russian goods. It also allows up to one hundred percent on goods from the top importers of Russian energy, a group that, as we reported Thursday, includes China and India. Sources still disagree on the passage and signing dates, so I won't assert one. And this is authority, not action. No tariff can bite before the eighteenth, and no country has been targeted. Separately, Treasury's sanctions office extended the Lukoil wind-down license through October 22.

Now revisit Thursday's sequencing argument. We said the Graham tariff and a November rare-earth lapse could fire in the same window. Half of that has changed. The broad tariff truce now runs to January 10, so the tariff side of the relationship has more runway. But the rare-earth suspension still appears to end in November. So the window we flagged survives in narrower form. Tariff authority goes live October 18, and the rare-earth term ends about three weeks later. Here is the conditional. If the administration aimed the new tariff at China as a top buyer of Russian energy, it would collide with a truce Washington itself just extended. That cost makes China an awkward first target. So what: the next two weeks test whether Washington actually reaches for a tool it just built, and whom it points it at.

Every lever so far can be delayed with a signature or a statement. The base layer can't. We owe you a second, smaller correction here. On Wednesday we said Google and Constellation's 890-megawatt nuclear uprate deal had no stated completion date. The American Nuclear Society's reporting gives one: the full 890 megawatts by 2032. Constellation, not Google, is putting more than four-point-three billion dollars into eleven reactors to get there.

Now set that against PJM, the country's largest grid. In PJM's latest capacity auction, only five hundred twenty-five megawatts of the hundred thirty-eight-point-three gigawatts secured was new capacity, per Canary Media. One hyperscaler contract will eventually add more new supply than that entire auction bought. The comparison shows scale, not matching timing. The auction buys for a single delivery year, while the uprates arrive in tranches through 2032. On the demand side, Power Engineering reports that nearly fifty-one hundred megawatts of the fifty-two-hundred-fifty megawatt rise in PJM's 2027-28 peak-load forecast came from data centers.

Put those two numbers together. New demand in PJM is almost entirely data centers. New procured supply is almost entirely existing plant. The inference is that existing generators' capacity value stays under upward pressure until uprates and new build arrive, which on today's evidence runs into the 2030s. That favors incumbent nuclear and gas fleets over anyone waiting on greenfield projects. Treat that as a read of the structure, not a price forecast. So what: the megawatts the AI build-out needs are being contracted now, and delivered on a clock no deal can accelerate.

Here's the pattern across all four stories. Deadlines that live in announcements are proving negotiable. A tariff truce moved from November to January with a statement. An arms package sits in abeyance. A tariff authority waits on a decision to use it. But the date Beijing controls most tightly, the rare-earth suspension, has not visibly moved at all. And megawatts don't move by announcement. You can't pull a reactor uprate forward with a press release. It still lands by 2032.

Watch five dates, in order. Wednesday, October 14, for September CPI, which is still unreleased. October 18, when the Graham Act's tariff authority goes live. October 22, when the Lukoil license runs out. November 10, for whether Beijing extends Announcement 70 or lets it lapse. And January 10, 2027, when the broad truce itself comes due.

That's the briefing. Stay cautiously optimistic, and we'll see you next time.
