Good morning, and welcome to Cautious Optimism Briefings. It's Tuesday, September 29th.

Let's start by grading a call this desk has been making for days. On Saturday, and again in Monday's watch list, we named $84,000 to $85,000 as the line in the sand for Bitcoin. That's the band where long-term-holder supply had flipped from resistance to support. This morning the line is under live test. Bitcoin is trading around $83,700 to $84,000, depending on the venue, after an intraday low near $82,600. That's a quote at one moment, not a daily close. So the fair description is a band being breached intraday, not a band that has broken. But it is being pressed from above, not defended cleanly.

Here's what makes that notable. Last week, US spot Bitcoin ETFs pulled in $2.39 billion, their strongest week since October of last year. That demand was real. But on Monday, the day price slid toward this level, the ETF bid all but disappeared. Net flow for the day was just $31 million, according to TechFlow data relayed by KuCoin. That's roughly three percent of the prior Monday's print.

The breadth was worse than the total. BlackRock's IBIT alone took in more than the day's entire net inflow. Fidelity's FBTC and Grayscale's GBTC both bled small outflows, which ate into what BlackRock brought in. So the broad buying that defined last week narrowed to a single fund, on exactly the day the market needed conviction. The streak technically survived. The support it was supposed to provide did not.

What do we make of that? Three readings are on the table, and none is settled. The first is macro risk-off. Yahoo Finance tied Monday's pullback partly to President Trump rejecting Iran's seven-day plan. That's the same rejection Strategic Power flagged in Sunday's Throughline as a risk to oil and the Strait of Hormuz. It's Yahoo's attribution, not a proven mechanism. Still, it's notable that geopolitical risk may now be reaching Bitcoin through the same channel it reaches crude.

The second reading is that sellers are simply meeting the ETF bid as it arrives. That's a maturing-market story, where bigger flows get absorbed without much price impact. The third reading, which we've raised before, is that part of last week's inflow was hedged basis trades rather than directional buying. The data can't confirm or rule that out.

The bull case deserves a plain statement too. A pullback of one to two percent after the biggest inflow week in a year is not a crash. And there was no net ETF outflow behind it. But here is the takeaway. Flows were necessary to get Bitcoin to the top of its range. They were not sufficient to keep it there once the marginal buyer stepped back. And if the band gives way while ETF flows stay positive, the price-setter at the margin is someone other than the ETF allocator. It could be leverage, geopolitical de-risking, or long-term-holder selling. This desk doesn't have fresh positioning data to say which, and that's the next thing to identify.

If the most speculative corner of digital assets can't hold its line, look at where institutional money on-chain is actually growing. That's the least speculative corner there is, tokenized government debt. BlackRock's BUIDL fund has reclaimed the top spot among tokenized Treasury products. Aggregator reporting puts it at a $2.8 billion market cap, about 18.5 percent of a $15.1 billion category. Treat the fund's exact size loosely, though. Another same-week report put BUIDL meaningfully lower, and nobody has published a clean primary snapshot yet.

BUIDL got back on top by retaking the lead from Circle's USYC. Over the past year USYC grew from roughly $600 million to nearly $3 billion. It peaked around $2.9 billion in late August before losing the lead. Notice who's on both sides here. Circle issues USDC, a stablecoin whose reserves sit in Treasury bills. Circle also sponsors USYC, a tokenized Treasury fund that pays the holder the yield a stablecoin doesn't. That's not a contradiction so much as Circle hedging its own product line. It owns exposure to the float and to the product that competes with the float.

This speaks, in a small way, to a question this desk raised on Monday. A reserve mandate governs the composition of stablecoin reserves, not their size. Tokenized Treasuries are different. They can add demand for short government paper that doesn't depend on the stablecoin float growing at all. The second-order effect points the same way. If these funds keep growing, they compete with bank deposits and traditional money-market funds for the same institutional cash.

But keep the scale honest. Fifteen billion dollars is roughly one-twentieth of the stablecoin market, using the September 10th reading for that total. And it's a rounding error against the traditional money-market-fund complex. Leadership changing hands between two funds of about three billion dollars each tells you the category is small and contestable. It is not a settled institutional standard.

There's also an open question the data can't answer yet. How much of that fifteen billion is genuine end-investor cash? And how much is crypto-native collateral being recycled, meaning tokenized bills posted as margin on trading venues rather than parked by a corporate treasurer? If it's mostly the latter, this is circular liquidity dressed up as new demand. So the verdict on tokenized Treasuries is real but small, with the quality of that demand still unproven.

Tokenized bills are the savings side of on-chain dollars. The payments side tells a different story about which token is actually being used. Tether's USDT is still the largest stablecoin by supply, at about $183 billion, or roughly 59 percent of the market. Circle's USDC has a much smaller supply. Yet in several periods this year, USDC captured 60 to 70 percent of adjusted on-chain transaction volume.

That's not a steady-state share. And "adjusted" volume depends on the methodology behind it. So don't read this as USDC overtaking USDT. What it does suggest is that the two tokens are doing different jobs. USDT's float looks more like trading and reserve collateral. USDC looks like it's doing more of the actual settlement work.

A concrete example landed last week. On September 23rd, the ad-tech platform RTB Digital went live settling advertising payments in real time in USDC. It runs through smart wallets linked to Coinbase accounts. Volume isn't disclosed, so treat this as a pilot, not proof of scale. But it's the kind of boring, real-world settlement that the institutionalization bull case actually needs. The point to bank is that the bigger float isn't necessarily the busier payment rail.

That usage question is about to run into a hard calendar. Treasury's proposed rule under the GENIUS Act is open for public comment through October 19th. That's separate from the Federal Reserve's stablecoin proposal we covered on Monday, so don't conflate the two. Then, from January 18th, 2027, it becomes unlawful to issue a payment stablecoin in the United States without a federal or state license.

That leaves every issuer, large and small, a little under four months against a dated deadline rather than a draft rule. Pressure like that tends to consolidate an industry around the players who can clear the compliance bar. Size of float alone won't be enough. The rulebook now has deadlines, not just drafts.

Three things to watch from here. First, whether Bitcoin closes back above the $84,000 to $85,000 band or loses it on a closing basis, alongside the next ETF flow prints. Second, whether tokenized-Treasury assets keep growing in aggregate, or just keep changing hands at the top. And third, October 19th, the first hard date on the GENIUS Act clock.
