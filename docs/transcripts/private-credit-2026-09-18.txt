Good morning, it's September 18th. This is Cautious Optimism Briefings, your private-credit and corporate-credit read.

Five straight sessions on this desk have argued the same thing. Private credit is getting worse at the bottom of the stack. A record default rate, non-accruals at a post-2017 high, a Blue Owl mark that went from near par to pennies inside two quarters. Today's news does not dispute any of that. It says something stranger. The industry is growing fastest in a place where none of those measurements reach.

Start with KKR. The firm disclosed this week that it has structured or syndicated more than eighty billion dollars of private investment-grade financing so far in 2026. That is already double everything KKR originated in the whole of 2025. The borrowers are not stressed mid-market companies. They are Kuwait Petroleum Corporation, Enbridge, and Samsung Electronics. Blue-chip, investment-grade names. The model is straightforward. KKR originates the loan directly alongside long-term credit investors, mainly insurers and pensions, and syndicates pieces out to other institutions later.

The number that carries this story is the doubling, not the headline dollar figure. Every stress statistic this desk has run this week measures sub-investment-grade, middle-market lending. The BDC non-accrual series. Fitch's private credit default index. The Loparex marks. KKR's eighty billion is investment-grade paper, and none of those series touch it. That is not a contradiction. It is a measurement gap. A book can be deteriorating and a book can be booming, and they can be entirely different books.

Here is the unresolved question, and it is the one that actually matters. Nobody has disclosed what spread these deals pay against what the same issuers would pay in the public bond market. We went looking specifically for that number this morning, deal by deal and in aggregate, and no fetchable source names a spread or a coupon on any of them. So we are not going to invent one. But state the stakes plainly. If a private loan to Samsung prices at roughly public levels, the illiquidity premium is close to zero, and the paper is being sold to insurers who will carry it at model marks for a decade with no market test in between.

Two more things we do not know, and both cut at the same interpretation. The eighty billion is structured or syndicated volume, not balance-sheet risk KKR retained, and the retained-versus-placed split is not broken out anywhere. Neither is the split between genuinely new financing and refinancing borrowers out of existing bank facilities. So this could be a real credit-creation story. It could also be a share-shift story dressed up as growth. The evidence available does not distinguish between them, and anyone telling you it does is selling something.

Notice what the share-shift reading does to a framing this desk has leaned on all week. On Sunday we covered Catalent refinancing out of a private term loan into a syndicated bank loan, saving roughly a hundred million dollars of annual interest. On Monday we covered JPMorgan's tracked pipeline of more than a hundred and thirty-eight billion dollars of buyout debt pulling borrowers back toward the banks. Now KKR is taking investment-grade share away from banks at the top of the stack. Banks to non-banks is no longer a one-way arrow. The migration is running in both directions at once, sorted by credit quality. The best sub-investment-grade borrowers are walking back to the syndicated market, and the best investment-grade borrowers are walking out of it into private hands.

This is not a nine-month anomaly, either. The Fed has been watching the ten-year version of it. In a speech back in May, Fed Vice Chair for Supervision Michelle Bowman noted that the bank share of corporate lending fell from forty-eight percent in 2015 to twenty-nine percent in 2025, with the US private credit market now at roughly one point four trillion dollars. Her argument was that capital rules perversely reward banks for lending to private credit funds rather than lending to corporations directly. Her proposed fix is to cut the risk weight on investment-grade corporate exposures from one hundred percent to sixty-five percent, so banks can compete again.

Do not treat that as coming. It remains a proposal. Its comment period closed in mid-June, and we could not confirm any Fed or interagency statement since then saying it has been finalized, withdrawn, or is still sitting on a desk somewhere. And even if it lands, it may be aimed at the wrong target. Banks did not lose investment-grade corporate lending to capital rules alone. They lost it partly because insurance liabilities are long-dated and match twenty-year private assets better than deposits ever will. A lower risk weight does not fix a duration mismatch. So the regulatory fix and the underlying migration may simply be pointing in different directions.

The funding mix also changes what kind of risk this is. This desk has spent weeks on the redemption-queue mechanic in wealth-channel vehicles, where quarterly tender caps meet investors who want out faster than the structure allows. Investment-grade private paper funded by insurers and permanent capital is close to immune to that. The liabilities are matched, there is no tender window, and nobody is forced to sell into a bad quarter. That is genuinely more stable. It is also far less observable. An insurance balance sheet publishes no quarterly NAV and faces no redemption that would force a price. The Loparex question, which was whether a model mark reflects reality, does not disappear here. It gets slower, larger, and much quieter.

Now, who is funding all of this, and the pattern is concentration rather than diversification. ICG is targeting fifteen billion euros for its sixth European direct-lending fund. That is roughly in line with the fifteen point two billion its prior fund raised in 2024, and that fund had itself beaten an original target of ten to twelve billion. Treat fifteen billion as a target, not a result. ICG has deployed about thirty-seven billion dollars in direct lending since launching the strategy in 2012, cumulative rather than current dry powder. And it is not alone at this scale. Hayfin raised more than fifteen billion euros for its fifth direct-lending fund earlier this year. Ares raised thirty billion euros for a European private-credit strategy in 2025. KKR, for its part, has pulled in fifteen billion dollars from third-party investors for its credit strategies this year, up twenty-nine percent on the same period in 2025.

Set that against what we reported on Tuesday, when direct-lending fund count was down thirty-five percent year on year while the funds that closed were roughly twice as large. ICG, Hayfin, and Ares are that consolidation arc showing up in named European vehicles. The so-what is uncomfortable. Institutional capital is not repricing private-credit risk in response to the stress data. It is concentrating that risk into a smaller number of managers whose internal marks are increasingly the only evidence anyone has of what the paper is worth. One honest limit: this is European middle-market lending, while every stress number we have aired this week describes a US book. Whether Europe is structurally safer or simply earlier in the same curve, we do not know.

A quick reality check on the existing book, because it has not gone away while KKR scales upmarket. Non-accrual debt at the ten largest BDCs rose to three point nine five percent of total debt at cost in the second quarter, up twenty basis points on the quarter. The count of distinct borrowers with at least one non-accrual tranche climbed by eleven, to a hundred and one. That count is the harder number to argue away, because it is not diluted by a growing denominator. And Loparex, whose marks this desk covered in full on Thursday, is still unresolved. Still in forbearance. Still no filing.

So where does the cycle actually stand. The deterioration is real, it is concentrated, and crucially it is measured. We can see it in non-accrual counts and BDC marks. The growth is investment-grade, insurer-funded, and largely unmeasured. We cannot see the pricing. We cannot see the retained-versus-distributed split. And the balance sheets holding it publish no quarterly mark that would force discovery. The biggest open risk in this market is no longer whether a mid-market loan gets written down late. It is whether the fastest-growing part of the lending machine is being paid anything at all for the illiquidity it is taking on, at twice last year's pace, inside vehicles that would not reveal a mispricing until maturity.

Watch three things from here. Whether the Fed's capital rule gets finalized, and at what risk weight. Whether Loparex files before its forbearance window closes this month. And November's third-quarter BDC disclosures, which will tell us whether that non-accrual borrower count keeps climbing past a hundred and one.

That's the briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
