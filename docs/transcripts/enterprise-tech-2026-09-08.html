Good morning. It's September 8th, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

This beat has now worked through four beat-and-sell-off stories in five briefings. Palo Alto Networks on the 4th, Zscaler on the 6th, Figma yesterday, and now MongoDB. In every one the delivered quarter was fine, and the forward guide did the damage. What today adds is a control case. In the same window, a private company raised money on almost exactly the growth profile public markets have been marking down. That contrast, not any single print, is the story.

Start with the quarter MongoDB actually delivered. Revenue came in at $771.8 million, up 30 percent year over year. That is the company's fastest growth rate since fiscal 2024. It beat on earnings as well, and it added a record 2,900 net new customers, taking its total to 70,600.

Management then raised the full-year revenue outlook to a range of $2.99 to $3.03 billion. Every one of those numbers cleared expectations. The stock still fell more than 12 percent in after-hours trading. Nothing in the quarter MongoDB delivered was the problem.

So what did the selling. It was the forward guide. MongoDB pointed the third quarter at roughly $756 to $761 million. Set that against the $628.3 million it reported in the same quarter a year ago, a figure from MongoDB's own release, and the implied growth rate is about 20 percent. That is a third off the rate it had just put up. One sourcing flag before we build on it. The guidance range itself comes from secondary reporting of the call rather than a document I could pull directly, so treat that arithmetic as near-primary rather than confirmed.

Atlas, MongoDB's managed database service, is where the anxiety concentrates. Atlas grew about 29 percent year over year, roughly where it has run for more than a year. On the same call, MongoDB raised its full-year Atlas growth outlook to approximately 27 percent, three hundred basis points above its prior outlook. That combination is more revealing than either number alone. You cannot raise a full-year growth figure to a level below the rate you are currently running and also have the second half hold. Management's own raised outlook embeds the deceleration. So this is not simply an outside analyst stacking inference on guidance math, which is how it first appeared. It is a company-disclosed slowdown, and the market repriced it within hours.

Now the case that cuts the other way. Databricks has raised $5 billion, in a round its own release describes as not yet closed. The company says it has, in its words, signed a term sheet for this round, which it expects to close later this summer. The valuation, stated plainly by Databricks itself, is $188 billion. The same release points to a revenue run-rate that has surpassed $7 billion, growing more than 80 percent year over year.

Hold that against MongoDB for a moment. Databricks discloses no breakdown of how much of that run-rate comes from AI or agent products. It publishes no net-new-customer count, no retention figure, and no quarterly forward guide, because it is under no obligation to. That is the actual mechanism here, and it is not superior insight. The investors paying up for Databricks are not pricing better information than the investors who sold MongoDB. They are pricing the absence of the specific disclosure that got MongoDB marked down. The variable being punished in one market and rewarded in the other is the existence of a checkable forward number, not the quality of either business.

One comparator, briefly, because we covered it in depth here on the 4th. Snowflake beat and raised in the same cluster and got paid for it, with product revenue growth accelerating to 37 percent. Snowflake is the control that shows a beat-and-raise can still work. So it is the content of the guide, not merely its existence, that decides these outcomes. But four sour reactions in five briefings against one clean win says the bar for what counts as a clean guide keeps rising.

Two consequences follow, and both are worth watching over the next couple of quarters. The first is competitive. Databricks and Snowflake are chasing the same enterprise data and AI budget dollars, but only one of them has to defend a quarterly forward number in public. That asymmetry is not cosmetic. It shapes how aggressively each can discount into a soft period, how much margin each can spend buying share, and how each behaves in a competitive bake-off where the customer is choosing a decade-long data platform. The privately funded competitor can absorb a bad three months in a way the listed one cannot. The second consequence is about pricing new issues. MongoDB has just set the comparable. A data platform growing at 30 percent that guides toward the low twenties gets marked down double digits in an evening. Any private data company funded at a premium now knows exactly what the public tape will do to that premium on the day it first has to publish a guide, which raises the cost of ever entering public markets at all.

The same discount shows up where the evidence is hardest to dismiss. The Army moved its TITAN targeting program from prototype into production on September 1st. Palantir took a $127 million delivery order, with Anduril receiving $65 million for hardware integration. The initial award covers eight systems over 18 months, and the Army expects a further order next fiscal year. Say plainly what that is. It is production work against a program of record. It is not a pilot, not an unfunded contract ceiling, and not a partnership announcement. It is the hardest form of evidence this category produces.

Palantir shares fell about 6 percent the day after the announcement. Be careful with that. The sourcing does not establish that TITAN specifically caused the drop rather than broader market moves that session, so do not over-read a single day's tape. The more defensible point stands regardless of causation. With Palantir trading near 144 times earnings after its August rally, an order that size simply is not large enough to move the financial model in either direction. When a multiple already prices in years of growth, real bookings evidence becomes informationally close to worthless to the share price. That is the same finding as MongoDB and Databricks, transposed into government contracting. Proof of execution and the stock price have stopped talking to each other.

There is a corporate-finance consequence to that which rarely gets named. A stock that does not respond to genuine wins is a weaker acquisition currency and a weaker retention tool for staff. It pushes a contractor toward paying cash for deals, and toward issuing explicit long-term guidance to reset expectations, which is precisely the checkable disclosure the rest of this briefing says gets punished.

If the market will not pay for proof, enterprise buyers still have to live with the consequences of who gets access to what, and when. OpenAI's staged rollout of GPT-6 Astra this month gave first access only to organizations enrolled in its Daybreak cybersecurity program. In the reporting's own framing, Plus, Pro, Business, and Enterprise ChatGPT subscribers, along with developers using the OpenAI API, were left out. Sam Altman apologized publicly, posting sorry for the messy rollout and promising to make it right.

More significant than the apology is what OpenAI disclosed alongside it. The company said it had paused certain frontier training runs for two weeks following a security incident involving AI agents and the Hugging Face platform. The scope of that incident is not disclosed, so I will not speculate about what was exposed. What matters is the precedent. That is the first labs-level admission that an autonomous-agent security failure can halt capability development outright. For procurement teams and CISOs, the lesson is not that OpenAI was careless. Staged sequencing may well be a deliberate control that other labs apply less visibly. The lesson is narrower and more useful. Contractual tier no longer predicts access order, so vendor risk assessments have to account for security-channel sequencing, not just subscription level.

Put the week together. Public markets marked down a company growing 30 percent because it published a guide that implied deceleration. Private markets paid up for a company growing more than twice as fast that publishes no guide at all. The rational vendor response to that incentive is obvious and worth watching for. Expect vaguer forward metrics, more emphasis on run-rate and adoption counts, and less exposure to any number that can be checked next quarter. The test that would tell us whether the market got this right is straightforward. Watch whether MongoDB actually delivers against the guide that caused the selloff. If it does, the discount was correct pricing rather than a mispricing, and the Databricks round starts to look like private capital paying a premium for the absence of scrutiny rather than for a better business.
