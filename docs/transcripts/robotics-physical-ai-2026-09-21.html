Good morning, and welcome to your robotics and physical AI briefing for Monday, September 21st, 2026.

On September eighteenth, this briefing graded Figure's Helix 2.5 result and said the headline had no denominator behind it. That gap has now closed, and what came through it is more interesting than the headline was.

Start with what is already on the record. Figure says Index pretraining alone raised zero-shot success in thirty unseen homes from nine percent to fifty-six percent. Index is Figure's human-behavior video dataset. That is Figure's own ablation, in Figure's own wording.

Now the denominator, and note where it comes from. It is not in the text of Figure's post. It reaches us through three separate trade outlets reading that post, and their counts agree with each other. The evaluation was four hundred and twenty attempts, one hundred and forty per task, with two hundred and thirty-seven successes. That is the fifty-six percent.

The spread underneath it is the finding. Bed making came in at sixty-seven percent. Towel folding, sixty-two. Tidying toys, forty.

Look at how Figure grades before you read that forty. Figure's own criterion is that a tidying trial counts only if all thirteen to fifteen toys end up in the basket. Put most of them away and leave two on the floor, and the run scores zero. One of those trade outlets, Startup Fortune, adds that Figure counted every safety intervention as a failure. That strictness cuts in an unusual direction. It means forty percent understates raw dexterity. But it is the honest number for deployment economics, because in a paying deployment an intervention is a cost whether or not the robot eventually finishes.

Here is my interpretation of the spread, and I want to be clear that it is interpretation. Bed making and towel folding act on one large deformable object in a roughly predictable place. Toy tidying has a variable number of objects in variable positions, and it is graded on completing all of them. Success falls as the world supplies more variance. That is the same failure shape as the robot-foundation-model startup Skild, whose sixty-six percent earlier this month was measured per step, not per task. A per-step number looks strong until the task supplies enough steps.

Now the qualifications, because the structure story is not proven. Figure's own page says one Index-pretrained base model was adapted to three distinct behaviors. So this is not one policy on one checkpoint, and the per-task gaps could also reflect different adaptation data and different rubrics. Figure does not disclose the size of that adaptation data. It gives only a ratio: half as much task-specific data as a representative Helix 02 behavior.

Three more things to hold onto. Every number here is Figure's own, with no outside evaluator and no replication. Zero-shot means unseen homes, not unseen tasks, since all three behaviors were specified with data collected elsewhere. And Figure discloses no intervention rate, no retry count and no cycle time, so nothing here converts to cost per completed task. Figure itself says general humanoid robotics is not solved.

The so-what is this. If reliability tracks task structure more than pretraining volume, then per-site work stays on the cost line. That means task decomposition, fixturing and environment design. Three days ago, the halved adaptation data looked like evidence that scaling was absorbing that integration work. This cuts the other way. The systems-integration effort that scaling was supposed to compress may be the thing that makes deployments work.

That leads to the commercial response. If a model cannot hold reliability in unstructured space, you either structure the space or you certify humans and robots sharing it. Agility's week is the second path, and it resolves something this briefing flagged as missing.

On September sixteenth we noted that no independent safety standard had been cited behind Agility's cage-free claim. Now they are named. Agility's Digit 5 materials say the company is a project leader and active contributor to ANSI/A3 TR R15.108, an American National Standards Institute technical report under development for dynamically stable industrial mobile robots, including humanoids. Agility also contributes to the international document, ISO 25785-1.

The resolution points the opposite way from what a vendor would want. Nothing has been certified against a document still being drafted. Agility is helping write the rule its own category will later be measured against. That is a potential moat and a conflict at once, and today it is neither.

The timing is worth noticing. Digit 5 early access is expected in the first half of 2027, with general availability by the end of that year. That is plausibly the window in which these documents get finished. Whether the product schedule was built around the standard is an open question. So is whether high-volume Chinese entrants can obtain conformity for American and European floors. Nothing in our sources answers that, so treat any lock-out claim as speculation.

Re-ground the commercial picture briefly. Agility says it had more than three hundred million dollars in multi-year customer orders for Digit 5 as of May. That is not backlog and it is not revenue, and there is no unit count, price, or split between binding and non-binding. With general availability at the end of 2027, there is no Digit 5 revenue this year. What Agility does have is tenure. It says Digit 4 has logged more than sixty-five thousand hours across nearly three years of commercial deployment.

Then capacity. Launch coverage puts RoboFab, Agility's plant in Salem, Oregon, at a design capacity of up to ten thousand Digits a year. Treat that as a company design figure relayed by reporting, not as output. We applied the same nameplate-versus-demand arithmetic to UBTech's Liuzhou plant on September sixteenth. Now it applies on the Western side. And now there is a price attached, because Agility is going public.

The vehicle is a pending merger with a blank-check company called Churchill Capital Corporation Eleven. Company-reported terms include more than six hundred and twenty million dollars of gross proceeds, assuming no redemptions. The deal has not closed. The shareholder vote, redemptions and SEC review are all outstanding. An order book, a 2027 product and a ten-thousand-unit nameplate are what public investors will be asked to underwrite.

The so-what on Agility is that model progress and conformity progress have to arrive together. Today one of them is forty percent in living rooms, and the other is a draft document.

Which brings us to the one corner of physical AI that already has an appropriations line instead of an order book. A note on timing first. This item is dated September second, and this briefing is picking it up late.

AeroVironment announced that the Army awarded it a production contract for its LOCUST laser under the Enduring High Energy Laser program. The Army's Portfolio Acquisition Executive for Fires valued it at four hundred sixty-four point eight million dollars. AeroVironment calls it the first-ever production contract for high energy laser weapon systems in United States history. That is the company's characterization, not an independent one.

Note what is missing. The release gives no unit count, no delivery schedule and no cost per engagement. The counter-drone economics are not demonstrated. This is a procurement milestone, not a fielded-effectiveness result.

Still, it fits the pattern. A fixed installation engaging an incoming airframe is about as structured a problem as this sector offers. It is the opposite of a humanoid improvising in a child's living room. Named buyer, stated dollar figure, production contract. That is what money follows when the environment is legible.

Pulling the threads together. For weeks this briefing has complained that general-purpose humanoids publish no reliability numbers. Now some exist, and the problem has changed from absence to interpretation, because what they measure is the environment as much as the robot. Sixty-seven percent on a bed and forty percent in a room of toys is a gradient, and the gradient is the finding.

Embodied AI is inflecting where the space is structured and the buyer is defined. Warehouses, single-object repetitive work and fixed counter-drone installations fit that description. It is still a demo where the world supplies the variance, and where nobody yet holds a certification to put a robot next to a person without a cage.

The open questions are these. Will Figure or anyone publish an intervention rate or a cost per completed task? Will an outside evaluator test Helix 2.5 on tasks outside Index coverage? Will ANSI/A3 TR R15.108 be published before Digit 5 ships?

Near-term, the date to watch is September thirtieth, when Tesla must give NHTSA its sworn answer on how the Cybercab meets federal safety standards. That is the same question in a different body: a vehicle moving through unstructured traffic, answering to a live regulator rather than a draft standard. The outcome is not yet known.
