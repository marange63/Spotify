Good morning. It's November 14th, 2026, and this is your briefing on artificial intelligence in biopharma R&D.

One disclosure up front, because it's load-bearing today. Anthropic makes the model behind this show, and Anthropic's CEO is one of the voices in this story. On November 10th, in an investor roadshow video ahead of Anthropic's IPO, Dario Amodei made a decade-scale promise. As reported by American Bazaar, he said, "Cancer, Alzheimer's, heart disease, mental illness — these will be relics of the past in, I think, less than a decade." Yesterday we covered the opposite pole. That was Vertex executive chairman Jeff Leiden's zero: roughly fifty AI vendors run against Vertex's compound library, and no working drug. Two more pharma voices sit in Leiden's camp, and they're new to this show. AstraZeneca's Pascal Soriot said, "Disease is complicated, biology is complicated." He added that "to say AI is going to cure cancer is maybe a little bit of a jump." Regeneron's George Yancopoulos said AI will not "just miraculously come in and tell us how to do things." Note the chronology. The Wall Street Journal reported all three before Amodei's roadshow, so this is not a reply to him. Both poles also carry incentives. A roadshow is a selling document for a listing. Pharma operators have their own reasons to manage expectations and vendor pricing. And both sides are arguing about outcomes a decade out, while neither is measuring the steps in between. The sharper question is narrower. Which parts of a drug's clock can AI actually compress, and do we have numbers yet? For the first time, a platform has put some on the table.

Since late October, this show has been asking AI-discovery platforms for denominators. Recursion's third-quarter call, on November 3rd, supplied the first ones we can quote. President Najat Khan told analysts the company is "synthesizing 80%-90% fewer compounds across our portfolio relative to industry benchmarks." That's an input-efficiency claim about discovery chemistry. Fewer molecules get made and tested per candidate, which lowers cost and time at the front end. It says nothing about whether those candidates succeed in people. That's why it can sit alongside Leiden's zero without contradicting it. The two numbers measure different things.

Recursion's second figure matters more, because it touches the clock itself. Khan said, "We're seeing enrollment run about 30%-60% faster than industry benchmarks." Faster recruitment is a genuine timeline lever. Trial duration is often set by how long it takes to fill the arms, not by dosing alone. But the call doesn't name the benchmark, the programs, or the mechanism. So don't assume this is AI-driven patient-finding, because that isn't in the quote. There's also a competing reading. Indication choice can do some of this work, since rare, well-defined populations can enroll quickly without any AI at all.

There's one clinical figure worth banking. Recursion said its oncology program REC-1245 reached a disease control rate at eight weeks of 91.7 percent. That's eleven of twelve patients, at doses of 45 milligrams and above. Treat it as early, small and uncontrolled. Disease control includes stable disease. It isn't response, and it isn't durability.

So here's the takeaway on Recursion. These are the first platform-side efficiency numbers this show can quote, and they are real claims about cost and speed. They are also self-benchmarked against comparators nobody has named. And they say nothing yet about the odds that a drug succeeds. If the compound figure holds at scale, our inference is that it runs downstream to the contract research and manufacturing organizations. Fewer compounds per program means less fee-for-service synthesis volume to go around. That's an implication, not an observed effect. For the AI-discovery equities, the next test is disclosure. A platform that publishes the benchmark behind its numbers would separate itself from every "AI-designed" press release.

Those savings land before and around the trial. The trial itself is the part that doesn't move, and the registry now shows how long it runs. Insilico Medicine's rentosertib is the pulmonary fibrosis drug with an AI-identified target and an AI-generated molecule. On November 9th, its Phase 3 status rested on Insilico's own release, not a registry check. We've now checked the registry. The ClinicalTrials.gov record lists the primary outcome as the annual rate of decline in forced vital capacity over 52 weeks. The study is randomized, double-blind and placebo-controlled. Its estimated completion date is October 30th, 2029. Insilico's release says the trial "is expected to enroll a total of 320 participants across 47 centers in China." The registry lists no sites outside China.

The FDA's only involvement in the sources is an Orphan Drug Designation from February 2023. We found no US IND and no American or European Phase 3 in the record. That's an absence of evidence, not proof that no Western plan exists.

Keep one earlier rentosertib number in its lane. You may have heard the drug tied to a lung-function gain of plus 98.4 milliliters of FVC, against a 20.3 milliliter decline on placebo. That came from the earlier Phase 2a, a 71-patient trial, per Insilico's announcement of the Nature Medicine paper. That trial's primary endpoint was safety and tolerability, which it met. FVC there was a secondary, exploratory measure. So the Phase 3 asks a harder question of a much larger population.

Here's the floor. However fast AI makes the molecule or fills the arms, each patient still needs about a year on drug before the endpoint reads. And the trial isn't expected to complete until late 2029, roughly three years out. No efficiency claim on the table today touches that stretch. There's also a regulatory question we can't answer yet. Can a pivotal trial run entirely in China support an FDA or EMA filing? No source states a plan either way. For anyone pricing these platforms on the promise of a faster decade, that 2029 date is the number to hold. It's the first hard checkpoint where an AI-designed molecule's clinical claim either holds or doesn't.

If you can't shorten the trial, the other lever on cost and success rates is running fewer losing ones. That's the frame for the freshest item today. Orakl Oncology, a Paris company, closed an oversubscribed 34 million dollar Series A on November 12th, per The Next Web. Orakl grows patients' tumors as organoids, tests drugs on them, and combines the results with clinical records to predict trial outcomes. That's a selection bet, not a design or speed bet. It chooses which trials to run, rather than running them faster. But the validation in the coverage is retrospective, against trials whose outcomes were already published. It also hasn't been peer-reviewed. Predicting an answer you already know is a much easier problem than predicting one you don't.

So here's where the evidence sits today. The quantified compression is real but self-reported, and it sits at the margins: fewer compounds synthesized, and faster enrollment. The clock that decides when a drug reaches patients is unchanged by anything quoted this week. That clock is a year of dosing per patient and a pivotal readout years out. Nothing quoted today moves the probability of clinical success either. The success-rate lever is selection, and its best current proof point is still retrospective. For R&D spend, our inference is that near-term savings show up as more candidates tested per dollar at the front end. They don't show up as a shorter path to approval. Three things to watch. Does Recursion name the benchmark behind its efficiency numbers? Does any selection platform deliver a prospective result, not a retrospective one? And does Anthropic's IPO prospectus describe biology in the same terms as Amodei's roadshow claim?

That's the briefing for November 14th. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
