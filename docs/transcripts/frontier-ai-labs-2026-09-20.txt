Good morning, and welcome to Cautious Optimism Briefings for Sunday, September 20th, 2026.

All week, this show has come back to the pacing pact among the leading labs. Our verdict was that it was rhetoric with no counterparty. There was no charter, no membership list, and no agreement anyone could point to. On Friday, someone pointed. A class action filed in the Northern District of California names Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, and Musk's SpaceXAI, the company behind Grok. It argues the four violated antitrust law when they agreed to coordinate a slowdown.

The complaint says the agreement largely took place on September 12th. That is the day Dario Amodei published his essay urging the industry to slow down, and the day Sam Altman, Elon Musk and Demis Hassabis publicly agreed with him. In other words, the plaintiffs are arguing that the rhetoric was the agreement. The complaint also cites a statement from July, signed by senior employees at several labs, acknowledging intense competitive pressure not to unilaterally slow. We know that statement only as the complaint characterizes it.

Now the skepticism, and there is a lot of it. This is an unadjudicated complaint. There is no ruling, and the coverage quantifies no damages. The plaintiffs are four people who pay for ChatGPT, Claude, Grok or Gemini. Their theory is that slower capability growth makes those subscriptions worth less. That is novel, and thin. Public statements of agreement are not ordinarily the same thing as an agreement, and parallel conduct is not a conspiracy. Meta, Mistral and the Chinese labs are not named, and those are exactly the players no pacing regime reaches.

So weigh the signal, not the merits. The signal is a legal conflict inside the defendant group. Amodei asked the US government for a narrow waiver covering certain kinds of safety conversations. Altman said, and I quote, we do not believe we need to wait for an anti-trust exemption. One of those positions is now the riskier one, and it is Altman's. The cost of being wrong is not a fine. It is that the labs have already incurred the liability without getting the standards body. Note too that SpaceXAI is a defendant in a slowdown conspiracy while its own Grok 4.7 is still unreleased, well past Musk's target.

So the coordination project just got more expensive to build. The structure most exposed is the FINRA-style standards body, which reporting says would have the power to coordinate industrywide slowdowns. That is close to a description of what the complaint calls illegal, and without government action the legal risk of building it goes up. There is a perverse second-order effect here. If coordinated public safety statements create liability, the rational response is silence and private bilateral arrangements. That makes the industry less legible, not safer. The other response is a lobbying push for a statutory waiver. That converts a voluntary safety story into a political one.

If you want to know whether anyone is actually slowing down, do not read the pleadings. Read the filings. Nscale, the data center builder, filed its S-1 for a US listing on September 18th. It discloses GPU services agreements with Anthropic, signed August 25th, for dedicated capacity at its West Virginia campus, built around Nvidia Vera Rubin systems. The filing's own words are aggregate payments of up to approximately 44.6 billion dollars. SiliconANGLE reads that as roughly 460 megawatts. That number comes from a registration statement, written by a counterparty with securities liability, not from a lab's own blog.

Two qualifications carry weight. The phrase is up to, so this is a ceiling on payments, not money Anthropic has committed or paid. And the contract predates the September 12th essay by two and a half weeks, so it is not a reaction to the pacing statements. It is evidence of what Anthropic intended to spend before anyone talked about slowing down. Our read of the filing is partial.

Now the seller's side, which is the more interesting risk. Anthropic is not the only anchor customer. Techstrong reports a parallel Microsoft contract of up to 43.8 billion dollars, and says both deals are conditional on delivery and service availability. It also reports that Nscale has not yet obtained binding commitments for the financing the buildout requires. So the contracts are signed, and the capacity behind them is not yet funded.

Then look at the gap between the backlog and the business. As of August 31st, Nscale had about 2.6 billion dollars of contracts actually running, against a signed backlog of 103.4 billion. Only a sliver of that book exists today. First-half revenue was 140.6 million dollars, against a net loss of about a billion.

Nvidia is in this picture, but not the way it first looked. The filing describes a subscription agreement dated September 15th, for a minimum of 3.1 billion dollars of convertible instruments, with Nvidia among the subscribers. Those instruments convert automatically into equity when the offering completes. So this is not vendor debt sitting on Nscale's books. It is a chip supplier taking an equity stake alongside new public shareholders. If Anthropic or Microsoft demand slips, the loss lands on that shared equity rather than on a creditor with seniority.

So the binding constraint on those megawatts is Nscale's ability to raise and deploy capital. That makes the IPO proceeds a direct input to Anthropic's capacity timeline, and it makes the neocloud listing wave a public read on AI compute counterparty risk. Fortune reported back in July that SpaceXAI rents Anthropic a large block of Nvidia GPUs across its Colossus data centers for 1.25 billion dollars a month. The defendant is also the landlord. Anthropic is buying compute from everywhere at once. Spending, not speech, is the falsifier for pacing, and so far the spending intentions have not changed.

That capacity buys exactly one thing, which is tokens. And the only independent measurement this month says tokens are where the competition actually moved.

Artificial Analysis benchmarked OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra, which launched September 2nd to 3rd. On its Intelligence Index, Astra scores 53, level with Anthropic's Claude Fable 5.1. On its Coding Agent Index the two are level again, Astra inside Codex against Fable 5.1 inside Claude Code. And the two models carry identical list prices for input and output tokens.

So capability and sticker price are a wash. Cost per task is not. Astra matches the score at roughly 40 percent of the cost per task, and the reason is token use. At maximum effort, Astra spends 27 thousand output tokens on a task. Fable 5.1 spends 78 thousand, close to three times as many.

Now the skepticism. Artificial Analysis is independent, but not neutral in construction. The cost comparison depends on their harness, and it runs Fable 5.1 in a max-with-fallback configuration, the setting most likely to burn output tokens. So the gap may narrow at lower effort. Still, the direction is the story. Capability is at parity, and the difference shows up on an invoice while staying invisible on a leaderboard. The pressure lands on Anthropic's effort settings, not on its sticker price.

There are two effects to work through, and they point opposite ways. Fewer tokens per task lowers enterprise total cost of ownership, and it squeezes Anthropic's gross margin arithmetic ahead of a listing. But it also means the same GPU fleet serves more tasks, which cuts against a simple read-through from token efficiency to chip demand. Efficiency could soften demand or widen usage, and the evidence does not yet say which.

Now the price floor underneath that argument. DeepSeek's V4.1 Flash is effectively free, at three tenths of a cent per million input tokens. That is the best case, a cache hit during off-peak hours. Its own evaluations claim coding parity. But DeepSeek's release shows Claude Opus 5 beating it 43.3 to 30.0 on Terminal-Bench 3.0, and beating it again on Terminal-Bench 4.0. The parity is benchmark-selected.

Google is holding the line in the middle of the market. Gemini 3.8 Flash keeps its price unchanged, but the coverage attaches an expiry date, because that price doubles on January 1st, 2027. So today's Western mid-tier floor is promotional. If Gemini reprices and DeepSeek does not, the commoditization thesis gets tested from below.

Here is the thesis. The first binding consequence of the pacing pact is legal, not technical. And the week's two freshest numbers, a securities filing and a third-party cost run, both show buildout and price competition accelerating, with the competitive edge moving from capability to token efficiency.

Three things would change that view. A court ruling, or a dismissal, on the coordination theory. A formal Justice Department or FTC position, rather than a company's own comfort level. Or an Anthropic response on effort settings or output-token pricing.
