Cautious Optimism Briefings

Strategic Power, Energy, and Institutional Change

Transcript · July 31, 2026

Good morning. It's Friday, July 31st, and this is your strategic power briefing.

For the first time this cycle, the world's two most important oil chokepoints are contested at once. The Strait of Hormuz has been effectively closed since the US-Israel air war on Iran began in late February. Now Yemen's Houthi movement has layered a second threat on top of it. On July 20th, the Houthis declared what they called a maritime embargo against Saudi Arabia, an eye-for-an-eye response after an exchange of missiles broke a years-long detente. Analysts estimate that a full shutdown of both chokepoints together could block up to a quarter of the world's oil and gas supply. Hormuz alone normally carries about a fifth of global crude, some 20 million barrels a day. This is a structurally different risk regime than the single-point Hormuz scares of the past few years.

So how did the market actually respond? Not with a shrug, and not with a full-blown panic either. Brent crude spiked hard on the news, jumping nearly 7 percent to just over $100 a barrel on July 24th, its first time above that level since late May. That is a real, proportional reaction to a real escalation. But by the morning of July 30th, Brent had eased back to roughly $92.65. So the honest read is spike-then-partial-retreat, not containment from the start.

Why the retreat? The evidence points to something more specific than "energy abundance absorbed the shock." Tanker-tracking data shows the Houthis are enforcing this blockade selectively, by cargo affiliation, not by geography. A Saudi-origin cargo with a Chinese crew and a Chinese destination sailed straight through Bab el-Mandeb on July 20th without interdiction. In the same week, other tankers carrying Saudi-linked crude and refined products turned back before reaching the strait, and Saudi-flagged tankers have themselves come under direct attack. The blockade, in other words, is shaping who can move Saudi crude, not whether crude moves at all. That is a meaningfully narrower threat than a hard chokepoint closure, and it is the more likely explanation for why prices retreated. There is no reliable, primary-sourced figure on OPEC or Saudi spare capacity to support the alternative story, that abundant supply is simply absorbing the disruption. So hold that thesis open, but don't declare victory for it yet. The real second-order effect is a costlier, slower rerouting of Saudi exports through non-Saudi-flagged intermediaries, not a straightforward volume loss. If enforcement ever broadens beyond Saudi-affiliated cargo, the next price move would likely be larger and stickier than July 24th's spike.

There's a flip side to this that runs straight to the American energy position. A two-chokepoint disruption in the Gulf, even a selective one, is a persistent tailwind for the exporters sitting outside the war zone. US LNG cargoes and domestic shale barrels become the marginal safe supply, and every week the rerouting drags on, the strategic case for American energy abundance gets more concrete. It also revives the debate over the Strategic Petroleum Reserve, whether to hold it as a war buffer or draw it down to cap prices at home. And it shows up in costs that never make the crude headline: war-risk insurance and tanker freight rates climbing as Gulf shipping stays dangerous, a slow pass-through into energy and goods prices that outlasts any single-session spike. That is the quiet expression of energy as the base layer of power, and it hands directly to the next story.

Because that geopolitical energy shock has now landed squarely inside US monetary policy. The Federal Reserve held its target rate at 3.50 to 3.75 percent on July 29th for a sixth straight meeting, but three regional presidents, Cleveland's Beth Hammack, Minneapolis's Neel Kashkari, and Dallas's Lorie Logan, dissented in favor of a hike. The hold itself isn't new. What's new is the statement's language. The Fed wrote, in its own words, that economic activity is expanding at a solid pace "despite elevated uncertainty that owes, in part, to the conflict in the Middle East," and that inflation remains elevated "in part reflecting supply shocks that have driven price increases in certain sectors, including energy." For months this energy-inflation impulse has been qualitative and hard to pin down. Now the Fed has named it explicitly, on the record, as a live input into the rate path.

So what does that mean. A central bank that has formally attributed inflation risk to an active war and a contested shipping lane is not going to cut rates quickly, even if growth data stays solid. That keeps financing costs elevated for the entire AI buildout, from data center construction to grid upgrades to the private credit funds lending against them, at exactly the moment power and compute demand keep climbing. It's worth noting how the market actually reacted to this news: equities sold off sharply, one of the worst reactions to a second meeting under a new Fed chair on record, while Bitcoin barely moved, holding near $64,000. That's a telling asymmetry. If this were a clean hard-money story, you'd expect Bitcoin or gold to catch a bid on the inflation read. Persistent energy inflation does strengthen the textbook case for gold and commodities over long-duration bonds. But instead of reaching for that hedge, investors this week look to be repricing duration risk, how long rates stay high. That complicates the standing thesis that geopolitical energy inflation automatically bids Bitcoin. It may still happen, but the data from this week doesn't confirm it.

One more thread belongs in this same bucket of supply-side inflation pressure the Fed is now navigating. In February, the Supreme Court ruled 6-3 that the emergency economic powers law used to impose sweeping tariffs did not actually authorize them. Rather than retreat, the administration has been rebuilding equivalent tariff exposure through other statutes. It finalized a broad forced-labor tariff under a different trade law in late July, let the North American trade pact lapse on July 1st, triggering a ten-year wind-down, and threatened a steep new tariff on Canada. Trump himself conceded the point directly, saying he now has to "go a harder way for the tariffs because the Supreme Court, in a very close decision, ruled against me." That's the cleanest illustration you'll find of this briefing's core discipline, distinguishing symbolism from substance. The legal mechanism changed. The economic exposure, by the administration's own admission, did not.

Put it together and the picture is this. Two contested oil chokepoints instead of one. A Fed that has stopped treating Middle East energy risk as background noise and started writing it into policy. And a tariff regime that survived a Supreme Court defeat by changing its legal clothes rather than its economic effect. None of these are new wars or new laws in the sense of fresh disruption, but together they raise the floor under inflation and the cost of capital for the physical buildout, energy, grids, chips, that this whole AI cycle depends on.

What to watch from here: whether Houthi enforcement broadens beyond Saudi-affiliated cargo, whether Brent breaks back above $100 on a sustained basis rather than a single-session spike, and whether the Fed's hawkish minority of three grows at the next meeting. Any one of those would turn a contained story into an uncontained one.

That's the briefing. Have a good weekend.
