Good morning. It's Friday, October 16th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

Here's the thesis for today. The AI buildout now hits different ceilings depending on where you stand. Inside China, the ceiling is policy. Outside China, it's physical tool supply. Let's take them in order.

Start with China. IDC data, as reported by Reuters, puts Nvidia's share of China's data-center AI chip market at 34% in the first half of 2026. Nvidia shipped 678,000 AI accelerators into China from January through June. That's down 44% from the same period a year earlier. Huawei shipped 572,000 units, for a 28% share, closing the gap sharply.

One detail settles how to read those shares. IDC put total accelerator shipments into China's data centers at 2.008 million units for the half. Nvidia's and Huawei's unit counts divide almost exactly into 34% and 28% of that total. Neither IDC nor Reuters states the basis outright. But the arithmetic says these are unit shares, not revenue shares.

The rest of the market isn't a mystery blob either. IDC named four more domestic vendors. Alibaba's chip-design unit T-Head held 7%, Cambricon 5%, Baidu's Kunlunxin 4%, and Hygon 3%. By our arithmetic, that still leaves roughly a fifth of the market unattributed to any named vendor. One more gap matters. IDC did not say which Nvidia products make up the 678,000 units. It didn't confirm whether current-generation B200 or B300 parts are in there. So the product mix behind Nvidia's China number is simply not disclosed.

Now separate the dimensions, because the headline "Huawei is beating Nvidia" runs several stories together. This is a unit count. It says nothing about performance per chip, and nothing about revenue share. Nvidia sells into China under US export controls, so this compares whatever Nvidia is permitted to ship against Huawei's domestic Ascend silicon. Take the dimensions one at a time. On architecture leadership, this data measures nothing. On software ecosystem strength, it measures nothing either. On domestic production, Huawei's half-million-plus units say Chinese supply is real at scale, but unit counts don't tell you at what performance. What changed is market access, and market access is set in Washington. So read Nvidia's 44% drop as a supply-permission story, not a demand-rejection story. Nothing in this data shows Chinese buyers walking away from Nvidia's architecture or its software stack.

That updates this show's running framework. On Tuesday and again yesterday, we split AI demand into compute-constrained and economics-constrained. China adds a third category. Call it policy-constrained. In that market, the binding constraint isn't tool availability. It isn't customer budgets either. It's licensing.

Two consequences follow, and they point in different directions. The first is our inference about fungibility. Global accelerator supply is capped by tools and wafers, as we'll get to in a moment. So capacity Nvidia can't sell into China is likely absorbed by buyers elsewhere. That means the China share loss may cost Nvidia less in revenue than the headline implies. The caveat is that China-specific, export-compliant parts aren't directly interchangeable with what the rest of the world buys.

The second consequence is the one that lasts. Every accelerator Huawei deploys into that gap builds a domestic developer base and a software ecosystem that doesn't need CUDA. That's the real long-run cost to Nvidia. It's an inference, not a measured fact, but it compounds. So Nvidia hasn't lost ground in China on merit. It's lost market access, which is more reversible than a competitive defeat. The exception is the ecosystem it's handing Huawei in the meantime.

Where Nvidia still has full market access, it's playing the ecosystem game rather than the chip-spec game. Nvidia is reportedly investing in d-Matrix, an inference-chip startup, and bringing it into its NVLink Fusion interconnect ecosystem. There are two ways to read that. One is that Nvidia is co-opting a potential inference competitor before it becomes a threat. The other is that Nvidia is hedging across inference architectures it doesn't want to build in-house. The reporting doesn't let us pick, so hold both. Separately, the Financial Times reports Nvidia is in early talks involving Reflection AI, and early talks is all it is so far. Either way, the pattern holds. Where Nvidia can't buy market access, it's buying ecosystem reach instead.

Now cross the Pacific, because outside China the ceiling is different. It's physics, and ASML just put a number on it. ASML has made a firm commitment to raise 2027 capacity by 30% for its low-NA EUV and DUV immersion tools. Yesterday we covered ASML's warning that planned supply for 2027 and 2028 won't fully meet customer demand. The new detail is that this warning already counts the 30% increase. That's the equipment maker furthest upstream in the chain saying it can't build its way out of the shortage over two years.

The guidance points the same way. ASML guided fourth-quarter sales to €14.2 to €15.2 billion. Against the third quarter's €11.7 billion, that's roughly a 26% step up at the midpoint, by our arithmetic. On Monday we said equipment was the one layer where the market was pricing a slowdown the fundamentals hadn't shown. This guide is the fundamentals answering.

The skeptical read matters here too. A seller saying demand exceeds supply is still talking its own book. And as we noted yesterday, ASML disclosed no bookings number to back it up. The 30% commitment also covers only low-NA EUV and DUV immersion. Nothing here tells us anything about High-NA capacity. Even with those caveats, a tool shortage running through 2028 hands ASML pricing power. That's consistent with its full-year guide of around 56% gross margin. The cost lands downstream. Foundry capex dollars may buy fewer machines than they used to, which raises leading-edge cost per wafer. That flows through to what every accelerator costs to build.

That tool ceiling shows up one step downstream, at TSMC. On its earnings call, CEO C.C. Wei said, quote, "AI-related demand is extremely robust and far above our supply capability." He added that TSMC did not see any sign of slowdown. TSMC also raised its full-year 2026 revenue growth target to roughly 44% in dollar terms. But note what didn't move. TSMC reiterated its 2026 capital budget of $60 to $64 billion rather than raising it. And as we flagged yesterday, it still won't put a number on 2027 capex.

Read that silence against ASML's ceiling. A foundry can't commit capital to capacity it isn't sure it can get the tools for. So the silence fits a supply limit as well as it fits caution. And keep in mind who's speaking. Wei's line is a pricing-power signal from a seller, same as ASML's. It tells you wafer demand is supply-bound. It tells you nothing about whether the economics hold at the other end. That's the hyperscalers and AI labs actually paying for all this capacity.

So, three ceilings. In China, it's policy. Outside China, it's physical tool and wafer supply. And underneath both sits the economics question, still unresolved. Yesterday's Throughline tracked rising credit stress around AI financing, running from CoreWeave's bonds up to Oracle's credit-default swaps. The chip prints can't answer that. Being supply-constrained says nothing about who can afford to pay for the supply once it ships.

What to watch from here. First, the next IDC read on China, and whether it names the fifth of the market still unattributed. Second, whether TSMC commits to a 2027 capex figure. Third, whether ASML ever puts a bookings number behind its demand language.

That's the briefing. Stay cautiously optimistic.
