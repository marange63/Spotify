Good morning, it's Friday, October 23rd, and this is your Cautious Optimism enterprise technology briefing.

Here's the thread for today. Incumbent software vendors are giving AI away inside the suite instead of selling it as a new product line. That means the payoff has to show up in bookings and renewals, not in a new revenue category. This week gave us two fresh data points on exactly that test, one from ServiceNow and one from SAP.

Start with ServiceNow. In Sunday's briefing, we covered ServiceNow's president denying that the company wanted to buy Decagon, the AI customer-service agent startup. The stock rose about five percent after the denial, and ServiceNow's line was that it prioritizes organic growth. About a week later, ServiceNow shipped the organic answer. On October 21st, per its own press release, ServiceNow and Nvidia launched general availability of Arc. Arc is a persistent personal AI agent. It follows an employee across ServiceNow workflows, email, and other productivity tools, and it can build missing integrations on the fly.

Here's what the release actually supports. Arc is included at no additional cost across all three EmployeeWorks tiers: Foundation, Advanced, and Prime. Mac support is live now, and Windows support is expected within weeks. On governance, the piece that's live today is Nvidia OpenShell. It reads ServiceNow's policy settings and runs the agent in a sandbox. That sandbox is designed to honor permission revocations by stopping the agent's execution.

The headline claim is a hardware kill switch, enforced on Nvidia's BlueField chips. That piece is explicitly not live. ServiceNow calls it AI Passport. The press release says it's expected in a future version. So today's governance is software-level and real. The silicon-level enforcement is announced, not shipped.

And there's no adoption number anywhere in the release. There's no seat count and no customer count for Arc. The only usage figure on the page is platform-wide: more than 100 billion workflows a year, which tells us nothing about Arc. On the evidence ladder, this sits at the bottom rung. It's a general-availability announcement, not a deployment story.

So what does bundling a free agent tell us? It's a seat-defense move, not a monetization event. ServiceNow is using Arc to protect its existing EmployeeWorks subscriptions rather than charging separately for agents. That has a cost. A persistent agent consumes inference every time it works, and with no price attached, there's no revenue line set against that cost. That's my inference, since ServiceNow disclosed no margin figure. But it means Arc only pays off if it lifts renewals or drives seat expansion. There's a longer-run tension too, again my inference. If agents end up doing work that used to justify a seat, a free agent inside a seat-priced bundle could eventually erode the very seats it's meant to protect.

Arc also extends a pattern we flagged in Tuesday's briefing. Back then, Microsoft looked set to turn agent governance into its own budget line, while Okta had already bundled its Agent SSO at no additional cost. Now Arc's governance arrives inside a no-cost bundle too. That's two vendors pricing the governance layer at zero, which undercuts the idea that governance becomes a standalone line item. If governance is free, the defensible asset is whoever holds the permissions data, not whoever sells the governance product.

There's a second-order effect for startups, again as inference. When the platform gives away both the agent and its guardrails, the price umbrella for independent agent and oversight vendors shrinks. And go back to Decagon. ServiceNow chose to build and bundle rather than buy. If platforms keep doing that, the strategic-acquirer exit for agent-application startups narrows. One caution: a denial isn't a strategy disclosure, and "no intention" today doesn't rule out a deal later.

The test for ServiceNow comes October 28th, when it reports earnings. Watch renewal and net-expansion commentary, not Arc headlines.

Why does that print carry so much weight? Because the stock isn't trading on fundamentals right now. Around October 16th, ServiceNow, Palantir, and Salesforce all fell four to five percent on investor worries about how the AI infrastructure buildout gets financed. By October 21st, most of the group was rallying again. Our research found no new revenue or contract data behind either move. These names are trading on financing-chain sentiment. So October 28th is ServiceNow's first real chance to separate story from substance.

Now to SAP, which offers the week's hardest data on whether bundled AI is landing in deals. And I need to start with a correction. In Thursday's briefing, we said nine of SAP's fifty largest deals included AI, and argued that most of its biggest contracts carried none. That was a misread. On the earnings call transcript, SAP said nine out of ten of its fifty largest deals included AI and the Business Data Cloud. That's not nine of fifty. It's roughly forty-five of fifty. We got that wrong, and it reverses the skeptical read we gave you. AI attach inside SAP's biggest deals is close to universal.

But universal attach without a disclosed AI revenue line is informative in its own right. It's the signature of bundling, not of a new profit pool. "Included AI" measures attach, not causation. SAP didn't say whether AI decided those deals or rode along with cloud migrations already under way. It also gave no separate AI revenue figure. The one adoption number SAP offered is that Joule Work Desktop, its AI assistant, is live for more than 110,000 employees. Name that for what it is: SAP's own staff, not customers. It's dogfooding, not market proof.

What is solid is SAP's backlog. That's contracted business, a rung above any announcement, even though it isn't yet recognized revenue. And that's exactly why the forward risk SAP flagged matters. Management tied that risk to customers who have been in financial distress. The part of the call we can quote doesn't spell out how that hits bookings, so treat the mechanism as unexplained. What SAP did say, in its own words, is that "this could weigh on Q4 bookings, and as a result, our 2027 trajectory." In plain terms, that's a warning that fourth-quarter bookings could come in softer, and that softness would carry into next year. It's a management-stated risk, not a result; the quarter hasn't happened. At the same time, SAP said it now expects to trend toward the upper end of its cloud and software revenue outlook. That's firmer, but it's not a raise.

Put those together and the fourth quarter becomes the real test. Can near-universal AI attach actually defend SAP's bookings growth? Or was some of this year's acceleration a pull-forward that shows up in 2027? The so-what: SAP's AI is everywhere in the deal mix, but the proof is still in bookings.

Now pull ServiceNow and SAP onto one line. Both are treating AI as a free or bundled feature that defends an existing franchise, rather than something enterprises pay for separately. That's not bad business; defending a franchise is valuable. But it means enterprise AI is a retention story right now, not yet a revenue story. So judge both companies on what comes next. For ServiceNow, that's October 28th: listen for renewal rates, net expansion, and any real adoption number for Arc. For SAP, it's whether fourth-quarter bookings hold up against the risk management itself just flagged. And watch whether ServiceNow's AI Passport, the hardware-enforced piece of Arc's governance, actually ships or stays a future release.
