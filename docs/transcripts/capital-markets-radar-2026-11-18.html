Good morning, it's Wednesday, November 18th, and this is Capital Markets Radar.

Here's the thesis for today. Nvidia just passed the demand test the market spent a week setting for it. It has not passed the discount-rate test. And the credit stress from a Fed priced to hike is finally showing up in prices. It's showing up in the lowest-rated corner of high yield, though, not in the AI trade.

Start with Nvidia, because this is the print we've been building toward all week. On Sunday, this desk said Tuesday would decide whether Nvidia's drawdown was a governance discount or a demand verdict. On demand, the answer is in. Nvidia's own filing, dated yesterday, puts third-quarter revenue at a hundred fifteen point eight billion dollars. That's up twenty percent from the prior quarter, and up a hundred and three percent from a year ago. Data-center revenue is the number that carries the AI capex story. It came in at a hundred seven point six billion, up a hundred and ten percent year over year. That is not a core business that's slowing. The demand half of the verdict is resolved.

But look at the guide, because the headline flatters itself. Nvidia guided fourth-quarter revenue to a hundred thirty billion dollars, plus or minus three percent. Two qualifiers matter. First, the guide includes an extra week, so it covers fourteen weeks against the thirteen just reported. Second, it assumes no data-center compute revenue from China. Now put both quarters on a per-week basis. On that math, the guide implies roughly four percent sequential growth. Nvidia just posted twenty. That's our own arithmetic from the two reported figures, not a number from Nvidia or any analyst. So the headline is a raise, but the run-rate is decelerating.

The zero-China assumption cuts two ways. On one side, it's a clean, conservative base. Any loosening of export licensing becomes pure upside from here. On the other side, Nvidia's own planning has now written that channel down to nothing. For holders, China shifts from a downside risk to a source of option value.

On capital allocation, Seeking Alpha reports that the board added a hundred fifty billion dollars to the buyback authorization. A buyback that large at these multiples can read as confidence in the business. It can also read as a shortage of better places to put the cash. Both readings are live, and nothing in hand settles it. The chairman also said Jensen Huang is not returning. So the key-person question is no longer a waiting game. It's now a permanent CEO search, with Colette Kress still running the company on an interim basis.

Now the reaction. Shares rose after hours. We have the direction, but no magnitude worth trusting yet. There are two over-reads to avoid here. The first is treating this beat as proof that AI demand is fine across the whole stack. A hundred-and-ten-percent data-center number is a backward-looking shipment fact. It tells you nothing about the neocloud and financing layer, which is where this week's stress signals came from. That means Nebius guiding to the low end of its revenue range last Thursday, and Dell sliding on industrywide debt warnings on Monday. So Nvidia's beat is necessary for AI-infrastructure credit, but it isn't sufficient.

The second over-read runs the other way. It treats the muted move as the market doubting Nvidia's execution. But with a quarter-point December hike priced at ninety-five percent, a capped multiple needs no doubt about the company at all. Earnings power can rise while the price the market pays for it compresses. The open question for today's session is the rest of the chip group. AMD and Intel both fell harder than Nvidia in Monday's selloff. Whether this guide lifts them is the second half of the verdict. Nvidia cleared its own bar. The group's bar is still pending.

If demand isn't the constraint on this stock, the discount rate is. And that same discount rate is now the story everywhere else. CME's FedWatch tool, via CNBC, put the odds of a quarter-point December hike at ninety-five percent as of yesterday. On Sunday, we called the hike a certainty, on a different cut of FedWatch. Don't read that gap as the market backing off. The source doesn't say whether the remaining five percent is hold odds or the half-point tail. Treasury yields did give up their gains on Tuesday, as investors digested retail-sales data and comments from Fed officials. One session of easing yields on the eve of Nvidia is not a duration bid. And the FOMC doesn't decide until December 9th, so this is market pricing, not an outcome.

Now, where that discount rate is actually biting, which is credit. And this desk owes you a callback here. Last Friday, we said the AI-credit transmission channel was identified, but not yet in use. Yesterday, we called Dell's slide an equity signal, not yet a credit one. Credit is now moving. It's just not moving where we were looking.

Vanguard's research, dated November 10th, puts it plainly. Spreads on CCC-rated bonds have widened by more than five hundred basis points from their lowest levels this year, to roughly a thousand basis points over Treasuries. Now set that against the whole market. FRED's daily series for the ICE BofA high-yield index had the broad spread at about three hundred twenty-three basis points on November 13th. So the bottom tier trades at roughly three times the index. That isn't a broad repricing of credit risk. It's stress piling up at the bottom of the stack.

The stress is concentrated even within that tier. Janus Henderson's breakdown finds that four issuers, out of more than a hundred fifty rated CCC, make up more than thirty percent of CCC spreads. Many of the most affected are telecom and media companies. Nothing in hand ties the widening to AI or data-center borrowers. Our read is that this is the textbook mechanism at work. When the market prices a hiking Fed, refinancing costs bite first on the weakest, most indebted balance sheets. Right now, those sit in legacy media, not new compute. There's a second-order point as well, and it's our inference rather than a reported finding. This is the same tier of borrower that private credit has been absorbing from the banks. If refinancing stress at the bottom persists, private-credit marks are where it surfaces next. And spreads tend to move before defaults do, so don't wait for default data to confirm this.

None of that makes AI-linked credit clean. CoreWeave's five-year credit-default swaps sat around eleven hundred basis points yesterday, per CNBC. Anything above a thousand is generally read as distress. But notice the comparison. Last Friday, we cited those same CoreWeave swaps above twelve hundred eighty-nine basis points, on November 5th. So CoreWeave is still distressed, but it's off its early-November spike, not newly breaking. It's also a single-name story. It isn't what's driving the CCC move.

So we have a bifurcation within a bifurcation. Broad high yield is contained. The weakest legacy borrowers are under real pressure. And AI-adjacent credit sits distressed in single names, running in parallel rather than driving the index. Reading the CCC move as the AI-credit channel opening would be an over-read. Dismissing it as noise would be an under-read, because the stress is concentrated, not absent. The AI-specific credit test is still ahead, at Thursday's ING data-center loan bids. The takeaway is that credit stress is concentrated, not systemic, and not yet about AI.

Briefly on crypto, where the same discount-rate force is still draining the institutional wrapper. Bitcoin fell more than twelve point six percent over the past week, per FXStreet, to close just above sixty-five thousand dollars. US spot Bitcoin ETFs have now logged two point three five billion dollars of net outflows in November, per KuCoin. On Sunday, we had a seven-session outflow streak at about one point nine one billion. The windows differ, and part of the November total re-counts flows we've already reported. So treat it as the running tally getting worse, not as a fresh shock. The thread is the same one that runs through Nvidia and credit. A rising discount rate is a net seller of zero-yield assets, whether that's an ETF wrapper or an AI chipmaker's multiple.
