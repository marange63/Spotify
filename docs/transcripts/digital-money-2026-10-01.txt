Good morning, this is your Cautious Optimism briefing on digital money for Thursday, October 1st.

Let's grade a call we've been running for a week. On Saturday we named $84,000 to $85,000 as Bitcoin's line in the sand. That's its largest long-term-holder cost-basis cluster. On Tuesday morning we reported that band being breached intraday. Yesterday was the monthly and quarterly close that Bitfinex's desk had circled, with Bitcoin boxed between that cluster and its yearly open. No confirmed closing print was available as we went to air, so treat the result as approximate. CoinDesk's live coverage assumed a close somewhere around $84,000. On that assumption, Bitcoin gained about 44 percent for the quarter, its best since early 2024. September alone finished up 6.14 percent. So the quarter was strong in aggregate. But it ended right at the level we said was the real test, not comfortably above it.

Yesterday also handed us a clean natural experiment on why. August core PCE came in at 3.0 percent year over year, below what markets expected. Bitcoin responded instantly. It moved from the low $83,000s to a session high near $85,650 within about an hour. Then it reversed back down. The reason matters more than the spike. Treasury yields did not fall on a soft inflation print. The 10-year hit a 24-year high above 5.30 percent on the same day. Good inflation news should pull yields down and lift risk assets. It didn't.

Two readings compete here. One is that the bond market simply doesn't believe the disinflation. The other is that something besides the inflation outlook is driving long rates, such as term premium or debt supply. Either way, the implication for crypto is the same. The "Fed easing means Bitcoin rallies" channel, which much of the ETF-flow commentary assumes, isn't operating right now. And this isn't only a crypto story. Our cross-asset coverage has tracked long-end yields shrugging off good news all week. Bitcoin's PCE fade is the same pattern showing up in a new market. So the ceiling right now is being set by the bond market, not by crypto positioning or sentiment.

So if the inflation data couldn't lift price, was the reversal at least a leverage story? The liquidation data says no. Roughly $60 million unwound in that first hour, split between longs and shorts. That's a two-way move, not a directional flush. The positioning backdrop confirms it. Coin-denominated Bitcoin futures open interest is at its lowest since March, almost 20 percent below its August level. That's the right measure to watch, because the dollar figure moves with price. Bitfinex's analysts say much of the leverage built during last month's breakout to $87,000 has been cleared. They describe perpetual positioning as close to neutral.

That partly answers a question this desk has carried since Saturday. Who is setting the price at the margin? It isn't leveraged longs anymore. And here's the fair bull case, stated plainly. With positioning this clean, any real breakout from here would have to be spot-led. A spot-led rally is structurally more durable than one built on borrowed money. The tempting conclusion is that the floor is in. That doesn't follow. Clean positioning only tells you the next move needs real spot demand. And spot demand is exactly what's missing.

Which brings us to the ETF tape, where the spot question gets answered. The record inflow week that pushed 2026 flows positive opened with nearly a billion dollars on Monday, September 21st. By that Friday, the daily print was down 87 percent, according to aggregator data relayed by KuCoin. We've tracked that decay since. On Monday of this week, as we reported Tuesday, the day's inflow was just $31 million. Tuesday brought a ninth straight day of inflows, but a modest one, at about $66 million.

Then yesterday, on the quarter-close session itself, the streak broke. U.S. spot Bitcoin ETFs posted a net outflow of $148.7 million. Fidelity's FBTC did most of the selling, at $125.6 million. BlackRock's IBIT gave up a far smaller amount. So the bid didn't just taper into quarter-end. It flipped to net selling on the one day price needed support most. These are aggregator figures, and daily attribution can be revised.

Keep the scale honest in both directions. That outflow is small against roughly $108 billion in total ETF assets. A single day of redemptions is not a redemption wave. The fairer alternative reading is a quarter-end rebalancing pause, not a structural reversal. But the skeptical read is hard to dismiss. These flows look like price-chasing. They were biggest on the squeeze, decayed as price stalled, and reversed when price failed to hold the band. That undercuts the idea of ETFs as a structural demand floor. It also has a business read-through. Issuer fee and asset-growth assumptions built on steady inflows look stretched if the bid only shows up after price moves. So the year-to-date total that only just turned positive is now what October has to defend. Yesterday made that job harder.

If markets won't supply the catalyst, Washington was supposed to. That's stalled too. The Senate's cloture vote on the CLARITY Act market-structure bill failed 49 to 50 on September 15th. The detail worth sitting with is why it failed. It wasn't a dispute over the SEC-CFTC jurisdictional split the bill was designed to resolve. It was a fight over ethics provisions on crypto holdings by public officials. That fight is inseparable from the sitting president's own crypto business interests.

There are two ways to read that. The bearish one is that a political conflict can sink a bill regardless of its substance, and that conflict isn't going away. The stronger bull reading is that the substantive framework itself didn't lose the vote, so it could return largely intact in the next Congress. For now, the calendar favors the bears. The Senate returns October 5th and sits through November 6th. That leaves fewer than 36 legislative days before a new Congress in January. A "CLARITY Act 2.0" substitute is circulating, but no vote is scheduled. Treat it as talk, not momentum.

Listed equities show how much the vote mattered. On the day it failed, Coinbase fell roughly 8 percent and Circle about 10 percent. Bitcoin dropped below $75,000 the same day. That tells you Coinbase and Circle trade partly as leveraged bets on legislation, not just as Bitcoin proxies. With this Congress's window nearly closed, that legislative optionality is largely gone from their valuations until next year.

With Congress stalled, the agencies are filling the vacuum. That's a weaker kind of durability. The SEC issued an innovation-exemption order for tokenized securities on September 17th, and it's open for public comment. The SEC's broader Regulation Crypto Assets is still a proposal. The law firm White and Case calls it a critical component of the new federal regime, if ultimately adopted. That conditional is doing real work. Proposals and orders are not statute. A future SEC or CFTC can rewrite them.

The Federal Reserve's GENIUS Act stablecoin proposals, announced September 24th, sit on firmer ground, because they implement an actual law. But even that track shows strain. Governor Barr supported the package. He also warned that its "significant or systemic" standard may have unknown effects on the Board's ability to verify that institutions run compliant programs. That's a supervisory-capacity concern, not a dissent. And the track is already late. The law's initial July 2026 regulatory deadline was missed. So even the one piece with statutory backing is behind schedule and questioned from inside the Fed.

Put it together. Positioning is clean, but the spot bid that has to replace leveraged longs just turned negative on the day that mattered most. Rates, not inflation data, are setting the ceiling. And the rulebook that was supposed to anchor all of this is being drafted by agencies, on comment periods, rather than passed into law.

Three things to watch into next week. First, whether Bitcoin reclaims the $84,000 to $85,000 band on a closing basis, not just an intraday spike. Second, whether early-October ETF prints keep the year-to-date flow total positive. Third, whether the Senate's return on October 5th produces any real movement on a CLARITY sequel.

That's the briefing for Thursday, October 1st. Have a good one.
