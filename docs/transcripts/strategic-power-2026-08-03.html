Cautious Optimism Briefings

Strategic Power, Energy, and Institutional Change

Transcript · August 3, 2026

Good morning. It's Monday, August 3rd, and this is your strategic power briefing.

We start with Ukraine's drone campaign, because it escalated again over the weekend. Overnight on August 1st, Ukraine's security service struck three refineries in Russia's Bashneft complex, in the city of Ufa, nearly 1,600 kilometers from the Ukrainian border. The targets were Bashneft-Ufaneftekhim, Bashneft-Novoil, and Bashneft-UNPZ, with a combined refining capacity the SBU says exceeds 23 million tonnes of crude a year. That's a real range escalation from the strike we flagged Saturday on Lukoil's Volgograd plant, deeper into Russian territory and hitting one of the country's largest refining hubs.

Now, Moscow disputes the damage. Bashkortostan's governor, Radiy Khabirov, confirmed drones targeted Ufa but said the attack was repelled, and he attributed the visible smoke to debris from a downed drone rather than refinery damage. No casualties were reported, and there's no independent confirmation of actual production impact. So treat the physical damage claim as contested: Ukraine says refinery, Russia says debris field.

What isn't contested is the policy response. Russia's government extended its ban on gasoline and diesel exports through January 31st, 2027, explicitly citing the need to stabilize the domestic fuel market after these drone attacks. And domestic gasoline prices have climbed 19% since the start of the year, reaching an average of nearly 78 rubles a liter as of late July. That's the tell. Even if you doubt each individual strike's damage claims, a government extending an export ban for six more months and watching pump prices rise is a government managing real supply stress.

And here's the mechanism that matters, because it shapes how this shows up in markets. These strikes hit refineries, not oil fields. So they don't remove Russian crude from the world; they remove Russia's ability to turn its own crude into diesel and gasoline. That's why the first visible effect is a domestic fuel squeeze and an export ban, not a spike in the crude price. It also means the global spillover, when it comes, lands on refined-product markets, on diesel and gasoline cracks, even while crude supply stays ample. Keep those two channels separate. So what: asymmetric drone warfare is functioning as a slow-motion substitute for sanctions on Russian war financing, whatever the state of any single refinery.

Now the other side of the energy story, because it complicates any simple "prices are spiking" narrative. OPEC+ met on August 2nd and approved a fifth consecutive monthly output increase, raising production by 188,000 barrels a day for August. At the same time, Saudi Arabia cut its official selling price for Arab Light crude to Asia to a dollar fifty a barrel below the Oman-Dubai benchmark, the largest monthly cut in that price since Reuters began keeping records in 2003. Crude itself closed near $84.67 a barrel on July 31st. Put those together and you get a genuine tension: a producer bloc adding barrels and simultaneously undercutting its own Asian customers on price is not a cartel defending scarcity, it's Saudi Arabia defending market share against non-OPEC supply. So the price signal in oil right now is coming from disruption risk around Russian refining, not from coordinated OPEC discipline or underlying demand strength.

That distinction matters for the Fed, because energy-driven inflation and demand-driven inflation call for different policy responses. A supply-side oil move, on top of a Fed that already named energy as an explicit inflation input, argues for patience on rate cuts, not a green light. And there's a second wire running from this section to the next. Higher oil and gas prices raise the marginal cost of gas-fired generation. A lot of the new power feeding data centers is going to burn gas. So an energy shock on the supply side doesn't stay in the oil complex, it raises the cost of the electrons the AI buildout runs on. Hold that thought.

Now pivot from the price of energy to the appetite for it, because that's where the AI buildout collides with all of this. Alphabet's second-quarter capital expenditure came in at $44.924 billion, roughly double what it spent a year earlier, and that spending pushed Alphabet to its first-ever negative quarterly free cash flow, negative $5.855 billion. Management's own guidance going into the year already had capex at $180 to 190 billion for 2026, six times what it was in 2022. The important signal isn't the size of the number, it's the direction: Alphabet is spending into the power and grid constraint, not pausing for it. A company that generates cash for a living just went cash-negative in a single quarter because it's racing to build compute capacity. So what: the demand side of the AI power story is accelerating even as the physical grid underneath it is not built yet.

Which brings us to who actually pays for that gap. In PJM, the country's largest power market, data-center demand forecasts have driven $21.3 billion of the $47.2 billion in total costs across the last three capacity auctions — that's 45% of the total. Pennsylvania Governor Josh Shapiro negotiated a price cap with PJM that has already cut $13.1 billion off capacity costs across the two auctions where it applied. That's a real, measured instance of state-level political pressure reshaping a wholesale power market in real time. Layered on top, federal regulators at FERC have made a preliminary finding that the tariffs governing large-load interconnection at the six major grid operators are unjust and unreasonable, with RTOs required to respond by August 17th either justifying their existing rules or proposing reform. Note the distinction: that's a preliminary finding and a response deadline, not new megawatts on the ground. The framework is moving faster than the concrete.

So what does the buildout do about it. If regulators force new large loads to pay their own reliability and capacity cost, the economics tilt. Advantage moves toward independent power producers, gas-turbine makers, and small modular reactor developers, the players who can bring firm, dedicated generation, and away from pure grid-tied cloud capex that quietly assumed someone else would build the power. It also nudges new compute toward grids with more headroom, parts of Texas and the Southeast, rather than PJM's congested queue. That's the investable edge of a cost-allocation fight that still reads like a procedural footnote.

So here's where that leaves us. Energy is the base layer on both sides of the meter this week: Ukrainian strikes and Russian export restrictions squeezing refined-product supply, OPEC+ defending market share while output claims a price stability it isn't fully delivering, and a hyperscaler capex wave slamming into a grid regulators are only now trying to rewire. The common thread is upward pressure on the cost of everything downstream, from Russian gasoline to American data-center power, and the same higher energy input shows up on both ends. Watch three dates: any further confirmation of damage at Ufa, the next OPEC+ meeting, and FERC's August 17th deadline for RTO responses. None of them resolve this week, but each one moves the line between announcement and actual capacity.

That's the briefing. Have a good Monday.
