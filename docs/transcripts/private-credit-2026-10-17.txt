Good morning. It's October 17th, and this is the private credit briefing.

Here's the thesis. The default rate in private credit is now openly a definitional choice. And the two mechanisms that would force managers to recognize deferred losses, redemptions and trading, are both still in the managers' hands.

Start with the lead. Moody's has a sector report dated October 14th covering 607 borrowers held across 47 middle-market CLOs, over the twelve months to June. One sourcing note: the copy circulating online is stamped draft, so treat these as Moody's figures as reported. The reported default rate was 4 percent. Count PIK amendments and stressed maturity extensions as default-equivalent events, and it rises to 5.8 percent. In count terms, 24 reported defaults become 35. The additions are six borrowers that switched to paying in kind, and five that extended maturities while showing weak credit quality.

Earlier this week, on Monday and Tuesday, we walked through Fitch's record private-credit default rate. That count was dominated by deferrals and extensions rather than actual payment failures. Now a second rating agency, working from a different sample, reaches the same structural conclusion by a different route. Two agencies agreeing on the method matters more than either headline number.

Keep the scope honest, though. This is not a private-credit-wide default rate. It's middle-market CLO collateral, a selected slice of direct lending, with data that stops in June. And eleven extra events in a sample of 607 is a small count. So read 4 versus 5.8 as directional, not precise.

The more revealing part is where Moody's draws the line. A PIK amendment or an extension only counts as a hidden default if the borrower has weak credit quality. Moody's defines that as high leverage, EBITDA covering interest less than one time, negative free cash flow, weak liquidity, and a rating-equivalent of Caa1 or lower. That's a demanding bar, not a loose one.

Now apply that bar to the PIK book. Of the 607 borrowers, 106 pay in kind, about 17 percent. Most of them aren't treated as distressed at all. Sixty-four, or 60 percent of the PIK group, had PIK built in at origination, typically to preserve cash for growth or acquisitions. Another 42 added PIK by amendment before the study window began, and they're still paying in kind. Only the six borrowers who newly switched to PIK during the window entered the default-equivalent count.

So whoever decides which PIK is a cash-preservation feature and which is disguised distress is effectively setting the default rate. By our reading, those 42 earlier amenders sit outside the count because of when they amended. That's a timing rule, not a credit judgment. And recall the point from Monday's lawsuits: managers can collect incentive fees on accrued PIK income. So the industry that classifies PIK can also get paid on it.

Here's the other half, and it's the one the industry will want you to stop at. Moody's says CLO investors have so far been shielded from this borrower stress by portfolio management, collateral tests, and structural protections. The stress sits inside the borrowers. It hasn't reached the tranches. Our inference is that when it does, the first visible damage shows up in CLO equity distributions and thinner collateral-test cushions, well before any rated tranche takes a loss.

The maturity wall is where this gets tested. Issuers with a Caa rating-equivalent, the weakest cohort, account for more than half of the sample's maturities in both 2027 and 2028. With limited refinancing flexibility, Moody's expects many of them to lean on extensions, PIK amendments, or sponsor support. Put differently, the weakest borrowers' main path through the wall is the very behavior Moody's now counts as default. That's the honest read: not a crisis, but a pipeline of future default-equivalents. And if this lens becomes the agency norm, the low-loss figures used to sell private credit into the wealth and 401(k) channels are resting on the lenient definition.

Deferred losses get recognized one of two ways. Investors demand their money back, or the loans actually trade. Start with exits.

Blue Owl capped redemptions again this quarter on its two non-traded BDCs, Blue Owl Credit Income Corp and Blue Owl Technology Income Corp. Investors sought to withdraw 4.2 billion dollars across the two funds in the third quarter. That's down from 4.7 billion in the second. At Blue Owl Credit Income specifically, requests eased to 16.8 percent of shares from 18.8 percent. By our own arithmetic, that's still more than three times the standard quarterly tender limit.

That fits the pattern we flagged yesterday at HPS's Corporate Lending Fund, where requests eased from 13.3 to 11.5 percent of shares. Exit demand is falling at the margin across platforms. But at Blue Owl it still sits at a multiple of what the funds will pay out in a quarter. That's a different situation from KKR's K-FITS, which we noted paid requests just over its cap in full earlier this month.

There's a less comfortable read on why requests are easing. Once a fund prorates every quarter, the same trapped holders resubmit again and again. So a falling headline number can reflect fatigue or resubmission mechanics as much as restored confidence. The second-order effect is structural. Prorated redemptions against a standing queue turn a semi-liquid vehicle into a managed-exit vehicle. Selling more liquidity into the wealth channel then depends on new inflows outrunning the queue. The gate, not the investor, is setting the pace of exit.

The second valve is price, and that brings us to Apollo. John Cortese, Apollo's co-head of corporate credit, told Bloomberg Television the firm has traded more than 35 billion dollars since it began making markets in private credit. That's across roughly 500 assets and 160 counterparties. About a third of the volume was in loans Apollo didn't originate, meaning other managers' paper.

Scale that properly. The 35 billion is cumulative since the desk began, not an annual run-rate. Set it against the roughly 850 billion dollars of credit Apollo now marks daily, and by our arithmetic it's about 4 percent of the marked book. Most of those daily marks remain model outputs, anchored by a thin tape.

The third-party share is the genuinely interesting piece. Apollo is generating trade prints on competitors' loans. That's the cross-manager comparison this show flagged on Monday would arrive with Apollo's asset-level marks on October 30th. Those marks are Apollo's own fair-value estimates, not market-clearing prices. But if Apollo's trades in shared credits become reference prints, holders of the same loans at higher marks will have to explain the gap.

There are two ways to read the desk. Constructively, a desk that clears other lenders' paper is the beginning of an outside price, in a market that has relied on quarterly, model-based valuations. Skeptically, the market-maker is also the firm whose own book is being marked. That isn't independent price discovery of the kind a public exchange delivers. Either way, a thin tape is anchoring a very large model-marked book.

So where does the cycle actually stand? This is a slow, deferred grind, not a turn. Nothing here says the credit cycle has broken. But the biggest unresolved risk is recognition timing. And right now that timing is controlled by the people with the incentive to defer it. Managers choose which PIK counts as distress. Managers set the redemption gates. And one manager is marking its own book while also making the market in it.

Watch four things from here. First, Apollo's asset-level marks on October 30th, when cross-manager comparison on the same credits becomes possible. Second, the alternative managers' third-quarter earnings calls in late October. Third, the next round of tender results at the non-traded BDCs, to see whether the queues keep shrinking or harden. Fourth, the first third-quarter non-accrual and PIK reads from the publicly traded BDCs, Goldman Sachs BDC and Kayne Anderson BDC among them, in early November. That's when deferred stress either stays deferred or starts showing up in the numbers.
