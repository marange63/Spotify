All week on this beat, one point has kept coming back. The number that would settle the AI spending argument is the number nobody publishes. Vendors give us counts of customers, activations and pilots. They do not give us recognized dollars set against commitments. On Friday, one company had no choice, because it filed a prospectus.

That company is Nscale, the London-based AI cloud provider, and its S-1 landed on September 18th. Start with the recognized side of the ledger. Reporting on the filing puts first-half 2026 revenue at about 140 point 6 million dollars. The same reporting puts the net loss over those six months at 1 point 0 2 billion dollars. So the business books a small amount of revenue and spends heavily to stand up capacity.

Now the commitment side, in the filing's own language. As of August 31st, total contract value under long-term take-or-pay contracts stood at 103 point 4 billion dollars. Only 2 point 6 billion of that is labeled active.

Here is the discipline this beat has to apply to that hundred-billion figure. It is not remaining performance obligation, and it is not revenue. The S-1 defines total contract value as the aggregate revenue contracted across the full committed term of signed agreements, measured at signing. It excludes optional extensions and renewals. So it is a multi-year aggregate. A report citing documents seen by The Information puts the average contract at nearly five point seven years, or around 18 billion dollars of annualized contractual income.

That changes how you should hold the comparison. Setting the contracted total against six months of recognized revenue compares a multi-year figure to half a year. The gap is real, but it is not an annual conversion ratio. The cleaner statement is the split inside the filing itself, between what is labeled active and the far larger amount labeled active and contracted. The filing does not define those two terms. My reading is that active means live today and the rest is signed but not yet delivered. That is an inference, so weigh it that way.

So here is what the lead actually tells us. The pattern we have been describing did break, but it broke because a securities regime forced it, not because a vendor chose candor. And the number that emerged says most of this book is still a promise to deliver capacity.

That leads to concentration, which is the real exposure. Reporting says the largest customer was 52 percent of first-half revenue. The filing's risk-factor language warns that a limited number of customers drive a substantial portion of revenue. The customer is not named in anything I could verify, so I will not guess. What the filing does say is that its Anthropic services agreements provide for aggregate payments of up to approximately 44 point 6 billion dollars, for dedicated GPU infrastructure at the Monarch Compute Campus. Note the words up to. Press reporting adds that delivery and service-availability conditions attach to the big contracts, which is why I would not call take-or-pay unconditional.

Here is how I would read the loss and the concentration together. A one billion dollar half-year loss is a financing profile. It is the cost of holding capacity open before it earns. The single-customer share is a counterparty profile. It decides whether the financing profile matters. If delivery slips, the first question is not demand. It is revenue timing and covenants, and the delivery risk sits with Nscale, not the customer.

Two second-order points. The reported target valuation is about 30 billion dollars, but the deal has not priced, so that is a hope, not a mark. If it does price near there, neocloud peers get a public comparable that discloses losses and concentration, and capacity financing gets cheaper. It also puts the circular lab, hyperscaler and neocloud financing question on a filing calendar every quarter.

Which raises the question of who pays to build all this capacity. Oracle gave its answer on last week's earnings call, in its own words. Remaining performance obligations increased 26 billion dollars from the prior quarter. Management said, quote, the vast majority of those new contracts were via prepay or bring your own hardware or a similar mechanic, so will not require incremental capital from Oracle.

Set aside the backlog and cash-flow debate. The point here is structural. When customers prepay or supply their own hardware, the buyer is financing the supplier's growth. That is a transfer of risk to the customer. It makes hyperscale AI capacity look more like colocation plus operations. Capital intensity falls for the operator, and so does margin per dollar of contracted value. Concentration also gets relabeled as capital efficiency. Remember that this is management's framing on a call.

Now the other side of Oracle's disclosure, which I think is more important. The bear case says GPUs strand, that older fleets lose value faster than depreciation schedules assume. Oracle offered two data points against that. GPU utilization was 97 point 9 percent in the quarter. And of all the GPUs that came up for renewal, that capacity was renewed or resold at a 20 percent premium to prior contracts.

Give the bears their hearing. A renewal premium priced in a supply-short market tells you about scarcity today, not about a full depreciation life. Very high utilization is a capacity-constraint statistic as much as a demand statistic. A fully booked fleet cannot take a new customer, which is part of why prepay and bring-your-own-hardware exist at all. The upshot is that the stranded-asset thesis has not been proven wrong. It has only failed to show up while capacity is scarce.

If capacity is prepaid and software is metered, the buyer's next problem is knowing what it is consuming. That brings us to Cisco. At Splunk's dot conf event, Cisco announced Splunk Agent Observability with new Tokenomics capabilities. Cisco says that gives organizations a real-time view into agent performance and AI token spend.

AI FinOps was always going to become a purchased category. Here it already has a product name. Cisco also announced Cisco AI POD for Splunk, which it says brings self-managed AI to on-premises, private cloud and air-gapped environments. The launch partners are Accenture, bitsIO, Wipro and World Wide Technology.

Now the skepticism. Cisco disclosed no customer count, no pricing and no revenue impact. Systems integrators as launch partners are not customers. Only the AI POD is described as available. The rest is roadmap, with Agent Launchpad later in the year. And the thesis that agents create more telemetry is convenient for a company paid on data volume. So ask plainly whether Tokenomics is a new capability or cost telemetry with a new label.

The second-order effect is what matters. Whoever measures the token bill sits between the enterprise and every model vendor it buys from. That makes it contested ground among observability vendors, cloud consoles and gateways. If token spend becomes a governed budget line, buyers gain leverage at renewal, and pricing gets pushed toward a cost curve instead of a capability curve.

One last item, which lands the thesis from the other end. On September 16th, Cohere and Aleph Alpha signed a definitive merger agreement. The combination is reported at around 20 billion dollars, and it is pending regulatory clearance. SiliconANGLE reports that Cohere's revenue reached 240 million dollars last year, and that Germany's Schwarz Group planned five hundred million euros in financing. Other outlets report different financing terms, so treat the structure as unsettled.

That reported price sits far above disclosed revenue. It rests on sovereignty and procurement politics, not on recognized dollars. It is the same gap as the lead, priced in equity instead of capacity. Public-sector buying cycles are slow, and none of the wins that would justify the number has been announced.

So pull the week together. The gap between contracted stories and booked dollars finally appeared on paper. It appeared because a regulator required it, and even then it needed care to read, because contract value spans years. Two things to watch. First, whether Nscale actually prices, and what more the final prospectus tells us about conversion from contracted to active. Second, whether any agent vendor publishes realized revenue instead of a count.
