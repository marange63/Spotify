Good morning. It's Wednesday, August 26th, and this is Cautious Optimism Briefings. Four instruments of state power are in play today, and in almost every one of them, the authority is being held rather than spent. Watch that pattern as we go.

Start with the Federal Reserve, because today is the deadline in the renewed attempt to remove Governor Lisa Cook. Back on June 29th, the Supreme Court ruled five to four on this fight. SCOTUSblog summarized the holding this way: Cook can continue to remain in her job while her challenge to the effort to fire her moves forward. Note what that does and does not settle. The Court did not decide whether the underlying mortgage-fraud allegations, which Cook denies, amount to legal cause for removal. It decided only that she was owed notice and a brief chance to respond before any removal could take effect.

That's why today matters, and why the obvious reading of it is backwards. On August 5th, presidential aide Dan Scavino sent Cook a letter citing that opinion. The letter gave her until today, August 26th, to submit evidence and argument. Most people will treat that deadline as a hurdle for the administration to clear. It's the opposite. Satisfying the Court's process requirement is what makes a removal legally available in the first place. Today isn't a decision point. It's the last box on a checklist the Court itself handed the White House.

Be careful about what has actually happened, though. As of this recording there is no confirmation that Cook has filed her response, and no confirmation that a new termination notice has issued. Both are same-day developments. Cook's attorneys have vowed to challenge any renewed removal effort as pretextual, and the merits question the Court left open is still entirely unlitigated.

The stakes are concrete. The Fed's own calendar puts the next policy meeting on September 15th and 16th. Absent a further court order, Cook remains a seated, voting governor for it. So the real risk here isn't one seat's vote. It's precedent. If this process produces a removal that survives challenge, it becomes the template for reaching any independent agency head — the FTC, the FCC, the Fed itself — outside the normal confirmation calendar. That's a channel that shows up in term premium and in demand for hard-money hedges like gold and Bitcoin over time, not as a single day's market move. There is no pricing data tied specifically to today's deadline, so treat that as a mechanism to watch, not an event that has already registered. And note the cheaper option the White House retains: an indefinite posture of considering removal costs nothing, while an actual notice buys a lawsuit.

That same distinction, between authority granted and authority used, runs straight into the power grid, where a regulator has just put a price on it. PJM, the largest grid operator in the country, has proposed a rule. Any new large load, including AI data centers, that hasn't secured its own generation by June 1st, 2027 would be curtailed ahead of other emergency measures. We've tracked that framework since PJM took it to federal regulators on August 13th. What's new is the money.

PJM's independent market monitor, Joseph Bowring, has attached a dollar figure to who is actually paying for the data center boom. In PJM's most recent capacity auction, data centers accounted for thirty-eight percent of total capacity charges. That is from a customer class that barely registered a few years ago. Widen the lens to the last four auctions and the share rises to forty-six percent, nearly half of everything charged. Bowring's own words: "You have to open your eyes and recognize that it is really a paradigm shift, and failing to do that imposes costs on other customers."

That reframes the whole debate. Reliability arguments are abstract. A bill with your name on it is not. Once a specific share of the cost attaches to who pays, state legislatures get a reason to act that a reliability warning never gave them. Expect that to surface as state-level cost-allocation bills, rules that assign capacity costs to the class that drove them, and expect it well before federal regulators finish with PJM's curtailment filing. That's what makes this politically durable in a way the earlier proposal wasn't. It also pushes capital allocation directly. Hyperscalers now have a hard incentive to bring their own gas turbines, small reactors, or batteries rather than lean on the interconnection queue. That favors independent power producers with fast-track generation over pure compute plays.

Two caveats, because this number is doing a lot of work. It is an attribution, not a metered bill. The monitor had to decide which load drove the capacity requirement, and no counterfactual auction exists to check it against. And there's a sharper question underneath his own figure. Set that against Wood Mackenzie's estimate that grid operators will firmly commit to only about twenty-eight percent of requested data-center load. If roughly three quarters of that load never energizes, then part of what ratepayers are already funding is reserve margin for data centers that may never be built. That is a heavier indictment than the one Bowring is making, and it cuts against hyperscaler forecasting and PJM's own planning at the same time. Remember too that the self-supply deadline is still a board-directed proposal headed to federal regulators, not an approved rule, so the terms can still move.

From a bill someone else is paying, to a weapon nobody has actually fired. The same dynamic shows up in Russia policy. On August 7th the Senate passed the Lindsey Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act, eighty-six to eleven. It authorizes the president to impose tariffs of up to one hundred percent on the top five buyers of Russian oil or natural gas, named as India, China, Azerbaijan, Hungary, and Slovakia. That is a striking bipartisan margin. But a bill that hands the actual decision to presidential discretion costs a senator very little to vote for. It still hasn't passed the House, and the waiver authority built into it is broad enough that House Democrats have argued it could blunt enforcement entirely. So treat the tariff as authorized, not imposed. And note that India and China are exactly the two counterparties the administration is least likely to hit at full strength while it is simultaneously managing chip-export policy with Beijing.

Set that legislative track against what is actually happening on the ground, because it's the sharper story. Russian oil refining fell to three point six million barrels a day in July. That is the lowest level since May of 2002, on data from EA Analytics reported by Bloomberg — a secondary chain rather than an official Russian or IEA print, so hold it as a well-sourced estimate. The cause is not sanctions. Ukraine struck eighteen refineries in a single month, including the Omsk plant, Russia's largest, sitting more than fifteen hundred miles from the Ukrainian border. Drones did more measurable damage to Russian refining capacity in one month than the sanctions architecture built for that purpose has managed in years. The authorization sits in Washington. The effect is already on the ground in Siberia.

One second-order wrinkle worth flagging. Less Russian refining doesn't automatically starve Moscow of revenue, because Russia can still export the crude it can no longer process. What it does is tighten refined-product markets globally. So watch diesel cracks, not the price of Brent.

Briefly, the same authorized-versus-exercised ladder closes with a move against Europe. On July 24th, China's Commerce Ministry placed fourteen EU-based entities on an export watchlist, including the defense manufacturer Rheinmetall. Chinese suppliers now need special authorization to ship them rare earths and permanent magnets. That is Beijing's reciprocal answer to the EU's own sanctions on Chinese firms tied to Russia, and it lands squarely on European defense manufacturing just as NATO members are raising spending targets. What has not happened in the weeks since is any published accounting of volumes actually blocked, and that absence is the tell. This looks like a licensing lever held over a defense supply chain rather than an embargo, and a lever like that is worth more to Beijing unused than spent. Note too that the near-term bite is concentrated in magnets and compound semiconductors, not silicon logic chips.

So the throughline today. A removal threat held rather than executed. A curtailment right proposed rather than approved by federal regulators. A hundred-percent tariff authorized rather than imposed. And a rare-earth watchlist that, unlike the others, actually has been exercised — just against Europe rather than the United States. Two things to watch from here: whether a new termination notice against Cook actually issues, and whether federal regulators act on PJM's curtailment framework.
