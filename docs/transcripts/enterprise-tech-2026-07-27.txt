Good morning. It's Monday, July 27th, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

Last week's enterprise-software story was the AI capex bill: debt-financed data centers, negative free cash flow, the market starting to ask hard questions about financing quality. This week the bill that's coming due is quieter, and it belongs to legacy vendors, not hyperscalers. It starts with a security liability that finally has a name.

On July 21st, Oracle published its July Critical Patch Update, a cumulative release that permanently closes a vulnerability chain in PeopleSoft PeopleTools. The core flaw, CVE-2026-35273, carries a CVSS score of 9.8 out of 10. Oracle's own advisory describes it plainly: the vulnerability is remotely exploitable without authentication, and if successfully exploited, may result in remote code execution. That is about as bad as it gets for enterprise software sitting on the open internet. Between May 27th and June 9th, the extortion group ShinyHunters used exactly this hole to breach PeopleSoft servers. Google's Mandiant unit has now notified more than 100 global organizations whose systems correlated with vulnerable endpoints; ShinyHunters itself claims 300 compromised instances across those organizations. Oracle actually moved fast on disclosure, issuing a rare out-of-band alert on June 10th, the day after the group began publishing stolen data, well ahead of this month's routine patch cycle. Google Cloud's own threat intelligence team adds a striking detail: 68 percent of the notified organizations were higher-education institutions, universities and colleges concentrated in one sector.

On Thursday, IBM's CEO blamed a chunk of his company's revenue miss on clients pausing deals to reassess their cybersecurity posture. That was a vague, industry-wide framing. This week it has a CVE number and a victim list. PeopleSoft is core HR and finance infrastructure at hundreds of universities and enterprises; when a flaw like this surfaces, IT budgets don't just absorb an incident-response cost, they freeze other planned purchases while security teams triage. That's the distraction becoming concrete.

There's an irony worth sitting with. In the same window as this patch, Oracle booked a win too: a roughly seven-billion-dollar Pentagon licensing deal, which this show covered last week. The win column and the liability column moved on the same balance sheet, days apart. That's a reminder that legacy, on-premise license revenue carries a security tail risk that cloud-native ERP vendors argue they've engineered away. And that concentration in higher education is the part to watch. When two-thirds of the victims sit in one vertical, the fallout doesn't stay diffuse; it clusters. Universities running on-prem PeopleSoft for payroll and student records now have a board-level reason to revisit what's still on the old stack, and that is precisely the opening a cloud-native ERP vendor like Workday works. Expect the next wave of higher-education modernization RFPs to lean harder on the "we've engineered this class of tail risk out" pitch. It won't show up in bookings this quarter, but breaches like this are how multi-year migration decisions get triggered.

One caveat, because precision matters here. Reporting also points to roughly ten additional flaws rated a full 10.0 in this same patch cycle, across WebLogic Server Proxy Plug-in, Oracle HTTP Server, and related Fusion Middleware products — that figure now has two independent secondary sources behind it, though Oracle's own advisory page wasn't directly accessible to confirm it. One of those flaws, in WebLogic, has been described elsewhere as under active probing. But the evidence on timing doesn't hold up: proof-of-concept code for that flaw went public back in January, and exploitation activity traces to January through March, months before this month's patch update. So this isn't a fresh, this-week exploitation story — it's an old wound Oracle is only now formally closing. The signal that does hold is the shape of the patch cycle itself: a legacy middleware install base that keeps generating critical, unauthenticated flaws is a recurring attack surface, and each cycle nudges enterprises toward migrating off it entirely, not just patching in place.

This same class of enterprise compromise is no longer just a data problem. Coca-Cola disclosed that its Fairlife dairy subsidiary, a roughly four-billion-dollar brand, discovered unauthorized access to its production systems on July 16th in a ransomware event, forcing a temporary halt of all US Fairlife output. The Anubis group claimed responsibility. Coca-Cola says the scope is still undetermined and product safety hasn't been affected, and the terabyte of stolen data Anubis claims is the attacker's own assertion, not a confirmed figure. Still, the plain fact stands: enterprise-system compromise now stops physical production, not just leaks data. That's an operational-technology story, and it's a tailwind for security budgets independent of any AI narrative. When a breach idles a factory rather than just exposing a database, the spend shifts toward network segmentation and OT-specific security — the kind of budget that grows whether or not a company has an AI roadmap.

Now, one layer up the stack, buyers are repricing risk and AI is commoditizing the middle of the software stack — and that's showing up as consolidation. Progress Software agreed on July 22nd to buy substantially all of Domo's AI and data platform assets for 400 million dollars in cash, financed with cash and its existing credit revolver. The deal brings over 2,400 business customers into Progress's data platform. Domo was once a prominent standalone business-intelligence vendor that never reached durable scale against Microsoft's Power BI or Salesforce's Tableau, and as AI-native analytics agents become a checkbox feature inside bigger data platforms, standalone dashboarding loses its reason to exist independently. Four hundred million dollars is the market's verdict on that category. It's also a useful counter-example to the hyperscaler capex arms race: Progress is playing a roll-up game, buying capability rather than out-spending competitors on infrastructure. If AI-native query agents keep hollowing out the dashboarding layer, Domo won't be the last sub-scale BI name to get absorbed into a larger portfolio rather than fight for independent scale.

The same discipline is showing up in developer platforms. Atlassian has rolled a Jira Coding Agent into its existing paid Jira Cloud plans at no extra charge — it converts tickets directly into ready-to-review pull requests, plugging into Claude Code, Cursor, and GitHub Copilot, with OpenAI's Codex coming. Alongside it, Atlassian shipped cost-management tooling that unifies spend and token usage across those third-party tools. GitLab moved in the same direction on July 16th, releasing version 19.2 with automated dependency-scanning remediation and a security review flow explicitly framed around governed AI that keeps humans in the loop. Neither company is charging a premium for the agent itself. Instead, both are competing on cost visibility and accountability — because the question enterprise buyers are asking isn't "can it code," it's "what will it cost, and who's responsible for what it does." That's incumbents using their existing billing relationships to control the pace of agentic AI adoption, rather than ceding that ground to standalone coding-agent startups. And it squeezes those startups from two sides: the pure-play cost-monitoring tools risk being absorbed into the platform as a free feature, while the standalone coding agents that don't already plug in, the way Cursor and Copilot do here, are left competing on raw model quality alone against incumbents who own the workflow and the invoice. These are still feature launches, not adoption data, so don't read confirmed uptake into them — but the strategic direction is clear.

So the through-line for the week: the AI-capex debate settled itself last week on the question of financing quality. This week's signal is where the money is actually moving on the ground — away from unmanaged legacy security risk, and toward vendors that own the security, consolidation, and cost-governance layers of the stack.

That's the briefing for today. Have a good week ahead.
