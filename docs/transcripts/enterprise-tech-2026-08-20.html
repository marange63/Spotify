Good morning. It's Thursday, August 20th, 2026, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

We're leading with a security story that's really a governance story. On August 18th, Microsoft shipped a patch for a Copilot vulnerability Varonis Threat Labs calls CoSnitch — a chained exploit in Microsoft Copilot Personal that let a single click on a crafted link silently pull data from a victim's connected accounts, including email, drive, and calendar. Varonis reported it to Microsoft in December of last year. That's an eight-month gap between disclosure and patch.

Here's how the chain worked. An injected prompt could query any service the user had already authorized, encode what it found, and then use Copilot's own built-in URL-fetch tool to send that data to an attacker-controlled webhook. In other words, the attack surface wasn't a new backdoor — it was the permissions the user had already granted. And it got worse from there: the exploit could poison Copilot's persistent memory store, and Varonis found that injection survives password changes, session revocation, and even device re-enrollment. The standard incident-response playbook doesn't clear it. NVD has now confirmed a CVSS base score of 8.8, rated high severity, sourced from Microsoft's own submission.

To be clear about what this is not: Varonis says it has seen no evidence this was exploited in the wild. This is a disclosed and patched flaw, not a confirmed breach. But the pattern matters more than any single bug. Varonis's own writeup states plainly that CoSnitch is the third Copilot flaw it has found this year, after Reprompt, which bypassed guardrails just by asking twice, and SearchLeak, a covert exfiltration channel in Microsoft 365 Copilot Enterprise. Three disclosures in one product line in eight months is not a technical curiosity. It's evidence that agent capability is outrunning the security-review cycle. The durable lesson for enterprise buyers isn't about this specific bug — it's that any AI assistant with broad connector access to email, drive, and calendar carries this exposure by design, not just Microsoft's. Expect procurement teams to start demanding tighter OAuth scoping defaults and independent red-teaming as contract terms, not nice-to-haves.

That expanding attack surface is also, mechanically, a demand driver for security vendors — which brings us to the other side of this story. Wells Fargo's channel survey of cybersecurity resellers found partner spend running a net 31% above plan, with weighted year-over-year growth accelerating to 16%, up from 13% the prior quarter. That's a bullish read heading into CrowdStrike's report on August 26th and Palo Alto Networks' report on September 1st. And yet, on August 19th, in the same session Truist raised its CrowdStrike price target to $245, CrowdStrike stock fell 7% to $199, and Palo Alto Networks fell 5% to $355.54. Both remain up sharply for the year — CrowdStrike 82%, Palo Alto Networks 103% — so this reads as broad software-sector selling into high multiples, not a company-specific verdict on demand.

Worth being skeptical here on both sides. The channel-check numbers are a single broker's survey of resellers, not disclosed company results — treat 31% and 16% as sentiment, not booked revenue. And neither company has actually reported this quarter yet, so any figure you hear attached to "this quarter" right now is guidance, not an outcome. The August 26th and September 1st prints are the actual test: if they confirm what the channel checks suggest, agentic AI's expanding attack surface converts from a narrative into real bookings growth. If they miss despite strong channel checks, that points to deal-timing or backlog slippage in large platform contracts, not to companies losing customers.

There's a more specific read-through underneath that sector number, and it ties straight back to the Copilot flaw. A vulnerability that queries already-authorized connectors and survives credential rotation is exactly the kind of exposure that traditional endpoint and network tools weren't built to see. That's the opening the platform security vendors are selling into — agent-aware monitoring and data-loss prevention that watches what an AI assistant does with the permissions it already holds, not just what crosses the network perimeter. CrowdStrike, Palo Alto Networks, and SentinelOne are all positioning for that layer. So the same expanding attack surface that makes CoSnitch a governance problem for Microsoft is a product roadmap for its security rivals. That's why the channel checks and the earnings prints matter beyond the two names that happen to report first — they're the early read on whether AI-agent security becomes its own line item in enterprise budgets.

Now the other side of the agentic-AI trade — not securing it, but billing for it. ServiceNow's Action Fabric is the integration layer any external AI agent, including Anthropic's Claude, has to pass through to read data or execute workflows inside ServiceNow. It meters usage in a unit called "assists," charged on top of a customer's existing subscription once their included allotment runs out. Anthropic is the launch design partner, connecting Claude Cowork directly into ServiceNow's governed system of action. The catch, and the reason analysts are pushing back, is that assist consumption per interaction isn't predictable in advance, which makes forecasting the bill genuinely difficult for finance teams.

This extends a pattern we flagged with Salesforce's Agentforce telemetry earlier this week: incumbent SaaS platforms are shifting from flat per-seat licensing toward metering agent output directly. That uncaps revenue per customer for the vendor, but it transfers budgeting risk to the buyer. If enterprises can't forecast what an agent rollout will cost month to month, that's a real brake on how fast finance departments will approve scaling it beyond pilots. Watch which vendors respond by offering committed-spend tiers or hard usage caps — because in a metered world, predictability itself becomes a feature buyers will pay for, and the platform that removes the budgeting anxiety may win the rollout even if its per-assist rate is higher.

It's worth marking the difference between metering agent work and proving anyone is actually doing it. Most vendor AI claims stop at a feature announcement. A rarer thing is a disclosed production-usage number, and Atlassian gave one this month. In its fiscal fourth quarter, reported August 6th, Atlassian said the MCP server and Teamwork Graph interface that lets AI agents query its work-context data crossed one million monthly active users, more than doubling in a single quarter. That's two weeks old, so treat it as context rather than today's news. But it's the kind of hard adoption figure — agents actually reaching a system of record at scale — that the ServiceNow and Salesforce toll-collector strategies are betting will compound. The pricing model only pays off if the usage underneath it is real.

Step back and there's one thread running through all of today's stories. A marketed number sitting above a harder one underneath. Copilot's capability marketing sat above an eight-month, unresolved security debt. Bullish channel checks sat above a same-day stock selloff. And "unlimited" agent access to your systems of record sits above a metering bill nobody can fully predict yet. The common test in each case is the same: does the number that gets reported next — the patch record, the earnings print, the actual invoice — hold up against the one that got marketed first.
