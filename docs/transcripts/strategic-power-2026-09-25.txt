Good morning. It's Friday, September 25th, 2026.

Today's thread: Washington and Beijing bought time this week, and neither of the two real chokepoints in this contest moved. Chinese rare-earth flows and American generating capacity are both being managed by deadline and by rationing, not relieved by new supply.

Start with the summit. Yesterday this beat said to watch the Trump-Xi meeting for anything with a number or a date attached. We got a date. As Xi Jinping arrived in Washington, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent announced on Fox News that the Busan Agreement, the truce reached last October, will be extended. It had been scheduled to end on November 10th. It now runs to January 10th, 2027. That is a two-month push on an existing deal, not a new one.

And in the same announcement, Bessent noted pointedly that Beijing still needed to fulfill more deliverables. That is the US Treasury Secretary putting a complaint on the record inside his own extension announcement. Bloomberg and CNBC both report that the meeting produced no detailed list of tariff cuts, and no firm new commitments on rare-earth supply or advanced chips. Taiwan and Iran were discussed without resolution. One Gulf News headline claimed a rare-earth supply lifeline. Bloomberg and CNBC don't corroborate it, so treat the cautious reading as the operative one. No White House or Chinese commerce ministry text on the terms has surfaced, so all of this rests on press reporting.

Bloomberg's framing is that neither side could afford a fight. There is a less comfortable reading of the same facts. Both sides may simply be running out the clock to the US midterms, and neither wanted to be bound before then. Either way, a truce that rolls forward two months at a time is not safer than a cliff. It turns one tail event into a recurring one, and it keeps chip and mineral supply chains in permanent policy limbo. So what actually happened is a truce on tariffs, not a settlement on the inputs that matter.

That brings us to the one concrete thing US officials did say. Bloomberg reports their view that Chinese rare-earth deliveries are still falling short of earlier commitments. Before the summit, on September 20th, a senior official called that performance not up to par. After the summit, the complaint is unchanged. It survived the meeting. Remember that US officials are a party to this negotiation, so the complaint is a bargaining position as well as a claim, and no tonnage has been put on it.

An Oxford Economics China economist, quoted by CNBC, reads the shortfall as deliberate. In that economist's words, Beijing has deliberately kept license approvals slow and selective, and appears willing to test the limits of the truce. That describes an active lever, not a logistics lag. And the customs data show what that lever looks like in practice.

China's August export figures, the most recent available, come from a trade-data analysis rather than a Chinese government release. They show US-bound rare-earth shipments falling again in August, and still well below pre-control levels. Shipments to Japan stayed at zero for a third straight month. That follows additional controls Beijing placed on Japan specifically in January. And shipments to South Korea actually exceeded pre-control levels. That made Korea the largest destination in the world for China's rare-earth compounds and metals.

That is not an embargo. It is differentiated treatment, country by country. One read is that Beijing is deliberately splitting the allied response. It rewards Korea while squeezing Japan and the United States. That puts the cost of China's leverage on specific allies, and it hands Beijing a bargaining chip in every bilateral relationship. A rationing regime like that is harder to answer diplomatically than a blanket ban, because the allies no longer share the same grievance.

A competing read is that Korea's role as a magnet and battery processor, re-exporting into third markets, explains some of that gap on its own. So treat the wedge story as a judgment, not a proven fact. And recall from Tuesday that the April 2025 controls were never part of the pause in the first place.

There's a market consequence, and this part is analysis rather than reporting. January 10th is now the next dated event risk for chip- and rare-earth-exposed supply chains. And if the truce keeps rolling in short increments, non-China magnet and separation capacity stays economically justified whatever the truce status. That favors building reshored capacity over waiting for a thaw. The one caution is that this August data predates the summit, so it can't yet tell us whether anything changes now that the clock has moved to January.

Rare earths are the input Washington can't make fast enough. Electricity is the one it can't build fast enough, and on Thursday we had Texas freezing data-center permits. America's largest grid operator, PJM, is now running into the same wall.

PJM's regular capacity auction for 2028 and 2029 delivery cleared at $325 per megawatt-day. That is the FERC-approved price cap, the maximum the market allows. At that ceiling price, it procured only 525 megawatts of new generation and uprates. Against PJM's own reliability requirement, that leaves a shortfall of 6,831 megawatts, roughly thirteen times what the auction actually attracted in new build. When a market pays its ceiling price and still gets almost nothing new, price is not the constraint holding supply back.

PJM's board chair, Paula Conboy, said waiting until 2027 for the next centralized procurement, given the pace of demand from new large loads, presents a heightened reliability risk. Large loads means data centers. So PJM moved up an emergency Reliability Backstop Procurement. The bid window opens September 30th and closes October 21st. Winners get fifteen-year capacity contracts, a term the regular market does not offer. Results are due in early December, so none of that 6,831-megawatt gap is secured yet.

On the same September 30th date, FERC's deadline lands. The commission has told PJM to adopt governance and stakeholder reforms by then, or FERC will impose its own. Its chair says PJM is facing a grave legitimacy crisis. Whether PJM meets that deadline is unknown as of this recording.

Now the skeptical turn. A fifteen-year contract is an admission that the one-year capacity product can't finance new build. But a longer contract fixes a financing problem. It does nothing for interconnection queues, permitting timelines, or turbine lead times. If the cap couldn't pull generation forward, it's an open question whether contract length alone will. There is also the same caution this beat applied to Texas's queue. Some of the projected shortfall may reflect optimistic load forecasts rather than confirmed demand.

My read, and it is analysis, is that December is the test. If the backstop under-clears too, it points to interconnection, permitting and equipment as the binding limit. That reads straight across to data-center timelines in PJM territory, and to independent power producers and gas-turbine suppliers, who hold the scarce assets. So America's largest grid is now filling its adequacy gap by emergency contract and regulatory ultimatum, not by its own market design.

Energy leverage cuts the other way in the Russia-Ukraine war. Ukraine's long-range drone campaign has pushed Russian oil production to a seventeen-year low. And this week the campaign produced the damage detail this beat said was missing yesterday. A major Moscow-area refinery, which processed roughly 230,000 barrels a day in 2024, halted crude processing after the strike around September 20th. That is 2024 throughput, not a measured loss today, and no restart timeline is known.

At the UN General Assembly on September 23rd, President Zelenskyy repeated his conditional offer. Ukraine would stop hitting Russian refineries and diesel if Russia stops hitting Ukraine's infrastructure. That same day, Russia struck two Kyiv petrol stations with jet-powered drones, killing one person and injuring at least seven. It was the sixth Ukrnafta station hit in recent weeks. A three-day truce collapsed almost immediately back in May. So the offer is a negotiating position, not a ceasefire in force. The side actually removing capacity right now is Kyiv, and it will trade that leverage only for reciprocity it hasn't yet seen.

So three chokepoints, all managed this week by clocks rather than by new supply. Watch September 30th, when PJM's backstop window opens and FERC's governance deadline lands on the same day. Watch whether September's rare-earth customs data show any change in China's country-by-country rationing after the summit. And watch January 10th, 2027, when tariffs, chips, and rare earths all come back into question at once. None of these problems got smaller this week. They got scheduled.
