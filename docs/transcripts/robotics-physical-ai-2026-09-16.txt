Good morning. It's September 16th, and this is your briefing on robotics and physical AI.

Humanoid robotics is now running on two clocks, and yesterday both of them ticked. One clock reads a date eighteen months out. The other is a factory that has already cut its ribbon. Start with the date.

Agility Robotics unveiled Digit 5 yesterday, its first humanoid designed to work without a safety cage around it. Skip the spec sheet and go straight to the number that moves the unit-economics needle. That number is battery. Digit 5 runs 90 minutes on a charge and refills fully in nine minutes. That is a ten-to-one run-to-charge ratio, up from two-to-one on the previous generation, and Agility says it supports more than 20 hours of work in a 24-hour day. Utilization is the denominator in every payback calculation this sector runs. This is the largest move on that denominator any humanoid maker has disclosed this month.

The second lever is the cage. Digit 5 carries continuous 360-degree people detection and an independent safety controller. The robot avoids, stops, or sits down depending on how close a person gets. Bloomberg calls that a milestone for an industry that normally separates robots from human workers. It matters because floor space and workflow redesign, not dexterity, have been the real brake on humanoid deployment inside existing plants. A robot that needs no cage can go into a brownfield facility rather than only a purpose-built one. That is a change in addressable footprint, not just in capability.

Now the callback, because this beat has been tracking Agility's paper trail. On September 12th we read Agility's registration statement for its merger with Churchill Capital Corp the eleventh: 1.78 million dollars of net sales last year against a 140 million dollar operating loss, plus a going-concern qualification. The company's own language says its cash will not fund operations for at least a year from the date those statements were issued. Attached to that filing was an order book north of 300 million dollars. One sourcing caveat, same as on the twelfth. Those filing figures reach us through analyst and trade-press reading of the S-4, not from a passage read out of EDGAR here.

Yesterday's unveil is the resolution of that catalyst, and it resolves into a timing problem rather than a revenue event. Follow-up reading of the filing sharpens what the order book is. It is not a diversified customer base. It is a single unnamed related-party customer, on a three-year robots-as-a-service contract for a thousand Digit v5 robots. The commitment is conditioned on milestones and product specifications that have not been met. It also carries a warrant sweetener of roughly 453,000 common-stock warrants, whose accounting treatment Agility says it is still evaluating. So the 300 million dollar headline reads closer to an option written against a related party than to a locked sales contract.

The product that number depends on now physically exists. But early access is planned for the first half of 2027, and general availability for the end of 2027. So a company carrying a going-concern disclosure has to fund roughly fifteen months of pre-revenue operation before any of those commitments can start converting into cash. It plans to do that on merger proceeds rather than on sales — more than 620 million dollars of expected gross proceeds, assuming no redemptions. Note the word expected. As of this run there is no report that the Churchill deal has closed; it still needs SEC review, a shareholder vote, and listing approval. The machine arrived well before the money can.

Two caveats before we move on. No independent safety body has been cited anywhere in the reporting on the cage-free claim — no ANSI or RIA standard conformity, no OSHA determination. The architecture is described by Agility and relayed by the trade press. It may hold up; it simply has not been tested by anyone outside the company. And that gap has a commercial owner. If cage-free co-location is real, the binding constraint moves from engineering to underwriting, because somebody has to price the liability of an uncertified 284-pound machine working next to people. The second-order read is a spending shift. Figure, Apptronik, Tesla and the Chinese cohort will be pressed to match this safety architecture, which pulls competitive budget toward safety controllers and sensing rather than toward hands and actuators. That is a different supply-chain story than the one this sector has been financed on.

That's one side's schedule. The other side skipped the schedule entirely. UBTech opened a humanoid factory in Liuzhou, China, yesterday. Fourteen thousand square meters, nameplate capacity of 10,000 units a year, and a claimed production rate of one industrial humanoid every ten minutes. It feeds a 65-square-meter automated warehouse holding 112 finished robots. The framing UBTech wants is robots building robots. The analytic content is different. Humanoid production capacity is becoming a strategic variable distinct from model capability, and it is being installed on one side of the Pacific now rather than promised for 2027.

Then discipline that number the way you would discipline any nameplate figure, because nameplate is the cheapest claim in manufacturing. No pricing, no orders tied to this facility, and no current output were disclosed. The ten-minute cycle time is company-stated and unverified. And this line builds the industrial Walker S and Cruzr products, not UBTech's separately reported consumer robot. Weigh the capacity against demand this beat has already measured. On September 10th we covered the largest disclosed humanoid order anywhere in the world, UBTech's own, at roughly 35 million dollars. Ten thousand units a year of capacity against order books that size does not read as a demand signal. It reads as a price war being built before the demand arrives.

So put the two clocks side by side. The Western leader discloses an order book and 2027 dates. The Chinese maker discloses floor space and cycle times. They are not being graded on the same metric, and that is worth remembering every time a headline compares them directly.

Now to the part of this sector actually doing quality-critical production work today, and it is on neither humanoid line. Skild AI, Nvidia and Foxconn are running dual-arm manipulators on a real assembly floor, using Skild's Brain to build Nvidia's Blackwell systems. The showcased task is installing a busbar and limit block, fastening sixteen screws, and adapting to disturbances mid-task. This beat graded Skild's capability claims on September 11th, so just the callback. Skild says its S1 model succeeds on new multistep tasks about 66 percent of the time per step, against 9 percent for a comparison system, and claims a hundred-million-dollar annualized revenue run-rate. Those are company claims, not independent benchmarks, and we already showed that a conventionally post-trained version of the same architecture beats the headline number.

What's new is the siting, and the siting is the story. This closes a loop where the compute buildout driving the entire AI capex cycle is itself being assembled by embodied AI. It also says where near-term value is accruing. It is accruing to a model layer riding on someone else's hardware, on someone else's factory floor — not to the humanoid makers. Watch the channel, too. This account reaches us through a blog post from Nvidia, which is simultaneously the chip supplier, the customer whose product is being assembled, and the beneficiary of the story. No unit count, no yield, no uptime and no cycle time were disclosed for that line.

And note who appears on both sides of today's briefing. Foxconn led the private placement in Agility's SPAC deal, and Foxconn hosts the factory running Skild's Blackwell line. When the same contract manufacturer is investor, host and customer, capacity gets allocated by ownership stake rather than by price. That is an edge for whoever the supply chain backs, and a cost problem for everyone else.

Pull the threads together. What genuinely inflected yesterday is utilization and siting, not reliability. Seven sessions into this beat, no vendor in physical AI has published a fielded reliability number: no uptime, no intervention rate, no cost per completed task. Yesterday added a battery ratio, a nameplate capacity and a deployment location. None of those is a reliability disclosure, and that gap remains the central open question here. Watch three things next. Whether the Churchill Capital merger actually closes, and whether Agility's related-party customer is ever named. Whether UBTech discloses real output against its nameplate. And November 10th, when China's suspension of its rare-earth export-control rules is due to lapse — a dated standing risk to Western actuator and motor costs, flagged now, with no figures attached until there is actual news.

That's the briefing. Thanks for listening, and I'll see you tomorrow.
