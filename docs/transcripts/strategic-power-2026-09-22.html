Cautious Optimism Briefings

Strategic Power, Energy, and Institutional Change

Transcript · September 22, 2026

Good morning. It's Tuesday, September 22nd, and this is your strategic power briefing.

Start with a number. Ninety-six dollars a pound. That is where UxC's long-term uranium price indicator sat at the August month-end, up two dollars on the month. TradeTech's rival long-term indicator ran a dollar higher, at ninety-seven. Both are term prices. That means the multi-year contracts utilities sign to lock in fuel years ahead, not the spot market. And the record is a nominal one. The prior high was ninety-five dollars, set in 2007. Adjust for nineteen years of inflation and today's print is comfortably below that. So the headline is real, but it is smaller than it sounds, and it belongs to the contract market rather than to today's trading screen.

Now the interesting part. The two agencies whose numbers define this market do not agree on why it is happening. TradeTech's own commentary blames buyers, not scarcity. In its words, quote, "Utilities recognize that there has been a fundamental shift in support for nuclear power and that, concurrent with a rise in electricity demand, demand for uranium and nuclear fuel is expected to climb." That is a demand story. Utilities are responding to a friendlier nuclear policy backdrop and to rising power-demand forecasts.

A competing read argues the reverse. It says producers are operating at minimal inventory with little left to sell. It also claims new utility purchasing has declined even as prices rise. That, it says, is the fingerprint of seller scarcity rather than buyer conviction. I cannot referee the dispute with what is on the record. TradeTech is a price-reporting agency publishing its own rationale. The inventory-exhaustion case is secondary commentary with no pricing data attached. And no public figure sizes utilities' uncovered requirements against annual mine production. So treat the mechanism as contested.

What is not contested is the producer response. Kazatomprom is the world's largest uranium producer. It has said plainly that no price will change its plan. The company confirmed this month that no uranium spot price would prompt it to accelerate or expand production. It also cut its 2026 nominal production capacity. The figure went from thirty-two thousand seven hundred seventy-seven tonnes of uranium to twenty-nine thousand six hundred ninety-seven. That is roughly eight million pounds off the plan. Note the word nominal. This is a planned capacity number, not realized output.

Hold both readings at once and the conclusion survives either way. Whether utilities are pulling the price up or thin inventory is pushing it up, the largest supplier on earth is choosing restraint over expansion. That is the actual signal. A record price that the marginal producer will not answer with more pounds.

So what is that fuel supposed to feed? The most-cited answer is a Tennessee Valley Authority small modular reactor program with ENTRA1 and NuScale. It is still paperwork. As one report on the deal put it, quote, "There's a non-binding agreement, but no power purchase agreement to buy nuclear energy or start the process of breaking ground for construction of the nuclear plants." No offtake, and no groundbreaking.

Meanwhile the load waiting on power keeps growing. Industry analysis, not the EIA and not a grid operator, puts US data-center electricity demand rising from twenty-three gigawatts in 2023 to forty-two gigawatts this year. Take that magnitude as directional. The same analysis puts wait times in the busiest markets at four to seven years. That is often longer than it takes to build the data center itself. So nuclear's contribution to the AI buildout this decade is currently a price, not a megawatt.

Two consequences follow, and both cut against the easy trade. The first is that a rising fuel cost lands on nuclear power purchase agreements before a single new reactor exists. A hyperscaler signing for nuclear power today is accepting input-cost escalation now, against megawatts that arrive at the end of the decade. That pushes the near-term bridge further toward gas turbines and behind-the-meter generation. The second is enrichment. A term price at a nominal record with a negative supply response is the strongest case yet for restarting Western conversion and enrichment capacity. But that is a multi-year capital cycle. It would be launched into the cost of capital this beat has tracked all week, with the ten-year Treasury near five percent. Money for slow physical assets has rarely been more expensive.

This is the same gap this beat has found all week in other mediums. Regulators conceding they cannot verify their own interconnection queues. Export-control authority that exists on paper and imposes nothing. Today it shows up priced in dollars per pound.

Now to the input where the constraint sits entirely outside American control. Rare earths. This beat has been treating November 10th as the date China's rare-earth export-control suspension expires, as if that were the day the tap could close. It is smaller than that framing suggests. Here the source is the legal summary tracking the measure, not any Chinese government notice this beat has seen. That summary says the suspension covers only the controls Beijing announced on October 9th, 2025. It explicitly does not touch the earlier April 2025 round. That round covers seven rare-earth elements and their derivative products. It has been enforced throughout the pause. So the tap the West has been watching was never the one holding back the flow.

Against that, the US-Australia critical minerals framework produced its first real capital move this month. The US Export-Import Bank issued Letters of Interest totaling more than two point two billion dollars to seven Australian critical-minerals companies. It is the bank's first minerals investment in over a decade. Be precise about what that is. A Letter of Interest is an expression of intent from an export-credit agency. It is not committed capital and not disbursed financing. And even fully drawn, it would mostly fund mining and early processing. That is not the separation and refining step where China's dominance actually sits.

So the shape of the Western answer is this. Real money starting to move, aimed at the easier third of the problem, while the controls that bind were never part of the pause. Read Beijing's suspension as controlled de-escalation rather than concession. It buys Western headlines about a thaw while the enforcement machinery keeps running. That drains exactly the political urgency that funds separation and magnet capacity. Detente and dependency can deepen together. Thursday's meeting between Trump and Xi is the next pricing event here, and nothing in this briefing predicts what comes out of it.

Last input, and the one where Washington, not Beijing, holds the lever. Compute. Nvidia's fiscal second quarter, reported in late August, showed revenue of eighty-nine billion dollars, up one hundred seventeen percent year over year. That is the headline revenue line, not a confirmed segment number. Alongside it the company said flatly that it, quote, "is not assuming any Data Center compute revenue from China in its outlook."

That is the clearest answer yet to a question this beat has been circling all week. Do export controls deny China compute, or do they just tax it? Nvidia's own guidance says the company has stopped modelling China sales at all, and it grew triple digits anyway. So the seller has formalized the loss. Reports that Huawei and Cambricon chip prices have jumped as much as fifty percent on grey-market memory sourcing are worth naming as a single reported claim, not a confirmed figure. But if that is directionally right, it points the same way. China is paying more for less rather than going without. That is a tax, not a denial.

And the second-order cost may not land on compute access at all. It may land on Beijing accelerating a subsidized domestic memory and advanced-packaging program. That is a larger strategic commitment than the chips it is currently missing, and it is the outcome the controls were meant to avoid.

Put the three together and one pattern holds across every input that decides who builds AI and defense capacity next. The price signals in fuel and minerals say scarcity. The parties that actually hold supply, Kazatomprom and Beijing's enforcement apparatus, are choosing restraint. And the Western response keeps arriving as financing and guidance rather than as capacity. Letters of Interest. A non-binding reactor agreement. A chipmaker updating its forecast.

Two things to watch. Whether a price-reporting agency publishes a firmer term-price print beyond these month-end snapshots. And whether anything from Thursday's meeting touches the April 2025 rare-earth controls at all, rather than just the suspension everyone has been watching.

That's the briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
