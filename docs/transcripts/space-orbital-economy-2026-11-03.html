Space & the Orbital Economy

Transcript · November 3, 2026

Good morning. It's Tuesday, November 3rd, and this is your space and orbital economy briefing.

The thread running through today is a shift in what counts as the scarce resource in orbit. It used to be getting there. Increasingly, it's controlling access once you're there, whether that access is physical, spectral, or regulatory.

Start with the physical version. This week we've covered two ways a commercial satellite gets caught in a war: Chinese lasers on Friday, and throttled Starlink service to Ukrainian drone units on Saturday. Today brings a third.

CSIS, drawing on commercial space-situational-awareness data, has documented a Russian satellite called Cosmos 2612 lining up on a commercial radar satellite that supports Ukraine. That satellite is operated by ICEYE, the Finland-founded radar-imaging company, and it is registered in the United States. This has been building for months. An earlier sibling, Cosmos 2609, closed to within two and a half kilometers of the same satellite on May 26th. Then, over two weeks beginning September 30th, Cosmos 2612 maneuvered to restore its orbital-plane alignment with it. Russia, for its part, has said it might treat commercial satellites supporting Ukraine as legitimate military targets. CSIS's conclusion is that policymakers should assume Cosmos 2612 could take an action that threatens the satellite.

Now push back on the alarm. CSIS's own language is conditional. Cosmos 2612 "could" act, and the operations "might" presage a weapon. That's a risk posture, not an attack. Close-proximity operations also have benign or ambiguous explanations: inspection, intelligence collection, rehearsal, or signaling. Signaling to commercial operators and their insurers may be the whole point, and it works without a shot fired. And the tracking data in hand runs only to October 15th, so what Cosmos 2612 has done since is unknown. This is a standoff in progress, not a single event.

Here's the desk's synthesis. The laser threat is answered by replaceability, meaning many cheap satellites. The vendor's switch is answered by supplier diversity. Patient stalking of one named asset is answered by neither. ICEYE's value sits in particular satellites serving particular contracts, and an adversary only has to match planes with those. The US registration matters here. It turns a Russia-Ukraine proximity operation into an American question about defending commercial assets. My inference is that Washington will eventually have to say what it owes a US-registered satellite working for one side of a war. For Earth-observation operators like ICEYE, Capella, Planet, BlackSky, and Maxar, the business model assumes uninterrupted low-orbit operation. When a key customer is fighting a war, that assumption is no longer free, and customer concentration becomes a risk the operator carries.

Ukraine also showed the other face of commercial connectivity as strategic infrastructure, and that face is the vendor, SpaceX. SpaceX is now reportedly trying to own the ground side of the phone connection too. On Monday, TechCrunch and other outlets, following the Washington Post, reported that SpaceX is in talks to buy EchoStar's Boost Mobile brand, or at least to gain access to the network Boost uses. These are reported talks, not a deal. No confirmation, denial, or filing from either company has surfaced. The same reporting says SpaceX first approached Verizon, AT&T, and T-Mobile about network access, and was turned down by all three. So Boost looks like the fallback route, not the first choice.

What would SpaceX actually be buying? EchoStar's second-quarter 10-Q is instructive. As of June 30th, EchoStar had about seven point four million wireless subscribers. That's down roughly a hundred and fifty thousand from three months earlier, so the base is shrinking. More important, Boost is not a self-built network that SpaceX could simply take over. The filing marks a change in Boost's network model on November 15th, 2025. Even before that date, Boost ran primarily as an MVNO on leased capacity, with its own network only secondary. Press coverage of the talks describes Boost today as running on network capacity leased from AT&T and T-Mobile. That's the obstacle the coverage flags. Those lease agreements may need the host carriers' consent to transfer to a new owner. If that consent requirement holds, two of the carriers that reportedly turned SpaceX down would get a say over its fallback route. So a deal would buy a licensed brand, a subscriber base, and carrier relationships, not independent infrastructure.

Keep that separate from SpaceX's existing EchoStar spectrum purchase. The FCC approved that transaction on May 12th. Per the same EchoStar filing, the final closing, when SpaceX actually takes the licenses, is targeted for November 30th, 2027. That's months later than the first-half-2027 close that had been reported. So even the spectrum SpaceX has already agreed to buy won't be in hand for roughly another year.

The market reaction tells you who feels threatened. AST SpaceMobile, the satellite-first direct-to-cell challenger, saw its stock dive eleven percent on the Boost news, according to Stocktwits. SpaceX's own shares reportedly rose on the same report. Here's the inference worth holding. If SpaceX gets terrestrial access, Starlink's direct-to-cell offer stops being a coverage add-on and starts to look like a carrier bundle. That widens SpaceX's competitive set from AST to the wireless carriers themselves. It also deepens the single-vendor concentration this desk flagged on Saturday. Direct-to-cell is becoming a spectrum-and-distribution fight, not a satellite-count race, and the balance sheet that can buy around the wait has the edge.

One beat on the hardware underneath that ambition. As of September 28th, Starship had flown fourteen times, with nine successes and five failures. Flight 15 will try to catch both the booster and the ship for the first time, and it still has no official date. Nine of fourteen is a test-campaign record, not a reliability rate. And a double catch would prove recoverability, not turnaround cost. The company that sets the industry's cost curve is still flying a test campaign.

If direct-to-cell is a distribution fight, so is Beijing's answer to Starlink. China flew another Guowang batch on October 25th, nine satellites on a Long March 8A. That came about two weeks after the 27th batch on October 9th. The launch report for that earlier batch counted eighty-six Guowang satellites deployed against three hundred ten planned for this year. Trackers disagree on those counts, so treat the gap as directional, but it is wide. The tracker Flight Atlas counts China at seventy-six of the world's two hundred fifty-seven orbital launch attempts this year, or about thirty percent. But launch count isn't mass, so don't read that as parity with SpaceX. And Qianfan, the more commercial Chinese constellation, has launched irregularly rather than on a steady monthly rhythm.

The firmer development for Qianfan is regulatory. Brazil's regulator has approved service from SpaceSail, Qianfan's operator, for up to three hundred twenty-four satellites. SpaceSail plans to begin commercial operation there this quarter. Our sources don't date the approval, but it upgrades what was a trial agreement into a licensed entry with a planned start date. Approval is permission, not deployment, and a planned start is not revenue. The counter-reading is that, for now, the value of China's constellations sits mostly in spectrum filings and geopolitical footholds. Brazil keeps recurring as the arena. AST won S-band spectrum there last week, Qianfan signed its trial there, and now there's this licence. In markets that don't have to pick a side, regulators and distribution decide the contest country by country. China is winning permissions before it wins orbit.

Pulling it together. What's genuinely inflecting is that commercial satellites are now both strategic targets and strategic levers. The scarce asset is access, orbital, spectral, and regulatory, rather than raw launch capacity. What's still a demo is any SpaceX-Boost transaction, SpaceSail's Brazilian service, and Starship's claim to operational status.

Now the catalysts. Watch for Starship Flight 15 to get an actual date. Watch Rocket Lab's third-quarter report next week. Neutron's debut slipped into 2027 after Rocket Lab found air pockets in the composite domes that cap its first-stage tanks. The question is whether that was a one-off or a process problem that could repeat across the production run. The shares fell twenty-five percent the week of that announcement, so the market wants the answer. Watch AST SpaceMobile's own earnings, now with a SpaceX overhang. Watch whether SpaceX or EchoStar confirms or denies the Boost talks. And watch whether Cosmos 2612 closes in.
