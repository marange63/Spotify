Good morning. It's September 3rd, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

Twice in the last four days, this show set a specific test for Broadcom. Would its next disclosure get priced to its calendar rather than its headline, and would it finally move its standing target of AI revenue exceeding one hundred billion dollars in fiscal 2027? Broadcom's fiscal third-quarter print, out Wednesday evening, answered both questions, and it answered them in a way nobody's playbook had. The company moved the target far higher. The stock fell anyway.

Start with what actually happened in the quarter. Broadcom's own release puts total net revenue at twenty-nine point six billion dollars, up 86 percent year over year. Inside that, AI semiconductor revenue came in at sixteen point seven billion dollars, up 221 percent year over year and 54 percent sequentially. On the call, management said that figure now represents 56 percent of total company revenue. AI silicon is now the majority of what this company sells. Worth separating that number from a second, larger one. The broader Semiconductor Solutions segment, which includes non-AI chips, ran twenty point eight billion dollars, up 127 percent year over year. So AI is the growth engine inside a segment roughly four billion dollars larger than the AI number alone. Two real, distinct figures, not competing labels for the same thing.

Now the turn, and this is the actual news. Broadcom guided fiscal fourth-quarter AI revenue to twenty-one point seven billion dollars, up 236 percent year over year, and total revenue to thirty-four point eight billion, up 93 percent. Both numbers are enormous. Both also landed slightly short of where Wall Street was sitting. Consensus wanted thirty-five billion and change. The operating margin guide is where it really stings: 66 percent, against a 66.5 percent consensus, on a quarter that had just delivered 68 percent. Half a percentage point, on a business tripling its AI revenue. Shares dropped about 5 percent in after-hours trading. The lesson isn't that Broadcom disappointed. It's that the market has stopped paying simply for AI revenue growth and started grading the quality of that growth.

Here's where I need to be careful, because the most-repeated explanation for that margin guide doesn't hold up. CNBC's coverage of the print said management called out higher memory content as the reason margins weren't stronger. But the closest primary-adjacent language from the call itself doesn't say that. What we can find management actually saying is that fourth-quarter operating margin should run approximately 66 percent, flat from a year ago, because strong revenue growth is driving substantial operating leverage. That is language about mix and leverage, not a named memory-cost drag. So the memory explanation is secondary reporting's interpretation, not a verbatim management admission, and I'm not going to hand it to you as more solid than it is. What is solid: margin came down on a richer, ASIC-heavy revenue mix, exactly the mix Broadcom is selling more of every quarter.

Take that one step further, because it generalizes beyond one company. Custom-silicon growth is structurally lower-margin than Broadcom's franchise networking business. The bill of materials is heavier, more of the value in each unit is bought-in rather than owned intellectual property, and the customers are three or four buyers with enormous negotiating leverage. If a market will punish fifty basis points on a business growing 221 percent, it will punish the ASIC-heavy suppliers hardest, because ASIC mix is precisely what dilutes margin. Expect that mechanism to be the recurring question for the rest of this earnings season, and expect it at Marvell, at Alchip, and at MediaTek as their custom revenue scales. Separate the dimensions rather than netting them. Broadcom gained on design-win share and on volume. It gained nothing on margin, and the margin it gave up is the price of the mix it is winning on.

On the other side of that margin miss sits the forward claim, and it's a big one. Broadcom's Hock Tan told the call he expects AI revenue to double next fiscal year to roughly one hundred fifteen billion dollars, then double again the year after to two hundred thirty billion. Rank that against what's actually contracted: an AI backlog near seventy-three billion dollars, disclosed last quarter and not confirmed as restated this quarter. So we have four different classes of number here. A delivered number, sixteen point seven billion. A guided number, twenty-one point seven billion. A backlog figure, seventy-three billion, that is a quarter old. And a management trajectory, one hundred fifteen and two hundred thirty billion, that is aspiration on a call, not booked revenue. Keep those four categories separate in your head, because the gap between the largest and the most solid of them is enormous.

The customer detail behind the trajectory is worth hearing. OpenAI's custom accelerator, the Broadcom-designed part known as Jalapeno, is expected to reach about one point three gigawatts of deployment in 2027, with line of sight past five gigawatts after that. Anthropic is expected to deploy roughly five gigawatts of Google's next-generation TPU capacity next year, with line of sight to ten gigawatts more. Two discounts apply here. Gigawatts measure power capacity, not chips shipped or dollars booked, the same caveat this show applied to AMD's commitments on Monday. And in both cases, Broadcom is narrating a third party's deployment schedule, not its own shipped product.

Whether those gigawatts can actually get built is a packaging question, and that's the real ceiling on the doubling claim. CoWoS is TSMC's advanced packaging process, the step that stacks memory onto AI accelerators. Its capacity is on track to grow from roughly thirteen thousand wafer starts a month at the end of 2023 to somewhere between one hundred twenty and one hundred thirty thousand by the end of this year. Call that close to a tenfold expansion in three years. And by the same estimates, those lines are still fully booked. I'll flag that these packaging figures come from industry-analyst estimates rather than a TSMC disclosure I could verify directly, so treat them as directional. But the conclusion holds either way. If capacity grows that fast and still sells out, then Broadcom doubling its AI revenue twice over isn't primarily a story about new packaging capacity appearing. It's a story about winning allocation away from Nvidia, AMD, and every other buyer bidding for the same finite slots. The same squeeze shows up one layer down in logic, where two-nanometer capacity is already allocated on lead times running into 2028.

Be precise about what that implies for Nvidia, because the two possible readings have very different timelines. If hyperscaler custom-silicon programs are compounding faster in percentage terms than merchant GPU sales, the pressure on Nvidia is a share-of-growth story, not a share-of-installed-base story. Nvidia's absolute AI compute revenue can keep rising while its share of each incremental packaging slot erodes. That is a slower, quieter form of competitive damage than losing customers, and it is much harder to see in a quarterly print. Which means the place to look for it is allocation commentary out of TSMC, not the revenue line at Nvidia.

One quick comparison before we close. Marvell, the other half of what's become a two-supplier custom-silicon field, struck its own warrant deal with Google in August. Up to nearly fifty-nine million shares at two hundred six dollars and fifty-eight cents apiece, a potential twelve-point-two-billion-dollar stake if fully exercised. That is a contingent ceiling, not a payment and not a booked order. Marvell got sold off then because its Google revenue was dated out to fiscal 2029. Broadcom just got sold off despite pulling its numbers forward and up. Both suppliers, in other words, are being marked down on the shape and timing of AI growth, not its size.

So here is the take-away. The design-win-as-good-news trade has stopped working, and the binding constraint on the doubling claim is packaging allocation, not demand. Carry one caveat with it: the biggest numbers in this story are also the least verified, from the hundred-fifteen-billion trajectory to the analyst-estimated packaging ceiling that bounds it. Watch two things from here. Whether Broadcom or its customers ever put a contracted dollar figure behind that two-year trajectory. And whether the margin softness widens or narrows when Broadcom reports again.
