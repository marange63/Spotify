Good morning. It's Thursday, November 26th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

The core of the AI-silicon stack still says supply is the constraint. The edges are where demand is starting to give. Today we walk that line from the foundry floor, out to the export perimeter, and into the memory market.

Start at the top of the stack. This week TSMC publicly pushed back on talk of a deeper AI correction. The company said global demand for AI "continues to grow, with its long-term outlook remaining upbeat." It added that demand for its leading-edge technologies "continues to be extremely robust." One more line is circulating alongside it, and it needs dating. C.C. Wei's remark that "the gap between demand and supply is still quite big" comes from October. The Taipei Times re-cited it. He didn't say it fresh this week. A supplier asserting robust demand is reassurance. It is not a new number. The closest thing to a number is last month's estimate from the Taiwan Semiconductor Industry Association. It sees output across Taiwan's chip industry growing more than forty percent this year, to about two hundred seventy-nine billion dollars. That figure is industry-wide and a month old. It is not TSMC guidance. The real test of TSMC's claim is its own reported revenue, which hasn't landed yet.

The more telling fact this week sits in a regulatory filing. A standing disclosure first: Anthropic makes the model behind this show. Per that filing, as reported by the Taipei Times, TSMC's investment subsidiary, TSMC Partners, bought just over six million Anthropic shares at eighty-three dollars apiece in Anthropic's IPO. By our own arithmetic, that's roughly half a billion dollars. That's small against TSMC's scale. And as this show noted yesterday, Anthropic's stock is trading near that same offer price, so the position is roughly flat. The significance isn't the money. It's the category.

Last Saturday, this desk laid out how the chip supply chain is increasingly financing its own demand. Fed Governor Barr had flagged that chipmakers like Nvidia often guarantee the resale value of pledged GPUs. Broadcom was offering Anthropic a loan facility against its TPU leases. Now a foundry, the most concentrated chokepoint in the stack, holds equity in one of its customers. The instruments are escalating, from guarantees, to credit, to equity. And that distinction matters. Credit exposure means you're owed money. Equity exposure means you share the customer's upside and its downside.

Two readings fit the evidence, and it doesn't pick between them. One is an aligned bet on a frontier lab's commercial success. The other is an ordinary IPO allocation that happens to cement a customer relationship. Either way, there's a question worth sitting with. TSMC's business model depends on serving every AI lab. What does it mean for that foundry to hold a financial stake in one lab's outcome? Here's our inference on the second-order effect. As suppliers take credit and equity positions in their customers, a stumble at one lab stops being just a lost order. It travels up the chain through balance sheets. Customer concentration risk starts to look like counterparty risk. At half a billion dollars, that exposure is immaterial to TSMC today. The so-what is direction, not size. The chokepoint's exposure to the AI cycle now runs through its balance sheet, not just its fabs.

TSMC says leading-edge demand is robust. The place where demand is actually being removed, by policy rather than economics, is the offshore China corridor. Last week, this desk covered the Commerce Department rule restricting Chinese firms' remote access to advanced Nvidia chips through third-country data centers. What's new is the first reported consequence. On Wednesday, CNBC, citing unnamed sources, reported that Nvidia's resellers are scrambling to find alternative buyers as that route closes. A White House official specifically accused the Chinese AI firm Moonshot AI of using a Thailand-based facility. None of the companies has confirmed the account. Separately, a bill called the Remote Access Security Act has passed the House but not the Senate. So it isn't law yet.

Remember that Nvidia's outlook already assumes zero data-center compute revenue from China. But that zero says nothing about Chinese firms renting capacity in other countries. So, on our read, revenue from this offshore route may still be counted inside Nvidia's guidance. If so, losing that route is a risk the guidance does not yet reflect. Nobody has sized it. Two readings compete here. In the first, this is a real hole in some vendors' non-China revenue, because that demand simply disappears. In the second, compute is still scarce, so those chips get re-placed with other buyers at little cost. What would settle the question is price. If displaced capacity clears quickly at full price, the supply-constrained thesis holds. If secondary-channel pricing softens, that's the first crack. Either way, the perimeter has moved from drawing lines to displacing demand.

That same Chinese demand shows up somewhere else this week: the memory forecast. In a release dated November 25th, TrendForce cut its 2027 DRAM industry revenue forecast to one point one five trillion dollars. That still implies sixty-nine percent growth year over year, but it is a downward revision. It's also the first forecast cut after two weeks in which this desk tracked rising memory-price projections, including TrendForce's own HBM4 price projections for 2027. TrendForce's framing is careful. It expects DRAM supply to "remain tight throughout 2027." But it says quarterly contract price increases "are projected to narrow progressively." On NAND, it's blunter. It lowered that forecast too, and expects NAND to "enter a downward pricing cycle as supply continues to expand."

Now the cause, which is where caution matters. Per TrendForce, some Chinese cloud providers began cutting their 2027 server-DRAM demand outside long-term supply agreements. The tempting story is that Washington's export controls are now leaking into commodity memory. The reporting doesn't say that. Per one news site's summary of the TrendForce research, the stated reasons are GPU shortages, heavy inventories, and a price war on AI tokens. Export rules aren't cited as the cause. That same summary treats looser export rules as something that could bring some orders back. That makes policy a reversal lever, not the named driver. Here's the nuance. A GPU shortage at Chinese clouds is plausibly where export policy enters, indirectly. That link is our own reading, not TrendForce's. Meanwhile, North American cloud providers have reportedly largely offset the lost bit demand. That blunts any read-through to overall DRAM tightness.

Set that against HBM, which remains capacity-bound. SK hynix plans to push its M15X fab in Cheongju to maximum output by year-end to supply HBM4. That's a report from early November, so treat it as context. SK hynix's own SEC filing cites IDC data putting it at fifty-six percent of HBM share in the first quarter. That's a stale number from an interested party. The mechanism behind the split also comes from TrendForce. HBM consumes three to four times the wafer resources of standard commodity DRAM. That's why ordinary DRAM supply stays tight even as its price growth slows. So this is a split cycle, not a broad memory downturn. HBM is structurally tight. Commodity DRAM is cyclically softening at the margin.

That revisits a call from last Saturday. When Micron's stock fell about thirteen percent last Friday, this desk weighed two readings. Either equity investors were capitalizing a cyclical peak, or the whole AI-silicon trade was de-risking at once. TrendForce's cut is the first datapoint outside the stock market that leans toward the cyclical-peak reading. But it applies to commodity DRAM, and Micron sells HBM too. So the split cycle is the honest frame for memory equities as well.

So, the scorecard, dimension by dimension. Leading-edge foundry: TSMC claims strong demand, with no new number, and now carries equity exposure to one customer's outcome. Export-exposed channel demand: displaced, unsized, and not yet priced. Commodity memory: prices still rising, but decelerating, per TrendForce's own revision. HBM: still capacity-bound. Put plainly, the center of the stack, leading-edge logic and HBM, is still constrained by what can physically be built. The edges are different, and mixed. Offshore China demand is being removed by policy. The Chinese memory cut looks like a blend of two constraints: buyers short of GPUs, and buyers squeezed by AI token pricing. One is availability. The other is economics.

What to watch. First, TSMC's own reported revenue against its robust-demand claim. Second, whether displaced offshore GPU capacity clears at full price or at a discount. Third, whether TrendForce's next bulletin extends this cut into HBM itself. And finally, whether any other supplier follows TSMC into equity stakes in its customers.

That's the briefing. Thanks for listening, and I'll catch you next time.
