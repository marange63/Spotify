Cautious Optimism Briefings

Digital Money, Crypto, and Market Structure

Transcript · November 6, 2026

Good morning, it's Friday, November 6th, 2026, and this is your digital money briefing. Yesterday we asked who paid for Bitcoin's post-election bounce. Thursday answered the follow-up question, which is who pays for the unwind. It was the longs, for the second time in a week.

Start with the reversal. Bitcoin fell 6 percent on Thursday, according to The Block, and touched an intraday low of $75,568. Ether fell harder, down more than 7 percent. Total liquidations reached nearly $1.1 billion on the day. Trackers disagree widely on that total, so treat it as directional, not precise. The split matters more than the size. In a tighter four-hour window, by our arithmetic, roughly 93 percent of the money liquidated came from long positions. Bulls were the ones forced out.

That matters because of the sequence. Longs were flushed at the end of October, in the leverage unwind around the Binance hack. Shorts were flushed on Wednesday, in the post-election squeeze. Then longs were flushed again on Thursday. Yesterday we warned that a surprise could set off a third round of forced selling. It arrived within one session, before this morning's payrolls report was even out. So the line we drew yesterday holds, and it holds harder. Leverage, not conviction, is setting Bitcoin's direction. Forced positioning is whipping the price back and forth. And there's still no sign of a new buyer stepping in to absorb either side.

So what pulled the trigger? Not crypto. The Block's reporting is explicit that a sharp selloff in U.S. technology and AI stocks spilled into cryptocurrency markets. The Nasdaq Composite fell 4.7 percent on the day. The VIX jumped about 60 percent. One estimate, from FXStreet, put the damage at $2.3 trillion of U.S. stock market value in a single trading window. This was a macro shock that crypto imported, not a failure inside crypto.

And it breaks a pattern this desk flagged on Tuesday. That day we reported Block Scholes' reading of a record 54 percent ninety-day correlation between Bitcoin and gold, with Bitcoin's equity correlation easing. On Thursday, Bitcoin moved with the Nasdaq, not with gold. One session of co-movement doesn't overturn a smoothed, long-window statistic, so we won't call this a regime change. But it needs explaining, and there are two honest readings. The first is that Bitcoin is now structurally high-beta tech exposure. The second is a common de-grossing event. Funds that share one risk budget cut everything at once when volatility spikes. That reading says more about who holds Bitcoin than about what Bitcoin is.

For an allocator, the outcome matters more than the diagnosis. On the day the VIX spiked, Bitcoin provided no hedge at all. That weakens the pitch for Bitcoin as an uncorrelated sleeve in a portfolio. It also ties crypto's near-term path to the AI-equity trade, which is where Thursday's selling started. If that link holds, the next move in Bitcoin may be decided by tech sentiment, not by anything happening on-chain. The plain takeaway is this. The digital-gold behavior the bulls point to didn't show up on the one day it would have mattered.

Which brings us back to the floor we flagged yesterday. Glassnode had put the short-term holder cost basis at $75,700. That's the average entry price of the market's recent buyers. Thursday's low sat only about $130 below it. That's a marginal intraday touch, not a confirmed break. The natural next question is whether the leverage reset was real. We went looking for open interest, funding rates, and realized losses around Thursday's flush. We found no verifiable, day-specific numbers for any of the three. So that question stays unmeasured. Don't assume the book is clean just because it has been flushed twice.

There is one level we can confirm, and it sits in the same band. Strategy, the largest corporate bitcoin holder, disclosed an average purchase price of about $75,446 per coin in its most recent 8-K, filed in late October. That's the same figure we cited on October 30th, and no newer filing has turned up. So it's the latest disclosure, not a fresh one. By our arithmetic, Thursday's low traded within about $120 of it. As we said then, nothing implies a margin call or forced selling. The point is cost of capital. Strategy funds its buying by issuing securities, and that model gets harder to sell when the book sits at breakeven. So two of the market's most-watched cost levels now converge in one narrow band. One belongs to recent buyers, and the other to the largest corporate treasury. A sustained move lower would push both below breakeven at the same moment.

Now the other half of the test. If leverage moved the price, did the spot ETF wrapper cushion the drop or add to it? On Thursday, U.S. spot Bitcoin ETFs posted a $430 million net outflow, on SoSoValue data. That reverses Wednesday's $74.6 million inflow, the one bright spot we flagged yesterday, which had broken an outflow run. It did not extend. By our count, four of the last five reported sessions have now been net outflows.

Size it honestly. Total spot Bitcoin ETF net assets stand at about $96.6 billion. Against that base, Thursday's outflow is under half a percent, by our arithmetic. That's a signal, not a stampede, and most holders are not leaving. But the timing is the point. This was a macro-driven down day, not a crypto-specific one. That makes it the cleanest test yet of whether these funds stabilize or amplify. The mechanism we laid out in late October still applies. Redemptions require authorized participants to sell spot bitcoin, adding supply exactly when the market is already selling. And as we noted on Tuesday, with this year's net new money near zero, there's no inflow cushion. A drawdown flows almost straight through to assets. In late October we called the insulation thesis wounded, not disproven. Thursday adds another data point on the wounded side. The institutional base is behaving like risk-budget money, not strategic reserve money.

Two caveats on that outflow figure. It comes from a single aggregator chain, and Farside's official tracker had not finalized Thursday's row when we checked. Same-day flow numbers get revised, sometimes by a lot. We also lack a reliable fund-by-fund breakdown, so we won't say which issuer drove it. The so-what still stands. On Thursday, the wrapper sold with the market rather than against it.

So here's the bull case, stated plainly, because the evidence gives it real weight. The shock came from outside crypto. It was an equity selloff, not a protocol failure or an exchange blowup. Leverage has now been flushed on both the long and the short side within a week. If the open-interest data confirms it, that leaves the book lighter going into the next shock. ETF assets remain overwhelmingly intact. And price is sitting on two converging cost-basis levels rather than falling through them. Whether this becomes a shakeout that clears the way higher, or the first stage of a capitulation, is genuinely open.

Three things to watch from here. First, whether the $75,000 to $75,700 band holds on a daily close, not just an intraday touch. Second, whether Thursday's ETF outflow extends into a second session, and whether Farside confirms the number. Third, this morning's October payrolls report, due at 8:30 Eastern. It lands after this episode publishes, so we're asserting no number ahead of it. It is simply the next macro test for a book that has already been flushed twice.

The thesis in one line. Thursday showed Bitcoin trading as leveraged tech beta rather than a hedge, and the cost-basis floor the bulls point to is now being tested by the very ETF flows they counted on to defend it.

That's the briefing for Friday, November 6th. Stay cautiously optimistic.
