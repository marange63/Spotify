Cautious Optimism Briefings

Digital Money, Crypto, and Market Structure

Transcript · August 12, 2026

Good morning. It's Wednesday, August 12th, and this is your digital money briefing.

The institutionalization trade has a new problem this week, and it isn't price. It's economics. Two of the cleanest public proxies for the digital-dollar stack, Circle and Coinbase, both reported earnings that show the machinery underneath slowing down, just as the regulatory tailwinds were supposed to speed it up. Hold that thought as we work from the business model out to the price.

Start with Circle, the issuer of USDC. Second-quarter revenue and reserve income came in at 701 million dollars, up 7 percent year over year. That's the slowest growth Circle has posted since it went public, and the first time that number has fallen into single digits. Adjusted EBITDA told the same story, up just 8 percent to 143 million. The more telling detail sits inside the release. Average USDC in circulation over the quarter was a record 76.5 billion dollars, but the quarter-end figure was only 73.3 billion. When the average sits above the closing figure, it means the float was shrinking as the quarter went on. Now, this is deceleration, not collapse. Circulation was still up 19 percent year over year. But the momentum is fading. At the same time, the reserve return rate, what Circle earns on the Treasury bills backing every USDC token, fell 66 basis points to 3.5 percent as short-term rates came down. So Circle's revenue is being squeezed from two directions at once. The float is slowing, and the yield on that float is shrinking.

The bull case is real, too. Onchain transaction volume rose 151 percent year over year to 14.8 trillion dollars, so the tokens are being used harder even as the pile grows slower. And Circle's Arc settlement chain launches its mainnet on September 16th, with BlackRock and DTCC involved, a sign Circle is trying to become infrastructure rather than just a reserve-income business. But the core point stands. The reserve-income model is now hostage to the rate cycle in a way that adoption growth alone can't offset. A September Fed cut would land directly on that 3.5 percent.

That matters double for Coinbase, because the two companies are joined at the hip. Circle just renewed its USDC distribution deal with Coinbase on existing terms, and Coinbase now captures roughly half of all USDC's economics and holds more than 30 percent of the token's supply on its platform. So the same rate exposure runs straight through Coinbase's income statement. And its second quarter looked ugly on the surface. Revenue came in at 1.22 billion dollars, down 18.5 percent year over year, and missing analyst estimates of around 1.3 billion by roughly 80 million. GAAP earnings came in at a loss of a dollar thirty-six a share, against expectations closer to a 42-cent loss. The headline net loss was 359.5 million dollars.

But dig into where that loss came from, and the story splits in two. About 209.5 million of it, roughly 58 percent, is a non-cash mark-to-market loss on crypto that Coinbase holds as an investment. That's a line item the company itself says doesn't reflect the underlying business, and it strips the item out of its adjusted numbers. The other 42 percent is real. Operating margin deteriorated to negative 9.3 percent, from negative 1.6 percent a year earlier, and transaction expenses ate 16 percent of net revenue. So this isn't purely accounting noise, and it isn't purely operating decay. It's both.

Meanwhile the underlying franchise kept setting records. Trading volume market share hit an all-time high of 10.3 percent, up from 9.1 percent in the first quarter. Average USDC balances on the platform hit a record 20 billion dollars. And adjusted EBITDA was 207.8 million, the fourteenth straight profitable quarter on that measure. So the so-what cuts both ways. A crypto rebound next quarter would mechanically reverse a big chunk of that 209.5 million loss, because it's just a mark on assets Coinbase still holds. But the transaction-expense pressure won't reverse with the price. That's the part to watch.

Now the price side, which is where this same disconnect shows up in real time. Spot Bitcoin ETFs pulled in 853.5 million dollars over five straight sessions in early August. That's a swing of about 915 million from the week before, when the funds bled 61.5 million. BlackRock's IBIT took about 81 percent of it, roughly 693 million. Then the streak snapped. On August 11th, the complex saw 144.67 million in net outflows, led by IBIT and Grayscale's GBTC, with only Grayscale's Mini Trust taking money in.

And through all of it, Bitcoin barely moved. It gained only about 2 percent last week despite that inflow surge. It's been stuck in a 62,000 to 66,000 dollar band for most of the summer, trading around 63,500 heading into today's CPI print. Crypto trading volumes overall have fallen to their lowest level in three years. Reporting attributes some of the price cap to OTC selling from miners and from Strategy, absorbing the ETF demand off-exchange. But that specific claim rests on one source with no filing behind it, and Strategy's public playbook has always been to accumulate, not sell. So treat it as a plausible explanation, not a confirmed one. What is confirmed is the pattern itself. A near-billion-dollar inflow burst that didn't move the price, and then reversed, is the clearest evidence yet that ETF flows aren't setting Bitcoin's price the way the adoption narrative assumes.

One structural change worth flagging under that. BlackRock cut the minimum size for converting bitcoin directly into IBIT shares, from 25 million dollars down to 1 million. That lowers the bar for large private holders to move coins into the ETF wrapper. It could thin the free float on exchanges further, even while the headline flow numbers stay muted. So the plumbing keeps pulling supply off the market, quietly, whether or not the price reflects it.

Briefly, on regulation. The Senate opened the first procedural step on the CLARITY Act, the market-structure bill that would finally give the SEC and CFTC a jurisdictional map for spot crypto trading. A cloture motion was filed early Saturday, the farthest the bill has ever advanced. But it's snagged on a revised ethics provision. That language bars officials and their spouses from sponsoring a digital asset, and it's enforceable only by the Attorney General, not by state AGs. It has been sitting with the White House for over a week, and drew a joint statement of concern from seven negotiating Democrats. Passage still needs 60 votes and roughly ten Democrats. Staffers say a real vote could come quickly once the Senate returns in September, but that's event risk, not a base case. Set that against the stablecoin side of the business, which already has a fixed regulatory calendar under the GENIUS Act. The money-moving rails are getting their certainty on a schedule. The assets riding on top of them are still waiting.

Put it together, and the tape this week is quiet, but the economics underneath digital money are compressing, and increasingly bound to the rate cycle. Whether it's Circle's reserve income, Coinbase's transaction margins, or Bitcoin's flows failing to translate into price, the same story keeps showing up. That's your briefing for August 12th.
