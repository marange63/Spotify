Cautious Optimism Briefings

Robotics & Physical AI

Transcript · September 12, 2026

Good morning, it's Saturday, September 12th, and this is your briefing on robotics and physical AI.

For five straight sessions this beat has kept returning to the same question. Where is the one number in physical AI that somebody other than the vendor has to stand behind? This week we got it, and it is unflattering. Agility Robotics, the maker of the Digit humanoid, filed a Form S-4 with the SEC on September 4th as part of its planned merger with Churchill Capital Corp the eleventh. The filing discloses that Agility generated 1.8 million dollars in net sales last year. Against that, it posted a 140 million dollar operating loss. One caveat on sourcing before we go further. Those figures reach us through trade-press reading of the filing, not through a passage read out of EDGAR here.

Sit with that ratio, because it is the number to bank. It works out to roughly 78 dollars burned for every dollar booked. And the direction matters more than the level. Operating spend rose to 111 million dollars last year, up from 71 million the year before, while revenue stayed stuck in the single digits of millions. That is a company scaling its cost base against an order book rather than against demand. This show reached the same finding on September 10th, when XPeng's robotics unit raised roughly 900 million dollars in the same week that the largest humanoid order disclosed anywhere in the world, UBTech's, came to about 35 million. Now that gap shows up on an SEC registration statement instead of a press release. Hold onto that difference. A press release is written by a marketing department. A registration statement is written under securities-law liability.

The bull case sits on the other side of that same filing. Agility discloses more than 300 million dollars in multi-year orders from a single, unnamed customer. Follow-up reporting on the filing's own language clarifies what that figure actually is. It is not a stack of firm purchase orders. It is committed orders for a thousand Digit v5 robots under three-year robots-as-a-service agreements. And those orders are explicitly conditioned on Agility hitting unspecified product and contract milestones. So the money does not arrive in one lump. It trickles in only as Agility ships, and only as the customer accepts each milestone, over years, if at all. An order book is a commitment. Revenue is a transaction. The entire equity story rests on the first converting into the second, and the filing gives you no schedule for that conversion.

Then there is a detail in the same filing that reframes the whole deal. Agility's own going-concern disclosure states there is substantial doubt about its ability to continue operating without this transaction closing. That is not a company cashing in on momentum. That is a company that needs the SPAC's cash to survive. And it is negotiating a 2.5 billion dollar valuation from exactly that position. Remember what a SPAC valuation is. It is a price agreed between two parties, not a price discovered by a market. The deal is expected to bring in more than 620 million dollars of gross proceeds, most of it from Churchill's trust, with a private placement led by Foxconn alongside it. Note the word expected. This is a registration statement for a deal that has not closed.

That Foxconn detail is worth pausing on. The contract manufacturer is taking equity in its own customer. That is the physical-AI version of a pattern this program's cross-topic digest has flagged repeatedly in AI infrastructure, where the largest commitments in the build-out keep settling in equity rather than in invoices. When the manufacturer becomes the investor, capacity starts getting allocated by ownership stake rather than by price. That is an edge for whoever the supply chain backs, and a cost problem for everyone else.

So if the revenue is not there, what are these machines actually doing? Agility says its Digit units have logged more than 65,000 operating hours across nine customer sites, including Schaeffler, GXO, Toyota's Canadian plant, Amazon, and Mercado Libre. Figure's humanoids have logged over 1,250 hours at BMW's Spartanburg plant across roughly eleven months, loading more than 90,000 parts. Those are real, sustained, non-teleoperated deployments, and they are genuine signal rather than staged demos. But both sets of figures are self-reported. Neither company publishes uptime, cycle time, or cost per unit alongside them. Here is the reconciliation that matters. That hours number is enormous for a company with almost no sales. Operating hours are being generated. They are not, at anything like the rate the capital implies, being paid for.

Now turn to the only public price anyone has for a humanoid platform. Unitree listed on Shanghai's STAR Market on August 19th and closed its first day up 460 percent, briefly valuing the company near 66 billion dollars. As of Wednesday's close, the shares had fallen nearly 40 percent from that first-day close, leaving Unitree at roughly 30 billion dollars. I am not going to assign a cause to that slide. Company-specific skepticism and a broad tech rotation look identical from outside. And note what the drawdown is not. Holders from the offering are still well above the issue price. This is a deflating pop, not a repudiation.

Here is the detail that makes it hard to dismiss anyway. Even at the reduced price, Unitree still trades at roughly 125 times last year's revenue. And this is happening to the strong case, not the weak one. Unitree's revenue more than quadrupled to about 1.7 billion yuan last year, which is a couple of hundred million dollars at an approximate conversion. The company is actually profitable. Humanoids overtook quadrupeds as its largest product line for the first time. A profitable, quadrupling company still carrying that multiple after giving back a large chunk of its peak tells you the market is still pricing dramatic forward growth into humanoids generally. That makes the read-through for pre-revenue names like Agility, Figure, and Apptronik harder, not easier. If the good student is being marked down this hard, ask what happens to the ones still failing the test. And note which mark has been tested. Unitree's has. The private marks have not, until Agility trades and gives Figure and Apptronik a daily price they have never had.

One dated catalyst to close on, and I want to be clear that this one is context rather than news. Back in late July, the FCC restricted imports of new foreign-made humanoid and quadruped robots on national-security grounds. The stated rationale cited findings that some Unitree models were transmitting data to servers in China without owners' knowledge. China holds an estimated 85 percent share of the global humanoid market, according to Barclays. But the restriction is narrower than the headline suggests. It exempts robots already purchased and models with prior FCC authorization, so its near-term commercial bite is limited. What makes it live again is the calendar. Twelve days from now, Xi Jinping is scheduled at the White House, where AI is expected to come up. I am not going to predict what lands on that agenda. But remember that the leverage runs against Washington here too. In humanoids the West is still the importer of components, so a reciprocal move on magnets or actuators would hurt American makers' unit costs more than the ban helps their market share.

Pull the threads together. This is the first week physical AI produced both a filing-grade number and a market-tested price, and both say the same thing. The capital is ahead of the customers. Watch three things from here. Whether Agility's merger actually closes, and what its first quarter as a public company reports. Whether the customer behind that order book is ever named. And whether a second humanoid listing clears the market at anything like Unitree's current multiple, or whether the repricing keeps going.

That's the briefing. Have a good day.
