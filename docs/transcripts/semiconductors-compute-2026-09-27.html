Good morning, it's Sunday, September 27th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

One new report drives today, and it moves a thread this show has followed all week. There's also a correction to our own record from Wednesday. Let's get into it.

Start with TSMC's 2027 pricing, because a report this week changes where the pricing power actually sits. On Monday we ran a July report, sourced to Nikkei, that had TSMC discussing a base price increase of 5 to 10 percent for 2027. That range covered both mature nodes and advanced processes at 7 nanometers and below. On Thursday, Digitimes reported a lower base range of 3 to 6 percent, starting in January. It appears to cover the same span of nodes. But the two reports come from different outlets and different unnamed supply-chain sources. Neither one is a TSMC statement. So treat this as the reported range narrowing, not as a confirmed cut to a single number.

What hasn't moved is the surcharge. Both reports describe the same premium. Customers who want HPC or AI capacity beyond what they already committed pay an extra 10 to 15 percent. In the Digitimes version, that surcharge sits on top of whatever the base rate turns out to be. The consistency matters more than either base range. Two separate reports, two months apart, agree on the toll for flexibility and disagree only on the floor. If you hold committed volume, your wafers cost a little more next year. If you show up wanting more than you locked in, you pay a second, larger premium for asking. TSMC's pricing power lives in that second number.

That connects to Friday's story. On Friday we covered reporting that TSMC had told Nvidia and Broadcom it couldn't supply all the capacity they want. The surcharge is the price tag on that refusal. It lands hardest on the largest AI buyers, and exactly where their demand runs above their commitments. My inference, not anything reported, is that someone has to eat that bill. Either the fabless designers absorb it in gross margin, or they pass it through and it shows up in hyperscaler capex. Watch for it in 2027 margin guidance.

The same report makes a much bigger claim. It says TSMC's capacity is effectively booked through 2030, across both advanced and mature nodes. That's the strongest claim in the report, and it rests on the weakest evidence. It comes from unnamed sources, not a disclosed backlog or a TSMC filing. It most plausibly describes long-term forecasts and framework agreements, not binding purchase orders. And multi-year foundry bookings have been walked back before. What would make it credible is TSMC putting a backlog figure on the record. Until then, it's a claim, not a fact.

Now the part of the report that cuts against consensus. It says TSMC's 8-inch fabs are running above 100 percent utilization. Every process at 45 nanometers and below is reportedly fully loaded. The consensus holds that mature nodes are oversupplied, because of new Chinese fabs. On its face, this says AI-driven demand has spread into the older nodes.

But there's a confound. TSMC has been shrinking its own 8-inch footprint for more than a year. TrendForce reported a plan to close its 8-inch Fab 5 by 2027. A separate TrendForce report, from February, had TSMC shifting roughly 80 percent of its own 8-inch output to its affiliate VIS. If TSMC is closing and handing off 8-inch capacity, utilization on what remains can climb even if demand doesn't. So the honest read isn't that AI has conquered mature nodes. A shrinking capacity base and rising demand are both plausible drivers. Nothing in this reporting lets you separate them.

If the demand side does hold, the effect reaches well beyond TSMC. Analog, power and automotive parts are built on 8-inch lines. A genuine squeeze there would drag the AI capacity story into chips that sit far from the AI rack. That's an implication of the utilization figure, not something the report says. So here's the pricing takeaway. The floor is edging up modestly, and it may be lower than first reported. The premium for capacity beyond your commitment is the larger piece. That makes committed allocation the scarce asset heading into 2027, for Nvidia, AMD and Broadcom alike.

The surcharge has a physical twin, and it's the packaging queue. An independent tracker, Silicon Analysts, puts TSMC's CoWoS advanced-packaging lead times at 52 to 78 weeks. That's a tracker estimate, not TSMC guidance, and we have no earlier figure to compare it with. But even the low end means a new order placed today waits a full year. In practice, anyone ordering now is mostly buying 2028 capacity, not 2027. That structurally favors whoever already holds committed allocation. And it's a real headwind for any challenger trying to get to launch without it.

Custom silicon doesn't escape that queue. The same tracker estimates that Broadcom holds about 15 percent of total CoWoS allocation. Broadcom's business is building custom AI accelerators for hyperscalers, as an alternative to buying Nvidia GPUs. So when a hyperscaler moves to custom silicon, it doesn't leave TSMC's line. It shifts the bill to a different customer standing in the same line, under the same allocation logic.

How much relief is coming? The same tracker puts TSMC's 2027 CoWoS capacity target at roughly 141,000 to 170,000 wafers a month. Yesterday we cited a Mizuho Securities Asia forecast of 190,000 to 200,000 a month for 2027. So even the tracker's high end falls short of Mizuho's low end. That's the latest case of outside estimates of this bottleneck disagreeing by wide margins, and none of them comes from TSMC. The dispersion is itself the finding. Investors are sizing the single most important AI bottleneck without a disclosed number.

Now to memory, the gate that sits behind packaging, and a correction. On Wednesday we told you SK hynix's 48-gigabyte, 16-layer HBM4 part for Nvidia's Vera Rubin had been shown, not shipped at scale. That rested on a display unit at an August trade show. The same tracker, Silicon Analysts, now reports that SK hynix began mass production of that part in the third quarter. If that holds, it's a genuine manufacturing and yield milestone. It would reinforce SK hynix's lead in the highest-margin memory category.

But keep the gates separate. Mass production, as reported by a tracker, is not qualified volume shipment into Vera Rubin systems. One is manufacturing execution. The other is customer qualification and allocation, and we have no sourcing on that second, harder test. So score SK hynix's move as a gain on execution, not yet a gain on allocation. It's an update to our record, not a confirmed reversal.

Put the threads together, and score each competitive dimension on its own. On pricing power, TSMC gained ground this week. On gross margin, the fabless designers are exposed, because they either absorb the overflow toll or pass it on. On manufacturing execution, SK hynix may have gained, pending qualification. Across all of it, scarcity is being charged and rationed at the margin, not at the floor.

That split also tells you which piece to trust. The modest base increase is the more durable part. The overflow surcharge is a scarcity rent. It should compress as capacity from the TSMC capex raise we covered Friday comes online. Investors who capitalize the surcharge as a permanent feature of 2027 pricing are making the error this beat has flagged all week. They're treating a cyclical rent as a structural margin.

Two dated tests to hold in mind. Micron reports fiscal fourth-quarter results this Wednesday, September 30th, after the close. Nothing about that print is known yet. It's the first memory read since this week's TSMC report. It will show whether memory makers are capturing the kind of margin pricing TSMC appears to be extracting on wafers. The second test is simpler. Does TSMC itself put any of this on the record? Until it does, today's pricing story is reported, not confirmed.
