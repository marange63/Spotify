On Friday we said this was a week about who funds the AI race. Today we get the first real look at what one lab has signed up to pay. Anthropic confirmed back in June that it had confidentially submitted a draft S-1 to the SEC. The contents of that draft have now leaked in detail, first through 24/7 Wall St. Reporting since then says the Wall Street Journal reviewed a more recent version covering the first half of 2026. That moves the story beyond a single aggregator's scoop. Still, nothing has been publicly filed, and every number below is reported, not confirmed by Anthropic.

Start with revenue, because it's the strongest line in the draft. Anthropic's first-half 2026 revenue was reportedly sixteen point three billion dollars, against one point one billion a year earlier. That's roughly fifteenfold growth, and it reflects real demand. One caveat carries over from Friday. Anthropic's revenue-recognition convention may not compare cleanly with OpenAI's, so hold off on lining the two labs up side by side.

Now the obligation side, which is the actual news. The draft reportedly shows about four hundred fifty billion dollars of cloud compute agreements over the next decade. The document itself calls them "massive, noncancelable" commitments. That's the headline everyone is repeating. But the same reporting on the Journal-reviewed draft puts total committed compute and infrastructure spending at more than six hundred billion dollars, up from a previously disclosed five hundred eighteen billion. No source reconciles those two totals. So treat four hundred fifty billion as the headline figure, and the larger number as an open question about how big the real obligation is.

Here's some rough arithmetic, and it's mine, not the filing's. Four hundred fifty billion over ten years averages about forty-five billion a year. First-half revenue annualizes to roughly thirty-three billion. The payment schedule isn't disclosed and is probably back-loaded, so this is not a coverage ratio. But the direction is clear, and it only gets starker if the six hundred billion figure is closer to the truth. Anthropic has locked in compute spending that today's revenue doesn't yet cover, and that spending doesn't go away if demand softens. That makes Anthropic a high-operating-leverage business, with fixed cost on one side and revenue that has to keep compounding on the other.

Then there's the loss, and here the headline misleads. Anthropic's first-half net loss was reportedly eighty point seven billion dollars. About seventy-two billion of that is non-cash, tied to convertible notes and other financing instruments. My inference about the mechanism is this. As Anthropic's valuation climbs, those instruments are worth more, and the remeasurement books as a loss. So the scarier the headline gets, the more it may be tracking the company's own fundraising success. Strip that charge out and you're left with roughly eight point seven billion dollars of loss for the half. The draft has no operating-loss line, so treat that residual as a proxy, not a disclosed figure, and certainly not as cash burn.

What the draft leaves out matters too. It reportedly gives no gross margin and no split between API and subscription revenue. Management has told investors something rosier, claiming positive free cash flow and an eleven percent adjusted operating margin at an investor day. Those are management's own adjusted numbers, not figures from the filing, and the gap between the two is exactly what the public S-1 will have to explain. One more detail did surface. About a fifth of first-half revenue came from just two customers. That's real concentration for a company about to ask public markets for a premium.

Now follow those commitments to the other side of the contract. One lab's noncancelable obligation is somebody else's contracted backlog. Reporting on the draft itemizes it. Google Cloud and AWS each hold roughly one hundred ten billion dollars of it. Nscale and Lambda together are owed up to eighty-six billion for Nvidia chip rentals. That Nscale line deserves a pause. On Thursday, our Enterprise Technology desk noted that Nscale says it hasn't yet secured binding financing for the contracts it already has. So the buyer's side of this deal is binding, while part of the seller's side is not yet funded.

That flips a pattern this show has tracked all week. Announcements kept running ahead of binding paper. Here the obligation is binding, and it's the revenue to cover it that isn't. It also ties into the cross-desk worry about the cost of capital, which ran from CoreWeave's bonds to Oracle's swaps to Meta's default protection hitting a record. Anthropic's filing shows the same exposure from the buyer's side. For the capex-bubble debate, it cuts both ways. The demand is contractually real. The revenue standing behind it is still being earned.

Fixed cost matters most when prices move, and that brings us back to yesterday's lead. Moonshot listed Kimi K3.1 Pro permanently at half the price the top US labs had converged on. A lab carrying noncancelable compute can't shed capacity when the top of the market gets cheaper. It has to absorb the hit in margin. That makes yesterday's revenue-mix question sharper. How much of Anthropic's revenue sits in price-sensitive coding and API work, and how much in stickier enterprise contracts? The draft doesn't say. With noncancelable compute, pricing power stops being a nice-to-have and becomes close to existential.

Which is exactly what OpenAI is choosing not to show you. We reported Friday that OpenAI is closing in on a round of up to thirty billion dollars, at a one point two five trillion dollar pre-money valuation, led by UAE investors and Coatue. That was down from earlier reporting at one point four trillion, and the round still isn't closed. Staying private keeps OpenAI's own compute commitments out of view. Once Anthropic files publicly, with marketing reportedly possible as early as the week of November 9th, analysts will hold OpenAI's private mark against Anthropic's disclosed obligations. That's a real asymmetry, and Anthropic loses it by going first.

There's a market-structure consequence, and this is inference. Fixed-cost-heavy standalone labs need continual access to capital. That favors whoever has the cheapest capital, which means the hyperscaler-backed labs and above all Google DeepMind, which sits inside Alphabet and never has to price a round. It's the winner-take-most-among-the-best-financed argument we made on Friday, and today's numbers strengthen it.

So what is a public order book actually being asked to pay for? The disclosure applies again here, because this comparison favors the model behind this show. On the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, an independent composite, Anthropic's Claude Opus 5.5 still leads, with Sonnet 5.5 second. OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra and Meta's Muse Wonder sit together a tier back. Muse Wonder shipped on October 12th, so treat that as standing context, not this week's news. On ProgramBench, a harder coding test reported in launch coverage rather than an independent leaderboard, Opus scores seventy point five against Muse Wonder's fifty. That's despite Meta's claims of rough parity. A few points on a composite index is not proof of a durable moat. But that capability lead is what Anthropic's IPO valuation rests on. Anthropic's draft reportedly says the offering could value Anthropic at two trillion dollars, and calls that a preliminary estimate. And that same lead is precisely the margin Chinese open-weight labs are attacking from below on price.

Here's the scorecard by dimension. On capital access, OpenAI's private flexibility beats Anthropic's coming public scrutiny. On disclosure, it's the reverse, because Anthropic is about to show its hand and OpenAI isn't. On compute commitments, the noncancelable bill is now Anthropic's biggest known liability. On capability, Anthropic's top model still leads on the independent index. On pricing power, the whole top tier is under pressure.

Three things to watch. First, whether the S-1 goes public, and whether it finally discloses margin and revenue mix. Second, whether OpenAI's round closes on the reported terms. Third, whether Moonshot's open weights land on November 4th and start biting into prices, just as fixed compute costs make that fight existential.
