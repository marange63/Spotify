Robotics & Physical AI

Transcript · November 18, 2026

Good morning. It's Wednesday, November 18th, and this is your Cautious Optimism briefing on robotics and physical AI.

This week's pattern is simple. Every headline in this sector shrinks once you read the fine print. A humanoid listing turns out to rest mostly on private money. A flagship humanoid deployment shrinks to one robot at a time. A robotaxi launch shrinks to employees, family, and friends. So the disclosure worth trusting right now is the denominator. That means the cash actually raised, the robots actually running, and the riders actually riding, not the headline sitting on top of them.

Start with the money, because it's the cleanest data we've gotten. Agility Robotics, maker of the Digit humanoid, began trading on Nasdaq on November 5th under the ticker AGLT. It got there by merging with a SPAC, Churchill Capital Corp XI. This desk has named Agility's first quarterly filing as the real operating test five times this month. That filing hasn't arrived yet. But the capital side has, and it's instructive on its own.

The original deal valued Agility at $2.5 billion before the new money. According to The Robot Report, what the transaction actually brought in was about $465 million in gross capital, before expenses. The companies tied that figure to the expiration of Churchill's redemption deadline. In other words, some public SPAC holders took their cash back rather than ride into the listing.

Now break down that $465 million. Only about $164 million came from Churchill's trust account. That's the money that came through the public listing mechanism itself. On our arithmetic, it's barely a third of the total.

The rest came from private hands. Foxconn led a private placement of about $201 million, priced at $10 a share. Agility also counted $100 million in equity it had raised separately after the SPAC was announced. So the Foxconn-led placement alone was bigger than the public trust. Strategic and private capital carried this deal.

There are two ways to read that. The first is a confidence vote. Foxconn anchoring a placement of that size signals real conviction from an industrial heavyweight. One caution, though. Nothing in the filings we found ties Foxconn to building Digit. As far as the record shows, its role is financial only. The second reading is less flattering. Public SPAC holders, offered the same story at a $2.5 billion valuation, chose redemption over participation. Both readings can be true. The so-what is that the public market's own mechanism supplied only a minority of a public debut.

The stock has leaned toward the second reading. Shares closed at $9.90 on Friday, November 6th, the day after the debut. That was down nearly 14 percent from Thursday's close. One session on a thin post-SPAC float is weak evidence by itself.

But the slide didn't reverse. According to the market-data site StockAnalysis, AGLT closed Tuesday at $7.17. That's a single data source we haven't cross-checked. If it holds, the stock sits roughly 28 percent below that Friday close, in under two weeks. That's no longer just noise.

Here's the second-order effect, and it's our inference. Agility's capital stack and its early markdown now become the public comp for any Western humanoid listing that follows, whether that's Figure, Apptronik, or 1X. If public SPAC holders keep redeeming, the next deal is likely to be carried by strategic investors like Foxconn rather than by public holders. Meanwhile, Agility's first quarterly filing stays the real test. It will be the first audited look at cost per Digit, deployments, and revenue from a Western pure-play humanoid maker. Until then, this is capital-markets pricing, not operating proof.

If Agility shows the money side of the denominator problem, Figure's BMW pilot shows the operating side. Figure's Figure 02 humanoid ran at BMW's Spartanburg plant for eleven months. Company and customer reports cite more than 1,250 hours of runtime and over 90,000 parts loaded, all on a single task. Those numbers have circulated widely as proof of a working humanoid inside an automaker.

A BMW-sourced account tells a narrower story. As relayed from a Forbes report, BMW said only one robot worked at a time. A second robot sat on standby while the first one charged. And BMW called it a non-production test with two or three machines. Weigh the sourcing plainly. This account is secondhand. It reaches us through Forbes and then other outlets, and we haven't seen the original wording. The underlying report dates to October and resurfaced this week, so this is a revived dispute, not breaking news. Figure hasn't publicly reconciled the two versions.

Here's the honest reading. Both sets of numbers can be true at once. On our arithmetic, 1,250 hours over eleven months is under four hours a day. One robot trading places with a charging partner could log that comfortably. So the dispute isn't necessarily about false numbers. It's about what kind of count is being reported. Cumulative hours and parts loaded say nothing about fleet size or production status.

That carries its own second-order effect, again our inference. If customer accounts keep shrinking vendor claims, the burden of proof for private humanoid valuations shifts. Investors will start asking for fleet counts the customer corroborates. Cumulative runtime figures lose their value as evidence. And that makes Agility's coming public disclosures worth more, as the one audited data point in the Western cohort. So carry one rule into every company-reported humanoid figure, Tesla's Optimus claims included. Ask for the denominator before you believe the headline.

Before filing this as a Western problem, look at China. Yesterday this desk said China scales while the West plans. Today's data point qualifies that line. ROBOTERA, a Chinese humanoid maker, said in a November 17th press release that its humanoids now run at 11 postal hubs across 8 cities in Guangdong province. Those hubs sit inside China Post's network. That's real expansion. But it's a site count, not a robot count. And there's no independent check on how many machines are working, or how autonomously. So China's humanoid cohort is growing its footprint faster than it's disclosing its reliability, same as everyone else.

The same gap between a launch and a denominator runs through robotaxis. Yesterday we had Zoox, Amazon's robotaxi unit, plugging into Uber's app in Las Vegas. This past week Zoox also began testing its purpose-built robotaxi on public roads in northwest Atlanta, around Atlantic Station, Woodward, and Home Park. But in Atlanta, it isn't taking the public at all yet. The service can only be used by Zoox employees, family members, and friends. Zoox says it will open a public waitlist "in the coming months," ahead of its Zoox Explorers early-rider program. It disclosed no fleet size and no disengagement data for Atlanta.

So the ladder in a new city runs like this. Employees and friends come first, then a waitlist, then early riders, then paid rides at scale. In Atlanta, Zoox hasn't reached the first public rung. Don't over-penalize that. Gating a new city behind an internal rider pool is standard de-risking. It says little about Zoox's driving capability, and it isn't evidence of a slipped schedule. What it does say is that counting cities is the wrong metric. The number that matters is public, paying riders, and in Atlanta that count hasn't started.

So where does that leave things? Capital is still reaching humanoids. Now it's doing so through a US public listing with a live stock price, and that's a real structural shift, even if the early verdict is skeptical. Footprints are expanding too, for humanoid makers and robotaxi operators, in the US and China alike. What's still unresolved is everything underneath them: fleet size, production status, and whether anyone is paying for a ride or a robot-hour. Watch four things. First, Agility's first quarterly filing. Second, whether BMW or Figure reconcile their accounts of Spartanburg. Third, any humanoid fleet count that a customer, not a vendor, stands behind. And fourth, when Zoox opens that Atlanta waitlist to the public.

That's the briefing for November 18th. Stay cautiously optimistic.
