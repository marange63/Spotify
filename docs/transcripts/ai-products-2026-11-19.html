AI Products & Applications

Transcript · November 19, 2026

Good morning. It's November 19th, 2026, and this is your AI products briefing.

Last week, OpenAI let AI agents spend your money, but only for its Pro subscribers in the U.S. Now Google is giving the same ability away to hundreds of millions of people. And the model prices underneath these agents are being published rung by rung, with a calendar attached. Start with the free rollout.

On November 17th, Google announced that Tasks in the Gemini app is now free. Google's own blog says Tasks is "rolling out free of charge for hundreds of millions of people across the U.S., Japan, Korea, Canada, Australia, and India." Per Google, Tasks can make phone calls, pay bills, and shop for groceries. It connects with, in Google's words, "more than 60 apps." And it runs on Google's new Gemini 4 Hydrogen model.

Set that against OpenAI's ChatGPT Wallet, announced November 13th. Wallet lets OpenAI's agents, which it calls dots, pay for things too. But it launched only for U.S. Pro subscribers over eighteen. So within four days, agent purchasing went from a paid, single-country feature to a free one across six countries. The mass-market test of whether ordinary people will let software spend their money is no longer running on OpenAI's narrow paid base. It's running on Google's free one.

Now look at the rails underneath. OpenAI built Wallet with American Express, Mastercard, and Visa. Google names the same three networks. But Google's post lists American Express as "coming soon," so only Mastercard and Visa are live for Tasks today. That overlap supports a read this show offered last week. The card networks, not the chatbots, are becoming the trust layer for agent commerce. Any assistant that wants to check out needs the same network relationships. Google took the wider audience, but with narrower rails at launch.

There's a second constraint on Tasks, and it isn't technical. Google says the calling feature "is not currently available in Indiana, Louisiana, Minnesota, Montana, and Nebraska." Google doesn't say why. Our inference is that the capability has outrun the permission to deploy it everywhere at once. What the agent can do is being gated state by state, not by the model.

What's missing from the launch is as telling as what's in it. Google published no completion rates, no transaction counts, and no success data. "Hundreds of millions of people" describes who is eligible, not who has actually asked Tasks to buy something. And "rolling out" means even eligibility isn't universal yet. This is distribution, not proven usage.

Two second-order effects follow, and both are our inference. The first is monetization. Google charges users nothing for Tasks. And OpenAI says it isn't taking a cut of Wallet transactions to start. So any revenue from agent purchasing is likelier to come from merchants or the payment side than from subscribers. That makes Wallet's Pro-only gate harder to defend as a differentiator. The second effect is shelf space. An agent wired into sixty-odd apps routes calls, bills, and orders to its integration partners. A service outside that set risks being invisible to the agent. So the takeaway on Tasks: the real test of agent spending now runs on Google's free base, and integration lists are becoming the new storefront.

Tasks runs on Hydrogen, the cheaper rung of Google's new model ladder. One rung up, Google just shipped something aimed squarely at coding agents.

On November 18th, Google released Gemini 4 Neon. According to 9to5Google, it's priced at three dollars per million input tokens and fifteen dollars per million output, "or twice as expensive as Hydrogen." Neon also replaces the older Argon tier outright. So Google's ladder is consolidating as well as extending, and every rung carries a published price.

Google pitches Neon against Carbon, its flagship tier. Google claims "performance comparable to, if not surpassing, Carbon across coding, reasoning, and agentic tasks." Neon also keeps Hydrogen's one-million-token context window and the same thinking levels, from Low up to Extra High. Google cites a top ranking on Artificial Analysis's Coding Agent Index, too. That index is third-party, but the ranking reaches us through Google's announcement, not an independent rerun.

Carbon's own price runs on a calendar. Google's Carbon announcement sets an introductory price of two dollars per million input tokens and ten per million output. In Google's words, that holds until "the introductory period expires on November 30, 2026." After that, Carbon's full price is four dollars in and twenty out. So for the rest of this month, Carbon is cheaper per token than Neon, the model pitched as the cheaper alternative. From December, Neon's list price sits a quarter below Carbon's.

But per-token price isn't cost per finished task, as this show has argued. Cursor, the coding-tool company, has now put numbers on that for Neon, using its own benchmark, CursorBench. At matched High thinking settings, Neon scored slightly higher than Carbon. It also cost an estimated six dollars fifty-four per task, against eight fifty-two for Carbon. That's nearly a quarter cheaper.

Which Carbon price did Cursor use? Cursor says it applies published token prices to each model's measured usage. It doesn't say whether that meant Carbon's introductory rate or its full one. Our arithmetic settles half the question. From December, Neon at High is the cheaper of the two per task either way, because Carbon's cost only goes up. November is the open half. If Cursor used Carbon's full rate, Carbon is probably cheaper per task until the promotion ends. If it used the introductory rate, Neon is cheaper now too. The sources don't say which.

The same results table holds a second catch. Push Neon to its Extra High setting, and the estimate climbs to ten sixty-seven per task. That's more than Carbon's High run on the same test. So the thinking level a team picks, not the sticker price, decides whether Neon actually saves money. Two caveats apply. These are Cursor's estimates, not billed costs. And CursorBench is Cursor's own test, not a neutral one. Still, it's the closest thing yet to a public cost-per-task comparison.

The so-what for builders, and this is our inference. Coding tools that resell model access now face costs that swing with the thinking dial and the calendar, not with the number of seats. A near-flagship model priced below the flagship's full rate only sharpens that. Expect more coding-tool vendors to move away from flat per-seat pricing and toward billing by usage or by task.

OpenAI made the opposite distribution call this week. On November 17th, it shipped its newest models, GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol, into ChatGPT Work, Codex, and the API. OpenAI's help documentation says the rollout doesn't add either model to regular Chat. The practical line is simple. Developers on the API and in Codex get the new models. People using regular consumer ChatGPT don't. OpenAI's pricing and benchmark claims for the pair reach us only through third-party trackers, so we're leaving them out.

Meanwhile, every ChatGPT plan, free included, got a smaller but useful fix. Live Dictation now streams speech to text as you talk, rather than waiting for the recording to finish. And per 9to5Mac, OpenAI says, "We've also removed the 10-minute recording limit." It's rolling out globally on iOS, Android, web, and desktop, so not everyone has it yet. There's also no accuracy or latency comparison against dedicated transcription tools. Our inference is that free, uncapped dictation removes the main reason casual users pay for a separate app to record long sessions.

Put those two OpenAI moves together, and its week mirrors Google's. Google handed consumers free agentic action. OpenAI handed consumers a utility, and kept its newest models for paid work and developer channels. That reading of intent is ours. The scoping could just as easily reflect capacity or cost as strategy.

So, to close the loop. Agent purchasing went from a paywalled U.S. launch to a free six-country rollout in four days. And the model economics under these agents now come as a published, time-sensitive price ladder, where the thinking dial matters as much as the sticker. Three things to watch from here. First, whether Google publishes any real usage data for Tasks, or switches on American Express. Second, whether OpenAI widens Wallet beyond its Pro tier in response. Third, whether anyone besides Cursor publishes an independent cost per completed coding task for Neon.

That's the briefing for November 19th. Talk to you tomorrow.
