Cautious Optimism Briefings

AI in Biopharma R&D

Transcript · October 8, 2026

Good morning. It's October 8th, 2026, and this is Cautious Optimism Briefings.

This week's money in AI drug discovery is going to biological data and wet-lab validation, not to models. Three stories make that case, and a fourth tells you who gets paid regardless of which model wins.

Start with the lead. On October 7th, Biohub, the nonprofit research institute, announced an expansion of its Virtual Biology Initiative. Biohub's own page puts the total at one point eight billion dollars in funding, data, computation, and new measurement technology. The Department of Energy alone contributes more than five hundred million dollars over five years. The headline for our purposes is the industry slice. In Biohub's words, "Google DeepMind, Isomorphic Labs, and Meta are collectively investing $300 million in the Virtual Biology Initiative." No split among the three is disclosed, and no period for the industry money is stated. And the stated purpose is data, not models. Biohub says the initiative exists to create "the technologies and multi-modal datasets needed to build predictive models of life." That is measurement and data, upstream of any model. Our read, not the funders' stated view, is that data has become the binding constraint.

Size it correctly. Three hundred million dollars sounds large until you set it against one biotech's budget. Recursion Pharmaceuticals cut its guided 2026 cash operating expense to under three hundred seventy-five million dollars on its second-quarter report in August. So three of the best-capitalized players in AI are jointly committing less than one platform company's annual burn. And their money is one slice of an effort six times its size. Read it as a modest pooled bet on a pre-competitive input, not a strategic pivot.

Here's the tension worth naming. Biohub calls the output, in its words, "an open resource for the research community." But a secondary outlet, American Bazaar, reports that investors "will receive an initial period of exclusive access to some data," for an unstated length. Biohub's own announcement neither confirms nor denies that. So carry both claims and pick neither. If the head start is real, the three funders get first look at the training data before smaller platforms and academics do. That cuts against a purely pre-competitive reading.

There's a second tension, at Isomorphic Labs. Last week, Isomorphic told a Semafor forum that all of its AI models are locked down in-house. Now it co-funds a shared data effort. The coherent reading is a split by layer. Data infrastructure is treated as pre-competitive, and models are kept proprietary. For smaller AI-drug-discovery platforms, that carries a conditional risk. If the frontier labs subsidize shared biology data, a proprietary dataset may become a weaker moat over time. That depends entirely on what the initiative actually releases, and when.

Isomorphic is also the funder with the most riding on whether better models reach patients, and its own clock is running. In January, Reuters reported Demis Hassabis saying that Isomorphic "expects to have its first clinical trials by the end of 2026." An independent analysis by BioPharmaTrend found that as of September 30th, Isomorphic had not announced an IND filing or a trial start. That leaves under three months against the company's own target. And at that same Semafor forum, Isomorphic declined to give a trial timeline at all.

Be fair about what this proves. A private company has no obligation to announce an IND when it files one. So the absence of an announcement is not proof of a miss. This is a watch item through December 31st, not a verdict today. And IND-enabling work is slow for any molecule, however it was designed. So even a slip would tell you more about the bench than about the model.

If models aren't the scarce asset, you'd expect them to change hands cheaply. Recursion just priced one. On September 15th, Recursion amended its existing master agreement with Tempus and licensed out its TxFM foundation model. The filing calls it "a non-exclusive, non-sublicensable, non-transferable, worldwide license to the Company's TxFM Model solely for use within the field of oncology." Tempus pays a twelve million dollar fee in two installments. Set that against Recursion's guided cash opex of under three hundred seventy-five million dollars. That works out to roughly three percent of one year's burn.

Note what kind of deal this is. It's an existing partner extending an existing relationship, not new outside demand for the model. And the filing excerpt discloses no royalty or milestone economics beyond the flat fee. Pharma has been renting AI from vendors on cancellable, back-loaded terms. This is the same pattern seen from the seller's side.

Two readings compete. The first says non-exclusivity and the oncology-only field cap the price. On that view, design capability is close to parity across platforms, so nobody pays up for exclusivity. The second says Recursion keeps the right to license TxFM again, field by field. On that view, twelve million dollars may be the first of several rentals, not the model's full value. Either way, the lesson holds. Licensing a foundation model is a real revenue line for a platform company. It cannot replace a clinical milestone as the funding path.

So if data and validation are the scarce inputs, who gets paid regardless? The vendor selling validation speed. This week, Quartz reported that Danaher is building an autonomous antibody discovery lab at its Abcam unit. It uses AI to propose, build, and test new antibodies in a closed loop, and it's expected to operate at scale in early 2027. The stated aim is to cut the time from idea to validated reagent by "up to 8 times." Treat that as a target, not a demonstrated result. And these are research reagents, not drug candidates, so it says nothing about clinical odds.

Here's the contrast with the platforms. Recursion and Isomorphic are exposed to whether their own models and molecules win. Danaher is exposed to the total volume of AI-driven design work across the industry, whichever model wins. That is the cleaner tools-and-instruments read-through. Design compresses bench iteration without removing the bench, and that favors high-throughput validation capacity. For cloud and compute providers, today's signal is thinner. The industry money in the Biohub effort is aimed at datasets and measurement, not at training runs.

If biological data is the constraint upstream, the same question applies downstream, in patient data. Here the honest answer is that this week brought nothing new. There was no fresh development on mining electronic health records, on stitching together the fragmented record formats hospitals adopted, or on HIPAA as a barrier. The one thread worth noting runs through Tempus, the licensee in the Recursion deal. Tempus expanded a multi-year biomarker-discovery collaboration with Merck in March. With TxFM, it now pairs a licensed biology foundation model with its own clinical and molecular data. The standing read is that real-world data pays off today in narrow, data-dense settings like oncology. There it helps with eligibility criteria, external comparators, and new indications. The binding constraint is less HIPAA than the cost of cleaning and linking messy records. The same split runs through the models. Language models carry the document and operations work. Every item today, by contrast, is a bet on specialized biology models trained on measured data.

Pulling the threads together, three open questions. What do the Biohub initiative's primary terms say about data access, and is the reported exclusivity window real? Does Isomorphic announce an IND before year-end? And does Recursion sign a second field license for TxFM, which would show whether twelve million dollars is a one-off or a pattern?

On clinical success rates, nothing in today's items moves the odds at the Phase 2 wall. The nearest lever on those odds is patient data used to design better trials, and that lever saw no news this week. What today's items do show is where the capital went. It went upstream, into the data and validation that sit before the model. Expect R&D spend on that upstream layer to keep growing, whichever foundation model leads the league tables. Better inputs are a necessary step toward better odds. They are not yet evidence of them.
