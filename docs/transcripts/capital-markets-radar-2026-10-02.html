Cautious Optimism Briefings

Capital Markets Cross-Asset Radar

Transcript · October 2, 2026

Good morning, it's Friday, October 2nd, and here's your Capital Markets Radar.

Yesterday this desk set today's payrolls as the test of whether the long end could finally fall on calming news. Thursday's tape gave a partial answer before the jobs number even landed. The long end did fall. It just fell from a new high.

Start with the ten-year. It climbed to 5.3 percent on Thursday, a new multidecade high, then eased back to 5.24 percent, according to Yahoo Finance. That give-back matters. It's the first session in this run where the long end set a new high and then surrendered ground within the day. Until now, it simply ground higher through every calming input. But don't call it a turn. That 5.24 percent is roughly where the ten-year sat on Tuesday. So the honest read is sideways-to-higher, with one crack in the pattern. What would confirm a real turn? A close back below Tuesday's level on a soft data day, not just an intraday fade from a spike.

The Fed gave the market little to lean on. Vice Chair Philip Jefferson spoke on Thursday. His quoted line was that he and his colleagues "will need to come to our own judgment, which may take more time." That's a statement about patience, not a hawkish signal. More time cuts both ways. It could mean waiting for proof before hiking again. It could equally mean waiting for proof before standing down. The market may be over-reading any single Fed voice as a signal. The fair framing for October 28 is a genuinely open hike-or-hold meeting, not one the market has already called. So what this means: duration risk hasn't resolved. It has only gotten noisier, and that noise is now showing up in other assets.

The clearest place is the dollar, because the tightening impulse didn't stay in the bond market. The dollar index traded around 102.18 on Thursday, a fresh year-to-date high, per FXStreet. That came on top of a gain of just over 2 percent in September. FXStreet's explanation is resilient growth, a firm labour market, and stubborn inflation feeding expectations of further Fed tightening. In its words, elevated Treasury yields also increase the appeal of dollar-denominated assets.

Note what that explanation doesn't tell you. A dollar rallying on US growth outperformance is a very different signal from one rallying on a funding squeeze. We don't have cross-currency basis or emerging-market spread data to separate the two. So treat the funding channel as a mechanism to watch, not as stress we can observe. The mechanism itself is straightforward. A stronger dollar tightens conditions for offshore and leveraged borrowers who fund in dollars. It also pressures anything priced in dollars. One caveat on durability: FXStreet flags the index as overbought on momentum. Part of this move could mean-revert even if the Fed stays hawkish.

Gold is the useful cross-check, and it's behaving oddly. Fortune had gold at 4,177 dollars an ounce on Thursday morning. That was down just 36 dollars from the same hour a day earlier. It's also roughly where this desk left gold yesterday. Fortune doesn't tie the move to the dollar. That link is our read. And the gold and dollar snapshots come from different hours, so treat this as suggestive. Still, a metal this exposed to the dollar barely gave ground on the day the dollar hit a year-to-date high. That hints the slide is stalling rather than extending. So what this means: the dollar, not just the ten-year, is now the transmission belt for tighter conditions. Watch whether emerging-market currencies and other dollar-priced commodities show more stress than gold has so far.

So where is anything still outrunning that discount rate? In earnings, and specifically Micron's. The memory maker reported fiscal fourth-quarter adjusted earnings of 33.42 dollars a share, against 31.83 expected, per Yahoo Finance. Revenue of 54.23 billion dollars beat estimates by more than two and a half billion. The more important line looks forward. Per CNBC, more than 75 percent of Micron's fiscal 2027 shipments are already covered by supply and capacity agreements. That's hard evidence that AI memory demand is being contracted, not just forecast, even as financing tightens everywhere else.

Now the skeptical read. A beat this size is close to the market's base case for the AI trade, not a surprise to it. And "covered by supply and capacity agreements" tells you about volume. It doesn't tell you about locked-in price, and we don't have the contract terms. There's also a cost side. Micron's scarcity is a cost line for the GPU makers and server builders who buy that memory. And as this desk has argued before, one firm's contracted AI revenue is someone else's future depreciation. So the net effect isn't a lift for all AI hardware. It's wider dispersion between the suppliers capturing scarcity and the buyers paying for it.

Then there's positioning. Yesterday this desk cited Goldman's disclosure that AI memory stocks were a concentrated slice of its own prime brokerage financing book, as of the end of June. Goldman's data also had information technology as the most net-sold US sector for a fourth straight week. Micron is the fundamental test of that crowded trade. One open question follows. Yahoo's Thursday coverage had chip stocks gaining as yields fell. Was Micron, rather than rates, driving that rebound in risk? The data can't settle it. So what this means: Micron validates the AI capex thesis on fundamentals, but it doesn't reduce the trade's fragility. If anything, it pulls more money into the same memory names that were already the crowded financing exposure.

The other asset that's supposed to outrun rising rates is the speculative one, and right now it isn't moving. Bitcoin opened Thursday at 83,566 dollars, essentially flat on the day, per Yahoo Finance. That leaves it pinned below the 84,000 to 85,000 dollar band our Digital Money briefing has called the real test level. Into that stillness, Citigroup raised its twelve-month Bitcoin target to 113,000 dollars, up from 82,000. It lifted its Ethereum target as well. Citi cites, in the firm's words, "stronger crypto market activity, a more supportive macroeconomic backdrop and a resumption of exchange-traded fund inflows."

Both of Citi's premises are contestable. Start with the macro claim. This is the week the ten-year set a multidecade high and the dollar hit a year-to-date high. On its face, that isn't a supportive backdrop for long-duration speculative assets. Then the flow claim. Yesterday's Digital Money briefing reported that US spot Bitcoin ETFs broke a nine-day inflow streak on the quarter-close session, with a net outflow of about 149 million dollars. So the most recent flow data this show has reported cuts against Citi, not for it. Also note where Citi started. Its old target sat below where Bitcoin was already trading. Part of this raise may simply be a target catching up with the market, rather than a fresh bullish signal.

Now the case for Citi. Bitcoin held flat through the yield spike rather than selling off. That could mean crypto is partly decoupling from the rate sensitivity it showed in prior cycles. Or it could just mean volumes are thin ahead of a catalyst. Both readings are live, and the price alone can't separate them. So what this means: Citi is effectively betting that flows overwhelm the dollar and rate backdrop. If the dollar and yields keep rising together into October 28, Citi's macro premise is contradicted directly.

That brings us to what we're not reporting. September payrolls are due at 8:30 Eastern this morning, and they had not printed when this briefing was prepared. We won't speculate on the number. Once it's out, it becomes the next real input into whether October 28 leans toward a hike or a hold. September CPI follows on October 14th.

So into the weekend, watch two things. First, whether the ten-year holds below Thursday's 5.3 percent high or revisits it. Second, whether the dollar keeps climbing alongside it. If both rise together, tightening has real teeth, and both Citi's premise and gold's stall come under pressure. If yields fade and the dollar follows them lower, Thursday's crack starts to look like the beginning of a turn.

That's the Capital Markets Radar for October 2nd. Have a good Friday.
