Good morning. It's Thursday, July 30th, and this is your strategic power briefing.

The thread running through today is that the AI buildout's costs are compounding from three directions at once. The grid is metering it physically, the chips are getting more expensive rather than cheaper, and the inflation those two things help generate is keeping the Federal Reserve hawkish. Each constraint is now raising the cost of clearing the next one.

Start with the grid, because it produced the hardest evidence yet that power, not capital or chips, is the binding constraint on American AI infrastructure. On June 30th, Energy Secretary Chris Wright signed two emergency orders under Section 202(c) of the Federal Power Act, authorizing PJM Interconnection to force data centers and other large loads onto their own backup generators during a brutal multi-day heatwave. PJM is the country's largest grid, covering the Mid-Atlantic, parts of the Midwest, and North Carolina. The orders covered any facility with at least 50 megawatts of peak load, took effect at 11:59 p.m. that night, and ran through July 3rd. PJM's own justification was blunt: an imminent electric reliability emergency existed that threatened transmission reliability. And the numbers explain why. PJM was forecasting demand of 166,147 megawatts on July 2nd, which would have broken the grid's all-time record of 165,563 megawatts, set back in 2006. So federal regulators waived pollution limits and pushed data centers onto diesel and gas backup rather than risk a blackout. That is a real, already-effective emergency action, and it is the clearest sign this year that grid capacity, not chips or cash, is what actually gates AI buildout in America's largest power market.

Now, a second PJM story followed on July 27th, and it's important not to conflate it with the first. This was not another signed federal emergency order. It was PJM's own board directing its staff to file a new large-load curtailment framework with the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission for approval. As of now, FERC has not approved it, and it curtails nothing today. The proposal only targets new large loads, meaning any single site with at least 50 megawatts of cumulative peak load within a one-mile radius, and it applies specifically to sites that have not brought their own generation online or otherwise secured supply by June 1st, 2027. If FERC approves it, those uncovered new loads would face curtailment ahead of PJM's other emergency load-management steps during future shortages. Existing, already-interconnected data centers are not the target of this particular filing. PJM tied the proposal to its most recent capacity auction, for the 2028-2029 delivery year, which cleared 6,831 megawatts short of the system's reliability requirement, a gap PJM itself is flagging as still widening from earlier auction cycles. PJM also points to roughly 70 gigawatts of new large-load growth forecast by 2038, against only about 15 gigawatts of generation retired since 2022. So the honest read is this: one instrument, the June order, is a real, effective emergency measure already used twice this year. The other, the July board proposal, is a not-yet-approved rule that would eventually shift curtailment risk onto future data centers rather than backstop generation costs onto everyone else's electric bill. They're different tools, at different stages, but they point the same direction: PJM is trying to make new compute demand absorb its own reliability risk. Either way, the grid is now the rate-limiting input on American AI capacity, not the GPUs.

Here's where that constraint becomes a trade. If new compute has to bring its own power or risk being curtailed, hyperscalers stop waiting in the interconnection queue and start buying generation they control. That means behind-the-meter gas turbines and small modular reactors, co-located at the data center rather than plugged into a congested grid. The beneficiaries are the turbine and equipment makers already sitting on multi-year order backlogs, names like GE Vernova and Bloom Energy, and the read-through points PJM's capacity prices higher for the 2027 and 2028 delivery years, which flows straight to the utilities and independent power producers that clear those auctions. The reliability problem, in other words, is quietly becoming someone's revenue line. But it cuts the other way too. Forcing data centers onto diesel and waiving pollution limits hands local ratepayers and environmental groups a live grievance, and that raises the political and legal risk of siting the next data center in PJM territory at all. So the buildout doesn't just need power. It needs power that doesn't provoke a backlash.

That same input logic runs straight into semiconductors. TSMC announced on July 16th an incremental $100 billion investment in US manufacturing, bringing its total American pledge across 2024 through 2026 to $265 billion. That buys four more advanced fabs in Arizona, for manufacturing and packaging, bringing TSMC's total US fab count to twelve. The company has already been awarded $6.6 billion in CHIPS Act funding. This is the largest reshoring commitment of the chip war so far, and it's tempting to read it as proof American semiconductor capacity is arriving. Resist that. A pledge is not a wafer. The open question is still how much genuinely leading-edge, high-volume output Arizona is producing versus how much remains promised. TSMC's Taiwan fabs remain ahead on process-node leadership for now, and that gap, not the dollar figure, is what determines whether this reshoring push changes the strategic picture or just adds a very expensive insurance policy. The lesson from the grid story applies here too: watch construction and output, not announcements.

Both of those constraints, expensive power and expensive chips, feed directly into inflation, which brings us to the Fed. The FOMC voted nine to three on July 29th to hold its benchmark rate at 3.50 to 3.75 percent. What's new is the dissent: three regional bank presidents, Cleveland's Beth Hammack, Minneapolis's Neel Kashkari, and Dallas's Lorie Logan, all preferred an immediate quarter-point hike instead. A three-way hawkish dissent bloc on the FOMC is unusual, and reporting frames it as the sharpest split in years. The linkage to tariffs and energy costs is qualitative rather than quantified in what's been confirmed so far, but the direction is clear enough: price pressure that isn't fading is now producing open disagreement inside the Fed about whether policy is tight enough. For the buildout story, that closes the loop. A higher-for-longer rate path raises the financing cost of exactly the data-center and grid infrastructure spending this briefing just walked through. The grid is the physical constraint, the chips are the cost constraint, and now the rate path is the capital constraint, all reinforcing each other rather than offsetting.

There's a longer-tail read in that dissent, too. Inflation has now run above the Fed's two percent target for more than five years, and a visible hawkish minority openly arguing that policy may still be too loose is exactly the backdrop that keeps the gold and Bitcoin hard-money narrative alive. When the credibility of the institution charged with fighting inflation is being contested inside the room where rates are set, hard assets tend to get a bid. That's not a forecast, it's a mechanism, and it's worth watching alongside the rate path itself.

Put together, the strategic read is this. The United States is not short on ambition or announced capital for AI infrastructure, it's short on physically deliverable power, and the institutions responsible for allocating that power are moving, cautiously and not yet definitively, toward making new demand bear its own reliability risk. Watch FERC's ruling on PJM's proposal, watch Arizona's output numbers against TSMC's pledges, and watch whether the Fed's hawkish minority grows. Each of those will tell you whether this is a temporary squeeze or a durable repricing of what AI actually costs to build.
