Good morning, it's Saturday, September 12th, and this is your private equity briefing.

For a week this show has argued that the exit window is the binding constraint on this asset class. Distributions are frozen, the argument goes, because sellers cannot get out. The UK has just produced the one thing that argument has been missing. A real, competed, published clearing price. Twice, in the space of a week.

Start with Bodycote, the British thermal-processing firm. Apollo proposed roughly one point five two billion pounds for it and then walked away in June. Two months later, Veritas Capital and CVC both arrived with competing bids. Veritas offered 914 pence a share, CVC 915. In the first week of September the board backed Veritas at 940 pence, made up of 932.8 pence in cash plus a 7.2 pence interim dividend. That is a 34.5 percent premium to where the stock traded before takeover speculation emerged in May, and it values Bodycote at one point six five billion pounds of equity, one point eight five billion including debt. Notice the shape of that sequence. The price went up, in public, because rival buyers pushed it up. That is what a functioning market looks like — on the buy side.

The same week produced a second one. Epiris agreed to buy the telecoms services group Gamma Communications at 1,120 pence a share. That is a 53 percent premium to Gamma's undisturbed price on April 7th. It ends a four-month contest that had also drawn Providence Equity and a consortium of Oakley Capital and Giacom. Three sponsor groups, one asset, four months, before a clearing price emerged.

One discipline before we build anything on either deal. Both are agreed, not completed. Each remains subject to shareholder and regulatory approval and to court sanction of the scheme. No cash has changed hands yet.

Now the callback. On September 8th this show flagged that UK takeover premiums were running near 44 percent on average, up from 40 percent a year earlier. That figure is AJ Bell's, measured against roughly 70 billion pounds of announced UK deal value as of early August. The warning attached to it was that sponsors chasing the UK's valuation discount to US peers were spending that discount as fast as they found it. Bodycote and Gamma are that warning with names on it. A 34.5 percent premium and a 53 percent premium are not evidence of cheap entry. They are the cost of entry, paid up front. That cost now has to be earned back through operations, in a cycle where leverage and multiple expansion have stopped doing the heavy lifting for sponsor returns. And there is a second point underneath the first. Two competed deals add two companies to the industry's unsold inventory. They do not remove two. Deployment was never the constraint here. Realization is.

Which brings us to Apollo's own read on that constraint. Scott Kleinman, Apollo's co-president, was quoted on September 9th putting it in six words. Exits aren't the problem, pricing is. Take that in its strongest form for a moment. If it is true, the fix for the industry's distribution drought is not a reopened IPO market. It is sellers accepting that their carrying values are wrong. But I want to be direct about how thin the sourcing is. What we have is a headline, not a transcript. The underlying article is paywalled, and we could not confirm the venue where Kleinman actually said it. No figure, no deal example, and no size for this supposed bid-ask gap has surfaced anywhere. There is also an obvious interest to flag. Apollo is a buyer waiting on the other side of that gap. A framing in which the problem is sellers holding out at stale marks flatters the party who benefits when those sellers finally capitulate.

It is tempting to read Apollo's own Bodycote walk-away as proof of exactly that price discipline. Resist it. Secondary reporting on the withdrawal describes Apollo's official statement as giving no reason at all. It said Apollo continued to hold Bodycote and its management in high regard, and thanked the board for engaging. That is boilerplate, and we could not retrieve the primary filing to verify it word for word. So we do not actually know why Apollo left. The sequence of bids is the fact. Apollo proposed roughly one point five two billion pounds, and the company was then agreed away at one point six five billion. Whether that gap reflects Apollo's discipline or something else entirely is an inference this show cannot make on the evidence in hand. And Kleinman's framing should not be allowed to supply, after the fact, a reason Apollo itself never gave.

Here is the sharper point underneath all of it. If pricing really is the constraint, then it matters enormously who gets to set the price. In Bodycote and Gamma, two and three independent bidders set it, competing against each other in public. Compare that with the channel now absorbing most of the industry's exit volume. That is the secondaries market, and specifically the GP-led continuation vehicle, where a manager sells an asset it already owns into a vehicle it also controls. Take the scale from Jefferies, whose review of 2025 is the last full year with figures we can stand behind. Total secondaries volume hit 240 billion dollars, up 48 percent year over year, priced on average at 87 percent of reported net asset value. But the number that is actually new here is penetration. Single-asset continuation vehicles crossed half of all continuation-vehicle volume for the first time, and nearly 80 percent of the hundred largest sponsors have now run at least one. That is the real story. GP-set pricing is not a cyclical workaround anymore. It is a structural feature of how this asset class returns capital. And a price set by the same manager standing on both sides of the trade is simply not the same kind of evidence about what an asset is worth as a price set by rival bidders fighting for it. Worth adding that Jefferies advises on many of the transactions it is counting, so this is not disinterested market research.

One correction while we are on this. In an earlier episode this show used a figure for first-half 2026 secondaries volume, north of a hundred billion dollars, with GP-led activity up sharply. On closer look, that specific first-half number traces only to low-provenance content sites with no named data provider behind it. It does not trace to Jefferies or to any bank we can stand behind. We are withdrawing it. The only 2026 figure Jefferies itself has published is a projection, expected first-half volume exceeding 100 billion dollars, and a projection is not an actual. That is the second time in a week this show has had to pull a secondaries number. The pattern tells you something on its own. The market whose statistics we rely on to judge liquidity is itself priced off self-reported marks, and its own reporting inherits that same opacity.

So where does the cycle actually stand. The entry side is competitive and getting more expensive. Sponsors are fighting over a narrow shortlist of quality UK assets and paying up to win them. The exit side still has no comparable contest. It has continuation vehicles pricing against their own marks. And the one senior executive willing to say out loud that the constraint is pricing rather than access is, whether he means to or not, telling you the marks are the problem. That is a worse diagnosis for limited partners than a shut window. A shut window reopens. An inflated mark means the number the distribution shortfall is measured against is itself too high. Watch three things from here. Whether either UK scheme draws a late counterbid or an interloper. Whether any named data provider publishes verified first-half 2026 secondaries actuals. And what the big managers say about the pace of realizations when third-quarter earnings land in late October.
