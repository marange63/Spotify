Good morning. It's Saturday, October 10th, and this is your digital money briefing.

Yesterday we told you to watch one level: whether bitcoin held eighty-three thousand dollars, a floor that had stood since late September. It didn't hold. Bitcoin printed eighty thousand, three hundred fifty dollars on Bitstamp, its lowest level since September 18th. That changes the job the level does. What was a floor now becomes overhead supply on the way back up. Any rally into the low eighties is a rally into resistance, not a breakout.

Start with the anatomy of the drop, because the mechanics matter more than the headline. CoinGlass logged one point oh nine billion dollars in crypto liquidations in the twenty-four hours to ten a-m UTC Friday. That's one aggregator's count, not an audited market-wide total. Longs accounted for one point oh five billion of it. On our arithmetic, that's about ninety-six percent longs. So this was a long squeeze, not fresh bearish positioning.

That composition is also the bull case. A flush that was ninety-six percent longs clears out forced sellers. Once that leverage is gone, the next move down has to come from someone choosing to sell, not from an exchange auto-liquidating a position. And the spot bid did show up. Bitcoin recovered to around eighty-two thousand five hundred dollars on Friday, more than two thousand dollars off the low. That's an intraday level, not a daily close. Still, it's a structurally cleaner setup than the one going into Thursday.

The same flush grades our own coverage. Earlier this month we warned of a crowded long book, then downgraded that call to deleveraging into strength. Two days ago we called the October 7th liquidation day, about five hundred fifty million dollars, contained rather than a cascade. Thursday's flush was roughly twice that size, and it was not contained. The lesson is how quickly "slowly deleveraging" becomes "forcibly deleveraged" once price gives it a push. As for what gave that push, the macro trigger is still unverified for a third session, so we won't name one.

With the leverage gone, the risk shifts to supply. Discretionary supply comes from two places. One is ETF holders, who pulled four hundred eighty-seven million dollars on October 7th alone, the most since June 25th, per SoSoValue. Whether they came back on Friday, we can't yet confirm from a quotable source. The other source of supply is newer, and it's the one that made headlines this week.

A wallet that Arkham tags as holding Bitfinex-hack seizure funds moved about twelve thousand two hundred sixty-seven bitcoin. That's worth roughly one point oh one billion dollars. The headline revived the standing question: does the government's seized bitcoin ever hit the market? Here's the nuance that matters. Per CoinDesk, no exchange deposit was recorded for that transfer. It moved wallet to wallet, a pattern more consistent with reshuffling than selling. So don't read this as the government liquidating a billion dollars of bitcoin.

What the evidence does say is narrower, and more interesting. A separate, smaller set of transfers from Bitfinex- and FTX-linked wallets did land in Coinbase Prime deposit addresses the day before. That was roughly three thousand two hundred bitcoin, about two hundred sixty-four million dollars, plus one hundred nineteen million in USDT. Even that isn't a clean sell signal. Prime is a custody platform as well as a trading venue, and CoinDesk notes a deposit there doesn't necessarily mean a sale.

So here's the signal to watch. It isn't the size of a wallet move. It's whether coins land on an exchange's trading side. The reserve order says forfeited bitcoin isn't to be sold, but it doesn't name these coins, so a sale can't be ruled out. A confirmed deposit to a trading book would be a discrete supply event, and a thin bid would have to absorb it. We haven't seen one yet.

That raises the other half of the supply question: who has the dollars to absorb it. On crypto venues, the cash leg is largely stablecoins. Notice that even those Coinbase Prime transfers carried USDT alongside the bitcoin. And the stablecoin pool is not growing. Total stablecoin supply was three hundred four point two billion dollars on September 29th, per a third-quarter industry report. That's about sixteen billion below May's peak of three hundred twenty point six billion. So this flush hit a market whose dollar layer is smaller than it was in the spring. Less idle stablecoin float means less dry powder sitting on venues to buy forced or discretionary supply. That's plausibly one reason a billion-dollar long squeeze could punch through a floor that had held for weeks.

The yield-bearing side of on-chain cash tells a different story, but a small one. The tokenized Treasury and real-world asset market stood at about fourteen point eight eight billion dollars as of October 6th, per RWA data. The three largest tokens are Circle's USYC, Ondo's USDY, and BlackRock's BUIDL, each between two and two and a half billion. On our arithmetic, that whole category is under five percent of stablecoin supply. It's real infrastructure, but it's a rounding error next to the stablecoin float.

Here's the capital-markets read. Stablecoin reserves and tokenized funds both largely sit in short-dated government paper. A stablecoin pool sixteen billion below its peak means this channel isn't adding net Treasury bill demand right now. And tokenized funds under fifteen billion can't offset that. So if you've been counting crypto as a structural new buyer of T-bills, the latest data say that bid has stalled. On-chain dollar liquidity is below its spring level, not expanding. That matters in both directions: less new bill demand for Treasury, and less dollar cushion under bitcoin when the next seller shows up.

The listed market is the last place this shows up, and its verdict is just as unsettled. Coinbase stock closed Friday at one hundred seventy-nine dollars and thirty-nine cents, up about four point three percent on the rebound day. That still leaves it near the bottom of its fifty-two week range of roughly one thirty-nine to four oh two. Per reported target changes, the Street can't agree on what it's worth. Goldman Sachs raised its target to two hundred forty-four dollars from two hundred nineteen. Barclays raised its target to one hundred forty-nine dollars from ninety-five. Barclays' new number still sits about thirty dollars below where the stock closed. A rising target that remains under the share price tells you a bear case is getting less bearish, not that a bull case has formed.

And the four percent pop is easy to over-read. Our inference is that liquidation-heavy, flush-and-rebound days lift trading volume, and trading volume is Coinbase's engine. So one day's gain on a day bitcoin bounced looks like beta plus a volume trade, not a re-rating on Coinbase's own merits.

So here's where that leaves us. The leverage that drove this week's flush is largely gone, which is constructive. The level we were watching broke, which is not. The risk that matters next is discretionary supply, from ETF holders and from government wallets that keep moving without confirming a sale. And the dollar cushion that would absorb that supply, the stablecoin float, is still smaller than it was in May. If the next drawdown comes, expect it to be supply-driven, not liquidation-driven.

Watch three things from here. First, whether bitcoin reclaims eighty-three thousand on a daily close, not an intraday poke. Second, whether any government-tagged wallet sends coins to an exchange's trading side rather than a custody address. Third, September's CPI print, due October 14th. It's the first scheduled macro test since the flush, and the first real chance to see whether rates, rather than positioning, are driving this market.
