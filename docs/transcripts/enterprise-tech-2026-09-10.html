Good morning. It's September tenth. This is your enterprise technology briefing.

For five straight editions of this beat, we found the same shape. A company reports an AI product line growing far faster than the business around it, and the growth rate of the business itself never moves. This week, one vendor's AI line finally moved the growth rate. It moved it down. That's the story worth building the whole briefing around today.

Start with ServiceTitan, the field-service software vendor, which reported its fiscal second quarter on September 8th. The quarter as delivered was fine. Revenue came in at 293 million dollars, up 21 percent year over year. Gross transaction volume, the total dollar flow processed through its platform, was 26.8 billion dollars, up 17 percent. Net dollar retention, by the company's own words, "remained above 110 percent." None of that reads like a company in trouble.

Here's the turn. ServiceTitan is rolling out an AI pricing product called Max. Enrollment has climbed to more than 700 locations so far this fiscal year, up from just over 200. That adoption curve is real. But the way Max gets billed created a two to three million dollar drag on subscription revenue this quarter, tied to how that revenue gets recognized. The company guided the current quarter to 285 to 287 million dollars, and the full fiscal year to between 1,139 and 1,144 million dollars.

The market did not shrug this off. Shares fell roughly 7 percent in the regular session, then dropped about 12 percent more in extended trading that evening. The decline continued into the next session, taking the stock down somewhere in the range of a quarter to nearly a third of its value across those two days. I want to be precise about the sourcing. That combined decline is pieced together from several outlets describing different trading sessions. It is not one clean, exchange-confirmed print, so treat the magnitude as directionally right but approximate.

There's a second sourcing problem, and it cuts at the story rather than the number. At least one outlet frames the same selloff differently. In its words, ServiceTitan shares fell in extended trading after results that topped Wall Street's estimates but shook up its management team. If a leadership change is doing part of the work, then this is not a clean natural experiment in how markets treat AI-transition costs. Hold the interpretation that follows loosely for that reason.

With that caveat attached, do the arithmetic the market apparently wasn't doing. Two to three million dollars against 293 million dollars of quarterly revenue is under one percent. That is not a selloff you can explain by the line item itself. What most likely got repriced is something else. It is the market's confidence in forward guidance from a vendor whose own numbers just proved malleable to a product decision. And revenue-recognition timing, by definition, defers revenue rather than destroying it. If enrollment holds, this drag is a candidate to reverse into a tailwind next quarter or the one after. ServiceTitan's release doesn't say either way. That's an analytical inference on my part, not something the company disclosed.

There's also a competing explanation that deserves equal weight. Gross transaction volume grew 17 percent while revenue grew 21 percent. That is a transaction base which may already have been decelerating independent of Max, in which case the AI pivot is the excuse, not the cause. What would resolve it is ServiceTitan disclosing a Max-specific ARR or bookings figure, separate from total subscription revenue. Notably, it chose not to. The release gives location counts for Max and no dollar figure anywhere. That's worth sitting with. The vendor most exposed to a checkable AI cost is also the one withholding the one number that would let anyone verify whether the drag is temporary.

That's the vendor punished for publishing a checkable cost. Now the vendor rewarded for publishing a number nobody can check at all: Salesforce.

Salesforce's August 26th quarterly release disclosed something new to this beat. It reported 7.0 billion Agentic Work Units delivered to date across its Agentforce and Slack products, with 3.2 billion of those landing in a single quarter. That's nearly half the all-time total in one three-month period. Against that, current remaining performance obligation grew 14 percent. That's the forward-booked revenue commitment, and it's the closest thing to a leading indicator here. So an enormous, accelerating activity metric sits next to a modest, steady booking number.

Push on the unit itself. An Agentic Work Unit is defined entirely by Salesforce. It carries no published dollar value and no published definition of what constitutes one unit. It spans two products, Agentforce and Slack, that are priced differently from each other. A metric that can jump by 3.2 billion in a quarter while forward bookings move 14 percent is doing one of two things. Either it is counting something with negligible revenue attached to each unit, or it is counting something Salesforce doesn't charge for yet. To be fair, that's not necessarily damning. In usage-based businesses, consumption metrics genuinely do run ahead of monetization, and an early-stage product line will look exactly like this by design. The honest read is that Agentic Work Units are unfalsifiable as a revenue signal, not that they're fabricated.

Line that up against where this beat has been all week. First it was customer counts. Then account counts. Then deal counts with no total attached. Now it's an abstract unit of work with no price and no denominator. Each rung has climbed further from a dollar figure, not closer to one.

One more comparison closes the loop, and it's a callback rather than new information. CrowdStrike, the cybersecurity vendor, has its own consumption-bundling vehicle, Falcon Flex. Ending annual recurring revenue from accounts that have adopted it grew 101 percent year over year, to 2.29 billion dollars. This show has already covered that figure, and already made the caveat that goes with it: the growth rate is flattered by a small prior-year base and by existing renewals rolling into the Flex envelope. Here's the point that matters today. Falcon Flex and ServiceTitan's Max are the same underlying move, repackaging a product around consumption. But they carry opposite signs on the income statement. Falcon Flex pulls customer spend forward into contracted, recognized ARR, and CrowdStrike raised its full-year outlook. Max defers revenue recognition, and ServiceTitan's quarter looked fine while its stock did not. The strategy is identical. The accounting sign is not, and that sign is most of the difference in how the market treated these two companies.

So what does a rational enterprise software vendor take from this week? Publish an adoption count with no denominator, and you get paid. Publish an actual dollar cost of your AI transition, and you get punished, even when that cost is under one percent of revenue. That is a bad incentive for anyone trying to underwrite these transitions from the outside, whether you're an investor or a customer negotiating a contract. If you're the customer, read the mechanics as leverage. A vendor deferring revenue to drive enrollment is a vendor willing to discount enrollment right now. That argues for negotiating contract flexibility rather than locking into multi-year pricing on a product whose pricing model is still being rebuilt. The one disclosure that would actually settle these arguments is a vendor breaking out its AI product's own ARR as a standalone line. Nobody has done it yet. Watch for who does it first.
