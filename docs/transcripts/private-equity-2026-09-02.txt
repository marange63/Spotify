Good morning, it's Wednesday, September 2nd, and this is your private equity briefing.

For a week now, this show has made one argument about the private-equity exit problem. Assets get priced by whoever controls the sale. Continuation vehicles let the sponsor set the number. NAV loans let the sponsor manufacture a distribution without selling anything at all. On Sunday and Monday, that pattern broke, at least partially. Apollo and its co-investor Triton agreed to sell Kelvion, a data-center and industrial cooling manufacturer, to the oilfield-services giant SLB for four point one billion dollars. That's roughly three point four billion in cash plus about seven hundred million of assumed debt. An outside strategic buyer set that price. Not another sponsor, and not a continuation vehicle. This is the arm's-length exit the industry has been waiting for, and it's real.

It's also not done. The deal was announced August 31st, and it doesn't close until the first half of 2027. Apollo disclosed no entry price in that release. So treat this as a pending exit, not cash in a limited partner's account, and don't count a dollar of it toward anybody's distributions this year.

Here's what sharpens the picture, though. Trade-press reporting at the time, not an Apollo release, has Apollo agreeing to buy Kelvion from Triton in August of last year, in a deal valuing the business at roughly two billion euros including debt. That purchase closed in mid-January. So Apollo held this asset for something like seven months before agreeing to sell it for four point one billion dollars.

You can't build a clean multiple out of that. Different currencies, different debt bases, and no confirmation of what capital Apollo injected along the way. But a seven-month move from closing to sale announcement is fast for a control buyout. And it raises a real question. Was Apollo developing this business, or did it buy Kelvion already expecting a strategic bidder to show up for the data-center cooling story?

Either way, that's the tell. The buyer that showed up wanted a data-center narrative, and Kelvion had one. Exits are clearing for assets with an AI-infrastructure label attached to them. Which means realizations are becoming narrative-dependent rather than purely performance-dependent. If your portfolio company can be credibly repositioned as a data-center supplier, you have a buyer. If it can't, you don't. Spread that across a whole fund, and the distribution of cash back to investors starts to depend on storytelling as much as on operating results.

Now the same firm's other hand, one day earlier. Apollo funds are putting nine billion dollars behind ONEOK's four point four two five billion dollar acquisition of Brazos Midstream's Permian gas-gathering assets, structured as nonvoting minority equity. Walk the arithmetic slowly, because it's the real story here. Nine billion dollars of Apollo capital against a four point four billion dollar asset purchase. Most of that check isn't buying the Permian assets at all. Five billion dollars of it is earmarked specifically to pay down ONEOK's existing debt.

And the return on all of it is capped at a seven percent internal rate of return for the first nine years. Apollo's own release adds that the cap doesn't simply expire. It steps up. Seven point three five percent in year ten, and a final cap of seven point eight five percent in year fifteen. ONEOK also holds a call option starting in year eight to buy Apollo out, at a price reflecting that same capped return. There's no disclosed path to voting control and no open-ended upside. That's not a buyout. That's underwriting priced like credit, wearing an equity label.

And according to Bloomberg's reporting on the deal — not Apollo's own release, which names only funds and affiliates managed by Apollo — the capital behind this is partly Athene, Apollo's insurance subsidiary, plus third-party insurers and other Apollo and client funds. Bloomberg also reports that Apollo is slicing the nine billion dollars into tranches by seniority. The stake is being converted into investment-grade-rated debt securities, held inside a new holding company that sits structurally subordinate to ONEOK's existing senior debt. Hold that attribution loosely. It's press reporting, not a company filing.

But if it's right, what looks like a private-equity minority investment is really an insurance balance sheet buying rated paper. The relevant competitor here isn't another buyout sponsor. It's a corporate bond desk. The pricing pressure comes from spreads rather than from rival bidders. And scale that format up, and manager economics separate further from fund performance. The firm earns on deployed capital, while the equity risk and the equity upside sit somewhere else entirely. That is a different business from the one carried interest was built to reward.

So if a strategic buyer will pay full freight for a thematically-branded asset, and an insurance balance sheet will underwrite a capped-return infrastructure deal, what happens to everything without a story attached? Two answers, both visible in the last two weeks.

First, the public market. Lyntris, Trive Capital's industrial roll-up, priced its IPO on August 18th at seventeen fifty a share, below a range of nineteen to twenty-two dollars. It cut the deal to seventeen million shares from twenty-four million in the prospectus, raising two hundred ninety-seven and a half million dollars. IPO pricing trackers put that at about a forty percent cut in deal size. Hold those decimals loosely, because this comes from a pricing tracker rather than a directly fetched filing. It's two weeks old, and it's still the freshest live read we have on the small-cap sponsor window. The window is open. The toll is roughly forty percent, and that's the toll for sellers without a strategic buyer waiting.

Second, the mid-market's negotiated alternative. Advent International signed a majority investment in New Zealand Clinical Research Group on August 20th, valued at around a billion dollars in local reporting. Existing owner Waterman Capital is rolling a minority stake instead of cashing out fully. No purchase multiple and no financing structure were disclosed, and the deal isn't expected to close until the fourth quarter. Read that rollover as a price signal, not a partnership gesture. It's usually the structure that gets a deal done when buyer and seller can't agree on the entry multiple. It bridges a valuation gap. It doesn't close one, and the seller stays exposed to the same asset.

And the assets with no strategic buyer, no IPO appetite and no negotiated majority sale go back into the sponsor-controlled channel this show has tracked all week. Single-asset continuation vehicles — one trophy company, not a diversified tail — now make up sixty-two percent of GP-led secondaries volume, pricing at an average discount to net asset value of just two point nine percent. We already covered the record secondaries totals last week. What's new is one layer down. The concentration has migrated from the channel into single hand-picked assets. And near-par pricing on a sponsor's chosen trophy tells you about selection, not about whether the rest of the fund's marks are honest. The sponsor decides which asset to sell and when. The unselected holdings are exactly the ones nobody has priced.

So where does the cycle actually stand. The exit market isn't reopening broadly. It's reopening by buyer type, and the door that's open is narrow and thematic. For the mid-market sponsor with no strategic bidder and no viable listing, the liquidity problem is quietly converting into a duration problem. The asset gets held longer, by the same people, at the same mark.

Watch three things from here. Whether a second strategic pays full price for a sponsor-owned industrial on the same AI-infrastructure logic Kelvion just tested. Whether Apollo's ONEOK equity investment closes on schedule in the first half of this month, confirming the capital source and structure Bloomberg has described. And how September quarter-end marks land in late-October earnings.
