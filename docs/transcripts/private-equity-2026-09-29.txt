Good morning, it's Tuesday, September 29th, and this is your private equity briefing.

We start with a real-money test of the industry's favorite liquidity trick, and the trick failed. Blackstone has shelved Project Eclipse, a roughly three billion dollar collateralized fund obligation. It would have packaged about seven hundred fund interests from Blackstone's own secondaries business, Strategic Partners, into bonds and an equity tranche. The point was to return cash to investors in funds as old as twenty-plus years, without waiting for an actual sale. The collateral was old. Eight percent of those interests are at least twenty years old, and another fifteen percent are between fifteen and twenty. Buyers were offered senior paper at around seven and a half percent and junior debt at up to twelve percent, and they still balked. Blackstone had even offered to retain the entire first-loss tranche itself. Even with Blackstone holding the first loss, the reported economics didn't work for Blackstone. Two caveats. This comes via trade outlets relaying Bloomberg's reporting, not a Blackstone disclosure. And the deal is shelved in its current form, not formally abandoned.

The first-loss detail is the one to sit with. If buyers had simply been rejecting the junior risk, Blackstone keeping the first loss should have fixed it. It didn't. There are three explanations, and the evidence can't separate them. One, buyers doubt the marks on the fifteen-to-twenty-year-old tail. Two, buyers accept the marks but won't lever slow, uncertain cash flows at that structure's leverage. Three, the senior terms buyers would accept left too little for Blackstone once it had to hold the worst-case exposure. Don't over-read this as "buyers don't trust Blackstone's marks." The quoted objection was narrower: the long-dated nature of the assets, particularly given the higher leverage. That objection fits all three stories. Still, it leaves an open question about how holdings that old are carried on the books, if they can't be financed even with the sponsor in the first-loss seat.

Keep the counterweight in view. Evercore still projects collateralized fund obligation issuance topping thirty billion dollars in 2026, more than double last year. That's a projection, not realized volume. But one flagship deal stalling on the oldest, most leveraged collateral doesn't mean the market is shut. It means levering the tail has a ceiling. On Thursday we argued that a lender with its own capital at risk is one of the few parties doing real price discovery. Eclipse is that price discovery, and this time the answer was no.

So if you can't borrow against the oldest stakes, you sell them. What are sales actually clearing at? Lazard's interim 2026 secondary market report gives the first read on dispersion, and it's more interesting than a simple widening story. Start with multi-asset continuation funds. Forty percent of their volume priced below ninety percent of net asset value in the first half of this year, up from sixteen percent in 2025. Commentary summarizing the report calls that a direct penalty for portfolio complexity. That is a clean discount widening, and it belongs to multi-asset vehicles specifically.

Single-asset continuation funds tell a different story. Their middle is disappearing. The pricing band a handful of points below par shrank from about a fifth of single-asset volume to a twentieth. But that volume didn't all move down. The share pricing above par nearly tripled, to almost a quarter of volume. So single-asset deals are sorting into a favored group clearing at or above par, and a middle ground that is vanishing. One sourcing note: these bucket figures come through third-party commentary on the Lazard report, whose own text wasn't directly accessible. And Lazard, like Evercore, earns fees on the volume it tallies.

On Sunday this show cited an adviser tally putting the average single-asset discount at about three percent, and said that average told us nothing about dispersion. Now we know something. A tight average can sit on top of a split market. Buyers are no longer pricing single-asset vehicles as a class. They're pricing individual sponsors and individual assets, and paying up for the ones they believe in. That above-par slice deserves its own scrutiny. It means buyers are validating some managers' marks at a premium for a narrow set of assets.

Issuance keeps coming regardless. AEA Investors filed a new continuation fund, OMN CF, with Evercore last Tuesday. M-One Capital filed the Arabica Fund with Lazard the day before, likely tied to its Scooter's Coffee stake. Neither has disclosed a size. So the valve is open, and failed financings like Eclipse push more of the aging tail toward it. But the prices flowing through it are no longer uniform. For complex multi-asset portfolios, they're getting worse.

The regulator is looking at the same question from the other side. On Monday, the SEC's Office of the Chief Accountant and its Division of Investment Management issued a staff statement. It reminds registrants to value private assets rigorously and disclose their valuation judgments. It is explicitly a reminder of existing requirements, not new rules, and it names no funds. It is also framed around private credit inside registered funds, so it doesn't directly govern traditional buyout-fund marks. The staff's rationale is the growing accessibility of private assets to a broader investor base. That logic travels straight into private equity's retail push. Evergreen and semi-liquid vehicles price off appraised NAV and promise periodic liquidity. The more of their NAV sits in hard-to-sell secondary stakes, the more any tightening of valuation practice could reach reported returns and redemption capacity. Valuation rigor is becoming the price of admission to the wealth channel.

And when the cash doesn't come back on schedule, the people closest to the deals feel it too. Bloomberg reported last week that dealmakers are missing out on carry windfalls as firms hold companies longer, and some are looking elsewhere for steadier pay. In response, some firms are renegotiating carry. They are moving away from whole-fund European waterfalls toward deal-by-deal carry with shorter vesting. UK tax changes add to the pressure. The evidence is qualitative. There are no attrition numbers, and no tally of which firms have actually switched.

The mechanism is still worth naming. Deal-by-deal carry pays dealmakers earlier on winners, before losses elsewhere in the fund are netted against them. That's a rational repricing of a comp model whose cash timing has broken. It's also a small transfer of risk onto limited partners. They lean harder on clawbacks, and alignment weakens on how the fund's weaker holdings get managed, just as hold periods stretch. It also echoes a Blackstone move we covered Sunday. Joe Baratta, the longtime head of its buyout business, is leaving, and Blackstone says it won't replace his role. Our read then was a franchise leaning on durable fees rather than star dealmakers, and carry is the part that waits. The backdrop is stark, though the best number we have is dated. According to an Allianz research summary, buyout distributions ran around six percent of assets in the year to June 2025, against a ten-year average near fourteen percent. That's the latest full-year read available, and it's more than a year old. Even so, it suggests a retention fix is arriving when limited partners have the least leverage to push back.

So where does the cycle actually stand? This was the week engineered liquidity got tested and partly refused. Blackstone couldn't lever its oldest stakes even while offering to hold the first loss. Secondary pricing is deepening discounts on complex portfolios while a narrow slice of single assets clears above par. The regulator is naming valuation rigor as the cost of the retail channel. And dealmakers' pay is being restructured in a way that quietly shifts risk toward limited partners. The binding constraint is still the exit-and-distribution logjam, now paired with a pricing-dispersion problem underneath it. Watch three things. Whether Eclipse comes back resized. Whether second-half secondary data confirms the split. And late October's alternative-manager earnings, for realized carry against fee income.

That's the state of the machine. Thanks for listening, and I'll see you next time.
