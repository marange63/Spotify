Good morning. It's Monday, October 12th, and this is Cautious Optimism Briefings, private credit.

Today's thesis in two sentences. The fight over whether private credit's marks mean anything now has a courtroom track, not just a supervisory one. And the same discretion that sets a mark also decides what counts as a default.

Start with the lawsuits. The suits were filed between May and July, and Semafor's reporting last week put three of them side by side. The first targets Ares Capital Corporation. A shareholder, Martin Siegel, sued derivatively in the Southern District of New York on May 26th. The defendant is the fund's adviser, Ares Capital Management, and the claims are breach of fiduciary duty and excessive fees. The second targets Blue Owl Technology Finance Corporation, the exchange-listed BDC known as OTF, over its adviser's fee structure. That suit was filed in July. The third is a derivative action against FS KKR Capital Corp. All three managers deny the allegations and describe their fee practices as standard for the industry. FS KKR says it will defend the matter vigorously. New York Fed supervisors have been examining private-credit collateral from the banks' side since the spring. These suits come at the same question from the investor's side. Can anyone trust the number a fund says a loan is worth?

Two examples carry the argument, and they need to be read differently. The first comes from Semafor. A janitorial-services borrower, Kellermeyer Bergensons Services, has debt marked anywhere from nearly 100 cents to about 10 cents on the dollar across FS KKR's book. That sounds damning, but there's an innocent reading. A first-out or senior piece can hold near par while a last-out or junior piece is close to wiped out. That's ordinary capital-structure behavior. The public record doesn't yet show which tranches carry which marks. So treat Kellermeyer as unproven either way.

The second example is sharper, and it sits inside the Ares complaint. It cites two first-lien loans to the software company Pluralsight, both held in Ares Capital's own book. One is marked at roughly par. The other is marked at roughly six cents on the dollar. The complaint says that shows the valuation process does not consistently reflect, quote, the economic reality of the borrower. Here the plaintiffs aren't pointing at a senior claim and a junior claim. They're pointing at two loans with the same first-lien label, to the same borrower, marked worlds apart. A shared label doesn't by itself rule out a structural difference between the two loans, and that is exactly what a court would have to test. No court has ruled on the merits in any of these cases, and none has reached discovery. The distinction still matters. A tranche story is a disclosure problem. Same-type dispersion with no structural explanation would be a valuation-process problem, and a far more serious one.

Now follow the fee. Per the Ares complaint, as summarized by Octus, payment-in-kind income was 34 percent of Ares Capital's 2025 net investment income, about 490 million dollars. The complaint alleges incentive fees are paid on that income when it's booked, regardless of whether it is later collected in cash. It also says advisory fees rose about 53 percent between 2021 and 2025, and calls them grossly excessive. The mechanism is simple. An incentive fee on accrued but uncollected income moves cash to the manager today, against income the fund may never receive. The obvious defense is that these fees are disclosed, not hidden. And some advisory agreements defer or claw back fees on PIK that never converts to cash. The Blue Owl complaint alleges that OTF's agreement contains no true clawback. That's the plaintiffs' reading of the contract, not quoted contract language, so it stays an open question.

Staying with Blue Owl, Semafor's headline numbers come with one attribution caveat. Semafor reports that more than a third of net investment income last year was PIK at what it calls Blue Owl's technology-focused lending fund. Semafor doesn't name the fund. OTF is the Blue Owl fund being sued over PIK-linked fees, so it is the likely referent, but that link is inferred, not stated. Semafor puts that fund's fee bill at 468 million dollars, including an estimated 62 million in incentive fees tied to PIK income. That's roughly an eighth of the fee bill. Those figures haven't been checked against a 10-K, so take them as reported. OTF's own first-quarter 2026 figure is lower, with PIK at roughly 25 percent of net investment income, or 43 million dollars. The periods differ, so don't read that as a trend. Even so, a quarter of income booked as paper interest is a large base for an incentive fee to sit on.

Here's why the venue matters. The other ways to test a mark are weak. Supervisors can only ask questions. Prices that managers publish themselves are still the manager's own models. And an outright sale of a BDC sets a real price, but such sales are rare. Litigation is different. If any of these suits survives a motion to dismiss and reaches discovery, it becomes the first adversarial process that can force a manager to justify a specific mark on a specific credit. Two consequences would follow. First, incentive fees on uncollected PIK become a contract-design issue across the BDC industry, with pressure to defer the fee until the PIK is actually paid in cash. Second, wide dispersion on a single credit undercuts these loans as collateral. Funds pledge their loans to banks to borrow more, so a bank lending against those marks needs to know which mark is real. So does anyone buying a BDC. The so-what is conditional but concrete. The marks fight now has a route to compelled evidence, which supervisors' letters and managers' own models don't provide.

Now the hand-off. The same discretion that sets a mark also decides what counts as a default. Fitch's U.S. private-credit default rate reached a record 6.0 percent on a trailing twelve-month basis at the end of the second quarter. That's second-quarter data, relayed by Kadenwood Group, so it isn't this week's news. Per that summary, maturity extensions made up more than half of Fitch's second-quarter default events. Strip them out, and the remaining rate is under 3 percent. Last week we made the same point about broadly syndicated loans, where liability-management exercises were two-thirds of default activity by count. We declined then to carry that over to direct-lending books. Now private credit has its own version.

There are two fair readings. The bear case says an extension is a deferred loss, and a payment-only count flatters the cycle. The bull case says an extension with a sponsor still in place often preserves value. On that view, counting every extension as a default overstates economic loss. The missing number is what these extended loans eventually recover, and nobody has it yet. So whether this cycle reads as a record or as moderate is a definitional choice, not new information about who is getting paid.

Briefly, the forward catalyst. Apollo expects to begin publishing asset-level prices to investors across its 850 billion dollar credit platform from October 30th. Apollo itself says those are estimated fair values from its internal methodology, not market-clearing prices. Still, a credit Apollo shares with other managers could then be compared mark against mark. That would surface, between managers, the same kind of dispersion the Ares complaint alleges inside one book, without any court ruling.

Pulling it together. On the latest hard data, the credit cycle is a slow grind rather than a sharp turn. PitchBook LCD put non-accruals at the ten largest BDCs at 3.95 percent of debt at cost in the second quarter, up just 20 basis points from the first. That is rising, but not breaking, and there's no fresh non-accrual print until November. The biggest unresolved risk is narrower. It's valuation and accrual discretion that pays managers fees today against losses not yet recognized. Watch four things from here. The procedural next steps in the Ares, FS KKR and OTF suits, starting with any motion to dismiss. Apollo's October 30th asset-level prices. Alternative-manager third-quarter earnings from late October. And November's BDC filings, which bring the first fresh non-accrual and PIK data since the second quarter.
