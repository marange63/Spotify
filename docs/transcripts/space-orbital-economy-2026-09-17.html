Cautious Optimism Briefings

Space & the Orbital Economy

Transcript · September 17, 2026

Good morning. It's Thursday, September 17th, and this is your space and orbital economy briefing.

Start with a correction this show owes you. Three days ago we walked through SpaceX's pre-listing financials. We flagged Starlink's roughly 39 percent operating margin as an artifact of launch priced internally. And we told you to watch one thing: whether SpaceX, as a public filer, would ever report Starlink as its own disclosed line. Our prediction was that visibility would get worse after the June listing, not better. That prediction was wrong. The answer was already sitting in a filing, and it is more detailed than we asked for.

SpaceX's second-quarter earnings release, filed with the SEC on August 4th, reports not two segments but three: Connectivity, AI, and Space. Total revenue was 7.8 billion dollars, up 92 percent year over year, against a Street consensus of about 6.9 billion. These are second-quarter numbers from six weeks ago, not news from this week. Two things make them load-bearing today. They settle the question this show posed on Sunday and got wrong. And Starship's next flight is five days out, with its economics landing inside a segment this filing puts on the record for the first time.

Connectivity is Starlink, and it is the only profitable segment. Revenue of 4.291 billion dollars, income from operations of 1.656 billion, a 38.6 percent operating margin, on twelve million subscribers — double a year earlier.

Now hold the read-across. Sunday's argument survives this filing almost intact. That margin is earned on launch the company buys from itself, priced by the same entity on both sides of the trade. Amazon's Kuiper, Eutelsat OneWeb and AST SpaceMobile all buy launch in an actual market. Nothing in this print tells you any of them can reach comparable unit economics. What improved this quarter is visibility. Comparability did not.

Here is the line that reframes the company. SpaceX reports Space — launch, Starship, the business the whole firm was built on — on its own disclosed line. Revenue there was 962 million dollars. The operating loss was 542 million. This is not our arithmetic on a hidden residual. It is the company's own reported segment. The rocket company's rockets are now its smallest revenue line, and they lose money.

Take one implication seriously. A lossmaking launch segment is what it looks like when a company prices launch to feed its own constellation rather than to clear a margin. That is a strategic choice, not a failure. But it means the sector's benchmark launch price is being set by a seller who is not trying to earn a return on it. Every independent launch provider competes against that.

Now the third segment, and it needs an introduction. SpaceX's AI segment is a cloud-compute business. The company sells compute capacity to customers under contract, and it signed 14.1 billion dollars of new cloud-services agreements in the quarter. Capacity went from 0.4 gigawatts a year ago to 1.4 gigawatts. To be straight with you about the limits here: the filing names this segment and reports its lines, and it does not explain how a launch company came to own gigawatts of compute. I am not going to fill that gap for you.

What the lines do say is that the segment sits in the middle on scale and dwarfs everything else on capital intensity. Revenue of 2.561 billion dollars, up 247 percent year over year, against an operating loss of 1.257 billion. That loss is an improvement from 2.47 billion in the first quarter, and that improvement is the only evidence either way on whether this ever scales into profit.

Then look at the capital going in. Fifteen point eight billion dollars of second-quarter capital expenditure sat in the AI segment alone. That is roughly six times what the segment earned in revenue over the same three months. This is a capacity build-out signature, not an operating-leverage story. It puts a company whose capacity is measured in gigawatts into the same queue as the hyperscalers for power, cooling and accelerators. Bank this one: the binding constraint on a large piece of the world's biggest space company is now electrical interconnect, not launch cadence.

Which leads to the part that should unsettle space investors specifically. If AI keeps outgrowing Starlink, the right comparable set for this stock drifts away from launch and satellite communications. It drifts toward infrastructure and compute. That makes the sector's flagship a poor read-across for Rocket Lab, AST SpaceMobile or Planet — the names most investors actually use it to justify.

Management's own forward claim rests on that same curve. On the earnings call, Chief Financial Officer Bret Johnsen said SpaceX expects to reach 100 billion dollars of annualized revenue run rate by the end of this year, on an expected-December basis. That is a guide, not a reported figure. And a December basis means a single month's revenue, annualized.

It also rests on hardware that has not flown. Starship Flight 14 is targeted for September 22nd. It would be SpaceX's first attempt at an actual orbital deployment rather than a suborbital test profile, carrying 26 Starlink V3 satellites. Those satellites weigh roughly 2,000 kilograms each, more than triple the prior generation. They are physically too large to fly on Falcon 9 in meaningful numbers. Only Starship can loft that mass at scale, which is exactly why the capacity step depends on it.

The date is a target, not a clearance. The FAA completed its environmental review on September 3rd. The FCC granted frequency and deployment authorization on September 2nd. But the FAA launch license itself — the actual gating item — had still not been announced as of SpaceX's own mission update on September 15th. The flight has not happened as of this recording.

One more note on framing, because this show made it yesterday. SpaceX's chief financial officer has called Flight 14 a revenue-generating flight. Remember that SpaceX is both the launch provider and the customer here. That is an intracompany transfer, not third-party revenue. And if Flight 14 slips or fails, the shortfall lands back on Falcon 9 and on the segment that is already losing money.

So that is one company, reporting revenue, margin, subscribers and gigawatts against a consensus number, checkable line by line. Now the contrast that carries this briefing. Here is what the same week's biggest claims look like when nobody can check them.

Air Force Secretary Troy Meink said on the record that the United States has, in his words, on-orbit space control weapons capable of defending the joint force against hostile adversary action. Yesterday this show told you to watch whether Beijing or Moscow would answer. They already had, within roughly a day of the remarks. China's foreign ministry urged the United States to stop expanding its military capabilities and preparing for war in outer space. The Kremlin called for the complete demilitarization of space. Grade that honestly. Both sides are competing through announcements rather than hardware. Neither has changed an observable posture. Only the language moved.

The other half came from Golden Dome's program executive. General Michael Guetlein said the missile shield now has a first operational site and has completed two tests, with space-based interceptors, command and control, radars and munitions under contract. That reaches us as an outlet's report of conference remarks, so treat it as a claim rather than a verified fact. No parameters came with it, no independent verification, no dollar figure, and the tests are classified by construction. Same week, two evidentiary standards: one counterparty files with the SEC, the other briefs a conference room. Weight your conclusions accordingly.

A quick close on a public name where the same split shows up at a scale you can actually buy. Rocket Lab flew its sixteenth Electron mission of the year on September 11th. That is a countable number, and it is real evidence of manufacturing discipline in small launch. Neutron, the medium-lift vehicle the equity story actually depends on, still has not flown, and its debut keeps slipping. Treat any date you hear for it as provisional. Sixteen flights prove the factory works. They prove nothing about the medium-lift question.

Watch three things from here. Whether an FAA license for Flight 14 appears before September 22nd. Whether SpaceX's third-quarter print holds on to this segment detail and validates the run-rate guide. And whether any American official ever puts a parameter or a dollar figure behind the on-orbit weapons claim, instead of a description.

That's the briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
