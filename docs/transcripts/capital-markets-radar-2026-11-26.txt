Good morning, it's Thursday, November 26th, and this is the Capital Markets Radar.

US markets are shut for Thanksgiving. So Wednesday's close is the last price on the tape for everything we cover until Friday. And Wednesday answered the question this desk set on Saturday. Would the bond market's new willingness to hedge survive the next inflation print? The print came in hot. Bonds rallied anyway.

Start with the number itself. October core PCE rose to 3.3 percent year over year, against a 3.2 percent consensus, per Trading Economics. It was also an acceleration from September's reading. That is a genuine upside surprise on the Fed's preferred inflation gauge. And it landed just two weeks before the Fed's December decision.

The textbook response is higher yields. Instead, the 10-year Treasury yield fell more than 3 basis points, to 4.866 percent, per CNBC. CNBC's explanation was blunt: investors were fleeing to bonds for protection. So Wednesday's bond buyers weren't betting that inflation is cooling. They were buying duration as a hedge against something else. The hot print didn't change their minds. The takeaway is simple. The hedge this desk has been watching since Saturday passed its first inflation test.

Before you call that a new regime, sit with how thin the evidence is. This was a 3-basis-point move against a one-tenth-point miss. It came in a holiday-thinned session, the day before Thanksgiving. That is not much to hang a thesis on. And there are three honest readings of it.

The first is CNBC's flight-to-safety story. Equity and AI-stock stress simply overwhelmed the inflation signal. The second is thin liquidity doing what thin liquidity does. It exaggerates a small move into something that looks like conviction. The third is only a hypothesis: policy error. On this reading, a hotter print makes it likelier that a hiking Fed overtightens into a slowdown. The long end then prices that as weaker growth ahead.

What would separate those readings is the split inside the 10-year. If the drop sits in real yields while breakevens hold, that is growth fear. If breakevens fell, the market is discounting the inflation print itself. The 2-year tests the policy-error case. A front end that holds firm while the long end rallies is the classic overtightening signature. Those numbers aren't in hand yet. FRED's real-yield series had not posted Wednesday's value at research time, and the 2-year close is unconfirmed. So hold the flight-to-safety read loosely, and retest it in a cleaner session.

The equity tape backs the risk-off reading, but it was a rotation, not a rout. The Dow actually rose about a third of a percent. The S&P slipped just a quarter of a percent. The Nasdaq fell 0.62 percent, more than double the S&P's loss. Those closes come from an AP-sourced market wrap.

The pain stayed concentrated in AI, and Nvidia needs a word of context. On Monday, Nvidia staged a record-level rally. CNBC tied it to the return of the company's chief executive, Jensen Huang. The stock has fallen every session since. It slipped on Tuesday, then dropped another 4 percent on Wednesday, per CNBC. So a rally built on one man's return has not held for even two days. A Barclays strategist called the reaction panic. In his words, "the AI growth narrative has hit a wall."

Options buyers paid up as well. One tracker had the VIX up 7.3 percent, to 28.29. Other sources show different levels, so treat that as indicative. But a jump of that size, on a day the S&P barely moved, is our clue to the posture. Investors were buying protection, not dumping stock.

So the picture is old-economy cyclicals holding up while AI-linked tech keeps bleeding. And that happened just as Treasuries caught a safety bid. That's one trade, expressed in two asset classes. Money is moving out of the most crowded AI risk and into the most defensive duration available.

There's a second-order wrinkle here for discount rates. A lower 10-year yield should give long-duration equities some relief. Nvidia fell anyway. Our inference is that the relief isn't reaching the names that need it most. Their own risk premium is rising faster than the discount rate is falling.

Now, the inflation risk the bond market looked past on Wednesday has a second source, and it burns slower. The US Trade Representative has proposed new tariffs on 14 trading partners, under Section 301 of the Trade Act. The target is what USTR calls structural excess industrial capacity. Per law firm Wiley's summary of the notice, the list goes well beyond China. It includes allies such as the EU, Japan, South Korea and Taiwan, alongside Vietnam and India.

The rates get steep at the top end. For China, the base proposal includes an extra tariff of up to 10 percent on a listed set of goods. But USTR says it is also considering a "substantially broader and higher tariff" on China, reaching up to 40 percent. Vietnam's proposed rate runs up to 35 percent.

None of this is in effect. Public comments aren't due until January 5th. The committee hearing isn't until January 25th, 2027. So the earliest this plausibly bites is well after the December Fed meeting. That makes it a next-year margin and inflation story, not a this-quarter one.

But it is a stagflationary tail, and Wednesday's bond rally didn't price it. That rally was a bet on growth risk. A tariff-driven inflation risk, still working its way through a hearing calendar, is a different animal. And if China ends up on the higher tier rather than the base case, the tail gets materially fatter.

You can already see the last tariff cycle unwinding in the metal underneath it. Copper on the London Metal Exchange slid below 12,000 dollars a tonne. It did so despite what ING describes as tight physical supply. Yesterday this desk left copper as an open question. Was Tuesday's futures drop trading AI risk appetite, or macro flow? ING offers a third answer.

The mechanism ING points to is telling. Copper stockpiled in the US ahead of earlier tariff fears is now being offered back to Chinese buyers. That's one bank's characterization, with no tonnage attached. But if it's right, this is not a demand story. It's tariff positioning unwinding in real time.

That's a warning for how to read the new USTR proposal. If this round revives the same stockpiling instinct, copper will be measuring trade-policy hedging as much as grid and data-center demand. Our inference is that near-term copper prices are a contaminated signal for the AI buildout, in both directions.

If Treasuries were where the protection money went on Wednesday, the speculative end of the market is where it came from. Bitcoin fell again, to around 58,600 dollars, down from roughly 60,000 on Tuesday. That's a second straight down session alongside equities, after it sat out Monday's rally entirely. So the asymmetric correlation this desk flagged earlier in the week has now held for three sessions.

The more structural number is on the funding side. Aggregate US-dollar stablecoin supply fell 1.9 percent in under two weeks, to about 282 billion dollars. That's DefiLlama data, cited by OneSafe. Because it covers the whole dollar-stablecoin float, it isn't capital rotating between tokens. It looks like dollar liquidity leaving the crypto rails.

One caveat. Part of that shrinkage could be trading firms de-levering, rather than investors leaving the asset class. Either way, the effect on market depth is the same. Pair a shrinking float with the ETF wrapper this desk has flagged all week as the marginal seller. You get a market with less cash on hand to absorb the next bout of selling. So any bitcoin bounce here is more likely to stay fragile than to prove durable.

Put it together, and the Fed walks into December 9th in a genuine bind. A hotter-than-expected core print strengthens the case for the hike markets already treat as near-certain. Visible stress in AI equities and crypto argues for caution. And a tariff threat sits behind both, on a slower clock. The bond market's answer so far is to hedge growth and look past the inflation number. But that's one session of evidence, not a verdict.

The next tests come fast. November payrolls land December 4th, the Fed decides on the 9th, and November CPI follows on the 10th. None of those has happened yet. The question to carry into those dates is this. Does the bond market keep hedging growth when the next print is about jobs instead of prices?

That's the radar for today. Stay cautious, stay optimistic, and enjoy the holiday.
