Good morning, it's Tuesday, September 29th, and this is your semiconductor and compute briefing.

Today's question: which layer of the AI hardware stack is capturing the marginal hyperscaler dollar? We'll work down the stack, from NVIDIA to memory to the foundry.

Start at the top. NVIDIA's board authorized an additional 150 billion dollars in share repurchases yesterday. That takes the total remaining authorization to 235 billion. NVIDIA's own release calls it the largest share repurchase authorization increase in its history. Jensen Huang tied the move to what he called a once-in-a-generation platform shift to AI and accelerated computing, paid for by the company's cash generation.

Now size it against the cash. In the quarter that ended in late July, NVIDIA generated about 21 billion dollars of free cash flow. In that same quarter it returned about 26 billion to shareholders, through buybacks and dividends combined. So it was already handing back more than it generated. And roughly 99 billion dollars was still unused under the old authorization at quarter end. The new money isn't refilling an empty pool. It stacks on top of a large unused balance.

One caution before reading too much into the size. An authorization is not a purchase. The release discloses no quarterly execution pace. Treat the new figure as a ceiling, not a schedule.

Here is the tempting read, and why it's wrong. Every layer below NVIDIA has spent this month rationing or repricing supply. So a record buyback looks like the company at the top of the stack handing cash back instead of locking up supply. NVIDIA's own disclosures undercut that. On its most recent earnings call, NVIDIA's chief financial officer said commitments for critical components rose from 119 billion dollars to 279 billion in a single quarter. She said the increase was, quote, primarily related to the procurement of memory.

So NVIDIA isn't choosing cash over supply. It is doing both at once. The biggest authorization increase in its history sits alongside a more-than-doubling of its component commitments. That's the more telling signal. NVIDIA doesn't own fabs or memory lines. For a fabless company, commitments are how cash turns into supply. On these numbers, NVIDIA is doing that at scale and still has cash left over to return. The buyback is not a retreat from the supply fight.

Those memory commitments raise the obvious next question: where does the cost show up? Watch the gross margin. NVIDIA's gross margin was 75 percent in the July quarter, and it is guided down about a point for the current quarter. The quoted guidance doesn't itemize the cause. But with the chief financial officer pinning the commitment surge on memory, it's reasonable to read memory costs into that step-down. So the buyback measures today's cash. The margin guide is the first place to watch memory prices reach NVIDIA's income statement.

Which brings us to memory itself. TrendForce, in a report from late August, projects that DRAM and NAND combined will account for 47 percent of hyperscaler capital spending in 2026. It sees that share rising to 68 percent in 2027. That moves the argument from memory prices to memory's share of the hyperscaler wallet.

Price is doing much of that work. Server DRAM contract prices already climbed through the second half of last year. On top of that run-up, TrendForce expects a further jump of roughly 270 percent during 2026. There is one brake worth naming. TrendForce also reports that long-term agreements are capping price increases for buyers locked into them. So the headline forecast may overstate what those contract holders actually pay.

Now take the forecast literally. TrendForce has total cloud-provider capex growing another 50 percent next year. Combine that with the share forecast, and the non-memory part of the budget actually shrinks, by roughly 9 percent, even as the total grows. Non-memory means accelerators, networking, buildings and power. To be clear, that's this show's arithmetic on TrendForce's figures, not a TrendForce claim. Its main use is as a plausibility test.

There are two ways to read the result. One is that memory really is crowding accelerators and everything else out of the hyperscaler budget. The other is that the definition is doing work we can't see. For example, the memory share may count HBM that's already embedded in GPUs bought from NVIDIA and Broadcom. That would double-count against accelerator spending. The quoted material doesn't disclose which it is. So don't conclude from this that GPU spending will fall. The takeaway is narrower. This forecast is a direct test of whether memory, not just packaging, has become the binding constraint on the AI buildout.

Tie that back to NVIDIA. HBM is a cost inside every NVIDIA system. If memory's share of hyperscaler budgets climbs as forecast, the pressure on NVIDIA's margin comes from the input side, not from demand. The current-quarter guide is a first hint. Whether that pressure is structural into next year is exactly what the forecast claims, and what nobody has yet shown.

So who inside memory captures that windfall? Third-party estimates, relayed by Benzinga, put SK hynix at 54 percent of this year's HBM4 market, Samsung at 28 and Micron at 18. A lead that size should give SK hynix the stronger hand when NVIDIA negotiates supply for its next platform.

Name the tension in those numbers, though. The same reporting says SK hynix is at volume on 16-layer HBM4 for NVIDIA's Rubin, and that Samsung has skipped that part. Yet it still credits Samsung with the second-largest HBM4 share. Those two claims sit awkwardly together. Neither one confirms that Samsung has qualified with NVIDIA. Samsung is the swing variable here. If Samsung qualifies, more HBM4 supply is relief for buyers and a margin risk for SK hynix. If it doesn't, TrendForce's call for HBM contract prices to rise another 70 to 140 percent in 2027 gets easier to hit.

The same capture shows up at the foundry. At its Snapdragon Summit on September 22nd, a week ago, Qualcomm confirmed that both new flagships, the Snapdragon 8 Elite Gen 6 and the 8 Elite Extreme Gen 6, are made by TSMC on its 2-nanometer process. In Qualcomm's words, all of the latest components based on the 2-nanometer process announced that day are manufactured by TSMC. That closes the standoff we covered on the 21st. Back then, Samsung was reportedly holding out on price rather than discounting for Qualcomm's volume. For this generation, TSMC won it.

Score that on two separate dimensions. On customer wins, TSMC keeps its most valuable mobile customer at the leading edge. On pricing discipline, the picture favors Samsung. It reportedly refused to cut price because it has other demand at that node. For a foundry long seen as the low bidder, that's a sign of health, not just a lost deal. Qualcomm also says it is, quote, taking a deep look at how we can leverage 2nm technology for our next-generation products, with Samsung as the candidate. That's evaluation language. It's the standard dual-sourcing move that keeps TSMC honest on price. It is not an allocation. The so-what is that the leading-edge price umbrella holds at both foundries. That's good for foundry margins, and a cost headwind for every fabless designer, NVIDIA and Qualcomm included.

So here's the scorecard across the stack. NVIDIA wins on cash. It paired its largest authorization increase ever with a more-than-doubling of its component commitments. Memory stands to win on share of the incremental hyperscaler dollar, if TrendForce's forecasts hold. At the foundry, TSMC wins the customer and Samsung wins on price discipline, and the leading-edge price umbrella holds at both. The open questions are whether TrendForce's capex-share math survives actual hyperscaler spending, and whether Samsung can turn an HBM4 share estimate into a qualified NVIDIA order. One dated test arrives tomorrow. Micron reports after the close on September 30th, and nothing about that print is known yet.
