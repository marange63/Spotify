Good morning. It's July 27th, and today's story is about states re-tooling the levers of strategic leverage. One side is loosening a guardrail. The other is tightening a chokepoint. And underneath both, the same hedge keeps quietly building.

Start with the guardrail. On July 22nd, the US and Saudi Arabia signed a Section 123 civil nuclear cooperation agreement. Energy Secretary Chris Wright and Saudi Energy Minister Prince Abdulaziz bin Salman put their names on it. What matters here isn't what's in the deal. It's what's missing from it. Every prior US nuclear agreement in the region, starting with the UAE, included what's called the Gold Standard: a flat pledge that the partner country won't enrich or reprocess uranium. This deal doesn't have that. It also drops the IAEA's Additional Protocol, the regime that lets inspectors show up unannounced at undeclared sites. Instead, Washington and Riyadh get a two-year joint study on whether Saudi enrichment could be commercially viable. If the US says no after that study, Saudi Arabia is barred from enriching independently for ten years. That conditional bar is reported by the Wall Street Journal, not confirmed in the agreement text itself, so treat it as reported rather than settled.

So what does this actually change. It resets a nonproliferation norm the US has enforced for two decades, and it does so to compete commercially against Rosatom and Chinese nuclear exporters for a market the Gulf badly wants, since more nuclear power at home frees up oil for export while meeting data-center and desalination demand. The deal now goes to Congress for roughly ninety session days of review. Blocking it requires a two-thirds veto override, a high bar that likely means this stands. Here's the second-order cost. Washington spent years refusing Iran exactly this kind of enrichment pathway. Offering a conditional version of it to a Gulf rival undercuts that leverage going forward. And other Gulf states are watching. The UAE may want to renegotiate its own terms, and Qatar could ask for the same latitude. If it converts, the winners are US nuclear vendors like Westinghouse and the enrichment-services complex, who gain a large new demand pool.

But that's the catch, and it's the caveat worth repeating. This is a legal framework, not capacity. No reactor vendor is chosen. No financing is in place. No construction timeline exists. Treat it as a template being set, not gigawatts being built. Keep that distinction handy, because it's everywhere in this space right now. Look at India this same week: on July 24th it earmarked more than two billion dollars for at least five small modular reactors by 2033, part of a stated plan to grow nuclear capacity eleven-fold, to a hundred gigawatts by 2047, from under nine today. That's a real appropriation attached to a genuinely long timeline. The Saudi deal isn't even at the appropriation stage. It's a diplomatic document. So the honest read across both is that the nuclear-abundance story is moving, but slowly, and the paper is always years ahead of the pour.

Now the mirror image, where a state is tightening rather than loosening. China's rare earth licensing is behaving exactly like a leverage instrument rather than a return to free trade. Chinese customs data confirm that exports of four critical categories, gallium, dysprosium, terbium, and yttrium, fell to zero to Japan in June. Yttrium exports to the US also stopped, for a second straight month. At the same time, China's broader rare-earth magnet exports to the US rebounded hard after June's trade truce. Shipments to the US jumped roughly 660 percent month over month in June, to about 353 metric tons, and overall magnet exports hit a six-month high of about 5,577 tons in July, up roughly 75 percent on the month. That combination is the tell. Beijing isn't opening the tap uniformly. It's naming specific minerals and specific countries, easing here, zeroing out there.

One number needs correcting from earlier reporting. You may have heard that US-bound magnet exports were down twenty percent year over year in the first half. That's not quite right. The actual comparison is China's January-through-June monthly average of 479 metric tons against the 2022-to-2024 monthly average of 586 tons, a pre-restriction baseline, not last year. So the honest framing is that US flows are running about twenty percent below the old pre-restriction run-rate, not down twenty percent from a year ago. The distinction matters because it tells you the restriction has been in place and compounding for years, not that something got dramatically worse in the last twelve months.

So what's the takeaway. Rare earths remain the one input the US and its allies genuinely cannot substitute quickly, whether for magnets in EVs, wind turbines, or defense systems. China driving four named categories to zero for Japan while easing aggregate magnet tonnage toward the US looks like a live test of whether Beijing can split allied coordination on critical minerals. If you can reward one ally and squeeze another with the same licensing desk, you make a unified G7 response on stockpiling and refining much harder to hold together. That's a tailwind for non-Chinese processing capacity, the MP Materials and Lynas type names, and for the broader thesis that allied countries need their own refining, not just their own mines. The mines aren't the choke point. The separation and magnet-making steps are, and those still run overwhelmingly through China.

Close the loop with the durable hedge against both of these games. Gold is down about twenty-eight percent from its January record of fifty-five ninety-five an ounce, trading near four thousand and thirty-eight dollars in mid-July, largely on dollar strength. But look past the price. The World Gold Council's central bank survey found eighty-nine percent of central banks expect their reserves to grow over the next year, and a record forty-five percent plan to add to their own holdings directly. Central banks bought a net 244 tonnes in the first quarter alone. Price is not the signal here. Accumulation is. Official reserve managers are hedging a dollar-centered order even as spot gold corrects. That's exactly the kind of quiet structural move that outlasts a single narrative about weak or strong dollar dynamics. When the US is willing to trade nonproliferation guardrails for position, and China is willing to meter strategic inputs by name, the people who manage other countries' reserves respond not with a headline but with a standing bid.

Three things to watch from here. Whether Congress makes any real move against the Saudi deal in its review window. Whether China's rare-earth licensing keeps discriminating by country as more months of customs data come in. And whether the Saudi nuclear framework ever converts into an actual signed reactor contract, because until it does, this is still paper, not power plants.
