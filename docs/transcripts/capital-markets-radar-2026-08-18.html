Good morning, it's Tuesday, August 18th. This is Capital Markets Radar.

The story this week isn't the September decision. It's that the long end of the Treasury curve is doing the Fed's tightening for it, whether or not Kevin Warsh moves in five weeks.

Start with the price action. The 10-year yield sits at 4.68%, per the Fed's own H.15 release for the most recent session, and the 30-year has pushed past 5.31% as of Monday, its highest level in nineteen years. That's a real reversal. Just last week, soft inflation data had the bond market pricing relief and the 10-year drifting lower. That relief rally is now gone. A $42 billion 10-year auction this month priced at 4.683%, the richest yield since the financial crisis, telling you real demand is thinner than the headline coverage would suggest.

Three forces are pushing the long end higher. First, Fed Chair Kevin Warsh has been unambiguously hawkish in his public language, saying the Fed "will deliver price stability" and will "not hesitate to act." Second, inflation expectations are not anchoring: the University of Michigan's year-ahead reading has now come in above 4% for a fifth consecutive month. On the third driver, be careful. There's been chatter that Japan's Ministry of Finance might resume selling US Treasury reserves to defend the yen, and that narrative has circulated as a driver of the selloff. It doesn't hold up. The one concrete, on-the-record statement from Tokyo, tied to the August 3rd coordinated intervention, is that Japan's finance minister said future dollar-selling intervention would be financed through the Fed's own FIMA repo facility specifically to avoid selling Treasuries. So the actual mechanism on record points away from reserve liquidation, not toward it. No official Japanese statement backs the sale fear. Treat it as unconfirmed market speculation, not a driver.

What matters is that September's policy decision is almost beside the point now. CME futures currently price about two-thirds odds of a hold and roughly a third for a quarter-point hike. Two-thirds odds of a hold sounds like an easy read. But a hold at these long-end levels does not mean easy financial conditions — term premium is doing the work the policy rate isn't, and that's a distinction the market is still digesting.

That distinction shows up immediately in equities. The Russell 2000 touched all-time highs three separate times last week, even as long yields climbed — which is backwards. Small caps carry more floating-rate and refinancing exposure than large caps, so they should be the first to flinch when the long end sells off, not the first to make records. There are two honest reads here. One, this is genuine broadening, with investors betting cuts still arrive further out the curve. Two, positioning and technicals are simply overriding fundamentals near-term, and that gap closes hard when it closes. I won't resolve it for you, but I'd watch this week's data for the tiebreaker: Home Depot reports before the open today, with consensus near $4.71 to $4.73 in earnings per share on roughly $47 billion in revenue — that print hasn't landed yet, so hold it as a catalyst, not a result. Walmart follows Thursday. Then the real test arrives in eight days, when Nvidia reports on August 26th, followed by Marvell and Broadcom — the AI-capex read-through that a steeper discount rate makes far more consequential than it would have been a month ago.

Credit is the corner most exposed to this repricing. High-yield spreads sit around 271 to 281 basis points over Treasuries, in the richest decile of history against a roughly 450 basis-point long-run median. Investment-grade spreads are near 77 to 81 basis points, levels not sustained since before the financial crisis. That's almost no cushion against a rising discount rate. Underneath that calm public surface, private credit tells a different story. Fitch Ratings puts the private credit default rate at a record 6% through the second quarter. Blue Owl Capital's flagship direct-lending fund saw defaults reach 2.8%, its highest in at least five years — even as the firm's co-CEO called credit health "strong." Redemption requests at the top ten non-traded BDCs averaged 13 to 14% of assets across the first half of the year, forcing several managers to activate gates. And Boston Fed research found the share of BDC loans structured as payment-in-kind nearly doubled, from 5.4% in early 2022 to 9.8% this year — a classic extend-and-pretend signal. None of this stopped the money: private credit fundraising hit $190 billion in the first half, up 53% year-on-year. So you have record inflows chasing a vehicle that's quietly showing record defaults. This isn't every manager — some larger books look closer to normal — but the divergence between tight, complacent public spreads and visible strain in the opaque private tier is the kind of gap that reprices fast, not gradually, once it goes.

That same rate sensitivity is now reaching into crypto collateral, which is the more durable story than day-to-day Bitcoin price action. Odds of the Clarity Act, the crypto market-structure bill, becoming law this year have collapsed — Galaxy Research's Alex Thorn cut his estimate to roughly 10%, down from 75% back in May, as a Senate cloture vote won't even come up until September 15th. That's a genuine regulatory reversal, and it lines up with spot Bitcoin ETFs seeing $390 million in net outflows last week, their largest in six weeks, alongside open interest easing from 760,000 to about 750,000 Bitcoin — positioning is de-risking, not building. Meanwhile the infrastructure keeps advancing regardless: BlackRock launched two new tokenized Treasury vehicles on Ethereum, including a tokenized share class of its $6.2 billion BSTBL fund with BNY as transfer agent, explicitly built to qualify as compliant reserve assets for stablecoin issuers. Ethereum now holds roughly 43% of a $15.2 billion tokenized Treasury market. As on-chain dollar collateral increasingly means rate-sensitive Treasury exposure, the transmission from a hawkish long end into crypto plumbing gets tighter than it was a year ago, Clarity Act timeline or not.

Briefly on real assets. Copper's tariff arbitrage remains unresolved — Commerce Secretary Lutnick's June 30th deadline to rule on a phased duty came and went with no announcement, while more than 200,000 tons of copper piled into US ports in July, the largest monthly volume since 2014. Whenever that ruling lands, it likely collapses the premium that pulled in that stockpile. Gold keeps printing records regardless — near $4,429 an ounce, up over 10% in a month — and that's worth separating from the rate story: it's a debasement and regime-uncertainty hedge, so it doesn't need a dovish Fed to keep climbing.

All of this converges in ten days. Jackson Hole runs August 27th through 29th, and Warsh delivers his first keynote as chair on the 28th, on a theme of financial innovation in payments — squarely where the hawkish-rates story and the stablecoin story now meet. The September 16th FOMC follows three weeks later. The gap between a long end that's already tightened and a policy decision still up for debate is exactly the setup for a violent repricing in either direction.
