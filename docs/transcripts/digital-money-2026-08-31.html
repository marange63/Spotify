Cautious Optimism Briefings

Digital Money, Crypto, and Market Structure

Transcript · August 31, 2026

Good morning, it's Monday, August 31st, and this is your digital money briefing.

Start with a quick bridge, because this show covered Friday's shock in full over the weekend. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh gave a hawkish Jackson Hole speech. Traders repriced the odds of a September rate hike from roughly 35 percent to about 60 percent. Bitcoin round-tripped from a three-month high near 81,000 dollars down through the high 76,000s, then settled near 78,000 into the weekend. That is yesterday's episode, and I'm using it as a starting point rather than re-reporting it. Everyone read that repricing as a hit to a risk asset. Almost nobody priced what the same repricing does to the other side of this market — the side that earns its living on short-dated Treasury yield. That's where we're going today.

Circle's own second-quarter disclosure settles a claim this show has been making without proof since the end of last week. On Friday we said that weaker reserve income at Circle was an analyst characterization, not a disclosed line item we could check it against. The issuer's press release supplies the line item, in its own words. Reserve income of 668 million dollars increased 5 percent year over year, primarily from the 25 percent growth in average USDC in circulation, partially offset by a 66 basis point decline in the reserve return rate. Read that sentence twice. The float backing USDC grew five times faster than the income it produced. That is the decoupling, in the issuer's own numbers.

One figure to anchor the scale. USDC in circulation stood at 73.3 billion dollars at quarter end, up 19 percent year over year. That's the hard number in an otherwise foggy supply picture. Total market-wide stablecoin supply gets reported anywhere from 300 to 315 billion dollars depending on whose methodology you use. When the aggregate is that soft, take the issuer's own audited figure as the one you can trust.

Now separate what's cyclical from what's structural, because that distinction is the whole argument. The 66 basis point decline is the rate cuts already delivered, finally landing in a disclosed number. Remember the timing. This is a quarter that ended June 30 and was reported in early August. It is backward-looking evidence about cuts that already happened, not a read on the path from here. If Friday's hawkish turn holds into the September meeting, that compression mechanically reverses, because Circle's reserves sit in short-dated Treasuries and bills that reprice with the front end. So the same speech that hurt bitcoin holders is, at the margin, a tailwind for the issuers of the dollar tokens those holders trade against. That's the two-sided read the market isn't making.

The genuinely structural question is a different one. It's how much of that gross reserve income Circle actually keeps. The release gives a partial answer. Total distribution, transaction and other costs — the money Circle pays partners for bringing in the float — came to 412 million dollars in the quarter. That line grew only 1 percent year over year, against reserve income growing 5. So the partner cut is not what squeezed Circle this quarter.

You can divide those two disclosed lines and get roughly 62 percent of reserve income going out the door before Circle keeps its share. Hold that loosely. It's my arithmetic on two figures, not a ratio Circle states, and the company gives no partner-level breakdown at all. The conclusion survives either way. The compression sits in the reserve return rate, not in a rising distribution bill. Which means the right valuation frame for a stablecoin issuer isn't an adoption story. It's a rates business wearing a technology multiple, and the direction of the front end matters more to that equity than any supply chart.

One honest limit before we move on. Circle discloses no rate sensitivity anywhere in that release. There is no stated dollar impact of a 25 basis point move in short rates. So the reversal I just described is directional, not quantifiable. Nobody is going to hand you a clean number for how much of that 66 basis points comes back.

If reserve income is rent, the next question is who's coming for it. That brings us to the Comptroller of the Currency. Speaking in Wyoming in mid-August, Comptroller Jonathan Gould committed the OCC to a final rule implementing the GENIUS Act's licensing and prudential framework for bank stablecoin issuers by November. His words: we are working with great speed here. That deadline isn't new to this show. What's new is the queue behind it. Gould disclosed that of the 40 new bank charter applications the OCC has received since the current administration took office, 23 carry some form of digital-asset activity in the business plan. He called that an eightfold increase on the prior four years. Banks are lining up to compete for exactly the float income Circle just showed you is under rate pressure.

Hold the skepticism here, though. An application is intent, not a business. Twenty-three filings sitting with a friendly regulator tells you demand exists. It tells you nothing about approvals, about time to actual issuance, or about whether any of these charters ever issues a dollar of stablecoin at scale. And the eightfold jump is measured against four years in which the prior administration discouraged the filings, so part of that multiple is a suppressed base rather than new demand. No bank stablecoin issuer is licensed today, because the rule isn't final.

There's a subtler point buried in that queue, and it cuts the other way. Not everything a bank wants to do on-chain waits on the GENIUS rule. Custody and tokenized deposits sit under existing banking authority. So the visible effect of that charter pipeline can arrive well before any stablecoin licence does, in the form of banks holding client assets and moving deposit balances on ledgers. Watch the custody announcements, not just the token ones.

The other regulatory track matters too, and it's stuck. As of the last clear status check before the August recess, the CLARITY Act — the market-structure bill that would sort out SEC and CFTC jurisdiction over exchanges — had no scheduled Senate floor vote. That status is fluid, and this show has flagged the stall repeatedly rather than treating it as news. The honest summary is that the gap between demand for bank-issued dollars and the rules that would let them exist is widening rather than closing.

One more piece closes this out. If issuers are barred from paying yield to holders, the yield-seeking dollar has to go somewhere, and it's already being built next door. BlackRock extended its tokenized cash line this month with a blockchain-based share class of an existing Treasury money-market fund — a fund that does pay yield. JPMorgan launched a comparable on-chain money-market product earlier this year. Now size it honestly before calling it a threat. BlackRock's BUIDL, the largest single tokenized-Treasury product, held roughly 2.37 billion dollars as of June. That's about three percent of USDC's float alone. This is an emergent product line being sold well ahead of its balances, not a competing money supply.

Here's the read-through for capital markets that ties it together. Whether a dollar sits in a stablecoin reserve, a newly chartered bank's token, or a tokenized money-market fund, it is overwhelmingly the same short-dated Treasury bill demand wearing a different wrapper. What changes between wrappers is the transmission channel and who captures the spread, not the quantity of bills bought. But there is a measurement consequence. If the yield-seeking balance keeps migrating toward the tokenized-fund wrapper, stablecoin float becomes a progressively worse proxy for how much dollar demand is actually moving on-chain. Remember that the next time a headline cites total stablecoin supply as evidence of anything on its own.

And the one hard catalyst that resolves the biggest open question here — how much of that 66 basis point compression comes back — is the September 16 FOMC decision. It has not happened. Every hike probability you hear until then is trader-implied pricing, not Fed guidance.

That's the digital money briefing for August 31st. Have a good one.
