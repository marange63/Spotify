Cautious Optimism Briefings

AI in Biopharma R&D

Transcript · November 3, 2026

Good morning. It's November 3rd, 2026, and this is Cautious Optimism Briefings on AI in biopharma R&D. Today's thesis: the first durable human data this show has seen from an AI-discovery platform in weeks comes from a platform that chose what to drug, not one that designed the molecule. And pharma is pricing that skill as a cheap option, not a proven capability.

Start with Recursion Pharmaceuticals. On November 2nd, at the CGA-IGC meeting, Recursion presented updated Phase 1b/2 data from its TUPELO trial of REC-4881 in familial adenomatous polyposis. The primary read comes after twelve weeks of dosing. Thirteen of fifteen evaluable patients, or 87 percent, had a reduction in total polyp burden. The median reduction was 47 percent, per the company's release.

Then comes the durability read, which is the more interesting number. At Week 25, twelve weeks after patients stopped the drug, eleven of thirteen remained below their starting baseline. In other words, the effect did not simply vanish when dosing ended.

There's also a signal in the polyps the release calls higher-risk. In a subgroup of eight patients with polyps larger than five millimeters, six had fewer of them after treatment. In four patients, that count fell by 80 percent or more. It's a tiny subgroup, so treat it as direction rather than proof. But activity in the larger polyps carries more weight than the headline burden figure.

Why this matters. Since October 23rd, this show has been asking AI-discovery platforms for denominators, and for a result that survives contact with the clinic. What we got instead were in-vitro hit rates, best-of-N antibody variants, and design tools with no validation metric. REC-4881 is the first item in that run with prospective human efficacy data and real denominators. That puts it a full evidence class above everything else we've covered in the window.

Now the skeptical turn, because the evidence is still thin. Fifteen evaluable patients in a single-arm study, with no comparator reported, is a signal, not a proof. Polyp burden is a surrogate endpoint. Nothing in the release establishes that FDA will accept it for registration. The durability claim is also softer than it sounds. "Below baseline" only says patients didn't fully revert. The release gives no off-treatment median, so we can't say how much of the 47 percent survived. And that read rests on thirteen patients, not the original fifteen.

Then there's the safety line. Four of twenty-six safety-evaluable patients had a Grade 3 treatment-related adverse event as their worst. None had Grade 4 or 5. In a late-line cancer setting, that would be an easy trade. FAP is different. It's a chronic, prevention-type setting, where patients weigh years of dosing against surveillance and surgery. So the commercial question isn't the response rate. It's whether a MEK inhibitor's dose and schedule can be made tolerable for years.

That brings us to the question that decides what this result is evidence for. How much of it is AI? According to reporting from Drug Discovery Trends, Recursion in-licensed the molecule from Takeda, where it had been studied in solid tumors. So Recursion's platform did not design REC-4881. What the platform did, per the same reporting, was identify MEK1/2 inhibition as a potential way to counter APC loss of function. That pointed Recursion toward an existing compound and a new indication. The sourcing is trade press rather than Recursion's own filing, but it settles the shape of the claim. This readout validates AI-driven target and indication selection on known chemical matter. It does not validate AI-driven molecule design. That's a lower bar than "AI designed a drug." It's also a more defensible one, and it's where the clinical signal is actually coming from.

Keep the timeline in proportion. Insilico's rentosertib, which we noted on October 28th is in Phase III, remains further along the clinical path. Recursion says it anticipates starting a registrational trial in the second half of 2027, pending FDA discussions. That's the company's own forward statement, not a regulatory commitment. For the AI-discovery names, the scoreboard has shifted from wet-lab hit rates to durable human data, and Recursion now has a dated catalyst. But that catalyst is a trial start, not an approval. No drug with an AI-platform discovery claim has reached full FDA approval. Our inference is that, on this timeline, a REC-4881 approval is years away.

If the value is in picking the target rather than designing the molecule, the natural next question is what pharma will pay for that skill. AstraZeneca gave a dated answer on October 15th, in a collaboration with insitro that this show hasn't covered before. The deal uses insitro's multi-omic "Virtual Human" platform to nominate targets in MASH and cardiomyopathies. AstraZeneca's release frames the rationale bluntly. In its words, "one of the most critical decisions in R&D is selecting the right target."

Now the price. AstraZeneca pays 40 million dollars upfront, against up to roughly 1.46 billion dollars in development and commercial milestones, plus single-digit royalties. On our own arithmetic, the upfront is under 3 percent of the disclosed milestone value. Bloomberg's headline put a larger total on the deal, but that's a press framing, not a figure in AstraZeneca's release. So AstraZeneca is buying a cheap option on target selection, and leaving almost all of the risk with insitro.

That structure has a direct read-through for platform business models, and this part is our inference. With a small upfront and contingent milestones, insitro's revenue from the deal tracks the progress of programs that don't exist yet, not the headline value. No targets or methodology were disclosed. So outsiders can't judge whether insitro's edge is genuine human genetics and multi-omics, or conventional triage with better branding. Any valuation built on aggregate biobucks overstates what has actually been validated. And don't read insitro and Recursion as the same evidence. One is a large-indication cardiometabolic bet with no data yet. The other is an orphan readout in fifteen patients.

Step back, and AstraZeneca's pattern is the more durable story. On November 2nd, this show covered AstraZeneca acquiring the pathology-AI company Modella, and partnering with Tremont AI on toxicologic pathology. Add insitro, and AstraZeneca now holds external AI positions in both target discovery and preclinical safety, without staking everything on one in-house model. Our read is that each option is small against AstraZeneca's R&D budget. That's exactly why these deals keep getting signed, even without proof that they move clinical odds.

Now the contrast at the other end of the stack. Isomorphic Labs, the Alphabet-backed company built on AlphaFold lineage, was set up to design molecules, not to pick targets. Per Bloomberg's interview with its president, published November 2nd, Isomorphic's own programs remain preclinical. Its leadership still says AI will eventually design most or all drugs. Being preclinical isn't evidence the approach fails. But it does say that structure-prediction excellence hasn't yet compressed the path to an IND. Biology, toxicology, and CMC remain the binding constraints. It also sharpens an argument from October 25th and November 2nd. As open models like RFD4-Proteina and Pinal ship their weights, a closed design engine needs a clinical asset to justify its premium. Today, the clinical asset belongs to a target-selection platform.

So where does that leave the read? For weeks, this show has concluded that the evidence moves cost and cycle time, not clinical odds. REC-4881 is the first item that touches clinical efficacy directly. It bends that conclusion without breaking it. One orphan, single-arm readout on a surrogate endpoint doesn't change industry success rates. But it's the first data point of the right kind, and it points somewhere specific. The realistic near-term gain in success rates looks like better choices about which mechanism to hit, and in which disease, applied to molecules that already exist. Two things will test that. FDA's view of polyp burden as an endpoint will define Recursion's path. And insitro's milestones will show whether target selection pays out beyond the upfront. As for R&D spend, options this cheap don't move the budget line. It moves only when one of them turns into a real pipeline, and that hasn't happened yet.

That's the briefing for November 3rd. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
