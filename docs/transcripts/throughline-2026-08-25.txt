Good morning, this is The Throughline for Tuesday, August 25th, 2026. Today's headline: a bankruptcy judge threw out First Brands' restructuring plan and converted the case to liquidation, the hardest outside verdict yet on private credit's own recovery math, and it landed in the same week a public stock tape marked the humanoid robot leader down more than forty percent.

Start with the macro backdrop. Capital Markets Radar has Treasury escalating its long-bond buyback in stages, and now reportedly weighing funding it out of its General Account cash balance rather than new bill issuance. That funding choice matters more than the headline size, because drawing down cash that already exists adds reserves to the banking system instead of just rearranging duration along the curve. Treat it as reported and under consideration, though, and note that nothing has actually been bought yet. The larger operations don't begin until September 9th, and the first relief rally reversed inside a day. The same briefing has Monday's Iran sanctions designating roughly sixty entities, and crude fell anyway, which weakens the inflation trigger this show has been watching and puts still more weight on Friday's Jackson Hole speech from Fed Chair Kevin Warsh.

Private Credit and the Corporate Credit Cycle has the verdict itself. Judge Christopher Lopez rejected First Brands' Chapter 11 plan and converted the case to Chapter 7 liquidation. He found the litigation recoveries the plan leaned on, an estimate running as high as twenty-five billion dollars, too speculative and too slow to fund anything today. The same briefing has a rival lender marking one shared borrower, the veterinary roll-up AmeriVet, as non-accrual two full quarters before another lender did. And retail redemption requests hit twelve point four percent of net asset value, the highest that data provider has ever recorded, with sponsors filling only thirty-eight percent of them. Three outside checks on private credit's own marks, and all three came in worse than the inside number.

Now the AI and compute stack, where the same test is running through equities. Semiconductors and Compute Infrastructure has Gartner lifting its 2026 chip revenue forecast to one point six trillion dollars. The composition is the story. Memory alone goes from twenty-seven percent of industry revenue last year to fifty-four percent this year, almost entirely on price. Equity investors answered that Monday by selling memory hard, SanDisk down nine percent and Micron down seven, on unconfirmed reports that Apple may be allowed to buy Chinese memory. Nvidia reports Wednesday, and that print is the first hard volume read behind a revenue record that, so far, is mostly a price story.

Frontier AI Lab Competition has the other side of that pricing story, in China. DeepSeek and Alibaba are both moving to charge for their strongest open-weight models. DeepSeek's new off-peak input rate is fifty-seven percent above its old flat rate, and Alibaba is reportedly preparing a revenue share on its largest Qwen model. The free floor under American model pricing is becoming a range rather than a zero. The same briefing has Nvidia reportedly financing more than a hundred billion dollars of an OpenAI data-center campus in Ohio, a vendor lending the money that buys its own chips. It also has a new standards group publishing the first outside grade of how prepared each lab is to contain a rogue model.

AI Products and Applications has one company answering the permission question twice, with the same answer both times: not the human. Anthropic's new Claude Security scanner reaches every Enterprise customer without ever exposing the model behind it. And its coding agent now approves its own commands by default, running a classifier that blocked eight hundred actions human testers had already waved through. The same briefing has Google's Agent2Agent protocol formally joining Anthropic's Model Context Protocol under one neutral foundation, the third straight day this show has watched agent plumbing move out of any single vendor's hands.

Enterprise Technology reads that donation as revealed preference. Nobody gives away a layer they think they can charge for, so Google is telling you agent-to-agent messaging isn't defensible. The layers nobody is donating, GPU capacity and hosting contracts, keep drawing nine-figure checks, including IBM and Together AI's two hundred forty million dollar cluster deal. And Salesforce's own telemetry has the time to stand up a new agent falling from about four days to under two, which shifts the integrator's business from building agents toward operating fleets of them.

Turning to the applied frontier, where that same public-versus-private split runs through humanoid robots. Robotics and Physical AI has Unitree down more than forty percent from its opening print, after analysis of the company's own offering materials put research and education customers at nearly three-quarters of humanoid revenue, and industrial applications at about nine percent. In the same session, XPeng's robotics unit signed agreements to raise over nine hundred million dollars privately, at a post-money valuation above six billion. The public tape is disciplining the category. Private capital, so far, isn't.

One thread ties the day together. Where a venue carries a daily mark, a bankruptcy court or a public stock exchange, an optimistic story got taken down hard this week. Where it doesn't, in private funds and private rounds, the same underlying risk is still being priced generously. Two scheduled events test how far that discipline travels. Nvidia's print on Wednesday, and Warsh at Jackson Hole on Friday.
