Good morning. It's Tuesday, August 25th, 2026, and this is your semiconductor and compute briefing.

The industry is on track for its biggest revenue year ever. The story this morning is what that number is made of. This is a repricing, not simply an expansion, and last week's tape suggests equity investors have started to treat that difference as real risk.

Start with the number itself. Gartner updated its 2026 forecast on August 24th. It now puts worldwide semiconductor revenue at one point six trillion dollars this year, up ninety-two percent from last year's eight hundred nine billion. The composition is the part that matters. Memory alone is forecast to be fifty-four percent of that total, up from twenty-seven percent in 2025. One cyclical component goes from roughly a quarter of the industry's revenue base to more than half in a single year.

Treat that as a forecast, not a reported result. And treat the forecaster with caution too. Back in April, the same firm called for 2026 revenue above one point three trillion dollars on sixty-four percent growth, flagging memory as roughly tripling on what it called memflation. Four and a half months later, the growth estimate went from sixty-four to ninety-two. Gartner is chasing realized pricing in real time, not reporting a settled figure.

Last Friday this show said anyone modelling AI compute supply off TSMC's revenue line is reading a price series as a volume series. Gartner extends that from one company to the whole industry. But be precise about what it settles. A memory share moving from twenty-seven to fifty-four percent is a mix decomposition, not a unit-versus-price split. No forecaster or supplier publishes bit-shipment growth alongside price growth for the same period. So the price side of memory is documented in detail, and the volume side simply isn't.

The realized numbers make the price side credible. Samsung's second-quarter DRAM average selling prices rose in the mid-forty-percent range quarter over quarter, with NAND up in the high sixties. SK hynix posted roughly thirty percent on DRAM and the mid-fifties on NAND. Those are TrendForce's compilation of quarterly results, expressed as ranges rather than company-disclosed figures. They confirm the contract-price forecasts and HBM4 per-gigabyte numbers this show aired on Saturday. That data is holding up, not breaking new ground.

Now consider what a fifty-four percent memory share does to semiconductor equities. If more than half the industry's revenue comes from memory, the earnings base under semiconductor index valuations has become majority-cyclical, at exactly the moment those valuations are defended as structural AI growth. A de-rating in the memory names is therefore a bigger index event than their market-cap weight implies. Which is why yesterday matters more than it looks.

On August 24th, memory stocks led a broad chip selloff. SanDisk fell nine percent, to just under fourteen hundred sixty dollars. Micron fell seven percent, to just under nine hundred. Western Digital fell seven percent and SK hynix five. These are stocks that had run enormously: SanDisk up five hundred seventy-two percent year to date, Micron up two hundred thirty-nine.

The trigger was specific and dated. Weekend reports said the Trump administration may permit Apple to source DRAM from China's CXMT and NAND from YMTC. Don't blur that into an earlier down day. On August 18th, Micron, SanDisk and Western Digital also fell, and that one was tied to rising Treasury yields compressing multiples across the AI and memory trade. Two different down days, two different triggers. And the Apple story is unconfirmed policy reporting about a possible decision, not a finalized rule. One analyst quoted the same day pushed back, arguing CXMT supply is unlikely to dent the shortage Apple is actually facing in DRAM.

Now the apparent contradiction. Micron has already agreed price and volume on all of its 2026 high-bandwidth memory output, including its newest HBM4 parts. A sold-out book and a falling share price are not in conflict. The book prices demand negotiated months ago. The equity prices the next risk to that pricing power, which here is a qualified Chinese alternative. Micron itself does not report until September 29th, so nothing in yesterday's move traces to a company disclosure. On Sunday this show dated the expiry of memory pricing power to the 2027-to-2028 fab wave, using Lam Research's own fab count. The tape suggests the market has started discounting that expiry now, rather than waiting for the wafers.

If that's scarcity at the leading edge of memory, look at the opposite end of the stack, where there is no HBM and no advanced packaging at all. SMIC, China's largest foundry, posted its first-ever three-billion-dollar quarter: three point zero one billion, up twenty percent sequentially. Gross margin expanded five hundred twenty basis points to twenty-five point three percent, on higher prices, better mix and utilization running at ninety-three point seven percent. SMIC guided the third quarter to a gross margin of twenty-six to twenty-eight percent.

Look at the split inside that quarter, because it is the one place today where volume and price are visible separately. Wafer shipments rose about fourteen percent sequentially. Per-wafer prices rose under six. At the trailing edge, growth is still mostly units. In memory it plainly isn't. Co-chief executive Zhao Haijun bounded the story himself, saying SMIC still needs to negotiate for fairer pricing given how far its prices trail the industry leaders. And roughly ninety percent of the quarter's revenue came from China, per the reported breakdown, so this measures domestic Chinese demand, not SMIC's ability to win business globally.

Two things follow. Rising prices, tight utilization and expanding margin is the same signature showing up at HBM, which means the scarcity rent running through this industry is system-wide rather than AI-specific. System-wide scarcity is exactly the kind that mean-reverts once capacity lands. And a Chinese foundry raising per-wafer prices while conceding it still undercuts the leaders tells you China's trailing-edge cost advantage is narrowing from the inside. That weakens the commercial pull keeping Chinese fabless customers on foreign legacy capacity, with no export-control change required.

One channel here gets less attention than the accelerator story. Memory price moves of that size flow straight into server and client bill of materials for any buyer without reserved allocation. A hyperscaler absorbs that inside an AI budget it has already decided to spend. Enterprise IT and edge-device makers absorb it as margin compression. Yesterday this show flagged the first sign of a ceiling there: trade reporting that downstream PC and smartphone makers can no longer pass the cost through. If that holds, memory's pricing power runs out at the demand end before it runs out at the supply end.

Every number in this briefing so far has been a price. Wednesday is when the sector gets a volume read. Nvidia reports fiscal second-quarter results after the close on August 26th, covering the quarter that ended July 27th. None of those results exist yet. Everything that follows is guidance, consensus, or history.

The setup is this. Six consecutive down sessions through Friday's close took the stock from about two hundred twenty-five dollars to about two hundred fifteen, its longest losing streak since 2022. Nvidia's own guidance calls for revenue near ninety-one billion dollars, plus or minus two percent, against eighty-one point six billion the prior quarter. Consensus earnings sit at two dollars and one cent a share, roughly double the ninety-nine cents posted a year ago. All twenty-six analysts tracked by TipRanks rate the stock a buy, at an average price target near three hundred two dollars.

Here is the wrinkle in that unanimity. Historically, Nvidia has averaged a two point eight percent decline the day after reporting, and five point three percent over the following two days, even across four straight quarters of beats. Unanimous bullishness has coexisted with a reliable post-print fade. The reaction function is about expectations, not results. Coverage into this print has also shifted from headline growth toward scrutiny of Nvidia's financing arrangements with its AI-lab customers. Treat that circular-financing worry as press framing, not a company disclosure.

What Wednesday can settle is the question this briefing circles. Strong volume commentary on Blackwell and Rubin ramp pacing would say the memory-heavy revenue mix reflects real physical demand, and that today's read here is wrong. A softer volume signal, with growth carried by price and mix, would say scarcity rent is doing more of the work than a headline of one point six trillion suggests.

So the revenue record is real. Its composition is the open question. And be honest about what today's read rests on: one forecaster's revision of its own four-month-old number, one day of tape driven by an unconfirmed policy report, and one Chinese foundry's quarter. Wednesday adds the first hard volume data point.
