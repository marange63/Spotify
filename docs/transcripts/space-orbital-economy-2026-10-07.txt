Good morning. It's Wednesday, October 7th, 2026, and this is your space and orbital economy briefing.

This week's two loudest stories in orbit are compute in space and a weaponized Golden Dome. Both are running ahead of what would make them real. For compute, that's flight-proven hardware. For Golden Dome, it's appropriated money. The quieter story, direct-to-cell phone service, is the one actually turning a demo into a dated path to customers. Let's take them in that order.

Start with the hardware. On October 1st, a SpaceX rocket carried Google's Tensor Processing Units into low-Earth orbit, aboard an experimental satellite called Project Suncatcher. Scientific American reports these are the chips Google uses to train and run its AI models. That makes this production silicon, not a mockup. Coverage places Suncatcher on the October 1st rideshare we mentioned on Monday, the one carrying more than a hundred spacecraft. We didn't name the payload then. Now we know what was on board. Orbital compute has lived in filings and renderings until now. This is its first physical step.

But keep the skepticism proportional to the step. One satellite is a qualification test, not a data center. The networking layer that would turn satellites into a compute cluster is laser links between them, and that layer hasn't flown at all. Google's next move, per Scientific American, is two more experimental satellites in 2027. Their job is to test how potential data centers would talk to each other by laser. So the piece that turns satellites into a system is more than a year from its first trial. And no on-orbit performance data has been published. The radiation-resilience claims in coverage come from ground simulation, not flight results. We simply don't know yet how these chips are doing up there.

Now set the scale question beside it, because the gap is the whole story. SpaceX has a pending FCC application, filed in late January, for a constellation of up to one million satellites dedicated to space-based data processing. In SpaceX's own framing, that unlocks "hundreds of gigawatts of potential compute capacity" from near-continuous solar power in orbit. On October 2nd we called that filing a spectrum and orbital claim long before it's a compute business. That still holds. An FCC application is a regulatory position, not an approval and not a capital commitment. What has changed is that a hyperscaler has now flown real hardware. One Google test satellite against a one-million-satellite SpaceX claim is the full distance between a demo and a business. And the topic has gone mainstream. The International Astronautical Congress, which opened in Antalya on Monday, put AI data centres in space on this year's agenda.

Which raises the obvious question of who pays. The clearest window into SpaceX's finances is still its first earnings report since June's IPO. That's the second-quarter print from early August, not a current number, and third-quarter results are not yet out. In that quarter, Starlink generated about $1.7 billion of operating profit. Total capital expenditure was $18.4 billion, up from $10.1 billion the quarter before. By our own arithmetic, not a reported figure, Starlink's profit covered under a tenth of one quarter's capital spending. Any orbital-compute ambition would compete for that same pool of capital with Starship and Starlink's own buildout. So for SPCX holders, the filing is a claim on the future. The capex line in the next print is the thing to actually watch.

That same tension between capital and claims is sharper on the defense side. Since September 30th, this show has tracked which companies win Golden Dome awards. The prior question is whether the money behind those awards exists. Golden Dome's funding runs almost entirely through reconciliation, a mechanism separate from normal annual appropriations. Breaking Defense reported on October 1st, the day the latest continuing resolution took effect, that less than $400 million of the fiscal 2027 request sits in the base budget. The rest is, in its words, "up in the air along with the reconciliation effort." Senator Chris Coons has said lawmakers will not deliver the $350 billion in reconciliation funding the Pentagon is counting on. And the cushion is thin. Of the $25 billion Congress gave the program in 2025, 90 percent is already obligated.

The stopgap doesn't help. It keeps the rest of government funded through December 11th. But Golden Dome has no prior-year baseline for a continuing resolution to extend, so the stopgap does nothing for it. Breaking Defense carries the warning plainly: "Without continued funding, Golden Dome will stop with everything we've already built and delivered." Until reconciliation money lands, read every Golden Dome contractor award, SpaceX's Starshield work included, as a conditional order book, not recognized revenue.

Now the higher stakes behind that budget fight. On September 14th, Air Force Secretary Troy Meink said publicly that the United States "now has on-orbit space control weapons capable of defending the joint force against hostile adversary action." He did not say what the system is, when it was deployed, or what it can do. Read it as an acknowledgment, not a technical reveal. The diplomatic reaction arrived fast. At Monday's IAC opening in Antalya, Turkish President Erdogan said that sending weapons to space "will be totally wrong." He called the emerging arms race "a direct threat to the joint future for humanity." The so-what: the diplomatic cost of militarizing orbit is arriving now, from a NATO ally, while the money for Golden Dome, the flagship space-defense program, is still unappropriated.

Against two stories running ahead of their own reality, here's one where the demo is turning into a service. On Monday, AST SpaceMobile and the Canadian carrier TELUS announced their first end-to-end network integration test. It validated direct satellite-to-cellular connectivity on standard, unmodified consumer smartphones. TELUS is targeting customer availability within the next twelve months, starting with texting and voice before broadband. That's carrier guidance, not a committed date. But it is a real engineering gate, cleared on ordinary phones rather than prototype hardware. SatNews reports peak speeds up to 120 megabits per second per coverage cell. That's a ceiling shared by everyone in the cell, not a per-user speed, so don't read it as individual broadband. And there's no subscriber or revenue figure attached to any of this.

AST's stock rose on Tuesday, and reporting credits a second catalyst. A joint U.S.-Japan government statement endorsing satellite-connectivity cooperation specifically named AST's partnership with Japan's Rakuten. On Friday we said the decisive lever in direct-to-cell is spectrum rules and carrier alliances, not launch cadence. Five days later, both of AST's catalysts are exactly that: a carrier test and a government endorsement, not a launch. The implication for modeling is a staggered revenue ramp, built carrier by carrier, not one global switch-on. And the government endorsement is an edge over the vertically integrated SpaceX and T-Mobile model only if it turns into spectrum priority or funding.

So where does that leave the sector? What's genuinely inflecting: real compute hardware has reached orbit, and direct-to-cell has cleared a test on stock phones with a dated, if soft, service target. What's still a promise: compute at million-satellite scale, and a missile-defense architecture whose money Congress hasn't delivered. The open question tying them together is whether capital can keep pace with the claims made in its name. For SpaceX that capital is private. For Golden Dome it's appropriated. Four things to watch. First, any on-orbit performance data from Suncatcher. Second, FCC movement on SpaceX's orbital data center application. Third, the fate of the reconciliation package before the December 11th funding deadline. And fourth, a launch date for AST's BlueBirds 14 through 16.

That's your space and orbital economy briefing. Talk tomorrow.
