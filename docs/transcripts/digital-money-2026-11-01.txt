Good morning, it's Sunday, November 1st, and this is your digital money briefing. Three days after the largest theft in crypto history, the question isn't how big the hack was anymore. It's who is actually paying for it, and where the damage is showing up on the regulated side of the market.

Start with Binance. Roughly 6.3 billion dollars in customer assets were stolen on Thursday, about four times the Bybit hack that had held the record. On Friday we noted that Binance's SAFU insurance fund covered only about a sixth of its pledge to make users whole. The rest rested on a corporate balance sheet nobody outside had verified. By Binance's own account, that gap has now closed. The company says it has replaced about 90 percent of the stolen funds. In its latest update to CoinDesk, Binance put that figure at roughly 95 percent. It says it is still buying the remaining affected assets through market and OTC channels.

The other open item from Friday has moved too. On Friday, the North Korea attribution came only from blockchain-forensics firms. Now the FBI has attributed the attack to a North Korean group. That's an attribution, not an indictment or a charging document. But it is the first government finding on who did this.

Here's the bull case, and it's real. Binance says the losses will not be passed on to users. And it appears to have found roughly six billion dollars of replacement assets in about two days. That implies a pool of free corporate liquidity that very few institutions anywhere could match.

Now look at how the number has moved. Ninety percent in one update, ninety-five in the next. Both are self-reported, and neither comes with an attestation date. Binance points to a proof-of-reserves snapshot claiming its ten largest asset balances are fully backed. But a snapshot is not a third-party audit. Binance hasn't disclosed how much came from the insurance fund versus company funds, or in which assets. So we don't know whether the backstop is stablecoins, BNB, or something more fragile.

There's also a first read on whether customers are leaving. DefiLlama-sourced data shows about 1.3 billion dollars of net outflows from Binance in the 24 hours after withdrawals began reopening. The seven-day figure is about 8 billion dollars. But that window includes the theft itself, so it can't be read as customer flight. Even the 24-hour number mixes routine activity with any loss of confidence. Call it a signal worth tracking, not evidence of a run.

So what this means is simple. The hole got filled, but by the least transparent balance sheet in the system. The industry's backstop for a six-billion-dollar failure is now one offshore firm marking its own homework. Any institution with basis trades or collateral at that venue now has to underwrite its corporate treasury, not just its custody.

If Binance absorbed the loss, the regulated market still took a hit, and it shows up first in the ETF flows. On Friday, 643 million dollars left the eleven US spot Bitcoin ETFs in a single session, reversing four straight days of inflows. Yesterday we described that outflow as concentrated in BlackRock's fund. That framing was wrong, and it's worth correcting plainly.

The fund-level breakdown tells a different story. BlackRock's IBIT lost 233.7 million dollars. Fidelity's FBTC lost 201 million, nearly as much. ARK's ARKB lost 129.5 million. By our arithmetic, those three account for roughly 88 percent of the day's outflow. That changes the reading. This wasn't one allocator base rotating out. It was selling across the client bases of the three largest issuers at once.

And it isn't confined to one bad day. On at least one tracker, the trailing 30-day net flow for US spot Bitcoin ETFs has turned negative for the first time since July. On Thursday we said the ETF bid was churning, not building. The churn has now tipped into net distribution. One honest caveat here. On Friday we cited a different tracker that already had the trailing month negative before the hack. So the exact turning point depends on whose window you use. The direction is the same either way.

Price tells the same story. Bitcoin touched an intraday low near 76,300 dollars on Friday, its first break below 77,000 in over a month. So the ETF wrapper didn't insulate anyone. The selling was complex-wide. It also landed alongside a broader tech selloff, so you can't cleanly separate hack-driven fear from macro positioning.

The same de-risking shows up in the dollar float. On DefiLlama, total stablecoin market capitalization stood at about 296.7 billion dollars on October 31st. That's down about 1.26 percent over seven days. A separate tracker had the total near 300.5 billion on October 25th. Comparing across trackers is approximate. But it implies a contraction of roughly four billion dollars over the week that contained the hack. That answers the question we left open yesterday. Yes, the dollar rail was shrinking through this window too.

So both of the market's cleanest entry points, the ETF wrapper and the stablecoin float, were de-risking at the same time. That's broad de-risking, not a Binance-only event. One caution, though. A seven-day window can't isolate the hack from the Fed or the tech selloff. And we have no split between redemptions to cash and migration across issuers. The Treasury implication is real but marginal. Issuers back that float largely with short-dated bills. A shrinking float means a slightly smaller marginal buyer of T-bills, not a shock to a market that size.

But one regulated venue was gaining share of that float even before it shrank. Coinbase's third-quarter numbers show average USDC held in Coinbase products hit a record 22 billion dollars. Stablecoin revenue was 307 million dollars, up 5 percent from the prior quarter. Now set that 22 billion against USDC's 71.6 billion dollar float on October 25th. By our own arithmetic, Coinbase products held something like 30 percent of that stablecoin. That's rough, because it compares a quarterly average with a single-day reading. Still, it's the strongest real number in this market behind the thesis that regulated venues capture stablecoin economics.

The caveats matter. Coinbase's quarter closed at the end of September, before the hack. Stablecoin revenue is rate-sensitive, so any move toward lower policy rates erodes that line over time. And concentration cuts both ways: more of USDC's economics now ride on a single distributor. Whether offshore users actually moved toward Coinbase after the hack is a fourth-quarter question. Those Binance outflows are the place to look first, once anyone can trace where they went. So the regulated-winner thesis has a real number behind it, but only through September.

Last point, and it folds into the close. On Friday we noted that no regulator had commented on the hack. We said agency rulemaking, not legislation, was the likely venue for a response. That's how it's playing out. According to reporting in The Hill, the CFTC has cited the Binance hack to push for federal oversight of offshore exchanges. That builds on an exchange framework the agency proposed on October 5th. It's a proposal and advocacy, not a rule. Congress, meanwhile, remains stuck. The CLARITY Act failed to advance in September and will probably have to restart in the new Congress in January. Agency action moves faster than legislation, but it's less durable. And how a US regulator actually reaches an offshore venue like Binance is still an open question.

So where does that leave you heading into next week? Watch three things. First, whether Binance produces a third-party-audited proof of reserves, rather than another self-reported percentage. Second, whether the 30-day ETF flow stays negative into November. That would be the first sustained institutional distribution signal since the ETF rally began. Third, whether the stablecoin float stabilizes or keeps leaking. The bill for this hack is being paid offshore. The measurable damage is landing onshore, in the wrappers and rails institutions actually rely on.

That's your briefing for November 1st. Stay cautious, stay optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
