Good morning. It's Sunday, October 25th, 2026, and this is your robotics and physical AI briefing.

The thread running through this desk's humanoid coverage all week has been simple. Builders talk capacity, and almost nobody talks output. On Thursday we asked when a humanoid maker would finally disclose what it has actually built and how well it works. That same day, Figure AI partly answered the question at a company all-hands. The answer reframes where the money in humanoids is going: less into building the body, and more into teaching it.

One ground rule first. Every Figure number in this segment is a company claim. It comes from CEO Brett Adcock's all-hands, as relayed by the trade outlet Humanoids Daily, not from a filing. Start with the hardware. Adcock said Figure 04 has reached design lock. He said Figure built its thousandth Figure 03 over the summer. And he said unit cost fell by more than 90 percent from Figure 02 to Figure 03.

That is a real cost curve, but it has no dollar base. A follow-up check this week found no traceable unit cost, bill of materials, or lease price anywhere in the public record. A steep cut off a low-volume prototype is the easy part of any cost curve. So what: until Figure names an absolute number, nobody outside the company can compute payback against the labor the robot is meant to displace.

Now the reliability number, which is the one that matters most. Figure's Helix 2.5 model succeeded on 237 of 420 household trials, across 30 rented homes it had never seen. That works out to about 56 percent. A separate account of the same release, from the outlet Origin of Bots, says the trials ran fully autonomously. There was no teleoperation assist and no pre-loaded map of the homes.

That account also gives a baseline. The same policy without pretraining scored just 9 percent zero-shot on the same tasks. So pretraining on Figure's data accounts for nearly the whole gap. That is the cleanest quantified evidence yet that data, not hand-engineering or a remote human operator, is doing the work.

Now the honest discount. Those trials covered chores the model had already been trained on. This is generalization to new homes, not to new tasks. And even on familiar tasks, the result is roughly a coin flip. A paying customer, in a house or on a factory floor, needs something much closer to working ten thousand times in a row. So what: Helix is real progress for a general-purpose robot brain, and it is still nowhere near deployable reliability.

So if the money isn't going into cheaper bodies, where is it going? Into teaching them. Adcock put Figure's data spending at roughly $8.1 million in a single week. That is one week's figure, not a disclosed run-rate. But it puts the cost of the data flywheel right next to a body that keeps getting cheaper. The spend feeds Index, Figure's video-collection program. Index uploads have more than doubled since August, to 37.5 million videos.

One caveat worth keeping. Humanoids Daily itself notes that uploaded videos are not the same as cleaned, annotated training footage. Still, the direction is unmistakable, and it answers a risk this desk named on Tuesday. That risk is that the robot brain commoditizes as frontier labs catch up on control. If the model becomes shared property, the moat is proprietary field data. Figure is spending to lock that data in before the big labs arrive. So what: in humanoids, the marginal dollar is now going to data, not actuators.

The compute side looks alarming at first glance, and less so on a closer read. Figure's commitment to the neocloud Nscale starts at $3.5 billion, with intent to scale past $6 billion. Figure's disclosed cash balance is above $2 billion. On those numbers alone, the commitment outsizes the balance sheet.

But Figure's own announcement of the deal says the capacity, up to 100,000 Nvidia Vera Rubin GPUs, isn't targeted to start deploying until the second half of 2027. Nscale is also taking an equity stake in Figure as part of the arrangement. And the commitment's term and binding status have not been disclosed. So this reads less like Figure straining its cash today, and more like a forward reservation of capacity, with a counterparty that also owns a piece of its customer. So what: the financing question is real, but it is a 2027 question. When it comes due, Figure's roadmap depends on continued fundraising or vendor financing, the same frame that hangs over the rest of the AI buildout.

Figure pays for all of this with venture capital and a compute partner's balance sheet. Tesla is paying for its version out of core auto earnings, right now. The Fremont Optimus line, covered earlier this week, is turning out what Tesla calls its first production-quality bots. What's new is the bill. Tesla's third-quarter net income fell 43 percent year over year, to $787 million. The company named higher AI and Optimus spending as one drag, alongside lower regulatory-credit sales.

Tesla didn't split out how much of that drop is Optimus, so don't read the whole decline as the robot's bill. It also still hasn't disclosed a current Optimus output rate. But the funding structure is the opposite of Figure's. So what: Tesla's own shareholders are underwriting the humanoid bet today, and they will keep seeing its cost in the quarterly numbers well before they see any robot revenue.

There's a second Tesla data point worth banking. Ashok Elluswamy, who runs Tesla's AI software, said unsupervised full self-driving for customers is now, in his words, "somewhere around Q2 next year." That is a slip from Musk's earlier guidance for the end of this year. It joins a pattern this desk has tracked all week. OpenAI's humanoid demo, Tesla's first external Optimus deliveries, and Agility's pilot with AGCO all now sit in 2027. None of that proves Optimus's target of 20,000 units a week by the end of 2027 will also slip. But Tesla's own timeline record argues for discounting that target rather than taking it at face value.

Both of these programs, and every Western humanoid maker, share one input they don't control: the rare-earth magnets inside their actuators. China's Announcement No. 70 suspended the expanded export controls Beijing imposed last October. That suspension itself lapses on November 10th.

Two qualifiers matter. First, a separate licensing regime from April of last year stays in force no matter what happens to the pause. It covers medium and heavy rare earths, including dysprosium and terbium. Second, American officials describe a broader truce running to January 10th. As of the latest reporting, no matching notice from China's commerce ministry confirms that date. So treat November 10th as the only confirmed deadline. A quiet extension is entirely possible, which makes this a dated risk, not a forecast.

Here's why it matters for this story. By industry tracker estimates, Chinese makers shipped over 97 percent of the roughly 19,100 humanoids shipped worldwide in the first half of this year. Trackers disagree on the totals and on what counts as shipped, but the gap is not close. Set that against Figure's cumulative thousand units. The makers who lead on volume are also the ones sitting inside the magnet supply chain.

What follows is our inference, not a confirmed price impact. If the pause lapses without renewal, every non-Chinese humanoid program absorbs a cost and lead-time hit just as several of them begin their first production runs. That would widen the Chinese cohort's cost advantage on top of its volume lead. So what: the binding risk on the robot body is a policy date, and no amount of model progress fixes it.

Pulling it together. What's genuinely inflecting is disclosure itself, along with a steep hardware cost curve and the first clean evidence that pretraining data drives capability. What's still a demo is household reliability at roughly a coin flip, and Figure's next-generation Helix model, which so far exists only as an internal preview. The biggest open question is who ultimately pays the data and compute bill once forward commitments like Nscale's come due. Watch November 10th on rare earths. Watch for the first humanoid maker to publish an absolute unit cost or lease price alongside a success rate, because that becomes the benchmark private valuations get repriced against. And watch whether Tesla's next disclosure gives an actual Optimus output number instead of another capacity target.

That's the briefing. Have a good Sunday.
