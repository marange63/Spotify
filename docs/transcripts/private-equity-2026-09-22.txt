Good morning. It's Tuesday, September 22nd, and this is your private equity briefing.

The lead today is a no. The chief investment officer of the New York City Retirement Systems turned down a commitment to a private equity fund. The Systems run roughly 326 billion dollars, per the city comptroller's own fiscal-2026 disclosure. The reason for the no wasn't performance. Reporting describes the manager's track record as solid. The reason was concentration: the fund carried too much exposure to AI.

One bound on the sourcing before we go further. This is Bloomberg's reporting, and I'm working from secondary summaries of it rather than the original, so treat the details as second-hand. Those summaries don't even agree on the chief investment officer's surname, so I'll refer to the office rather than the person. The fund and the manager were never named.

Here's why this is a different kind of no than the ones this show has been tracking. Every piece of limited-partner pushback we've covered lately has been about liquidity. Two days ago we led with Hawaii's employees' retirement system, a 26 billion dollar fund, reassessing a private-markets tilt of roughly 50 percent because the exposure had cost it returns. That is an allocator asking where the cash is. New York City is not that. It wasn't complaining that money wasn't coming back. It was refusing to send more money out, on portfolio-construction grounds, to a manager it otherwise trusts. That is a much harder problem for a general partner to solve. There is no realization, no distribution, no proof point it can produce that fixes it. You cannot out-perform a concentration screen. One caveat before this gets inflated: a declined fund is not an allocation cut. Nothing in the reporting says New York City lowered its private-equity target or its commitment pacing.

So how big is the concentration problem, actually? Goldman Sachs is cited putting AI-infrastructure companies at roughly 40 percent of the S&P 500's total market capitalization. Separately, and from a different source, Apollo's data puts AI-related deals at 87 percent of this year's venture funding. So the largest slice of the public market and almost the entire venture market are now the same bet. Whatever an allocator does from here, that bet is already in the book.

The question is whether anyone can measure how much of it they own. The Los Angeles County pension fund, LACERA, tried to size its own AI exposure and landed on a range of 8 to 19 percent — and the summaries don't agree on whether that range covers its private holdings or its entire portfolio. Either way, that is an enormous spread for a fund's own estimate of its own book. A range that wide isn't a measurement. It's an admission that the exposure can't be priced at the portfolio-company level. And that's the real point. Allocators can see AI concentration in their public books, where holdings are disclosed and marked daily. They cannot see it in their private books, where the general partner sets the mark. New York City's own chief investment officer said, in effect, that almost every fund the office commits to will bring more AI exposure regardless. That is an allocator naming a problem it can't screen its way out of.

Hold the classic denominator effect next to this, because it runs backwards. Normally a public-market drawdown mechanically pushes an allocator's private allocation up as a share of the total, forcing more commitments almost by accident. What's happening here goes the other way. Public-market AI concentration is making an allocator unwilling to add the same exposure through a private vehicle. And if that concentration ever unwinds, it hits both sides of the ledger at once — the AI-heavy public names, and the private funds quietly carrying the same bet. That is the opposite of the diversification this asset class is sold on.

So where does that capital go instead? It doesn't disappear. If large public plans start screening out AI-heavy managers, the demand routes to buyers who don't run those screens. Last week gave us the template. The Baldwin Group, a listed insurance brokerage, agreed to go private in a 7.7 billion dollar deal led by Michael Dell's family office and a firm that calls itself a permanent holding company rather than a fund. Shareholders got an 88 percent premium to the undisturbed price. That is permanent capital and family-office money outbidding for assets a screened drawdown fund would have chased. The same capital also routes to the semi-liquid wealth channel, where the end investor has no concentration overlay at all and simply prices off the manager's own net asset value. Notice what that does to fund economics. A concentration screen bites at the primary fundraise, which is the input to the fee base. This is the first mechanism in this cycle to touch fee-related earnings directly at the point of commitment, rather than only pushing carried interest further out.

That brings us to the exit side of the machine, where the pressure has been building for longer. In late June, the SEC's enforcement division opened a formal investigation into general-partner-led continuation vehicles. That's the structure where a sponsor sells a portfolio company out of an aging fund into a new vehicle it also controls, sitting on both sides of the trade. I want to correct something this show said two days ago. We called ILPA's draft continuation-vehicle guidance the first institutional constraint on a sponsor pricing its own asset. That was wrong. The SEC's probe predates it by three months. But bound the correction, because it's weaker than it sounds. There is no enforcement action and no named target. More importantly, there is no bespoke rule behind it, since the 2023 private-fund adviser rules that would have required independent fairness opinions were vacated. The agency has to work case by case.

Still, the numbers behind the probe are real. Manager-led secondary transactions, mostly continuation vehicles, hit 106 billion dollars in 2025, up from 70 billion the year before. That is the industry's primary liquidity valve, growing by half in a year, precisely because IPO and strategic-sale exits are constrained. And the losses aren't hypothetical. Fortress, Ares, and Blackstone together took a combined 1.4 billion dollar loss in a 2021 Platinum Equity continuation fund. Clearlake's Wheel Pros, also held in a continuation vehicle, later filed for bankruptcy. Both are illustrative of losses in these structures generally, not confirmed targets of the inquiry. What sits behind all of it is the inventory: Bain's June count puts more than 30,000 portfolio companies unsold. We used roughly 33,000 on Sunday from the same firm's midyear report, which tells you that even the stock estimate isn't stable.

And here is the distribution baseline against which all of that should be read. CalPERS reported a preliminary 17 percent return on its private equity book for the fiscal year ended June 30th — its second-best asset class. The release disclosed no distributions figure, no cash-versus-paper split, nothing on what actually came back. That is the state of allocator disclosure right now. The marks are published and the realizations are not. So when a general partner points at a number like that as evidence the machine is working, the honest answer is that we cannot yet tell whether it is cash or carrying value.

Put the two stories together and the squeeze is on both ends of the machine at once. The exit valve now carries fresh regulatory friction just as it has become indispensable. And the entrance, new fundraising, is starting to face a concentration screen it has never faced before. Neither one drains the backlog. Each just changes the price of getting through it.

So where does the cycle actually stand? Price discovery has been loud at entry, engineered or absent at exit, and now, as of this week, contested at the moment of commitment itself. The constraint is migrating backwards through the fund's entire lifecycle. Three things would tell you whether that read holds. A first SEC action or named target in the continuation-vehicle probe. ILPA's final guidance, once it moves past draft form. And whether a second major allocator screens a fund on AI concentration rather than the usual distributions complaint. Manager earnings land in late October, and they'll show whether fee income is still insulating these firms, or whether the commitment-side screen has started to bite.
