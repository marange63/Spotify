Good morning, it's September 1st, and this is your briefing on AI in biopharma R&D.

One line of continuity before today's items. Roughly eight years into the AI drug discovery boom, no medicine discovered or designed with AI has been approved in any major market. And Phase II, the expensive kind of failure, remains untouched. That frame still holds. Today is not a news day. It is a correction to where this beat has been looking, built from three items that together relocate where the value in this stack actually sits.

Start with the lead. Genentech has exercised the first option under its collaboration with Recursion, the first of up to forty potential programs in that deal. The target is for an undisclosed neurodegenerative disease, and it came out of Recursion's microglia map: forty-six million cell images spanning seventeen thousand genes and thousands of compounds. By both companies' own account, the target was validated in wet-lab experiments on both sides before Genentech optioned it into its early discovery program.

Be precise about what that is and isn't. This is not a drug program. It is an early discovery program, one step past a hypothesis, with no disclosed indication and no clinical timeline. No dollar value was disclosed for this specific option exercise, and you should discount any figure circulating for it. What makes it worth leading on is narrower. This is the first item on this beat that touches the target-discovery layer with a large pharma partner putting its own discovery resources behind an AI-nominated hypothesis, rather than paying for data access and calling that validation.

Recursion's second-quarter numbers put a dollar scale on the collaboration around it. The company has received two hundred sixteen million dollars in cumulative upfront and milestone payments from Roche and Genentech since the deal began in 2021. That is a to-date total across everything, and the release does not break out how much of it, if any, is attributable to this particular exercise. Against that, each of the up to forty programs carries potential for more than three hundred million dollars in development, commercialization and net-sales milestones. Do the arithmetic and the disclosed maximum runs past twelve billion dollars.

Here is the so-what, and it is a valuation point rather than a science point. Nobody should be pricing that twelve-billion ceiling. One program out of forty has cleared even the option-exercise stage, five years in, which means the market has been pricing a conversion curve with no observable points on it. Today it has exactly one point. If the eventual conversion rate turns out to be single-digit, four or five programs out of forty over the life of the deal, then the honest description of this business is a data-licensing contract with option upside, not a pipeline. That changes the metric you should be tracking: dataset scarcity and partner renewal rates, not program count.

The balance sheet makes that concrete rather than abstract. Recursion held roughly five hundred fifty-seven million dollars in cash, equivalents and restricted cash at the end of June, against a net loss of a hundred thirty-one million dollars in the second quarter alone. At that rate, the cadence of option exercises is not just a narrative question. It is a financing question, and a slow conversion rate shows up as dilution well before it shows up in a pipeline chart.

Notice what actually changed hands here. Not a molecule, a dataset. That is now the third time in a week this beat has landed on the same pattern. On August 19th, Tempus AI, the genomics and sequencing-data company, rose almost twenty-four percent in a single session on a Merck and Moderna trial readout it was not a party to. The market was pricing the tumor-sequencing data sitting underneath somebody else's clinical result. Roche, separately, already owns two of the largest curated real-world oncology data assets, Flatiron Health and TriNetX. And today Genentech options a target out of Recursion's image library. Roche is on the buying side of two of the three. Leverage keeps accruing to whoever owns the proprietary data, not to whoever runs the model on top of it.

Now the capital side of the same argument. Isomorphic Labs, the AlphaFold-lineage drug-design spinout out of Google DeepMind, raised two point one billion dollars in a Series B in May. Thrive Capital led it, with Alphabet, GV, CapitalG, and state-linked capital from MGX, Temasek and the UK's sovereign AI fund all participating.

Correct this beat's own record on that. On August 27th I told you Isomorphic had raised six hundred million dollars in March of 2025 and was slipping its first human trial to the end of 2026. The six-hundred-million round was real. It was simply the prior one. The account was stale by a round and by more than a year, and the current number is more than three times larger.

Isomorphic says its new Drug Design Engine more than doubles AlphaFold 3's accuracy on the hardest structure-prediction cases. Treat that exactly the way this beat treats every vendor benchmark: company-published, unreplicated, not peer-reviewed. One thing about the company remains genuinely unknown. Whether Isomorphic now has anyone dosed in a first-in-human trial is unconfirmed. Nothing public settles it either way, so treat the clinical status as an open question against the end-of-2026 timeline I gave you on the 27th, not as a status update.

So what sits at the design layer is two billion dollars of fresh capital against a field-wide approval count of zero. The composition matters as much as the size. A round anchored by sovereign wealth funds and a corporate parent is insulated from the discipline a venture syndicate would impose after a bad readout. That capital does not have to mark itself to a clinical result. This is the capital-versus-evidence gap in its cleanest available form, and the practical consequence is that the design layer can stay funded straight through evidence that would defund a normal biotech.

If the money is concentrating in discovery and design, where does the regulatory weight actually land? Not there. The EU deferred the AI Act's stand-alone high-risk obligations from this August out to December of next year, a sixteen-month slip. But the more important fact is that medicinal products were never listed in the Act's high-risk annex to begin with. That reading comes from a compliance advisory rather than from the legal text itself, so hold it loosely. Drug-discovery models, manufacturing-process optimization and pharmacovigilance systems sit outside the high-risk obligations entirely. That was true before the deferral and it is true after it.

Where the burden does land is companion diagnostics regulated as devices, now pushed to August of 2028, plus the transparency obligations on other AI systems, which did not defer at all and took effect this August. So the popular framing that the AI Act hits pharma hard has the geography wrong. It hits diagnostics and digital-pathology vendors, and it largely misses the discovery platforms holding the capital. That is a durable margin differential inside what investors often treat as a single AI-in-pharma bucket, because the imaging and IVD names carry notified-body cost and audit overhead the discovery platforms simply do not. Read the deferral itself skeptically too. Sixteen months was granted because standards and notified-body designations were not ready, which is a reason to expect further slippage rather than to treat December 2027 as a firm date.

Briefly, on real-world data, because it is the one thread that could change the spend forecast. Friends of Cancer Research presented an external-control-arm feasibility project in May, with Aetion, Flatiron Health and Truveta involved, aimed at oncology trials. Right now only the project's existence is established. No results, no regulator's position, nothing quantified. But it is the one mechanism on this beat that would cut cost at the expensive end, by shrinking or replacing a control arm in a late-stage trial rather than making an earlier decision faster.

Pull the threads together. Capital sits at the design layer, with Isomorphic's raise the clearest marker. The one checkable conversion is happening at the data layer, where Genentech and Recursion just produced the first point on a curve that had none. The regulatory burden sits, counterintuitively, at the diagnostics layer. And none of the three touches Phase II, which remains the honest constraint on this whole thesis. So the standing read on R&D spend holds: reallocation toward earlier, cheaper failure, not reduction. Name the one thing that would break it. A regulator formally endorsing an external control arm built from real-world data would move the saving from the cheap end of development to the expensive one. That has not happened, and until it does, the spend forecast does not change.
