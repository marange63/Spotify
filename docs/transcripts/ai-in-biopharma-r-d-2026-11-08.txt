Good morning. It's November 8th, 2026, and this is your Cautious Optimism Briefing on AI in biopharma R&D.

Today's thesis has two parts. The newest human data again shows AI earning its keep as a selector of what to hit, not as a designer of the molecule itself. And the field's most-cited AI-designed drug turns out to have a thinner verified record than this show has been telling you.

Start with Evaxion's EVX-01, a personalized cancer vaccine built on the company's AI-Immunology platform. In a November 6th release tied to the SITC meeting, Evaxion reported new biomarker data from its Phase 2 melanoma trial. The number to anchor on is 89 percent. That's the share of the vaccine's AI-predicted neoantigen targets that triggered a tumor-specific immune response in patients. In other words, it's a hit rate on the platform's own predictions, measured in people rather than in a dish or a simulation. Since late October, this show has been asking AI-discovery platforms for denominators. This is closer to one than any design-layer claim we've covered.

Three qualifications matter immediately. First, the 89 percent has no comparison point. We found no published baseline for how often conventionally predicted neoantigens prove immunogenic. So we can't say how much the AI added over standard methods. Second, the evidence that the AI's scoring matters is modest. Per OncLive's coverage, observed immune responses tracked the AI's prediction scores with a Spearman correlation of 0.38, and a p-value below one in a thousand. The p-value says the relationship is real, not noise. It doesn't say the relationship is strong, and a correlation of 0.38 is weak to moderate. So the AI's ranking carries genuine signal, but it is nowhere near a deterministic predictor. Third, immunogenicity is not efficacy. A T-cell response against a target is a mechanistic checkpoint, not proof the vaccine shrinks tumors.

Now the clinical numbers. At two-year follow-up, the company reports a 75 percent objective response rate, twelve of sixteen patients, including four complete responses. That sounds striking, but the trial design limits what it can tell us about the AI. This is an open-label, single-arm study with no control arm. EVX-01 is given together with pembrolizumab, the checkpoint inhibitor sold as Keytruda. And the patients had never received a checkpoint inhibitor before. Without a control, you can't separate what the vaccine added from what pembrolizumab would have done on its own in that population.

Every figure here is company-reported, and we found no independent statistical review. Nor could we find a stated Phase 3 or registrational plan from Evaxion. So the AI-relevant number is the 89 percent. The response rate is encouraging context, not evidence that the AI-chosen targets are what's working. The prediction step is getting validated. The drug isn't, yet.

Here's the second-order read, and it's our inference. If AI epitope selection keeps validating in patients, the value in personalized cancer vaccines accrues to the prediction layer. The antigen-selection model becomes the differentiator, while manufacturing and delivery start to look like services. At sixteen patients, that is a hypothesis, not a finding.

That's the second time in under a week that human evidence has come from AI choosing rather than designing. On November 3rd, this show covered Recursion's early-stage readout in familial adenomatous polyposis, where most evaluable patients saw their polyp burden fall. Recursion in-licensed that molecule from Takeda. Its AI chose the target and the indication, not the chemistry. Evaxion is the same pattern one layer down. AI chose which neoantigens to target, and a conventional vaccine format did the delivering. Two data points make a pattern worth tracking, not a law. But for now, selection is where the human data is showing up. Design, meanwhile, is where the venture money and the partnership headlines still cluster.

Which brings us to the one AI-designed molecule everyone points to as the design thesis's proof point. That's Insilico Medicine's rentosertib, a TNIK inhibitor for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. First, the older data, for grounding. The Phase 2a trial enrolled 71 patients in China for twelve weeks, and its primary endpoint was safety. Per Dealroom's summary of the results, the 60-milligram arm showed a mean gain in forced vital capacity of 98.4 milliliters. Placebo patients declined by 20.3 milliliters. That's not news. And a trial that size wasn't powered to prove efficacy, so treat it as directional background.

The new piece is a paper in Nature Biotechnology. It applied six separate proteomic aging clocks to serum from a subset of the trial's patients. In the paper's words, "All six models consistently indicated a reduction in predicted biological age among patients treated with rentosertib." That's an intriguing signal. But it's an exploratory reanalysis of a small, safety-primary trial, run on part of its cohort, and not a prespecified efficacy endpoint. Here's the strategic risk, and again it's our inference. If AI-native biotechs start leaning on aging clocks in investor materials, they get a reusable surrogate that can differentiate a pipeline well ahead of hard outcomes data. Sophisticated investors should discount that, because a biomarker story can run far ahead of the clinic.

Now the correction this show owes you. We've described rentosertib as being in Phase 3 three times: on October 28th, November 3rd and November 5th. Some secondary sources do describe it as in, or moving toward, Phase 3. But this week's research could not confirm a Phase 3 initiation, timeline, or site list from any primary source. That doesn't mean the claim is false. It means we can no longer stand behind it, so we're flagging it as unverified rather than repeating it. The upshot is uncomfortable. The molecule most often cited as proof for AI design rests on a thinner verified clinical record than its boosters, including us, have been describing.

One smaller correction. Yesterday we said Chai Discovery gave no binding-affinity range for Chai-3, the model behind its new Roche deal, but HIT Consultant's coverage cites "nanomolar-range binding affinities," so we were wrong. The broader critique stands, because there's still no absolute hit rate and no independent wet-lab validation.

Now to where pharma is putting multi-year money down. On November 6th, we quoted Sanofi's stated ambition "to deploy integrated AI agents company-wide," with no numbers attached. That ambition now has a contract behind it. Sanofi has expanded its relationship with Owkin into a multi-year collaboration to co-develop biopharma AI agents. It's backed by a five-year license for Owkin's K Pro "AI Scientist" platform. No financial terms were disclosed. And per Contract Pharma's coverage, Owkin would lead development of the agents. So this is a commitment to build, not a finished deployment.

Still, the structure matters. Compare it with the target-selection deal AstraZeneca signed with insitro in October. That one put just 40 million dollars upfront against much larger contingent milestones, which made it a cheap option. A five-year platform license is a deeper commitment. It also extends this week's pattern that deployed pharma AI lands first in operations and agents. And it revives the custody question we raised on November 6th. Under a five-year platform license, who controls Sanofi's proprietary data, and the model weights it helps shape? Nobody has said, so treat that as an open question, not a disclosed term.

Pulling the threads together. Selection is producing real, if early, human evidence, first Recursion and now Evaxion. Design is still waiting on verified late-stage data, and its most prominent proof point, rentosertib, is less confirmed than the narrative suggests. On capital, keep two pools apart. Venture money and partnership headlines still cluster around AI design platforms. But the one long-dated pharma commitment we saw this week, Sanofi's five-year license with Owkin, went into agents and operations instead. And selection, the layer the human data actually supports, is still being bought as small options, like AstraZeneca's with insitro. So neither pool of money is yet paying up for what's working. On clinical success rates, there's genuine but tiny mechanistic evidence at the selection layer, and nothing yet that moves the odds through Phase 2. On R&D spend, nothing this week changes the picture. Nor was there a new development on real-world data or on AI regulatory guidance. Three things to watch. Does Evaxion name a next development step for EVX-01? Does a primary source confirm rentosertib's Phase 3 status, one way or the other? And do Sanofi and Owkin ever disclose what that five-year license is worth?

That's today's briefing. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
