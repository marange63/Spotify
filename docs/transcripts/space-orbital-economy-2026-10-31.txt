Good morning. It's Saturday, October 31st, and this is your space and orbital economy briefing.

Today's story is that SpaceX's lead has turned into a dependency, and that dependency gets priced two different ways. Militaries treat it as a vulnerability. Bond markets treat it as a reason to lend cheap.

Start with Ukraine. Starlink and Starshield military terminals serving Ukrainian drone units were throttled starting in mid-October. Ukraine's presidential adviser Serhiy Beskrestnov put the cap at 100 miles an hour. Above that speed, he said, the terminals stopped working, and that hindered drone operations. That's his figure, from an interested party, not a SpaceX disclosure. As of October 23rd, CNBC was still reporting the restriction in place.

Then, on October 24th, Ukraine's defense ministry said its own monitoring systems recorded the restoration of Starlink and Starshield speed. The same statement confirmed that those systems had previously recorded the limitation. So the throttling happened, and the ministry says it has been lifted. Treat that restoration as the ministry's own claim. NBC noted the ministry stopped short of saying service was fully restored, and nothing more recent in hand settles it.

Elon Musk went on record that same night. He said Ukraine's military had violated Starlink's terms of service by using it for offensive drone strikes. That loophole, in his words, was closed last week. He framed it as rule enforcement, saying SpaceX simply enforced its terms of service when they were bypassed. But NBC's account also has Musk saying that, for Starshield specifically, speed and usage limits are decided by the US military and ultimately the president. That's a paraphrase, not a verbatim post, and it points the authority back at Washington. Meanwhile SpaceX the company did not respond to CNBC's request for comment. So the only explanation on record is Musk's personal one, and it names two possible owners of the decision. Who actually held the switch is still not independently settled.

Here's why that matters beyond Ukraine. Yesterday this desk argued that in a contested orbit, a satellite's value depends on how fast you can replace it. The threat there was an adversary, Chinese lasers in a Defense Intelligence Agency assessment. This is a different failure mode. Proliferation answers the laser. It does not answer the vendor, or the government standing behind the vendor. A drone unit with a thousand working satellites overhead still has exactly one owner to negotiate with. That's the desk's inference, not a reported policy shift. But it's the single point of failure allied militaries now have to price in.

If allies want an alternative to that single point of failure, the obvious place to look is Beijing's state constellation. China SatNet's Guowang program launched its 27th batch on October 9th, nine satellites on a Long March 12 from Wenchang. By the count in that launch report, China planned 310 Guowang satellites this year and had deployed 86. Satellite trackers disagree on these counts, so treat the ratio as directional. Even so, it's a program running far behind its own plan, with the year's final quarter already underway.

Scale the comparison against the leader. The tracking service Flight Atlas, as of October 28th, puts SpaceX at about 77 percent of all payload mass carried to orbit in 2026. China sits at roughly 11 percent. Methodologies differ across trackers, but that's a seven-to-one gap. Mass, not the launch count, is the number that tells you who's actually moving freight to orbit.

Now the counter-argument, because missing a deployment target isn't the same as losing the broader race. Spectrum filings and orbital-slot claims can run ahead of deployment, and a state program can surge launches when it chooses. And Qianfan, the more commercially oriented Chinese constellation, has already signed trial service agreements in Brazil, Malaysia, Kazakhstan, and Turkey. Apply the same discipline this show applies to AST SpaceMobile's partnership list. A trial agreement is optionality, not revenue. Still, those are footholds in markets where the Western alternative is otherwise SpaceX. So China's challenge to Starlink right now isn't a satellite-count race. It's a regulatory and distribution contest in markets where buyers don't yet have to choose sides.

So the dependency on SpaceX is real, for allies and rivals alike. Here's how the credit market prices that same concentration, as safety rather than risk. A Motley Fool analysis of S&P Global data puts SpaceX's 2031 bonds at a 6.8 percent yield. Its five-year credit default swap spread is 230 basis points. Oracle's five-year CDS sits at 304. CoreWeave's sits near 1,040, roughly four and a half times SpaceX's spread. That's a single secondary source, so hold the precise levels loosely. The ordering is the point.

What separates those names is revenue that doesn't depend on AI data centers. The same analysis says SpaceX's GPU borrowing is backed by revenue from Starlink and launch services. That ties back to a deal this show covered on October 29th. According to a press release from the investment firm Apollo, SpaceX reached a binding commitment to enter a roughly 40 billion dollar master finance lease for GPUs. The lenders are led by Apollo and Goldman Sachs. In plain terms, outside financiers pay for the chips, and SpaceX pays them back over time. That deal still has to clear closing conditions. Today's Motley Fool piece instead describes 40 billion dollars of GPU debt backed by Starlink and launch revenue. The two descriptions don't fully match. So treat the structure as unreconciled, and don't read it as a new or closed raise.

Musk added his own read on the sector. He said debt markets have almost entirely shut off money for companies without substantial revenue independent of AI data centers. He said more than two hundred neoclouds face what he called a guppy-level death and consolidation. Take that with a grain of salt. SpaceX runs its own compute business, so forecasting rivals' deaths is also competitor commentary.

Still, the pattern holds. What worries a defense ministry is exactly what reassures a bondholder. And it cuts into the Starlink spin-out question this show keeps returning to. Suppose Starlink and launch cash flow are the collateral that lets SpaceX borrow this cheaply for compute. Then spinning Starlink out as a separate company would pull that collateral out from under the AI build-out. That's the desk's inference, not a reported plan. But it's a real tension for anyone pricing a spin-out as inevitable.

What could widen that lead next is SpaceX's own flagship vehicle. Starship Flight 15 still has no firm launch date. Reporting, including Spaceflight Now's on October 30th, now points to mid-November. That's press expectation, not a SpaceX schedule. Musk says he expects Starship to fly weekly at some point in the first half of next year. That's his expectation, not a plan of record, and the next flight isn't even dated yet.

Notice the pattern this extends. Yesterday this desk pointed out that Neutron's date came from an analyst and New Glenn's from trade press. Now even Starship's next date comes from trade press, not from SpaceX itself. The company that sets everyone else's pace doesn't publish its own date either. And the milestone that matters is the ship. SpaceX has caught Super Heavy boosters since October 2024, but no Starship upper stage has been caught. Recovering that ship is what would extend SpaceX's lead from launch share into cost per kilogram. Until it happens, the gap between announced cadence and operational reality persists even at the top of the industry.

Pulling it together. The orbital economy's genuine inflection this week isn't a new rocket or a new constellation. It's that SpaceX's scale has become financial infrastructure for the AI build-out. Bond markets that trust Starlink's cash flow price it tighter than Oracle. The parts still running on demo-stage uncertainty are China's state constellation, which is behind its own plan, and Starship's cadence, which still rests on an unproven ship catch.

The open question worth sitting with is whether allied governments decide that independence from a single commercial operator is worth paying for. This month showed that the switch exists, and that someone else holds it. Watch Rocket Lab's earnings on November 9th, and ULA's Vulcan return, slated for November 13th. Watch Starship Flight 15, whenever it actually flies. And watch Vast's Haven-1 station, still targeting the first quarter of next year, and riding, once again, on a Falcon 9.
