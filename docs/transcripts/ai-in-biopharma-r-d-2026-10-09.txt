Good morning. It's Friday, October 9th, 2026, and this is your briefing on artificial intelligence in biopharma R&D.

Bloomberg reported on October 8th that Isomorphic Labs, the Alphabet drug-design spinout, is in early talks to raise new capital. Bloomberg's headline puts the target valuation at "at least $40 billion." Its sources put the range at forty to fifty billion dollars. Isomorphic pools biology data with its rivals, and it keeps its own models private. This fits a pattern: share data, lock up models. Today's question is what investors are actually paying for at that price.

Start with the number itself. Isomorphic's prior round closed in May of this year and raised two point one billion dollars. No valuation for that round has been disclosed anywhere we can verify. So there is no clean multiple to compute here, and I'm not going to invent one. What we can say is simpler. Five months after a round with no disclosed price, the company is reportedly being talked about at forty to fifty billion. These are early talks, not a closed deal. According to coverage of the Bloomberg report, it doesn't name investors or say how much money Isomorphic is seeking.

The pattern has been building on this show. On October 3rd we reported that Isomorphic keeps its models locked in-house. On October 8th we covered Isomorphic co-funding a large, shared biology-data initiative alongside Google DeepMind and Meta. That day we argued the money in AI biology was moving into data rather than models. Today's report refines that call rather than reversing it. Data gets pooled because it's pre-competitive. Models stay locked because that's where investors are pricing the premium.

So what is that premium actually pricing? Isomorphic's headline evidence is a self-reported, in-silico benchmark. The company says its IsoDDE platform is "more than 50% better" at finding binding pockets than the closest competing tool. That is the company's own number, not independently validated. And it's a computational benchmark, not a wet-lab result, let alone a clinical one. As of September 30th, Isomorphic had not announced an IND filing or a trial start, and it has not publicly named a drug candidate. Its partnership economics are real but modest next to the valuation talk. Eli Lilly and Novartis together paid just over eighty million dollars upfront in 2024. The headline deal values, worth billions more, are milestones that haven't been earned. The January 2026 Johnson and Johnson collaboration has no disclosed financial terms at all.

Compare that to the one filed price we have for access to a biology foundation model. Recursion's deal with Tempus, which we covered yesterday, licensed its model for twelve million dollars, non-exclusive, oncology only. A license fee and an equity valuation aren't measuring the same thing, so this isn't an arbitrage. But the gap is instructive. Renting access to a model is priced in the low tens of millions. Owning the company that builds one is reportedly priced in the tens of billions. That gap only makes sense on one of two readings. Either investors believe Isomorphic will eventually own clinical assets outright, capturing drug economics rather than service economics. Or they're paying for something else entirely: Alphabet adjacency, and scarce frontier AI talent and compute. Based on what's disclosed today, the evidence supports the second reading far more than the first. That matters, because the second reading holds up even if no Isomorphic molecule ever works in patients. The first reading needs exactly that proof, and none exists yet.

There are consequences either way. If a round closes anywhere near that range, it resets the private comparables for every AI design platform. Public AI biotechs will face pressure to explain why they trade so differently. It also commits Isomorphic to a path. A valuation that size is hard to justify on partnership milestones alone. It implies Isomorphic has to own clinical assets eventually. That means more capital into IND-enabling and clinical work, where the binding constraint is biology and trial timelines, not model quality. So the price is less a verdict on the model than a bet that Isomorphic becomes a drug company.

If that's the price being put on a model with no clinical asset, what does real preclinical evidence from a rival platform look like right now? That brings us to Xaira Therapeutics, one of the most heavily funded companies in de novo design. On October 2nd, Xaira published its first pipeline disclosure, its first public look at output since launch. For its lead oncology program, Xaira used its X-Design model to design 182 single-domain antibody sequences de novo against one tightly constrained epitope. That's a design count, not a hit rate. A secondary newsletter has circulated hit-rate figures, but we couldn't match them to Xaira's own release, so I'm leaving them out. What would make the 182 meaningful is a primary hit rate, functional data, and a candidate nomination.

The more interesting claim is a second program, against a complex GPCR target. There, prior antibody-discovery efforts had failed to produce a functional antibody, including display and immunization campaigns and a llama immunization campaign. That's a materially different test from AIntibody, the blinded competition this show covered on October 3rd. In results published in August, AIntibody found that generative design, at best, only matched conventional methods. And that was on an easier task: improving an antibody that already existed. Xaira is claiming something else. Finding an antibody where classical methods found nothing is a claim about expanding the addressable target space, not about working faster. If it holds up, it matters more than a speed claim would.

The second-order effect, if it generalizes, lands on discovery services. Hard-target antibody campaigns run the classical way, through display libraries and animal immunization, are exactly the work this would displace. Spend would shift from campaign labor toward high-throughput validation capacity, which is the tools-vendor read. That is conditional on replication. One self-selected disclosure is not replication.

And Xaira's work is, right now, entirely preclinical. There is no IND, no named candidate, and nothing we can verify on functional activity. A company's first disclosure also tends to show its best cases. Nor is Xaira entering an empty market. In August, Chai Discovery signed Bristol Myers Squibb, its fifth major pharma platform deal in under eight months, following Eli Lilly, Pfizer, Novartis, and argenx. Chai's terms remain undisclosed, the same logos-not-commitments caveat this show raised last week. Chai's predecessor model self-reports de novo hit rates of roughly 16 to 20 percent. Hit rates don't compare cleanly across targets and assays, so treat that as a rough reference point, not a head-to-head. So the real question for Xaira isn't just whether its science works. It's whether Xaira competes as a vendor pharma rents alongside Chai, or builds the owned pipeline its funding implies it should.

Now step away from discovery, to where AI value is measurable today. Simulations Plus disclosed a funded partnership with an unnamed global pharma company. It will apply its Turin AI platform to quantitative systems pharmacology inside an active program. The test is whether AI-assisted exploration of model structures can cut the time needed to build and evaluate mechanistic PK/PD models. The partner and the dollar figure are both undisclosed, and this is a test, not a demonstrated result. But it sits in the part of the stack this show has argued is already paying off. Language models are carrying document and operations work, and real-world data is earning its keep in narrow, data-dense settings like oncology. Neither area produced fresh news this week. What they share with the Simulations Plus pilot is a payoff in cycle time and cost, which can be measured now. Discovery platforms, by contrast, are still selling a promise the clinic hasn't tested.

So, open questions heading into next week. Does the Isomorphic round actually close, and at what price? Does Isomorphic confirm a clinical trial start before year-end, its own stated target? Does Xaira publish primary hit-rate and functional data, or nominate a candidate? And does the Simulations Plus pilot report an actual time saving?

On clinical success rates, the best evidence is still an aggregate read across AI-discovered programs. It says those molecules clear Phase 1 safety trials well above historical norms. In Phase 2, where a drug has to actually work, they succeed at about the industry's ordinary rate, and the completed sample is still small. So the demonstrated edge is early safety and speed, not efficacy. Today's items can't move that picture, because neither Isomorphic nor Xaira has a molecule in the clinic.

On R&D spend, the renting-versus-owning split points one way. Pharma keeps discovery budgets roughly flat, and moves spend toward validation capacity and outsourced design through modest or undisclosed deals. Private capital, meanwhile, pays frontier prices to own the model layer. What's new is the widening gap between what private markets will pay for that layer and what the clinic has actually confirmed. That gap is the story to watch.

That's today's briefing. Have a good one.
