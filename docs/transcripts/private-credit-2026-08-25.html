Private Credit & the Corporate Credit Cycle

Transcript · August 25, 2026

Good morning. It's Tuesday, August 25th, 2026, and this is Cautious Optimism Briefings.

On Sunday, this show flagged the First Brands confirmation ruling as still pending, and named it first among the things to watch. It came Monday. It came against the debtor.

Judge Christopher Lopez rejected First Brands' Chapter 11 plan and converted the case to Chapter 7 liquidation. The plan had proposed to pay administrative and priority claims out of future litigation recoveries against former executives and lenders, rather than out of asset sales. Lopez found the math didn't work. Those lawsuits would need to generate roughly one point nine billion dollars before administrative claims, which get paid first, were even satisfied in full. The plan also wanted to defer payment on at least two hundred twenty-two million dollars of debt racked up during the bankruptcy itself. And the headline number the plan leaned on was bankruptcy expert Marc Kirschner's estimate that the litigation claims could eventually be worth up to twenty-five billion dollars. According to the reporting, Lopez treated that estimate as too speculative and too slow to fund a plan today. His own stated reason was blunter than the math. The sales process, he said, simply didn't produce the prices anyone wanted.

The analytic point is narrower than private credit's fraud problem. Separate two claims that usually get blended together. The alleged factoring fraud at First Brands is idiosyncratic, a specific off-balance-sheet scheme at one auto-parts roll-up. What isn't idiosyncratic is that a recovery model built on selling collateral into a market failed when that market wouldn't pay the assumed price. That's a structural risk in any sponsor-backed roll-up financed on optimistic exit assumptions, fraud or no fraud. So hold the recovery number open. Nobody yet knows what a broken senior claim actually pays here. But don't let the industry's one-off-fraud line stand unchallenged either, because what failed in court this week was the process, not just the accounting. One consequence is already visible in the documentation. Fraud representations, reporting cadence and third-party collateral verification get tightened by a court outcome rather than by a default rate.

One more data point, dated but relevant. An industry analysis from last November found twenty BDCs had lent directly to First Brands over the prior two years, with fifteen still exposed at the Chapter 11 filing. On what both reported as a forty-seven-million-dollar first-lien position, Monroe Capital Income Plus marked it near a hundred and one percent of par. Prospect Capital carried its stake at just under ninety-three. That's eight points of daylight on the same borrower's senior paper, on marks that appear to predate the fraud becoming public. It's old data, and the gap may reflect different priority tranches rather than a clean apples-to-apples comparison. Treat it as suggestive, not proof. But it previews a pattern we're about to see again, live.

Because if a court won't take a lender's recovery math at face value, look at what happens when a rival lender won't take another lender's mark. In the second quarter, Ares Capital moved five borrowers to non-accrual. The largest was an eighty-four point six million dollar subordinated loan to AmeriVet Veterinary Partners, priced at eight and a quarter percent PIK and now marked around sixty-two cents on the dollar. FS KKR had already put AmeriVet on non-accrual two quarters earlier, for the period ended last December. Same borrower, same credit, two lenders, and two opposite conclusions about when it stopped performing. That's mark dispersion made concrete, not abstract.

The mirror image sits at Crescent Capital, where non-accruals fell from five point seven to four point eight percent of debt at cost after three restructurings closed in the quarter. A falling non-accrual ratio looks like healing. But a restructuring can embed the loss in equitized debt or an extended maturity, where the percentage never shows it. Kayne Anderson went the other way. It added two more names to non-accrual, took a nine point four million dollar realized loss liquidating a position called Sundance, and saw PIK income drop to four and a half percent of total investment income from seven and a half. That prior-quarter figure was flattered by a one-time catch-up payment, so the decline overstates the underlying trend.

None of these are macro names. Veterinary roll-ups, print and marketing platforms, flooring distribution. This reads as stress concentrated in over-levered, sponsor-driven consolidation plays, not a broad cyclical turn. But the aggregate underneath the name-level noise is harder to wave away. Median dividend coverage across listed BDCs was point nine nine times in the first quarter, and point eight nine times once you strip out PIK interest. A meaningful share of what shareholders are being paid isn't covered by cash income. That's a distribution-sustainability problem, and it moves before any loss gets formally recognized.

Now the third venue where this asset class is getting priced from outside itself. Jefferies Credit Partners is targeting roughly a billion euros for a new evergreen fund, split about half investor equity and half leverage, structured to buy loans out of its own existing book while also writing new ones. It's the private-equity continuation-vehicle playbook imported into credit. The manager generates liquidity and a fresh valuation print on its own holdings without a genuine third-party sale. Be fair about the structure. Evergreen secondaries are legitimate liquidity technology, and a manager buying its own paper at least has full information on it. But a bilateral price is not a market-clearing price. So the test that matters isn't that this fund exists. It's whether the vehicle eventually prices above, at, or below the loans' last reported NAV.

Last venue, and the one that's already returned a partial verdict: the retail redemption queue. Stanger's data shows repurchase requests hit twelve point four percent of NAV in the second quarter, up from ten point four in the first, and the highest level the firm has ever recorded. Sponsors filled only thirty-eight percent of those requests, returning five point nine billion dollars for the quarter. Blackstone's BCRED, the largest non-listed BDC, had to prorate at its five percent cap after requests came in near ten percent of shares outstanding, twice the limit. That's a sharp reversal from the first quarter. Back then, requests ran at seven point nine percent of shares outstanding, also above that five percent cap, and BCRED met every one of them in full. It did that by upsizing the repurchase offer and putting in roughly four hundred million dollars of Blackstone and employee capital. The read-across is that the implicit sponsor put in a retail wrapper is discretionary, and it does not scale with the queue. And meeting a cap means selling what is saleable, which is the most liquid, most senior paper. The residual book degrades by composition without a single new default.

Stanger compares this to the BREIT real-estate cycle of 2022 and 2023, where the first elevated quarter also ran near twelve percent of NAV, the next peaked just under twenty, and elevated demand persisted six more quarters after that. The early trajectories, Stanger says, look similar. Weigh the source and the analogy. Stanger is an investment bank to this channel. And real estate gated on a rate shock that repriced the underlying, while direct lending is floating-rate and still cash-generative. So this doesn't settle whether the second quarter was the peak or only the second inning. It does argue the queue is more likely early than exhausted, on a channel whose new money had already dried up. Gross fundraising into non-traded BDCs was two billion dollars in the second quarter, down eighty-two percent from a year earlier. Which is the awkward backdrop to opening retirement plans to this asset class while the existing retail wrappers are prorating.

Pull the threads together. This isn't proof the credit cycle broke. It's the first week where the pricing of this asset class got tested from outside it in four separate venues: a bankruptcy court, a rival lender's earlier mark, a manager buying its own paper, and a redemption queue. Three of the four have already answered, and in each one the outside number came in worse than the inside one. The fourth, the Jefferies vehicle, hasn't priced yet, and where it prints against carrying value is the cleanest test still ahead. The biggest unresolved risk isn't default frequency. It's recovery, the gap between what private marks assume and what a contested sale, a rival lender, or a fleeing investor base will actually pay. And with coverage already below one times, the distribution is what breaks before the losses do. Watch what a Chapter 7 trustee actually realizes on First Brands. Watch the next repurchase window in the retail channel, at Cliffwater, close on August 31st. That's the first read on whether the queue rolled into the third quarter. And watch the third-quarter non-accrual and PIK prints. Any one of those would confirm or break this read.
