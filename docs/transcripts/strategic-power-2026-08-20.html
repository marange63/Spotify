Cautious Optimism Briefings

Strategic Power, Energy, and Institutional Change

Transcript · August 20, 2026

Good morning. It's Thursday, August 20th, and this is your strategic power briefing.

Start in the Gulf, where the energy chokepoint that underwrites the abundance narrative just reopened as a live fault line. On Wednesday the UAE suspended all trade and financial transactions with Iran, and its foreign ministry framed the halt as open-ended, in its words, "until further notice." The trigger was renewed missile fire the UAE says it took on August 18th, with one projectile reportedly landing inside its own waters. Iran's foreign ministry denies launching anything, so treat the precipitating incident as alleged, not established. What isn't in dispute is the scale of what's being severed. Before this war, the UAE supplied more than thirty percent of Iran's imports, worth roughly twenty-one billion dollars, according to WTO figures cited by Fortune. That makes the UAE Iran's single largest trade gateway, and an open-ended suspension of that scale is a real additional squeeze on an economy already under sanctions, not a symbolic gesture. Here's the second-order read worth holding: a gateway that size, if the suspension actually holds, strains Iran faster than sanctions alone have, which cuts two ways. It could push Tehran toward a face-saving off-ramp on the Hormuz standoff, or it could remove its incentive to de-escalate at all. Note the word "if": the UAE announced no end date, but it also named no enforcement mechanism, so open-ended in wording is not yet durable in practice.

This lands against a Strait of Hormuz that has effectively stopped functioning as a commercial artery. Before the war, roughly a hundred and thirty vessels transited the strait; over August 4th through 6th, that was down to eight to fifteen. Iran's position, laid out by its foreign minister, is that the strait doesn't reopen until Washington ends the war and pays reparations. And on Wednesday, President Trump flatly denied any talks were underway or scheduled with Iran, directly contradicting his own adviser Jared Kushner's earlier description of "robust" conversations. So the diplomatic track is opaque, not collapsing on any visible timeline. The market reaction has been unambiguous: Brent crude pushed toward ninety-two dollars a barrel on Wednesday, its fourth straight session of gains, explicitly tied by traders to the stalled Hormuz talks. The US Energy Information Administration doesn't expect Middle East output to get back near pre-conflict levels until early 2027, and pegs Brent's 2026 average at eighty-seven dollars. The so-what here: a normally neutral Gulf state is now aligning further against Tehran, and that keeps a durable war-risk premium in crude right at the moment the "America has energy abundance" story needs the rest of the world to stay cheap and stable. It doesn't.

That same elevated, stickier energy cost curve carries a specific consequence at home. A war premium in oil doesn't just sit in gasoline; it feeds through the fuel complex into the gas-fired generation that hyperscalers are leaning on to power the AI buildout, raising the cost of exactly the marginal electron they need most. And it meets a grid that cannot approve power fast enough to match what AI developers are asking for. Georgia offers the starkest instance yet. Proposed data-center projects in the state imply somewhere between twenty-nine and fifty-eight gigawatts of demand — a wide range, since only a fraction of projects have disclosed real numbers, so treat it as directional, not exact. Georgia Power's actually approved capacity is about four gigawatts. CEO Chris Womack has disclosed that the OpenAI data center project alone, in Effingham County, will push the utility past that ceiling. This isn't confined to one state. PJM Interconnection, the grid operator spanning much of the mid-Atlantic and Midwest, ran its latest capacity auction and procured a hundred forty-five thousand seven hundred seventy-seven megawatts, six and a half gigawatts short of what reliability rules require for 2027 and 2028. PJM's reformed interconnection process was overwhelmed in April with eight hundred eleven new project applications totaling roughly two hundred twenty gigawatts. The operator's response, built out over the past month, is a curtailment framework: large new loads that don't bring or contract for their own new generation capacity can now be cut off first during a shortage. The so-what: the gigawatts hyperscalers announce in press releases are running well ahead of the gigawatts actually interconnected to the grid, and utilities are now openly rationing rather than simply approving. That caps how fast AI capex converts into live, running compute, no matter what the capital commitments say.

That rationing has a predictable downstream effect, one this beat has been tracking all week. When the grid tells a data center it can be curtailed first, the developer stops waiting on the interconnection queue and brings its own generation — behind-the-meter gas turbines, on-site storage, and increasingly a nuclear order. So the curtailment rules aren't just a constraint; they're a forcing function pushing the largest loads off the public grid and onto private power. And there's a political tail here worth flagging. When data-center-driven grid investment starts showing up in residential power bills in a swing state like Georgia, the buildout stops being a purely technical story and becomes a state-level policy risk — a vector for siting rules and rate backlash that could slow the most aggressive timelines further.

The engineering answer to that gap arrived this week too, and it's worth hearing precisely because of how far out it still is. TerraPower is preparing its first dedicated data-center nuclear project, building on the backdrop of Meta's January agreement to buy eight Natrium power plants from the company. The design's pitch for AI workloads is a molten-sodium heat-storage system: the reactor keeps generating at a steady rate while banking surplus heat, then draws on that reserve to spike turbine output when demand jumps. That matters because conventional reactors can only ramp output by about five percent a minute, and small modular reactors only about ten percent a minute, while gas turbines have been struggling to handle the spiky load profiles AI training and inference actually produce. If load-following reactors prove out, they undercut the assumption that only gas peakers can serve this demand — a genuine medium-term case for nuclear-linked power producers and uranium. But groundbreaking on this project isn't expected until 2027. So-what: this is a signed order and a design on paper, not an operating watt, and it belongs in the same bucket as the Georgia and PJM numbers above it — an announcement standing in for capacity that doesn't exist yet.

Pull the thread across all three and the pattern is the same one this beat keeps surfacing: energy is the base layer of strategic power, and it's being squeezed at both ends simultaneously. Abroad, a reopened Gulf war-risk premium undercuts the case that American energy abundance insulates the rest of the world from Middle East risk. At home, the grid that's supposed to power the AI buildout is rationing interconnections faster than it's approving them. And the supply-side fix everyone points to, whether it's nuclear load-following or bilateral trade realignment, keeps landing on a calendar dated years out while the demand is now. Watch whether the UAE's "until further notice" language turns into an actual enforced rupture, and watch whether Georgia Power's ceiling holds once Effingham comes fully online — those are the two tells that will tell you whether this is a temporary squeeze or a structural one.

That's the briefing for August 20th. Stay cautiously optimistic.
