Good morning, it's Wednesday, September 9th, and this is Capital Markets Radar.

We're leading with the long end of the Treasury curve, because it just told us something the Fed didn't. Secretary Bessent's Treasury tried to talk yields down this week. The tool was a six billion dollar buyback of ten- to twenty-year debt. Yields went the other way. The ten-year surged to as high as 4.85 percent on the announcement, according to CNBC. Sources put the current level in a band from 4.77 to 4.85 percent.

Here's why the size of that buyback matters more than the headline. More than eight point four trillion dollars of Treasury securities roll over between now and year-end, against a federal debt stock above forty trillion. Six billion against eight point four trillion is less than a thousandth of the rollover. A purchase that small cannot move the supply-demand balance of the long end. So if yields moved anyway, they moved on what the buyback signalled, not on what it bought. That is the more uncomfortable reading, not the reassuring one. It looks like the Treasury tried to manage a price it does not actually control, and the market noticed. Strategists are now watching 4.8 percent as the level where, in their words, fiscal risk starts to spill into other assets. That is their framing, not a mechanical trigger. And I'll flag one attribution problem honestly. The same week contained a payrolls beat and a twenty-five percent move in oil. Isolating the buyback's own contribution to any equity reaction is not something this sourcing supports.

So that's one tightening force, and it isn't coming from the Fed. Here's the second, and it isn't either. Brent crude broke back above a hundred dollars a barrel today, for the first time in six weeks. That extends a run of roughly twenty-five percent since early August, as fighting between US and Iranian forces intensifies. Yesterday this desk quoted Brent at 98 dollars 66, so the hundred-dollar break is one session old. The genuinely new piece today is the Strategic Petroleum Reserve. It is down to about 289.7 million barrels, its lowest level since 1982. In the oil shocks of 2022 and 2023 the US had a far larger release option to lean on. It doesn't now. So an energy shock has to transmit more fully into the inflation data, rather than being partly absorbed by a reserve release.

Before we leave oil, one pushback. Reporting puts roughly ten million barrels a day offline because of the war, equal to about a tenth of global demand. I'd treat that number skeptically, and the reason is the price itself. This conflict already printed a hundred and twenty-six dollars a barrel earlier on, before crude round-tripped. A twenty-five percent run to today's hundred implies Brent was back near eighty in early August. So this break is a recovery toward the highs, not a new extreme. Barrels equal to a tenth of world demand going missing should be taking us through that hundred-and-twenty-six-dollar peak, not back toward it. And the ten million figure is aggregated secondary reporting, not an EIA or IEA count. Either the estimate is inflated, or demand destruction is quietly doing more work than the headline suggests.

Both of those tighteners land on a Federal Reserve that has just gone quiet. The committee entered its blackout period this week and cannot speak again until the 17th, the day after its decision. The setup, briefly. August payrolls came in at 162,000, roughly triple consensus, and hike odds on CME FedWatch now sit in the high fifties for a quarter-point move. Here is the new point. Last week a single conditional sentence from Governor Waller pulled those odds from roughly two-thirds back to a coin flip. That input is now switched off. Between here and the decision, the only things that can move the pricing are tomorrow's producer price index and Friday's consumer price index. Neither has been released, and I am not going to predict either one. What I will say is that the Fed's working assumption is getting harder to defend. That assumption is that tariff- and energy-driven inflation is temporary and fading. Oil through a hundred dollars with no reserve buffer left is precisely the fact pattern that tests it.

And there is one more input the Fed cannot mute, because it isn't coming from inside the building. The president, the vice president, the Treasury secretary and a senior economic adviser have all publicly urged the Fed not to raise rates. That does not show up as a rate move. It shows up as term premium, which loops straight back to the long end we opened with. So some of this week's yield move may be a credibility charge on Fed independence, rather than a growth or inflation forecast.

Now, how is all of that being expressed in the tape. Gold traded near 4,424 dollars an ounce today, up about one point seven percent on the day. Bitcoin sat in the seventy-eight to seventy-nine thousand band it has held since last week, with the trackers split on whether it rose or fell. So I won't tell you Bitcoin fell. What I can defensibly say is that gold outperformed Bitcoin under the same Middle East shock.

Score that against our own record. On September 5th we argued that real yields, not geopolitics, owned the hard-asset complex, with gold well down from its January record. Gold's bounce here does not fit that framing as cleanly as it did four days ago. The question that follows is a sizing question. If Bitcoin keeps trading as the high-beta risk asset while gold does the hedging, it belongs in the risk sleeve rather than the real-asset sleeve. Two things could undo that read. We have no ETF flow or open-interest data this week, so spot selling versus a leveraged unwind stays unresolved. And if the Fed does hike on the 16th, the penalty on non-yielding assets hits gold exactly as hard as it hits Bitcoin. So today's divergence may not survive the event that is supposedly driving it.

That rotation is visible in equity leadership too, not just in cross-asset pricing. The S&P 500 closed down about six tenths of a percent yesterday. The sectors underneath tell the real story. Energy and utilities were the only sectors up meaningfully, with tech barely positive. Healthcare fell more than two and a half percent, and financials fell nearly one and a half. Financials dropping on a day yields rose runs against the textbook net-interest-margin trade. On one session's evidence, I'd read that as a credit-quality or duration-mark worry rather than a clean rate trade.

Credit itself still isn't confirming any of this. We have been calling for high-yield spreads to widen, and for a fourth straight session they haven't. The number I can put on that is the ICE BofA high-yield index at two hundred sixty-six basis points. Treat it as a September average rather than yesterday's close. It is essentially unchanged from the two hundred sixty-five basis points this desk quoted last week. Spreads holding flat through a payrolls shock, a hundred-dollar oil break and a long-end repricing is a complacency print, not a stress print. Yesterday we called that relocation rather than vindication, and the explanation stands. The strain moved to private credit, which marks quarterly rather than by the second.

One loose end before we close. The dollar index slipped to around 98.8 for a second straight session, even as yields rose. That is an unusual decoupling. The proximate driver looks like yen strength on Bank of Japan tightening expectations, which may make this a yen story rather than a dollar story. But a dollar that will not rally into hawkish Fed repricing is at least circumstantial support for the fiscal-risk-premium read of the long end.

So here is what would prove this whole framing wrong. A cool producer price print tomorrow, a soft consumer price print Friday, or a real retracement in Brent that takes the inflation leg off the table. Producer prices on the 10th, consumer prices on the 11th, the Fed on the 16th. None of those numbers exist yet. Everything here is positioning, not outcome.
