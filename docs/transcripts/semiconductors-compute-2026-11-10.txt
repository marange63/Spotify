Good morning. It's Tuesday, November 10th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

Yesterday this desk said the market hadn't priced the Nvidia board's investigation into Jensen Huang as of Friday's close. On Monday, it did. Nvidia shares closed down 4.27 percent. The allegation itself hasn't moved. A board committee is reviewing Huang's relationship with a woman U.S. intelligence reportedly considers a Chinese spy. She has been affiliated with Megaspeed, a Singapore-based chip company. As we reported yesterday, CNBC says Megaspeed has been accused of helping restricted Chinese companies acquire Nvidia AI chips. All of that remains reported concern and accusation, not a government finding. And the board says it has no reason to believe secrets or national security were compromised. Two things are new. In an internal email, Huang told staff he had "fallen short." And China's Foreign Ministry called the spy claims "entirely baseless." That's Beijing's predictable diplomatic line, and it's evidence of nothing in either direction.

Here's what the 4.27 percent actually measures. The obvious worry is sector beta. This tape has been selling AI hardware since last Thursday's rout. But Monday wasn't a selling day for Nvidia's peers. In the same session, Advanced Micro Devices closed up 13.26 percent, and Broadcom closed up 8.78 percent. Reporting tied Broadcom's gain to a broader chip rebound and to optimism about AI revenue. For AMD, the reporting in hand names no company-specific catalyst on Monday. Its quarterly results came out a week earlier, on November 3rd. So Nvidia fell on a day its two closest challengers rallied hard. That rules out the lazy explanation. The decline belongs to Nvidia, not to the sector.

There's one competing reading worth holding, and AMD's missing catalyst is what makes it plausible. Some of that AMD and Broadcom strength may be money rotating out of Nvidia and into its rivals, rather than independent good news. If so, Nvidia's real governance cost is bigger than Monday's 4 percent drop, because part of it shows up as gains in AMD and Broadcom. Either way, score it by dimension. Nothing here touches Nvidia's architecture lead, its manufacturing access, or its software stack. What's being marked down is leadership continuity and political standing in Washington. The discount is on the people and the permission, not on the product.

If investors are marking down Nvidia's leadership, the real question is who's positioned to take share. On that, the evidence splits between reported results and a seller's claims. Start with the reported numbers. Advanced Micro Devices' Data Center segment hit a record 8.5 billion dollars in the third quarter, up 97 percent year over year. That's from the earnings release AMD put out on November 3rd. Total company revenue was 13.4 billion dollars, up 45 percent. So the data-center line is growing more than twice as fast as AMD overall. That's reported revenue, not a roadmap promise.

Be precise about what's inside that number. The segment blends EPYC server CPUs with Instinct GPUs. The release doesn't split the growth between accelerator share taken from Nvidia and server-CPU share taken from Intel. Then there's the customer evidence. AMD has a strategic agreement with Anthropic to deploy up to 2 gigawatts of Instinct MI450 GPUs in AMD's Helios racks. A quick disclosure: Anthropic makes the model behind this show. "Up to 2 gigawatts" is a ceiling written into an agreement, not a shipment count. And the release gives no MI450 unit volumes or qualification status. Still, this is the only share-shift evidence in hand that shows up as reported revenue. On the production and customer dimensions, AMD is the challenger with receipts.

Broadcom is the other challenger, and it's making a bigger claim on thinner evidence. This week's coverage recycles a line from Hock Tan, Broadcom's chief executive. He said Google's TPU 8i is, quote, "comparable, if not surpasses the Vera Rubin GPU." He also claimed a second custom design runs at less than half the cost. But he said both things on Broadcom's fiscal third-quarter earnings call, around early September, not this week. Neither claim came with a benchmark, a workload, or a metric. And chip-level parity says nothing about volume or software, which is where Nvidia's moat sits. So on architecture, Broadcom's challenge is asserted, two months old, and unbenchmarked.

The better-sourced Broadcom story is the commercial structure underneath. Broadcom's filings describe a long-term agreement to develop and supply future generations of Google's custom TPUs, plus networking components through 2031. That's the customer-relationship dimension, locked in for years. The filings also describe an arrangement giving Anthropic access to about 3.5 gigawatts of TPU-based compute starting in 2027. Consumption, in the filing's words, depends "on Anthropic's commercial success." And Broadcom's quarterly filing describes a financing platform meant to enable more than 20 gigawatts of custom-accelerator compute for frontier labs through 2028. That's an enabling target, not booked revenue.

Put that next to a separate exposure this desk flagged on Sunday. Broadcom's filing also discloses a backstop of up to 29 billion dollars on an AI-accelerator lease, for a customer it doesn't name. The pattern across AMD and Broadcom is the same. Nvidia's challengers are competing less on chip sales alone and more on financed, multi-gigawatt commitments to the labs that consume the compute. Here's the second-order read, and it's the desk's inference, not either company's statement. Those order books are now tied to whether Anthropic, OpenAI, and their peers succeed commercially. A slowdown in frontier-lab demand wouldn't just soften a pipeline. It would travel through the financing arrangements underneath it. The supplier's balance sheet has become a competitive weapon, and it now carries the customer's risk.

Now bring that back to Nvidia, and again this is the desk's inference. A lingering governance cloud over the chief executive of the company that anchors the AI-compute stack strengthens the hand of every hyperscaler and lab negotiating with it. It also sharpens their incentive to qualify second sources, however the board's inquiry concludes. Those second-source channels already exist at multi-gigawatt scale on paper. AMD's revenue line shows at least one of them turning into reported sales.

Last beat, and it closes the loop on the China channel where this episode opened. Three governments are now tightening the China perimeter, each from a different side. Start with Washington. Its controls on advanced chips flowing into China are the frame this desk tracked all last week. Next, Beijing. China has drafted its own list, which would restrict 23 AI-related technologies, including chip designs, from going out. Comments on that draft close November 15th. And now Taipei has joined. Taiwan's legislature held a hearing on amendments to its Trade Act covering semiconductor materials and equipment bound for mainland China. The proposal would raise the maximum prison sentence for violations to 10 years. That's a proposal at the hearing stage, not enacted law, and penalties only bite if enforcement follows.

Two inferences follow, and both are the desk's. First, every vendor moving AI hardware through Asian channels, not just Nvidia, faces a rising compliance cost on top of the existing export regime. That's the same channel layer at issue in the Megaspeed accusations from the top of this episode. Second, tougher Taiwanese penalties could further squeeze mainland fabs like SMIC on specialty chemicals and used equipment. The takeaway is that the cost of doing business in this channel is now rising from three capitals at once. None of that depends on how the Huang inquiry ends.

So here's the state of play. Nvidia's governance risk got its first market price on Monday, and that price belongs to Nvidia, not the sector. Share absorption by its challengers is best evidenced at AMD, in reported revenue, even if that revenue doesn't isolate the GPU share. At Broadcom, the architecture case is still a two-month-old seller's claim, while the financing commitments are large and contingent. Watch four things. First, what the board's inquiry actually finds. Second, whether anyone produces an independent benchmark or volume disclosure behind the custom-silicon parity claims. Third, what Beijing's November 15th comment deadline produces. Fourth, the rare-earth date this desk flagged on Sunday. China's suspension of its expanded rare-earth export controls was due to lapse today, and at research time no outcome had been confirmed either way.

That's the briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
