Strategic Power, Energy, and Institutional Change

Good morning. It's Wednesday, November 25th, 2026, and this is Strategic Power.

Here's the thread for today. The bill for AI power is arriving years before the supply meant to cover it. What fills that gap this decade is not new nuclear. It's gas, existing reactors, and whoever controls the hydrocarbons in between. That's why a military agreement in Caracas belongs in the same briefing as a capacity auction on the largest grid in the country.

Start with that grid. PJM's independent market monitor, Monitoring Analytics, has put a new number on what data centers are costing its customers. What's new is that the cost now shows up in the all-in wholesale price, not just the capacity line. According to trade-press coverage of the monitor's third-quarter report, wholesale power averaged 111 dollars and 61 cents a megawatt-hour from January through September. A year earlier, it was 79 dollars and 19 cents.

The monitor attributes 9 dollars and 95 cents of today's figure to data-center load in the capacity market. On our arithmetic, that's roughly a third of a rise of about 32 dollars. So data centers are a real driver of the wholesale bill, but a partial one.

What explains the other two-thirds? Other contributors include electrification of heating and transport, plant retirements, transmission investment and fuel costs, though the coverage doesn't size any of them. And two cautions apply to the third that is attributed. It's a modelled counterfactual, not an audited bill. And the monitor is an advocate in this fight, as you're about to hear. So the political story that data centers alone broke the bill overstates their share.

That advocacy is aimed at December. PJM's next capacity auction runs December 9th through 15th, and it prices delivery for 2029 and 2030. On Sunday, we noted that the last auction cleared at its cap and still came up short of the reliability target. The monitor now expects December to do the same.

The cap this time is 337 dollars and 83 cents per megawatt-day. If the auction clears there, the monitor estimates another 5 to 10 billion dollars of data-center-driven cost lands on bills. That would come on top of the 29.4 billion dollars the monitor says data-center load has already added to capacity costs, cumulatively. The December number assumes a clear at the cap, so treat it as a projection.

The monitor wants the auction postponed. That's a recommendation, not a decision. The new detail is that it's not alone. Eight state consumer-advocate offices wrote to PJM's board on November 18th. They asked it to seek federal approval to postpone, until forecast data-center load comes out of the model. They asked for a reply by November 23rd. As of the latest reporting, the board had not said whether it would reply or seek a delay. No filing at the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission has been reported either. That matters, because PJM needs FERC's approval to move the auction. So for now, December is still on the calendar.

Who benefits if December clears at the cap? The likely winners are the owners of existing nuclear and gas plants in the PJM footprint. Think Constellation, Vistra and Talen. A clear at the cap extends their firm capacity revenue into the 2029 delivery year, and ratepayers carry the cost. For hyperscalers, the logic points the same way. Rising grid capacity costs make self-supplied gas, and contracts on reactors that already run, look better than waiting on the grid.

Here's the point worth sitting with. December prices capacity for 2029 and 2030. Now look at the flagship new reactor being built to serve a data center. That's Google's project with Kairos and the Tennessee Valley Authority, called Hermes 2. It has been under construction since April, and it targets first power in 2030. At best, that lands at the edge of the window December is pricing. So whatever scarcity gets priced next month can't be met by new nuclear. It has to come from plants that already exist, from gas, or from demand response.

So who can actually deliver power on that timeline? Physically, almost nobody new. The International Energy Agency, as reported by The Ecologist, projects about 320 gigawatts of new capacity built to power data centers through 2035. Nuclear supplies only about 20 of those gigawatts. That's a scenario, not a commitment. The Ecologist also notes that no data center anywhere yet runs on a new reactor built for it. The AI build is simply running faster than concrete can pour.

That gap now shows up in prices. The long-term uranium contract price sits at a nominal record of 99 dollars a pound. Meanwhile, one analysis has the two largest uranium-equity funds down 46 to 50 percent from their 52-week highs.

Those are two different signals. The record term price says utilities are still paying up for fuel. On our reading, that's fuel for reactors that already run or are restarting. The equity selloff looks more like a repricing of timelines. Investors had front-run a new-build schedule that physical construction can't meet.

There's a competing explanation, too. On November 20th, with markets pricing a Fed rate hike in December, this desk argued that higher rates hit the longest-dated assets first. Some of this drawdown may simply be long-duration capital re-rated for higher rates. That would not be a verdict on nuclear demand at all.

For investors, then, the next re-rating in uranium and small modular reactor names depends on physical milestones. That means first-power dates and construction progress, not announced offtake deals. And a record term price supports the miners' fundamentals more than it supports the new-build developers.

So if new nuclear can't bridge this decade, something else has to. Goldman Sachs, in a November 24th note as reported, said up to 52 gigawatts of additional US data-center capacity could come online in 2028. It said that expansion could tighten power markets and lift natural-gas demand. Treat the 52 as a ceiling, not a build schedule.

The IEA's renewables-heavy 2035 mix isn't a contradiction. It's near-term speed against long-run composition. This decade, gas and existing reactors carry the load.

If hydrocarbons carry the bridge, then control over hydrocarbon supply is strategic, not incidental. That's why Washington's move in Venezuela matters. On November 19th, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth flew to Caracas. He signed a military cooperation agreement with the government of acting President Delcy Rodríguez. Al Jazeera reports it covers joint training, intelligence sharing and defense equipment. Outlets disagree sharply on how far it goes. Nothing we have supports calling it basing or a permanent presence.

Now the oil side. The sanctions regime has not lifted. Treasury's Venezuela sanctions program page, read on November 23rd, still lists the Venezuelan government and the state oil company, PDVSA, as blocked. What has changed is a layer of general licenses that carve out specific permitted activity. General License 50A, for example, covers oil and gas operations for named companies, among them Chevron, BP and Eni.

Rodríguez says PDVSA has signed 84 new contracts since January. That's her claim, relayed secondhand. And no verified production figure has surfaced to back it up. The evidence so far is political and contractual, not physical. Contracts are not barrels.

On Tuesday, this desk argued that Beijing controls critical minerals, and Washington controls advanced compute, through license desks rather than outright bans. Treasury's general licenses are the same instrument, applied to crude. Venezuelan supply now runs on revocable permissions, layered over a blocking regime that is still legally in force. That's a throttle Washington holds, not a market that has reopened.

For oil markets, that's the so-what. Any added Venezuelan barrels can be switched off by license. They shouldn't be priced as durable non-OPEC-plus supply growth until someone actually counts them.

One last item, on minerals, because it has a deadline this week. Last November, Beijing's Commerce Ministry suspended its ban on exporting gallium, germanium and antimony to the United States. That suspension expires this Friday, November 27th. These metals feed semiconductors, optical fiber and munitions. A separate January 10th date has caused confusion. According to a trade-law firm's summary, Beijing's Announcement Number 58 keeps six other rules suspended until then, covering rare earths, magnets, batteries, graphite anodes and synthetic diamonds. Gallium, germanium and antimony aren't on that list. So, on our reading, Friday's deadline stands on its own, and we've found no official notice on what happens then. Washington, for its part, is extending tariff exclusions on 178 Chinese products as its side of the September trade truce.

So pull the thread. The AI power bill is landing now, in PJM's wholesale prices. The flagship new reactor for a data center doesn't deliver until 2030. In between sit gas, existing reactors, and Washington's licenses. Two dates to watch. Friday, November 27th, when Beijing's suspension of its gallium and germanium export ban runs out. And December 9th, when PJM's auction opens. The real test there isn't the clearing price. It's whether the postponement push ever reaches FERC before bidding starts.
