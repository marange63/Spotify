Good morning. It's Sunday, July 26th, and this is your robotics and physical AI briefing.

The pattern this week has been consistent: model capability keeps advancing, but the binding constraint has shifted to economics and regulation. Today gives us both halves of that story in sharp relief.

Start with the lead. NHTSA has escalated its investigation into Tesla's Full Self-Driving system, and it's further along than it looked. This isn't a fresh probe — it's a formal Engineering Analysis, designated EA26002, which NHTSA opened back on March 18th by upgrading an earlier preliminary evaluation. An Engineering Analysis is the agency's last procedural step. From here, NHTSA either closes the file or starts pushing for a recall. On July 2nd, the agency sent Tesla a 24-part demand covering roughly 3.2 million vehicles that have ever run FSD in the US, tied to nine crashes in reduced-visibility conditions — sun glare, fog, airborne dust — including one fatality and one injury. The centerpiece of the demand is an internal Tesla document titled "Radar Saves Us." NHTSA wants all internal communications and test records tied to it, calling the document "particularly relevant" to understanding the limitations of Tesla's vision-only object and event detection system in exactly these conditions. Tesla pulled radar from its vehicles back in May 2021. We don't know what the document actually concludes — nobody's seen the text — but its title alone tells you what NHTSA suspects it will show. Tesla's response is due August 12th; noncompliance carries civil penalties up to roughly $27,900 a day, capped near $139 million. Separately, Senators Markey and Blumenthal have formally asked NHTSA whether it has evaluated Tesla's use of a five-second disengagement window in its public FSD safety statistics, against the 30-second window NHTSA's own Standing General Order requires for crash reporting. That's a senatorial question, not an adjudicated finding, but it's a second angle of pressure on the same reporting stack. So what's the stake here: this is the sensing bet that underpins Tesla's entire robotaxi commercialization timeline, and it's now one procedural rung from a recall. If the Engineering Analysis breaks against Tesla, it strengthens the relative case for the lidar-and-radar approach that Waymo and Zoox have taken, with knock-on effects for who wins the sensor supply chain.

That same demo-versus-durable gap shows up in the warehouse, at the company with the deepest pockets in the business. Amazon has quietly shut down Blue Jay, its warehouse sortation robot, roughly six months after unveiling it in October 2025. Blue Jay was designed to collapse three separate assembly-line functions — pick, stow, consolidate — into one machine. Amazon is folding the underlying technology into a new project called Flex Cell and has reassigned the Blue Jay team elsewhere. The public reporting only gives qualitative reasons — cost, manufacturing complexity, implementation problems — no specific loss figure, so don't read more precision into it than that. The point isn't the dollar amount, it's the pattern: this is the second disclosed leader-level failure in a week, after Zoox's fleet recall a few days ago. Staged capability keeps failing to survive contact with real-world cost and reliability, even at the operator with the most capital and the most warehouses to test in. And the read-through runs well past Amazon. It's a caution against taking any warehouse-automation vendor's unveiled-to-production timeline at face value — Symbotic and the rest of the automation incumbents included — because the gap between a working demonstration and a cost-viable line is precisely where these projects tend to die, and that gap is not priced into most of the sector's capex assumptions.

Now turn to the other side of the ledger, where capability is genuinely progressing. Figure AI's newest humanoid, Figure 03, arrived at BMW's Spartanburg plant in late June and went straight into Hall 52, an assembly and logistics hall — its first deployment on a logistics and sequencing workflow rather than the scripted pick-and-place task its predecessor, Figure 02, performed. Figure 02 contributed to the assembly of 30,000 cars at that plant over the prior year, so this isn't a pilot bolted onto nothing — there's a production track record underneath it. Figure 03 runs on Helix 02, the company's "pixels-to-actions" vision-language-action model, which coordinates hands, arms, torso, and feet to handle precise placement of thin-walled parts and forceful whole-body manipulation, adapting when parts show up misplaced or out of position. That adaptation claim is Figure's own, untested by any third party — there's no disclosed uptime, cycle-time, or intervention-rate data, which is the same missing number that dogged Figure's manufacturing-throughput disclosure last week. Still, the task itself getting harder — from a scripted motion to something that has to handle the long tail of a real logistics line — is a genuine test of the general-purpose-robot-brain thesis, not just more units of the same trick. If it generalizes, it's evidence that Helix is closer to a real manipulation policy than to task-specific choreography, and BMW Spartanburg becomes the reference deployment other automakers point to when they justify humanoid capex. If it doesn't, we'll find out the same way we always do with this sector — quietly, months later, in a shutdown announcement rather than a press release.

Briefly, two more threads worth tracking. In defense autonomy, Anduril and Archer Aviation unveiled Thunder at the Farnborough airshow, an armed, hybrid-electric autonomous tiltrotor built as a "loyal wingman" for the Army's Apache and future Cheyenne helicopters. It's been in development for three years under an exclusive partnership between the two companies. This extends manned-unmanned teaming from the fixed-wing fighter world into Army rotary-wing attack aviation for the first time, and it's a clean template for how the drone-industrial base keeps forming — a venture-backed eVTOL company paired with a defense-scale autonomy integrator, rather than a traditional prime going it alone. It also fits a pattern worth watching: loyal-wingman programs keep consolidating around Anduril as the autonomy layer, first in fixed-wing, now in rotary-wing, which tells you something about where value is accruing in the defense-autonomy stack. There's no awarded production contract yet and first flight isn't targeted until 2027, so treat it as a concept, not a deployment.

And capital keeps flowing into humanoids regardless of the deployment constraints above. UK-based Humanoid raised a $152 million Series A at a $1.35 billion post-money valuation, led by Prime Movers Lab, with Bosch and Schaeffler among the strategic backers, bringing total funding to $270 million. The notable part isn't the number, it's who's writing the check: German industrial conglomerates taking direct strategic stakes rather than leaving this to financial VCs — European incumbents hedging directly into their own future supply chain. It also raises the pressure on the American cohort, Figure and Apptronik among them, to court the same kind of strategic industrial money rather than pure financial rounds, because a Bosch or a Schaeffler brings a supply chain and a first customer, not just a check.

Pull the threads together. Capability is advancing where the task itself is getting harder, as Figure's move into adaptive logistics shows. But economics and regulation are what's actually gating deployment at scale, whether that's NHTSA closing in on Tesla's vision-only bet or Amazon's own warehouse robot failing to survive its unit economics. Capital isn't waiting for either question to resolve — which is exactly the gap to keep watching, because the money is being committed years ahead of the reliability data that would justify it. The near-term catalysts to watch: Tesla's August 12th response to NHTSA, and whether Figure or BMW ever disclose a real reliability number.
