Good morning. It's Saturday, November 14th, 2026, and this is Strategic Power. This week's loudest strategic signals each misplaced the real constraint, and the story was rarely where the headline pointed. Where it's our read rather than the reporting, we'll say so.

Start with Nvidia, because it's the clearest case. Nvidia's board accepted Jensen Huang's resignation on Wednesday, and the company announced it Thursday. He gave up his posts as president, chief executive and board member. Colette Kress, the chief financial officer, has agreed to serve as interim CEO, and reporting says she keeps the CFO role too. Harvey Jones, a director since 1993, becomes Nvidia's first-ever board chair. Per TechCrunch, there was no heir apparent or succession plan.

First, the backdrop. Last Sunday, November 8th, Huang told staff in an internal memo that he'd had a romantic affair with a woman TechCrunch describes as an alleged Chinese spy. In that memo, he said the board had opened an independent investigation into the spying allegations. The board says it has no reason to believe national security or Nvidia's secrets were compromised. No finding has been reported. Days later, Huang was gone. So the most important company in the AI build is now run by an interim arrangement.

The market reaction is real, but it needs separating from the scandal. CNBC reports Nvidia fell another two percent on Friday. It closed below four trillion dollars in market value for the first time since July of last year. The stock lost nearly thirteen percent on the week. That was its sixth straight weekly decline, the longest such streak since 2012.

A six-week slide began well before Huang's memo came to light. So our read is that part of this drawdown belongs to the rising-rate pressure we covered Friday, not to the scandal alone. Several Wall Street analysts told CNBC the departure could cost Nvidia as much as a fifth of its market value. Treat that as a ceiling, not a consensus forecast.

Here's the harder point. A resignation is not a finding. The reporting still calls her an alleged spy. Nvidia's own 8-K filing is purely procedural. It says Huang resigned effective immediately, and it names his successors. It gives no reason for the departure. And it never mentions the board's investigation, or any finding from it.

That silence won't stop the politics. A security scandal at this company hands cover to every chip hawk in Washington, whether or not it's ever substantiated. Our expectation is that the exit enlarges that cover. Congress and the market are apt to read the resignation as confirmation, even though nothing has been proven. Expect closer scrutiny of foreign access to chip executives and their IP across the industry, whatever the probe concludes.

And that's the real lesson. Washington's leverage over Nvidia never ran through Jensen Huang personally. It runs through Congress. Majority Leader John Thune and Armed Services Chair Roger Wicker have acknowledged there's no final defense-bill deal yet on the chip-control bundle. On November 12th, the day after the board accepted the resignation, Senator Mike Rounds put it plainly. "Which plan takes priority in the final product, I don't know yet." Thune did call added China chip restrictions a "high probability." But that's a leader's forecast, not conference text. None of these measures is law.

Here's the second-order effect. Nvidia heads into its next earnings report and its Washington fight led by an interim chief who is also running finance. That narrows its capacity to argue its China position just as Congress writes the rules. It also opens a window for rival chips and hyperscalers' custom silicon to pitch buyers on continuity. Nvidia lost its chief executive. Congress is still the gatekeeper.

Nvidia's chokepoint is a permission. The energy chokepoint this week is physical, and it's behaving even more strangely.

The UN Security Council describes Iran as having all but closed the Strait of Hormuz. An aggregator citing IMF PortWatch counted three ship transits on November 8th. A normal day sees about eighty-five. And yet Brent crude held near ninety-six dollars a barrel on Friday. Market reporting from Invezz says the International Energy Agency now sees the first global supply surplus since the Iran war began. A shut strait and an oil surplus, side by side. That tension is the real story.

That surplus is a headline, with no reliable figure for its size. The one hard number behind the calm is a record emergency release. In March, IEA members committed 426 million barrels of emergency stocks. That was the largest collective action in the agency's history. Two other explanations are plausible, though we can't measure them. Demand may be softening at ninety-six-dollar oil, with the Fed leaning toward hikes. And more crude may be moving through bypass pipelines.

Now the catch. Stock releases aren't production. Those barrels have to be rebuilt eventually. So this calm is borrowed against future inventory, and the market sits one escalation from a sharper move.

That forces us to qualify our own framing. On Friday this desk called Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline the insurance policy, and a Hormuz closure the bigger exposure. But with the strait already all but shut, the bypass is closer to the main route than to insurance. That makes Thursday's claimed Houthi attack on the line more consequential than we implied. There's still no confirmation of damage either way. The market is absorbing a closed strait. But it's doing so on borrowed barrels, through a bypass that has become its single point of failure.

Oil shows a market adapting around a real, physical chokepoint. America's grid shows almost the opposite problem: a demand story that's bigger in the forecast than in the concrete.

Howden research published Wednesday finds US data-center electricity demand has grown about nineteen percent a year. That's roughly ten times the pace of grid capacity expansion. Treat the ratio as directional, since sources disagree on the base year. It's the number behind every grid-panic headline.

Now set it against a figure from PJM's independent market monitor, reported by Energy Live News. Forty-two percent of forecast data-center load growth, for the three delivery years running through 2029, has already been cancelled or delayed. Two cautions. That figure lumps cancellations with delays, and delayed load still arrives. And it reaches us secondhand, not from the monitor's report itself.

Those two numbers aren't a contradiction. One measures realized growth. The other measures a forecast pipeline. Together they say the grid constraint is real, but the forecast it's benchmarked against is inflated. This is the measured version of a pattern that's been building since Monday, when Oncor called its own queue a planning signal. Announced gigawatts keep getting discounted, and now we have a rate.

Our read for markets. Power-equipment and gas-turbine order books priced for the forecast buildout likely carry more downside than the headline suggests. Utilities and independent power producers whose rate cases lean on forecast load share that exposure.

OpenAI's move points the same way. It has pledged to cut demand at its directly developed US campuses during grid emergencies, by up to a gigawatt at its Georgia site. That's a pledge, not a contract or an observed curtailment. It's the voluntary version of what ERCOT's chief asked Texas lawmakers for: power to order data centers to cut use before a grid emergency, not just during one. Hyperscalers are drifting toward becoming demand-response resources. The grid problem is real, but it's being priced against a forecast that keeps shrinking.

One more signal outran its substance this week, this time at sea.

Per France 24's account, Taiwan's defense ministry counted forty-nine Chinese aircraft on November 2nd. That was the most on any day this year. By November 8th and 9th, the ministry counted just one aircraft and eight ships near the island. No named drill was ever declared.

Taiwanese officials, as reported by the Japan Times, call this mode silent intimidation. A pulse and a retreat, with no exercise name attached, is cheap coercion, not a shift in conflict posture. But a quiet week isn't de-escalation of intent either. The likely result is a standing risk premium on Taiwan-exposed chip supply chains, one that never quite triggers. That favors diversification as a slow structural trade, not an event trade. This is coercion designed never to give markets a marker.

A short watch list. First, whether Nvidia's upcoming earnings report and the briefing House chairmen requested proceed on schedule, and who speaks for the company. Second, any defense-bill conference text on the chip provisions. Third, any Saudi or Aramco statement confirming or denying damage on the East-West line.

Four stories, one pattern. In each, the headline number and the durable constraint were two different things. That gap is where the next surprise comes from.

That's Strategic Power for November 14th. Have a good weekend, and stay cautiously optimistic.
