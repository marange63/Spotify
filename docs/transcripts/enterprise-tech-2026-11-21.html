Good morning, this is your enterprise technology briefing for Saturday, November 21st, 2026.

Today's spine is disclosure quality. This week, enterprise AI was priced and promoted on a private mark, a government list, and a product label. None of those counts how much AI customers actually run in production. And the one filing that should carry that count could not be verified for broadcast. So keep one question in mind through four stories: what is actually being measured?

Start with the freshest one. The Wall Street Journal reported, and Reuters carried the report yesterday, that Databricks is in early talks to raise several billion dollars. The round would value the company at about 225 billion dollars. That is up from 190 billion dollars just three months ago, in August. By our arithmetic, that's a step-up of roughly 18 percent in a single quarter. Treat it as early talks. The report is sourced to people familiar with the matter, and terms are not final. No lead investor is named, and Databricks did not respond to Reuters' request for comment.

Here is the skeptical part. What has Databricks actually disclosed? Two product-level run-rates. Its Lakehouse data-warehousing business has surpassed a 1.5 billion dollar revenue run-rate. Its Lakebase database product has exceeded 100 million dollars. Those are floors for two products, not a company total. The reporting carries no company-wide revenue figure, so there is no honest way to compute a multiple against the new mark. That absence is the point. A buyer at this price is paying for expected growth, and for scarce private exposure to an AI data platform. It is not paying against counted adoption.

Why raise again only three months after a 5 billion dollar round? There are two plausible readings, and the reporting confirms neither. One is that Databricks needs the capital to fund compute and AI-agent workload commitments. On that reading, the raise is a cost of staying in the agent race. The other is an opportunistic markup ahead of an eventual IPO. On that reading, the price is a marker set for a future listing. Hold both until terms are disclosed. Either way, notice what the buyer gets. Nothing in the reporting suggests a company-wide revenue number comes with the new price.

One small product note belongs inside this story. Microsoft's Azure Databricks release notes show that machine-to-machine OAuth authentication is now generally available for the Salesforce, ServiceNow, and Jira connectors in Lakeflow Connect. That shipped on November 18th. It is a feature release, with no usage or revenue attached. But it matters for the incumbents on the other end of those connectors. The bet for systems-of-record vendors like Salesforce is that controlling the data and its permissions keeps customers paying, even as agents take over the interface. Here is our inference. A neutral platform that pulls system-of-record data out under automated credentials weakens that moat. The more easily the data leaves, the less that rent holds. It also means the data-platform contest is increasingly fought over who owns the pipes into the systems of record, not just who stores the data.

So the Databricks story lands like this. The reported price is being set for a platform whose disclosed evidence is two product run-rates and a connector release, not a company-wide revenue number.

Compare that with what a public listing is supposed to force. In the interest of disclosure, Anthropic makes the model behind this show. Anthropic began trading on Nasdaq this week, and a listed company files quarterly reports that analysts can quote line by line. Its S-1 may already carry a customer-tier adoption ladder. Several outlets have summarized one, but each says it is paraphrasing the filing, so no counts go on air until the filing's own language is verified. When they are, the test will be definitions. On metered billing, a customer crossing a trailing-twelve-month spend threshold shows spend that ramped, not a commitment that holds.

Against that standard, look at what the incumbents offered this week. Salesforce said the U.S. Department of Commerce picked its consortium as one of 33 initial consortia in a federal export program. That is a genuinely new kind of commercial vehicle for an enterprise AI and data-governance stack. But a government list is a channel, not a deployment. With 33 consortia chosen, it is not exclusive either. No dollar figures, contract counts, or country deals have been disclosed.

Here is the second-order read, and it is inference. Suppose the designation turns into facilitated deals in sovereign and regulated markets. Then export compliance and government relations become a sales differentiator. Rivals would chase the same designation, opening a competitive axis that has nothing to do with model quality. That stays unproven until a facilitated deal is disclosed. Meanwhile, Salesforce still has not disclosed a production-usage number for Agentforce, a gap this show has flagged all week. So file the export designation as a channel to watch, not evidence of adoption.

The same substitution of a label for a measure shows up at Sage, the accounting and ERP software incumbent. Sage reported full-year 2026 results this week. According to Investing.com's write-up of Sage's results slides, more than 60 percent of its revenue now comes from products with embedded AI capabilities. The reporting doesn't say what counts as an embedded AI capability. That gap matters. If a product has any AI feature at all, its entire revenue could count toward the 60 percent. On that reading, the figure is closer to a relabeling of the existing cloud base than a measure of AI monetization.

This is the cleanest example this week of the rebranding problem. An AI revenue share arrives with no AI-specific attach, usage, or pricing uplift anywhere in the coverage. The risk is that the metric spreads, and this is inference. If share of revenue from AI-enabled products becomes a standard investor metric for ERP and accounting vendors, analysts will need to discount it toward whatever attach rates they can actually verify.

But Sage also cuts the other way, on a separate point. Its annual recurring revenue rose 12 percent, to about 2.9 billion pounds. That is a long-established subscription incumbent still compounding at double digits. It is evidence against the view that agents are about to hollow out incumbent subscription revenue in the near term.

The quality of that growth holds up too. Underlying revenue grew 11 percent, and 10 percent on an organic basis. So acquisitions are doing very little of the work. Recurring revenue growing a touch faster than revenue is a modest sign the base is still building. Still, nothing in the reported figures isolates how much of that growth comes from AI. So the label proves nothing about AI monetization, while the ARR line argues against panic. Until Sage discloses AI attach rates or pricing uplift, that 60 percent describes its catalog, not its customers.

So here is where that leaves the week. A private mark, a government list, and a product label were each offered in place of a counted number. And the filing that should carry one couldn't yet be quoted. Here is what follows, as inference. Once one AI vendor reports customer tiers every quarter in a quotable filing, vendors that report only run-rates, designations, or labels face a widening evidence gap. Expect one of two outcomes. Either more vendors start disclosing in dollars, or the market starts charging a discount for opacity.

Watch three things from here. First, whether the Databricks round actually closes, and whether it comes with a company-wide revenue figure. Second, whether Anthropic's first quarterly report as a public company carries customer tiers under a stable, quotable definition. Third, whether Salesforce's next earnings report finally puts a production number on Agentforce. Beyond those, the next security and cloud reads arrive in early December.
