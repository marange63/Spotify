Cautious Optimism Briefings

Strategic Power, Energy, and Institutional Change

Transcript · October 1, 2026

Good morning. It's Thursday, October 1st, 2026.

This week the binding constraint on strategic capacity wasn't money, and it wasn't physics. It was governance: who pays, who signs, and who enforces. We'll see all three today.

Start with the grid. On Tuesday this beat called September 30th the first real-world read on whether paying for time-to-power pulls forward new supply in America's largest electricity market. That read didn't happen. The regulator suspended the mechanism, and PJM pulled the auction.

Here's the setup. PJM had proposed an emergency backstop procurement to plug a 6,831-megawatt shortfall against its reliability target for the 2028-29 delivery year. That gap is driven largely by data-center load. FERC's order on September 29th accepted the mechanism in form, but suspended it for five months, to February 28th, 2027. The commission found that three pieces of PJM's filing, in its words, "may be unjust and unreasonable." The first was cost allocation. The second was the treatment of a transmission owner that exits PJM. The third was the collateral required of load-serving entities.

Cost allocation is the heart of it. FERC said PJM "failed to adequately show that its plan for allocating the costs of the auction meets the agency's 'just and reasonable' standard." Commissioner See was more pointed. In See's words, PJM "did not adequately ensure that the procurement's costs will be allocated to the customers who drive its need." The collateral finding had a concrete victim. According to Utility Dive's account of the decision, Northern Virginia Electric Cooperative would have had to post roughly two billion dollars in collateral under PJM's design. That's a single cooperative, in the heart of data-center country, facing a balance-sheet shock before a megawatt is procured.

FERC's chair, Laura Swett, was blunt. "This commission will not be forced into accepting a deeply flawed, eleventh-hour procurement mechanism with billion-dollar implications for consumers." In a concurring statement, she added that the late filing left no time "to rehabilitate the mess we received."

So correct the timeline this beat gave last Friday. The bid window that was due to close October 21st is gone, and so are the early-December results. And be precise about what happened. This was a suspension, not a rejection. FERC accepted the mechanism. It just won't let it run yet. Don't read this as the regulator blocking data centers. Don't read it as proof the grid is physically maxed out, either. The objections on the record are about cost allocation and process, not about whether new megawatts exist to be bought.

Two readings compete, and both can be true. One: FERC is shielding residential ratepayers from paying for hyperscaler load. That's a distributional fight dressed up as market design. Two: every month of delay makes the 2028-29 gap harder to close, because new generation needs lead time, and the runway just got shorter. Whether any new build can still make that delivery year is unresolved. So is whether PJM's next regular auction holds its schedule.

Now my read, and it's analysis. Commissioner See's cost-causation language points toward data-center load carrying more of its own capacity cost. If that's where FERC lands, grid-connected AI siting in PJM gets more expensive relative to self-supply. That raises the value of behind-the-meter and co-located generation for hyperscalers, at least until February. It also moves the ratepayer fight up a level. Last week this beat covered Texas freezing data-center permits, a state siting fight. Cost allocation for data-center load is now a federal regulator's fight too. The so-what: the scarce input in PJM right now isn't steel or turbines. It's an agreed rule for who pays. Until that rule exists, no amount of available money buys the capacity.

If PJM's problem is who pays, Washington's other answer this week was to let an ally pay. The US and South Korea signed a framework under which Korea would put up to 120 billion dollars into new nuclear reactors on US soil, built on Westinghouse technology. Brookfield's chief executive, Connor Teskey, said the combination "will mobilize significant capital and accelerate deployment at scale." That is allied capital aimed at exactly the dispatchable baseload PJM is short of.

But read the language. It's a framework. "Up to" is a ceiling, not a commitment. The verbs are future tense. Nothing in the evidence names a site, a license application, or a construction date. That isn't necessarily damning. Financing coalitions often form before sites are picked. The real question is whether Korea's money turns into binding equity or loan commitments, and when.

For the far end of the announcement spectrum, take Valar Atomics' "Project Beehive." It's a proposed complex on more than 9,000 acres of federal land near Price, Utah. It would pair data centers with some 456 small nuclear reactors, plus fuel production and waste storage. It is a proposal, with no verified licensing, financing, or timeline.

Here's the market read, and again it's analysis. For fuel-cycle and uranium names like Cameco, the Korea framework is multi-year optionality, not a near-term earnings event. Anyone pricing it as capacity is front-running permits that don't yet exist. And note what it doesn't touch. Allied capital answers the financing question. PJM's backstop just stalled on a different one: who carries the cost, and on what terms. Money from Seoul doesn't settle that.

The same gap between the rule on paper and the plumbing underneath shows up in chips. Reporting by Asia Times on a California-based server maker, Aivres, found it exported at least 5.6 billion dollars in advanced technology to Southeast Asia between April 2024 and February 2026. More than 3 billion dollars of that was computers equipped with Nvidia's Blackwell chips. Those servers ultimately served Chinese customers, including ByteDance and Alibaba.

Yesterday we noted that the gate on Nvidia silicon now sits in Beijing, which decides which tier of chip it lets in. The Aivres evidence shows Washington's own gate has a side door. The export-control regime governed where the physical chips sat. It did not govern who ended up using the compute. "Ultimately served" most likely means remote access to servers outside China. That's a different enforcement problem from smuggling, and it may be legal under current rules, so this isn't a story about evasion. Two more caveats. The data runs through February of this year, so it measures a past flow, not today's. And 3 billion dollars of Blackwell systems is real, but it isn't frontier-cluster scale next to the domestic hyperscaler build.

Nor did last week's Trump-Xi summit close that side door. On AI, the two sides "did agree to discuss AI risks formally in coming weeks." That's an agreement to talk, not a decision. The so-what cuts two ways. For Southeast Asian data-center hubs, being a documented conduit to ByteDance and Alibaba adds regulatory risk to their pitch for foreign investment. For Nvidia, indirect China exposure stays a live political target, whatever the summit's tone.

Pull the three together. A regulator suspended an emergency power auction over who pays. An ally's capital arrived in a framework with no sites attached. And an export-control regime turned out to govern geography, not usage. In each case the declared policy was clear, and the enforced capacity was not. The constraint has moved from money and hardware to governance and enforcement. That's a slower, messier fight, and markets pricing announcements as capacity are early.

On the calendar: PJM's backstop is now set to take effect February 28th, 2027, so watch FERC's follow-on proceedings between now and then. And watch how much of the Korea framework converts into binding contracts, versus staying a ceiling.
