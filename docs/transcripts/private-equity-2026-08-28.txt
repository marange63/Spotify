Good morning, it's Friday, August 28th, and this is your private equity briefing.

Start with the one place where private-equity marks actually face an outside bid. Not a consultant's model, and not a quarterly valuation committee. An actual buyer, writing an actual check, for a portfolio it did not pick. That is the secondary market, and Jefferies' full-year review of it gives us a price. Average pricing on limited-partner portfolios finished 2025 at 87 percent of net asset value, a 200 basis point decline from the year before. Break it down by strategy and the spread is what matters. Buyout interests cleared at 92 percent of NAV. Real estate cleared at 70. That data is roughly eight months stale now, so treat it as last year's asking price rather than today's. Even so, it is the clearest evidence we have that when an allocator tries to sell its whole book, the market marks it down from what the general partner is carrying it at.

Don't take that as a verdict that the marks are wrong. A secondary buyer discounts for three things that have nothing to do with valuation integrity. It discounts for the time value of the unfunded commitments it has to carry. It discounts for its own return hurdle, because it has limited partners of its own to pay. And it discounts for underwriting blind to information the general partner can see. A discount is partly the price of immediacy, and immediacy is a real service. The strategy split argues against a single story of opacity, too. The gap between buyout and real estate is not one number about how much you can trust a mark. It is two different stories about what the underlying assets are actually worth.

Now the same-period read, which this show ran on Wednesday and is worth re-grounding, because it is the other half of the argument. Evercore's first-half 2026 secondary review put total volume at 121 billion dollars. Inside that, the sponsor-controlled channel — general-partner-led deals — came in at 65 billion. The channel limited partners use to sell for themselves came in at 56 billion. Those figures come from trade-press summaries of the Evercore review rather than from the document itself, so hold the decimals loosely and the direction firmly. Jefferies had GP-led at 48 percent of volume for all of last year. Evercore has it a shade over half in the first half of this one. Different houses, different periods, same trajectory. The sponsor-controlled channel keeps taking share.

And here is the pricing contrast that matters most, because it sorts this market by seller rather than by asset. Single-asset continuation vehicles — the deals a sponsor structures around the one asset it wants to keep — priced mostly at par, with some clearing as much as 14 percent above net asset value. Multi-asset vehicles, which are the closer analog to an allocator selling a whole portfolio, priced below par 71 percent of the time. That is a distribution, not an average, so read it as adverse selection rather than as sponsors systematically beating their own marks. Same market, same broad asset class, different seller, different price. When the sponsor controls what gets sold and when, it clears at or above the mark. When the allocator sells on its own timeline, it takes a haircut. That is a governance fact as much as a valuation one.

There is a second axis underneath that, and it is the newer piece. Discounts on limited-partner interests in quality, established buyout funds have narrowed to somewhere between 5 and 10 percent. Challenged sectors — office real estate, and venture funds from the peak vintages — are still discounted 10 to 25 percent. So the LP-led channel is bifurcating internally by asset quality, not only against the GP-led channel. That range is a proxy, and it comes from the same second-hand summaries, so it is not a like-for-like update to last year's blended figure. But if it holds, the honest reading is narrower than the doom version. The good books are close to clearing at their marks. The distress is concentrated in the sectors and vintages everyone already suspected.

So what does a limited partner do about a market that prices its exit that way? It stops arguing about the mark and changes the scoreboard. Distributions to paid-in capital — cash actually returned — is now the metric 21 percent of limited partners call most critical, up from 8 percent three years ago. That comes from survey data relayed in Allianz research. Over the same period, the share naming internal rate of return as the most critical measure fell from 42 percent to 35. There is no disclosed sample size and no field date in what we have, so hold the precision loosely. The direction is harder to argue with, because it shows up in behavior and not only in answers. Average buyout holding periods have stretched past six years. And limited partners are cutting general-partner relationships by an estimated 20 to 30 percent — an estimate in the source, not a hard count.

That pruning sounds punitive. It is actually rational, and the reason sits in the exit data. Global exit value did rebound in 2025, to 905 billion dollars. But roughly 78 percent of that value came from mega-exits. If realizations are concentrated at the very top of the market, an allocator trying to maximize cash back does not need a long roster of managers. It needs the handful of platforms that can actually sell something.

Follow that through, because it is a transfer rather than a retreat. The same dollars concentrate into the largest franchises — the ones already posting record fee-related earnings, as this show has covered all week. Mid-market and emerging managers get starved of capital, even while headline industry fundraising totals still look healthy. Aggregate statistics will hide that for a while. What they will not hide is whose next fund actually closes. The realized-return gap between the top platforms and everyone below them widens in cash first, and in fundraising outcomes second.

Put the two threads together and the allocator's choice is genuinely unattractive. Sell the whole portfolio and take a discount — high 80s as a blended number last year, better than that if your book is quality buyout. Or wait for liquidity on the sponsor's terms, through a continuation vehicle timed and priced by the general partner. That version pays you at or above par, but only on the one asset somebody else picked, on a schedule somebody else set. That is the mechanism by which a record secondary market and limited partners who still are not getting paid on their own terms coexist in the same cycle. Volume is not the same thing as satisfaction.

One closing item on the buy side, because the same name keeps turning up. Apollo agreed on August 6th to take EasyJet private for 5.7 billion pounds, at 7 pounds 15 a share. Rival bidder Castlelake withdrew from the process, so there was no bidding war. The deal is announced, not done, and completion is not expected before the end of March next year. Now note who the buyer is. This show reported on Thursday that Apollo is one of the managers selling bespoke exit financing to other sponsors' stuck funds. So the same balance sheet is monetizing the liquidity drought at one end. At the other, it is adding a regulator-adjacent airline with a long approval runway to the industry's unexited inventory. And an uncontested take-private, after the only rival walked away, is thinner evidence that financing markets are wide open than it first looks. The absence of a bidding war is the more informative fact here than the deal itself.

So where does the cycle stand. The secondary market is real liquidity, and it is now large enough to be structurally important rather than a niche release valve. But it is priced by who controls the sale, not by asset quality alone. And limited partners have responded not by fighting over marks, but by rewriting what they measure and shortening their manager lists. Watch three things from here. First, a genuine blended pricing print on limited-partner portfolios for the first half of this year, to replace a figure that is now a year old. Second, September quarter-end marks landing in late-October earnings, which is where a stale mark would finally have to show itself. Third, whether the sponsor-controlled share of secondary volume keeps climbing, or whether the allocator's channel finally gets a real bid. Any of those would confirm this read or force a rewrite.

That's the state of the machine. Have a good weekend, and I'll see you next time.
