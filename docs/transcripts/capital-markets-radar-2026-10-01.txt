Good morning, it's Thursday, October 1st, 2026, and this is your capital markets radar.

Yesterday this desk set a test. The question was whether long yields would keep climbing on data that should calm them. That test resolved within hours, and the answer was yes.

Start with the print. August core PCE, the Fed's preferred inflation gauge, rose 3.0 percent from a year earlier, per CNBC. That was down from 3.3 percent in July, and lower than economists had forecast. Headline PCE missed expectations by even more. It came in at 3.4 percent, against estimates of 3.7, per Yahoo Finance. By any normal reading, that is a cooler report than the market was braced for.

Now the reaction. The 10-year Treasury yield still closed the day higher, up about 4 basis points to roughly five-point-three percent. Invezz calls that the highest level in 24 years. Take that as one outlet's framing. Last week this desk described the 10-year as trading at its highest since 2007, and we won't pretend the two descriptions line up. What matters is the direction. A soft inflation print met a rising long end.

That makes three calming inputs the long end has shrugged off in eight days. Oil fell on Tuesday the 22nd, and the 10-year barely moved. Oil fell again on Friday the 25th, and yields rose anyway. Now a soft core inflation print has drawn the same response. Two instances was a pattern worth naming. Three says the bond market is not trading the next inflation print. It is pricing something stickier underneath.

So what is it pricing? There are three live explanations. The first is term premium. On this view, investors want more compensation to hold long duration, given heavy issuance and the fiscal backdrop, whatever a single print says. The second is growth. Invezz itself pins the move on resilient economic data, energy-driven inflation concerns, and rising fiscal debt. A yield rising on a strong economy is a very different signal from one rising on supply. The third explanation is new, and it concerns the print itself.

The Bureau of Economic Analysis rewrote how it prices three components inside the index. They are portfolio management fees, legal services, and computer software. According to TechTimes, economists at Goldman Sachs and JPMorgan had expected the rewrite to shave a little off core PCE. The actual drop of three tenths of a point exceeded even those estimates. So part of the cooler read may be a measurement change, not a genuine slowdown in underlying prices. If traders saw it that way, they had reason to discount the headline. Treat that as a plausible contributor, not a proven motive. The bank estimates reach us secondhand, and nobody has shown the market traded on them.

Gold offers a way to separate these stories, because it is the asset most exposed to real yields. Between September 21st and 25th, the real 10-year yield rose from 2.62 percent to 2.83, per Crux Investor. That climb carried gold's late-September pullback. Note the timing, though. It all happened before the PCE print. So it describes the shape of the selloff, not yesterday's move. But that shape points to real rates, the component policy and term premium push, rather than to an inflation scare.

Gold itself traded near 4,182 dollars an ounce on October 1st, per Trading Economics. That is down about 4.7 percent over the past month. As this desk said yesterday, that may still be a drawdown inside a longer uptrend. The point today is narrower. The asset most sensitive to real rates has been telling the same story as the long end.

Now layer in the policy backdrop. Two weeks ago the Fed hiked for the first time since 2023, to a range of 3.75 to 4 percent. The median dot still calls for one more quarter-point hike before year end. Set that against a core print of 3.0 percent, and you have the real policy question. Does a cooling core number weaken the case for that last hike? Or does the committee look through it, the way the bond market apparently has? Today's data can't answer that. Friday's payrolls and the October 14th CPI report are the actual tests.

So what does this mean for portfolios? Disinflation alone no longer buys duration relief. If the 10-year can't fall on good inflation news, long bonds become a less reliable hedge against a drawdown in risk assets.

When the risk-free rate won't come down, the first place it bites is wherever prices come from a model rather than a trade. That brings us to private credit. On Wednesday, Metrics Credit Partners, which Bloomberg calls an Australian private credit giant, suspended redemptions across wholesale funds managing more than 9 billion dollars. The trigger was its auditor. KPMG declined to sign off on the full-year accounts of three listed Metrics vehicles. Per Bloomberg, KPMG disagreed with how fair value was calculated on unlisted commercial real estate equity stakes. Metrics responded by cutting 168 million dollars from those valuations.

This is the marking problem this desk named on Sunday, now tested by a third party. On Sunday the worry was that private marks have no daily price to check them against. This week, an auditor checked some and said no. One line on the backdrop. Fitch has the US private credit default rate at a record, but other methodologies put defaults anywhere from 1 to 19 percent. So treat any single default number with skepticism.

Resist the urge to call the Metrics gate contagion. It's one manager, in one jurisdiction. The disputed assets are commercial real estate equity stakes, not core direct-lending loans. Nine billion dollars is small next to the global asset class. And we have no data on the redemption queue, so this may simply be an orderly response to an audit delay.

But the mechanism is the part that travels. Once an auditor won't accept a model-based mark, the fund cannot strike a net asset value. A semi-liquid fund that can't strike a NAV has to gate. That logic doesn't depend on jurisdiction. Any semi-liquid vehicle holding marked rather than traded assets carries the same exposure. And higher-for-longer rates are exactly the condition that makes those marks harder to defend.

Model marks are one place leverage hides. Crowded positioning is the other. Goldman Sachs prime brokerage data shows hedge funds had their worst underperformance against the S&P 500 in more than 20 years back in July. The cause, as Yahoo Finance reported it, was the unwinding of the single most crowded trade in the market, artificial intelligence. Fundamental managers were, in Goldman's words, aggressively cutting the AI longs that had generated their entire year-to-date alpha.

The exposure wasn't confined to the funds. Goldman disclosed that roughly 16 percent of its own prime brokerage financing book was tied directly to AI memory stocks at the end of June. That figure is three months old, and the July unwind has likely shrunk it. But it shows the lenders were as concentrated as the borrowers.

More recently, information technology was the most net-sold US sector for a fourth straight week, per Goldman prime brokerage data as reported. You can read that two ways. It may mean a crowded trade is quietly de-risking itself, which lowers fragility. Or the book may still be concentrated enough that a negative AI catalyst moves markets through financing and margin calls, not just fundamentals. Think of a weak hyperscaler capex guide, or a new export restriction. Either way, the names carrying the market's capex story are also carrying its leverage. That's worth watching closely into third-quarter earnings season.

Put it together, and the common thread across rates, credit, and positioning is the same. The market is no longer getting paid for calming news. The next tests are dated and specific. Payrolls land on Friday, October 2nd. CPI follows on the 14th, and the Fed decides on the 28th. None of those has printed yet. Watch whether the 10-year can actually fall on a soft payrolls number. If it can't do that either, the premium in the long end isn't a data story anymore. It's structural.

That's the radar for today. Trade carefully, and I'll see you tomorrow.
