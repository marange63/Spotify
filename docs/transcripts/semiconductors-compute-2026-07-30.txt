Good morning. It's Thursday, July 30th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

Start with the lithography chokepoint, because a report out this week says it just got a crack in it. China has begun mass-producing its first domestic immersion DUV lithography tools, the workhorse machines that pattern everything from legacy logic to DRAM. The producer is Shanghai Aishengna Electronic Technology Group, a state-owned company formed in August 2023 with about a billion dollars of registered capital, built by folding in teams from the startup Yuliangsheng and from SMEE, China's existing lithography maker. The plan is to ship about five machines this year and roughly twenty in 2027, with first deliveries to SMIC, Hua Hong, and the memory maker CXMT. I want to be precise about the sourcing here: this traces to a single Reuters exclusive, attributed to one unnamed person who asked for anonymity given how sensitive the subject is. It is not a government announcement, not a company disclosure, and Reuters' own reporting says the tool still needs further testing and remains far from matching ASML's competing systems. JPMorgan, quoted in that same piece, makes the sharper point: building a handful of tools is not the same as high-volume manufacturing, where yield, overlay accuracy, throughput, and reliability across thousands of wafer runs are what actually matter, and none of that has been demonstrated publicly. So treat this as a credible first breach of the equipment wall, not a proven one. The market didn't wait for the proof. ASML fell 6.3% on the report, closing at $1,645.65 and shedding roughly 44 billion dollars of market value, even though an analyst at Degroof Petercam had just called ASML's own quarter "blow-out results across the board." China is already down to about 20% of ASML's 2026 sales from 33% last year, and this report reads as a warning that even that shrinking base is at risk over time. The realistic scope, though, is mature-node and commodity chips, not the EUV-class tools that print leading-edge AI accelerators — immersion DUV tops out around 7-nanometer-class work with multipatterning. If it holds up at scale, expect allied governments to look hardest at what's still uncontrolled: Japanese photoresist and the equipment that deposits and develops it, plus mask-related tools, which currently carry zero export restrictions at all.

That lithography story pairs with something that happened just days earlier: China's memory ambitions found capital markets. CXMT, the country's leading DRAM maker, listed on Shanghai's STAR Market on July 27 and raised nearly 58 billion yuan, the largest mainland China IPO since 2010, at a roughly 85 billion dollar valuation. I covered the debut pop and the same-day Samsung and SK hynix selloff already, so here's the one number that reframes it going forward: CXMT is on track to exit this year with about 350,000 wafer starts per month, within roughly 25,000 of Micron's own capacity. That's production scale, not product parity — more than 98% of CXMT's revenue is still plain-vanilla DRAM, with essentially no HBM presence, so it's a generation behind in the memory that actually feeds AI accelerators. Put the two China stories together and the picture is coherent: self-sufficiency became fundable and tooled in the same week, but both wins sit at the mature, commodity end of the stack, aimed squarely at conventional DRAM pricing just as Samsung and SK hynix are diverting their own capacity toward higher-margin HBM. And that profit pool is enormous right now. SK hynix says its memory capacity is essentially sold out for 2026, and Samsung has pushed the price of a 32-gigabyte DDR5 module up about 60% since last September, from 149 dollars to 239. So the market CXMT is walking into is a bifurcated one: scarce, high-margin HBM the incumbents guard, and conventional DRAM where a well-capitalized Chinese entrant can add supply and, over a few years, cap the pricing power that's funding everything else.

Now to what's paying for the incumbents' side of that trade. Nvidia disclosed the scope of a deal wave that reportedly tops 750 billion dollars — a partnership with SK Group worth more than 500 billion, a guarantee of up to 250 billion for OpenAI's ten-gigawatt Ohio data center, roughly 350 billion more in discussed OpenAI chip financing, plus smaller deals with Safe Superintelligence and Naver. None of that aggregate is a signed, confirmed number in a single filing — it's a mix of committed and still-negotiated pieces. What is confirmed is the market reaction, and it showed up somewhere new. Nvidia's five-year credit default swap spread hit a record 82 basis points on July 27, its largest single-day jump since that contract started trading in November 2025. That's a credit-market signal, not an equity one — it means bond investors are now pricing incremental balance-sheet risk from these vendor-financing-heavy deal structures, which is arguably more diagnostic than a stock move. Nvidia shares still fell nearly 5% that day, wiping out about 250 billion in market value and handing the title of world's most valuable company to Apple, at roughly 4.94 trillion dollars against Nvidia's 4.75 trillion. Jensen Huang rejected the circularity criticism outright, calling it "ridiculous." Both the IMF and the Bank for International Settlements have separately flagged this kind of circular AI financing as a systemic risk worth watching. So what: the AI-capex skepticism that's been building all week just moved from stock prices into the credit market, a venue that tends to reprice risk before equities catch up. And if that widening sticks, it won't stay contained to Nvidia. Credit tends to reprice the whole neighborhood — the data-center landlords, the power-infrastructure names, anyone whose revenue leans on the same buildout — before the equity in those names moves. It also feeds back on Nvidia itself: the more it leans on guarantees and vendor financing instead of straight equity, the more its own cost of capital tracks that spread.

Against that backdrop, Intel offered the clearest Western execution proof point in months. The company now targets risk production on its 14A process for internal products in the second half of 2027, a year earlier than previously planned, with high-volume production in 2028 — matching TSMC's cadence instead of trailing it into 2029. CEO Lip-Bu Tan says 14A is tracking ahead of even 18A on defect density and transistor performance, though that's Intel's own characterization, not an independently benchmarked figure, and the process design kit outside customers need to start real layouts, PDK 0.9, is only on track for October. The harder evidence is Tesla, which has committed to using 14A for chips at its Terafab AI complex in Austin — Intel Foundry's first confirmed external 14A customer. That's the proof skeptics wanted since Intel warned it might abandon the node without an anchor tenant. An external tenant matters because a fab that only prints Intel's own chips can't cover its capital cost; a second customer is what turns 14A from a cost center into a foundry business. Still, a design win and an accelerated roadmap are not shipped volume, and 2028 high-volume production remains a target, not a result.

So here's where the week leaves the global chip order. China spent it building an independent base at the mature node and in commodity memory, forcing a rethink of how durable the export-control wall really is. At the same time, credit markets started asking hard questions about how the leading edge — the GPUs, the HBM, the AI factories — actually gets financed. Both threads point the same direction: the easy assumptions on both sides of this industry are getting tested at once.
