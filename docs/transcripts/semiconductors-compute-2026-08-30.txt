Good morning. It's Sunday, August 30th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

This week the industry stopped arguing about whether AI demand is real and started arguing about dates. Across custom silicon, foundry, and Intel's own roadmap, commitment after commitment slid to the right. The one thing that did not slide is the memory bill. That's the spine of today's briefing.

Start with Marvell. On Thursday the company disclosed a custom-silicon agreement with Google that could generate up to 120 billion dollars in cumulative sales through fiscal 2033. The work sits adjacent to Google's TPUs, spanning model execution, storage management, and moving data across the network. Google also received a warrant for nearly 59 million Marvell shares, worth roughly 12 billion dollars if fully exercised. Now here is the mechanism that matters. That warrant vests in blocks, one block for every 500 million dollars of custom-chip revenue Marvell actually books from Google. The schedule runs from the third quarter of this fiscal year through fiscal 2033. Read that structure carefully and the headline number stops being a contract. A hundred and twenty billion is the ceiling you reach if Google buys at maximum volume for seven years. It is arithmetic derived from an incentive schedule, not an order backlog, and nothing in it obliges Google to buy anything.

The market worked that out fast, because the seller put a date on it. On the earnings call, CEO Matt Murphy said the programs now in execution "will contribute much more significantly in fiscal 2029." He also reiterated what he called an old target, "a ten billion dollar-plus number for custom in fiscal 2029." Marvell's shares fell about eight percent in premarket trading on Friday. Reporting is explicit that the decline was about the timing of Google revenue rather than the quarter itself, which beat expectations, with fiscal 2027 revenue now guided to grow roughly forty-five percent to about twelve billion dollars. So the market had a strong number to reward and a distant date to punish, and it chose the date. There is a less flattering reading available too: investors may be discounting not just the lag but the possibility that Google's own TPU roadmap absorbs this work in-house or hands part of it to a second vendor, in which case the later vesting blocks never unlock at all. Either way, the takeaway is the same. Design wins are now being priced by their shipment schedule, not by their headline addressable market. Watch whether the next custom-silicon disclosure from Broadcom gets read the same way.

If a design win is only as good as its date, so is a foundry relationship, and Samsung just supplied the next data point. Qualcomm's next-generation flagship Snapdragon processor is reportedly moving production entirely to TSMC, because Samsung's two-nanometer yield, though much improved, still isn't there. Be careful with the number. This show cited roughly sixty percent on Thursday; the reporting behind today's Qualcomm story puts it closer to fifty-five. Neither figure comes from Samsung. Both are trade-press estimates of the same undisclosed quantity, so the honest read is a band somewhere in the mid-to-high fifties, against the roughly sixty percent generally needed for stable mass production and the roughly seventy percent Qualcomm is said to require. That seventy is a rule of thumb, not a published specification.

Samsung isn't walking away empty-handed. It has landed a Tesla order for AI5 and AI6 autonomous-driving chips worth roughly twenty trillion won, about 14.5 billion dollars, targeting mass production at the Taylor, Texas fab starting in 2027. But don't let that offset flatter the picture, because these are separate competitive dimensions and they don't net against each other. Samsung gained revenue and it gained utilization at a fab that needed loading. What it did not gain is leading-edge yield credibility. An automotive part entering production in 2027 is a materially lower bar than winning a flagship mobile system-on-chip that competes on today's leading-edge yield. The dimension Qualcomm's departure damaged is precisely the one Tesla cannot repair: whether a hyperscaler or a fabless designer can treat Samsung as a qualified second source when negotiating against TSMC. So the practical effect runs the wrong way. TSMC's leverage as the industry's sole trusted leading-edge source just got extended rather than narrowed.

Intel has the same problem, and its most important customer is Intel itself. The company detailed its Xeon 7 "Diamond Rapids" server platform this week. As reported, it runs up to 256 cores spread across 22 chiplets, including sixteen compute dies built on Intel's 18A-P process. The launch, originally slated for 2026, has slipped to 2027. No cause has been disclosed, and it would be a mistake to assume a yield problem. This is also a large architectural re-scope that drops hyperthreading entirely to chase high-performance computing workloads. Validation and platform readiness are just as plausible culprits as the node. Note also that these specifications reach us secondhand, through news aggregation rather than an Intel primary disclosure. But what matters is simpler than the cause. Intel Foundry's case for being a credible second source to TSMC rests on proving that 18A-P can carry volume silicon on schedule, and its own flagship server part just missed that schedule by a year. That pushes a genuine second leading-edge source out toward 2028 rather than 2026.

Now the turn. Everything so far moved right in time. Memory did not. HBM4, the high-bandwidth memory that feeds AI accelerators, is running toward thirty-one to thirty-two dollars per gigabyte, nearly double HBM3E's seventeen to eighteen. Those are trade estimates from research aggregators, not disclosed contract pricing from the memory makers. But even taken loosely, that cost lands on GPU and ASIC bills of materials regardless of when any given design win actually ships.

And there is now a legal test of why it lands. A proposed class action filed in June in the Northern District of California accuses Samsung, SK hynix, and Micron of shifting capacity toward HBM in a way that curtailed consumer DRAM supply. The complaint alleges the three firms, which together control nearly the entire global DRAM market, cut DDR3 and DDR4 output while ramping high-bandwidth memory. That is an allegation, not a finding. No company has been found liable, and an innocent explanation sits right next to the accusation: moving capacity toward the higher-margin product is exactly what a rational, non-colluding firm does. But the case matters less for its merits than for discovery. Capacity-allocation decisions between consumer memory and HBM are the one undisclosed variable that determines whether today's pricing power is genuine scarcity or simply allocation, and litigation is currently the only mechanism pointed at that question. So while every revenue schedule in this briefing slid into 2027, 2028, and 2029, the input cost is arriving on time, compressing everyone sitting between an announced design win and a contracted memory bill.

One more cost channel to flag, briefly, because it's preliminary. The Trump administration is reportedly weighing tariffs that would extend beyond bare chips to finished products like laptops, servers, and game consoles, potentially reversing data-center exemptions granted earlier this year. Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick is said to favor tying duty-free import allowances to a company's commitment to build fabrication capacity in the United States. Nothing here is enacted. No rates, timing, or country treatment have been set, and this should be read as a live policy option rather than a decision. But note what that mechanism would do if it happened. It would make customs treatment a function of where you site capex, which is industrial policy delivered through the tariff schedule instead of through grants, and far more discretionary than anything CHIPS-style. It is also a second cost channel hyperscalers cannot design around, layered directly on top of the memory bill.

So here's what today's read rests on: one chief executive's own dating of a multibillion-dollar deal, two sets of trade-press yield estimates that don't agree with each other, a chip specification reported secondhand, and an unproven legal complaint. Treat all of it accordingly. And watch whether the next round of custom-silicon and foundry announcements gets priced to its calendar rather than to its headline.

That's the briefing for August 30th. Thanks for listening, and I'll see you next time.
