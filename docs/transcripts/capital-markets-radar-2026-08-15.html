Good morning, it's Saturday, August 15th, and this is Capital Markets Radar. All week the trade has been simple: cool inflation data comes in, the market cheers, yields fall, stocks make new highs. Friday broke that pattern for the first time, and that crack is where we start.

Here's what happened. July's CPI, out Wednesday, rose just 0.1% on the month, with core CPI at 0.2% and the core annual rate down to 2.5% from 2.6% in June. Thursday's producer prices came in flat, missing the 0.2% consensus, with core PPI up 0.2% against a 0.3% forecast. That one-two of soft prints pulled the market-implied odds of a September rate hike down to roughly 35%, from about 55% a week earlier. Yes, hike — this cycle the debate has been about the Fed raising, not cutting, with three FOMC members dissenting in July in favor of tightening. That's a regime most listeners aren't calibrated for, and it matters for how you read everything that follows. For two years the reflex has been to price duration and long-multiple equities off a cut. Flip the sign, and the same rich valuations that a cut would justify start to look exposed. The ten-year yield tracked the relief lower, dropping from a nineteen-month high of 4.75% on Tuesday to around 4.65% by Thursday.

Then came Friday. Retail sales fell 0.6% in July, the largest monthly drop since May of last year, with the ex-auto, ex-gas measure missing badly too — down 0.2% against expectations for 0.3% growth. On its own, that's dovish — a growth wobble that should pull yields down further. But the same morning, the University of Michigan's preliminary August sentiment reading collapsed to 51 from 55.2, and buried in that survey, one-year inflation expectations rose to 4.3% from 4.2%. Bonds sold off on that combination. The ten-year backed up five basis points to 4.67%, and the thirty-year jumped more, to 5.27%. So the market was handed a growth miss and chose to sell duration, not buy it, because the inflation-expectations signal read as hawkish. For three straight days this week, cool data meant risk-on. Friday, it didn't. That's a stagflation-adjacent tension the market has not resolved. And notice which end of the curve moved most: rising long-run inflation expectations keep term premium elevated and can re-steepen the long end even if the Fed never touches the policy rate. That's the mechanism to watch — the curve can tighten financial conditions on its own, without a single hike, if the inflation-expectations story hardens. Worth carrying into next week rather than dismissing as noise.

Now, that unresolved tension is sitting on top of a market priced for near-perfection, and that's where the real risk lives. High-yield credit spreads are running around 271 to 281 basis points over Treasuries, in the richest decile of history against a long-run median near 450. Investment-grade sits near 81 basis points, and the ratio between high-yield and investment-grade is close to its normal average, which tells you the tightness is broad, not a quirk of one credit tier. It's worth noting where the strain is more visible. In private credit, business-development-company portfolios are clustered around 400 to 600 basis points over the reference rate, and non-traded BDCs logged their first-ever quarterly outflow earlier this year. So the private side is already showing redemptions and leverage limits while public high-yield still prices perfection — an early tell that liquid credit may be under-compensating for risk that's surfacing first in the less-liquid tier. Headline volatility looks equally calm — the VIX has been trading near its lows for the year. But look underneath the index, and the picture changes. Global semiconductor sales just posted their fifteenth straight monthly record, yet the SOXX and SMH chip ETFs have pulled back roughly 20% from their June highs, and options positioning in the space has turned bearish, with investors bidding up downside protection specifically in the AI-chip complex. So you have a calm aggregate sitting on top of a nervous component. Spreads this tight and equities this rich mean the market is offering almost no compensation for exactly the growth-plus-inflation surprise Friday just delivered. When cushion is this thin, repricing tends to happen fast, not gradually, and the fault line investors are actually hedging isn't the broad market — it's the AI-capex trade specifically.

That policy-regime uncertainty is exactly what's showing up in gold. Gold traded at $4,375.50 an ounce on Friday, up 7.76% over the past month and up more than 31% over the past year, and China added roughly 20 tonnes to its reserves in July — its twenty-first consecutive month of accumulation. Under normal real-rate logic, gold should be struggling here: yields are elevated, the Fed isn't cutting and might even hike, and the dollar, while it slipped 0.36% Friday to around 99.6, is still historically firm. Gold rising anyway tells you the bid isn't about real rates. It's central banks and reserve managers hedging policy-regime uncertainty itself, a slow structural story distinct from the day's rate trade, and one that's been building for nearly two years now. Twenty-one straight months of official buying is not a trade you put on for a rate cut; it's a reserve manager quietly diversifying away from the dollar. Oil added a small reflationary twist to the same picture, firming to around $81 to $82 a barrel on Friday, up over 1% on the day — a reversal of the demand-weakness narrative that had been dragging crude lower earlier in the week. Put a firmer oil price next to a softer dollar and you get mild upward pressure on import prices heading into the next CPI print, which reinforces the rising-inflation-expectations story rather than easing it. And the same electrification demand keeping uranium near $86 a pound and bidding up copper is the physical layer under the AI-datacenter power buildout — real-asset strength and the capex trade are increasingly the same story.

Let me close with a quick read on crypto, because it echoes the same theme in miniature. Bitcoin traded near $63,120 on Friday, and the Crypto Fear and Greed Index sat at 29 — squarely in fear territory — even as the S&P 500 hovered just below Wednesday's record close. That's a real correlation break: crypto is pricing fear while equities price complacency, which suggests selling pressure specific to crypto positioning rather than a broad risk-off signal. I'll flag rather than resolve one data conflict here: one dataset shows roughly $850 million of net inflows into spot Bitcoin ETFs over the past week, led by BlackRock's IBIT, while another shows a same-day net outflow of $56.2 million, also led by IBIT redemptions. Those numbers don't reconcile cleanly, likely because they're measuring different windows, so treat the trend — a market that's nervous even at good headline prices — as the signal, not the single-day figure.

Pull it together and the spine of the week is this. The disinflation-relief rally was real, but it's priced very unevenly across assets: credit and volatility assume the good news is durable, gold and now Friday's bond reaction suggest the market isn't actually sure, and crypto is already showing fear underneath a near-record equity tape. Friday was the first session where the marginal price got set by inflation expectations rather than disinflation, and that's the thread to watch into next week.
