Good morning, it's Wednesday, July 29th. This is your private credit briefing.

The smart money that built this asset class is now signaling it wants out of its core trade, even as fundraising hits a two-year high and deployment collapses. And today, the first hard Q2 asset-quality catalyst the sector has been waiting on started to land. Let's start with the buy side's own verdict.

Rede Partners published its 2026 Private Credit Market Intelligence Report on July 23, surveying the institutional limited partners who actually fund this industry. Seventy percent of them expect diversification away from direct lending to be the dominant trend over the next twelve months. Only six percent plan to add to mid- and upper-mid-market direct lending, the strategy that built the industry in the first place. Ask them why, and seventy percent point to competition-driven compression of spreads and terms, and fifty-eight percent point to portfolio problems or fraud tied to poor underwriting standards. This matters because these are the same allocators who took private credit from under ten percent of their private-markets book in 2022 to eighteen to twenty percent today. They're not macro tourists reacting to rate moves. They're naming spread compression and underwriting quality as the reasons to rotate, which is the skepticism this show has been building toward, now coming from inside the tent rather than from outside critics. It's worth being precise here too: this is a reallocation, not an exodus. Rede's own data shows those same LPs rotating into asset-based finance, special situations, and evergreen semi-liquid structures. So what it means is that core direct lending, the plain-vanilla unitranche business, is losing its most sophisticated backers even as private credit broadly keeps growing.

That LP verdict lines up almost mechanically with a second data point. PitchBook LCD figures show US direct-lending deal volume fell fifty-five percent quarter on quarter in Q2, to thirty-three point six billion dollars, the lowest level since the second quarter of 2023, with deal count down to one hundred fifty-four from two hundred seventeen. At the same time, Preqin data show North America-focused closed-end direct-lending funds raised sixteen point two five billion dollars in the same quarter, up from just one point three billion in Q1, the fastest fundraising pace in two years. Put those together and you get the dry-powder overhang made explicit. Capital committed to the strategy is growing at the fastest clip in two years at the exact moment lenders are pulling back on putting it to work. The fee-earning assets under management that managers report to their LPs are increasingly decoupled from the deployed, performing capital that actually produces a credit return. And there's a nasty second-order effect hiding in that gap. A widening pile of unspent capital chasing a shrinking pool of financeable deals pressures managers to compete harder on price and on covenant looseness, which is the opposite of the underwriting discipline a deployment slowdown might otherwise suggest, and it's exactly the spread compression the Rede survey's LPs are already fleeing.

Now to the catalyst the sector has been waiting on. Ares Capital, the largest business development company, reported second-quarter results before the market open this morning. This was set up to be the first hard test of whether Q1's asset-quality deterioration kept running. Here's the honest limit: the specific non-accrual rate, PIK income share, and NAV from that release couldn't be independently confirmed in time for this briefing, so I won't hand you a number I can't stand behind. Treat Ares's print as a catalyst that has now landed, not a result you can act on yet. The figures should firm up over the next day as the filing works through the market, and the number that matters is whether non-accruals pushed above the two point one percent of cost Ares reported in the first quarter.

That uncertainty is a fitting backdrop for the third leg of this story, retail liquidity. Blue Owl's two flagship non-traded BDCs took a combined four point seven billion dollars in Q2 redemption requests. Blue Owl Credit Income Corp saw three point six billion dollars of requests, equal to eighteen point eight percent of its shares outstanding. Blue Owl Technology Income Corp saw one point one billion dollars, equal to thirty-eight point one percent of its shares. Both funds capped redemptions at the standard five percent quarterly limit, meaning most of those exiting shareholders will only get out gradually over several quarters. There's a tell buried in Blue Owl's own reassurance. Management said its Credit Income fund does not need to sell a single private loan to meet the tender. Take that at face value and it cuts the other way. If the gate comes down while liquidity is ample, the cap isn't protecting against a cash shortfall, it's protecting the net asset value, holding the marks steady rather than letting forced sales price the book. That's the mark-to-model versus mark-to-market question at the center of this whole asset class, and gating is one way to keep it unanswered.

And this isn't a Blue Owl problem. Apollo capped its Debt Solutions fund after sixteen point eight percent of shares sought redemption. Cliffwater capped its Corporate Lending Fund after roughly seventeen percent sought to withdraw. Partners Group restricted withdrawals from its eight point six billion dollar Global Value fund on the same pattern. When nearly every major sponsor hits the same wall in the same quarter, that's not idiosyncratic stress at one manager. That's the liquidity mismatch built into the evergreen retail structure itself. So what it tells you is that gating is becoming a permanent feature of this product, not an emergency valve, right as the industry is trying to push further into wealth-channel and 401(k) distribution, and right as regulators eyeing illiquid alternatives in defined-contribution plans get a live demonstration of exactly what that liquidity promise is worth.

One more entrant is piling into this same crowded trade. Bloomberg reported on July 28 that BlackRock's combined GIP and HPS private-debt platform now manages roughly two hundred twenty billion dollars, which still only makes it the fourth-largest player, behind Blackstone Credit and Insurance at four hundred sixty-five billion, Apollo at four hundred five billion, and Ares at three hundred thirty-five billion. A fourth mega-scaled competitor chasing direct-lending assets deepens exactly the spread and term compression the Rede survey's LPs just named as their biggest worry. More capital hunting a deal pool that just shrank fifty-five percent doesn't end well for pricing discipline.

Step back and look at who is actually funding this asset class, because the three marginal sources are now pointing in different directions. Three days ago this briefing flagged the insurers, the marginal buyer that has kept the growth story alive, still leaning in hard, with a majority of them planning to add exposure even as they named that same shrinking premium out loud. Today the institutional LPs are leaning out of the core trade. And the retail base is being gated on the way out. Insurers buying, LPs rotating, retail trapped. That divergence across the funding base is the real story underneath the headline redemption caps, and it says the easy, unanimous capital that built direct lending is gone.

So where does the cycle actually stand? The binding risk right now isn't the headline spread level. It's the decoupling between capital raised and capital deployed, and the fact that the sophisticated LPs who built this asset class are now naming spread compression and underwriting quality as their reasons to rotate out of the core trade. That's a harder signal to dismiss as bearish noise than anything coming from outside critics. The near-term catalysts to watch are concrete. Blue Owl's formal Q2 results land tomorrow, July 30th, which should confirm or update today's preliminary redemption figures. The rest of Q2 BDC earnings season, including a cleaner read on Ares, follows close behind. Watch whether non-accrual rates and PIK reliance keep climbing once those prints are in hand.
