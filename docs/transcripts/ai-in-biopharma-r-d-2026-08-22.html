Cautious Optimism Briefings

AI in Biopharma R&D

Transcript · August 22, 2026

Good morning. It's Saturday, August 22nd, and this is your briefing on artificial intelligence in biopharmaceutical R&D.

All week this beat has made the same argument without a number behind it: that AI is improving drug developability, not drug efficacy. Today the argument finally gets a denominator.

An analysis synthesized across trade press this month tracks 117 AI-enabled therapeutic assets, from 63 companies, that have entered human trials. Sixty of those have completed Phase I. Eight have completed Phase II. Start with Phase I, because it's the flattering half. AI-designed candidates are clearing Phase I at 80 to 90 percent, against a historical industry baseline of 40 to 65 percent. Call it roughly double. That gap tracks with what generative design is actually good at. More selective, more soluble molecules, with cleaner pharmacokinetic and toxicology profiles, walking into first-in-human studies with fewer surprises.

Now the harder half. On Phase II, Clinical Trial Vanguard's own words are blunt. Quote: "the AI era has produced no new answer. The success rate sits at roughly 40 percent, the same figure the industry has lived with for decades." That is not a close call dressed up as a coincidence. The reporting frames it as flat equivalence. A second and independent source, an aggregator citing a 2025 Nature Medicine paper, reaches the same read: AI-discovered drugs have, in the paper's words, "experienced similar levels of phase 2 trial failure as non-AI-discovered drugs." Two separate trails, one conclusion.

So here is the reframe. AI has solved a chemistry problem, not a biology problem. It has improved the properties a molecule carries into a human being — solubility, selectivity, tox behavior. It has not moved whether the target was the right one to begin with. A Bristol Myers Squibb executive quoted in that reporting put it plainly: "AI is moving rather than removing bottlenecks in drug discovery... plausibility was never the hard part." A Gilead executive framed the asymmetry more sharply still: "Speed is bought. Trust is earned in Phase III."

Discipline that finding before you extrapolate from it. Eight Phase II completions is a thin sample. It is too thin to declare the rate permanently unchanged. It is only thick enough to say the rate has not moved yet, and that read is explicitly reversible as the rest of the cohort reports.

The sourcing needs the same discipline. Every account of this comparison collapses it into a single editorial judgment of equivalence. None of them sets a separately sourced number for the AI cohort next to a separately sourced number for the historical baseline. That is a real evidentiary gap, even though three independent outlets arrive at the same place. The census underneath it is a moving target too. Different trackers put the AI-enabled pipeline anywhere from 117 to roughly 175 assets, depending on whether the bar is any AI touch anywhere in the process, or AI actually generating the molecule or the target hypothesis. And the primary conference data behind all of this — an ASCO 2026 presentation — remains unreached. Everything here is trade-press synthesis. Well-corroborated synthesis, but secondary.

One of the eight completed Phase II programs can plausibly be tied to a name. Recursion's REC-4881, a MEK inhibitor for familial adenomatous polyposis, posted Phase 1b/2 data showing a median 43 percent reduction in polyp burden at week thirteen, across six patients. Hold that loosely in both directions. It is a six-patient combined Phase 1b/2 readout, and the link between it and the tracked cohort is inferential, not confirmed. Treat it as an illustration of what these programs look like, not as evidence about the eight.

The honest read of that 80-to-90 percent figure is that it's exactly the number you'd expect from a young, self-selecting cohort. An industry only recently putting AI-designed molecules into humans is naturally advancing its most developable assets first. Phase I clearance is precisely the metric survivorship would flatter. Phase II sits further downstream and is much harder to game. That is where a genuine uplift in probability of success would have to show up, and on the evidence in hand it hasn't.

That has a direct valuation consequence. AI-native platform companies — the Recursions, the Insilicos, the Isomorphics of the world — have in places been priced on an assumed uplift in clinical success probability, not merely on speed and cost compression. Speed and cost compression are real and economically meaningful. They are also a much smaller prize than a genuinely higher hit rate, and right now there is no cohort-level evidence for the bigger prize. If the remaining assets in that 117 keep landing near the historical base rate, the market has to reprice these platforms as faster, cheaper option factories rather than higher-hit-rate ones. That is a different multiple, and a weaker negotiating hand against big pharma partners.

There is a second consequence, and it lands on somebody else's income statement. If the rational use of these platforms is faster and cheaper go/no-go decisions — killing bad molecules earlier and for less money — then the fee pool that compresses is discovery-stage screening and chemistry services. The trials that actually decide outcomes do not get any cheaper. The work that gets squeezed is the work done before the expensive part starts.

If biology is the risk AI hasn't touched, watch where the frontier is placing its next bet, because it just moved. On August 18th, GenBio AI, founded by 2024 chemistry Nobel laureate David Baker, previewed AIDO Cell, pitched as the first general-purpose world model of the human cell. The company describes it as modeling, quote, "entire cellular systems — proteins, RNA, and DNA — allowing users to predict how molecular machinery behaves when genes are knocked out or drugs are introduced." Baker's own framing of the ambition, set against AlphaFold: "With AlphaFold, you're predicting the structure of one house or learning about one person in that city, whereas in AIDO Cell, you're sort of predicting how that whole city works."

That is ambition, not evidence, and it should be read that way. This is a preview release. No benchmark data is public. No funding figure is public. No head-to-head against rival virtual-cell efforts, like Xaira's X-Cell, has been published. The one demo disclosed is a retrospective reproduction of imatinib's known mechanism of action in a well-characterized leukemia cell line. That is validating biology we already understood, not predicting biology we didn't. A Nobel laureate's involvement buys attention that the demonstrated capability, on its own, would not yet earn.

But the direction is the interesting part. This is frontier capital and frontier talent moving back up the stack, toward the exact layer today's lead statistic just identified as unimproved: biological and efficacy risk. And that exposes an asymmetry which explains a lot about where money actually flows. Proving a virtual cell reduces biological risk requires years of prospective validation on biology nobody has seen before. Proving an operations-layer or data-layer claim — a faster trial-protocol draft, a cleaner regulatory submission, a better-curated dataset — can settle inside a quarter. That asymmetry is why prestige keeps landing upstream, on cell simulation, while auditable capital keeps landing downstream, on the boring plumbing.

Which is exactly where the missing input lives. If the binding constraint is biological understanding, the scarce and defensible asset is the data that would teach a model that biology, not the model architecture itself. Flatiron Health's Panoramic program, launched back in February, illustrates the shape of that answer. It is a federated, cross-jurisdiction prostate cancer dataset spanning, in the company's words, "the records of nearly 400,000 patients" across the United States, the United Kingdom, and Germany, built without centralizing protected health information in any one place. That federated architecture — harmonizing balkanized post-HITECH data formats in place rather than pooling them centrally — is emerging as the practical workaround to HIPAA's constraints, rather than HIPAA itself blocking progress. This is structural evidence, not a fresh headline, and scale is an input claim rather than an outcome claim. Nobody has shown that a bigger federated cohort changes a go/no-go decision. But it is the concrete instance of the argument: whoever owns or generates that data holds a more durable asset than whoever owns the model.

Pull the threads together. AI has demonstrably compressed the chemistry side of drug discovery. Better molecules are entering trials, and fewer of them fail early. It has not yet demonstrated it can pick better biological bets, and the field's own numbers, thin as they are, currently say it hasn't. The next real signal comes from the rest of that 117-asset cohort completing Phase II. Watch that number, not the next demo.

That's the briefing for today. Have a good weekend.
