Enterprise Technology & Software

Good morning. It's Saturday, August 22nd, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

This week gave us the first genuine outside look at what businesses will actually pay for artificial intelligence. The answer puts a ceiling on something the market has been assuming was uncapped.

Start with OpenAI's side of the ledger. At an investor meeting on August 14th, chief financial officer Sarah Friar told the room that annualized recurring revenue hit forty billion dollars in July. That was up twenty percent in that single month, with the count of business customers up thirty-two percent alongside it. It also arrived two quarters ahead of the company's own schedule. The more interesting number is the mix. OpenAI entered 2026 with sixty percent of revenue coming from consumers and forty percent from the enterprise business, and Friar had guided investors to expect the two sides to reach parity by year-end. Her words at that meeting were that the lines have now crossed. Enterprise is the bigger half, and it got there early. Take that at face value and the dollar-volume story is not just intact, it is accelerating.

Now the check on that story. Ramp publishes a monthly AI Index drawn from the more than seventy thousand US businesses that pay through its corporate card and bill-pay platform, and this week's August edition is the first third-party read on where enterprise AI dollars actually land. On breadth of adoption, Anthropic still leads. Roughly forty-three and a half percent of tracked businesses use it, against just under forty percent for OpenAI. Treat those decimals as approximate, because different reports of the same month differ by a few tenths. The direction is clearer than the level. OpenAI is closing the gap, posting a quarter-over-quarter enterprise growth index of eighty-two against Anthropic's seventy-six.

Then there is the number that actually matters. Anthropic's flagship, Fable 5, runs about ten dollars per million tokens, roughly double what OpenAI charges for GPT-5.6 Sol. Despite being positioned as the superior model, Fable 5 has taken only six percent of the tokens businesses buy from Anthropic. Ramp's own economist drew the conclusion out loud, saying that with Fable 5, they had found a new upper bound for how much businesses are willing to spend on AI. Total spend keeps compounding. Pricing power at the premium tier does not. Those are two different things, and the market has been treating them as one.

That breaks a pattern this show has been running. On Thursday, our frontier-labs coverage had Anthropic's annualized run rate past sixty-five billion dollars, comfortably ahead of OpenAI's roughly forty billion, framed as a straight revenue scoreboard. Ramp's business-level data splits that scoreboard apart. Anthropic still leads on how many businesses use it. But it is losing the growth race, and it cannot move its most expensive product. Revenue leadership and product traction are no longer the same story.

Before anyone banks the obvious conclusion, look at Ramp's dollar figures rather than its token figures, because token share is not revenue share. Fable 5 accounts for eleven point four percent of the dollars businesses spend on Anthropic models, against six percent of the tokens. GPT-5.6 Sol runs the other way, taking twenty-three percent of OpenAI's dollars against twenty-five percent of its tokens. And Ramp reports that in July, Fable 5 generated about three-quarters as much model-attributed spend as GPT-5.6 Sol. That is precisely the signature you would expect if buyers are routing a small, expensive slice of high-value work to the premium model while defaulting to cheaper models for volume. So the honest read is not that Fable 5 is being rejected. It is that rational routing and a hard price ceiling produce the same numbers, and neither lab has a clean case yet. One caution on the arithmetic. Ramp publishes these splits inside each company's own model mix. It does not publish an overall OpenAI-versus-Anthropic dollar share, so nobody should construct one.

Grade both datasets before leaning on either. Ramp only sees businesses that transact through its own card and bill-pay products, which skews the panel toward small and mid-market companies. Large enterprises buying on committed contracts, exactly where premium pricing tends to survive, are largely invisible to it. And it counts businesses transacting, not seats and not contract value. On the other side, OpenAI's figures come from an investor meeting relayed by the press, with no filing, no transcript, and no published definition of what counts as enterprise revenue versus API or prosumer spend.

One more thing worth naming precisely. Since the Ramp data landed, Anthropic has made no dated, on-the-record pricing or packaging move on Fable 5. The most recent changes to that product's pricing all predate the index, so they cannot be read as a response to it. The ceiling question is open. It has been neither conceded nor contested. Watch whether the eventual defence comes on price or on enterprise governance features, because that choice will tell you which theory Anthropic itself believes.

Step back and there is a structural point underneath all of this. Businesses in Ramp's panel are not picking a lab and standing behind it. They run more than one, and they route work by cost. That makes the frontier model layer behave like a substitutable input rather than a platform. Inputs do not hold premium pricing for long. Platforms do.

Which is why the interesting money is moving one layer up. If the model layer cannot hold a premium, the margin accrues to whoever integrates and governs it. That is the logic behind IBM's August 13th announcement that it is joining OpenAI's Elite partner tier. IBM is building a dedicated OpenAI Practice, with thousands of consultants and engineers certified through OpenAI's Partner Network, and folding GPT-5.6 and Codex into IBM Consulting Advantage. IBM's own framing of the thesis is better than any outside analyst's. The challenge, IBM says, is not access to AI technologies. It is integrating AI securely and at scale into complex enterprise environments. That is the third flag planted on the same rail in a month. Cognizant tied up with Anthropic in late July, MongoDB shipped its governed managed MCP server on Monday, and now IBM. The integration-and-governance layer keeps being the ground vendors choose, because that is where the durable margin now sits.

Keep the skepticism here too. No contract value, no revenue target and no timeline came with IBM's announcement, so this is a capability launch, not a bookings number. And IBM still runs its own watsonx platform and its own Anthropic relationship, so this is not a bet on OpenAI. It is multi-sourcing. That is the point rather than a contradiction. The integrator's business model requires neutrality, and neutrality is precisely what caps any single lab's ability to lock in a regulated enterprise customer, Elite tier or not.

One closing data point from the application layer, because the same buyer resistance should show up there. HubSpot's second-quarter results beat headline estimates and the company raised full-year earnings guidance. But it trimmed full-year revenue guidance to between three point six eight and three point six nine billion dollars, below both its own prior range and the Street's three point seven one billion. Margin up, revenue down, is the shape you would expect if a customer base cannot fully absorb an AI-driven repricing. Say the caveat plainly, though. That causal link is reporting's inference, not anything HubSpot has stated on the record, and the mundane alternative is ordinary small-business budget softness. The gap is modest. This is a hint, not a demand break.

Put it together and the volume of enterprise AI spend and the price of it have decoupled. Total dollars keep climbing while the premium tier runs into real resistance, and that resistance is already redirecting where the margin lands. It also reads back onto the metered-billing shift this show has tracked all week. Charging per agent action instead of per seat only uncaps revenue if buyers will pay the meter, and Ramp is the first outside number suggesting there is a ceiling on that willingness. The next test is close. Palo Alto Networks reports on August 24th, CrowdStrike is expected within days after that, and Zscaler follows on September 3rd. Those are vendors selling AI capability into exactly the budgets Ramp says are turning price-sensitive.

That's the briefing for today. Have a great weekend.
