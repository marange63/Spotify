Good morning, and welcome to your Private Credit briefing for Monday, October 26th, 2026.

All week we have tracked redemption gates one fund at a time. Today the aggregate data arrived, and it says the question has moved. It is no longer only about flows and sentiment. It is now about credit.

Start with PitchBook LCD's third-quarter Global Private Credit Survey, published Friday. LCD fielded it between October 7th and 19th. Respondents now name default risk and market stress as the greatest challenge facing the asset class. That is a change in what practitioners worry about. Earlier in the year the worry was perception and outflows. Now it is losses.

The harder fact sits in the flow data. LCD reports that net flows at the non-traded BDCs it tracks turned negative for the first time in the dataset's history, in the second quarter of 2026. That is the aggregate version of what the individual gates have been telling us.

Two cautions. First, this is second-quarter flow data, not third-quarter realized losses. Second, the survey measures sentiment, not audited portfolios. A plurality of respondents call the outflows temporary and tie them to AI and software fears. One negative quarter is a data point, not a trend.

Still, the so-what is plain. The semi-liquid wrapper is now a measured net drain, and the people who run this market are naming defaults as the thing that could come next.

So where do defaults actually stand? That brings us to the first hard primary-source read this week, and it comes from Apollo.

Apollo Debt Solutions filed its monthly 8-K on October 21st. It cites a par-weighted leveraged loan default rate, including distressed exchanges, of 2.08% in September. That is the lowest since January 2023, and it sits below the high-yield default rate of 2.68%. On its face, that is a benign number.

But the same filing carries the number I would hold against the marketing. According to KBRA, as cited in Apollo's 8-K, the direct lending default rate declined to 2.2% in September. It rises to 4.0% when you include loans that have stopped making interest payments. One note on sourcing. The filing names KBRA in the text, but the footnote points to the JPMorgan Default Monitor, so hold the attribution loosely: KBRA, as cited by Apollo.

Notice the gap. The inclusive measure is nearly double the headline. The 8-K calls private credit defaults, quote, "relatively contained." That sentence is doing a lot of work. Loans that have stopped paying interest but have not yet been declared in default are the buffer, and they are where stress hides before it becomes a loss.

This is a theme we have flagged before. Fitch's private credit default rate, which counts deferrals, ran well above the headline numbers earlier in the week. Apollo's own filing now shows the same pattern. The stress shows up in the definition before it shows up in the loss.

One caveat on all of this. These are index and market measures. They are not Apollo Debt Solutions' own book loss rate. They tell you about the neighborhood, not the house.

Now the house itself. Apollo Debt Solutions reports net asset value of $23.78 per share at September 30th. Software represented approximately 10.5% of its investments. Net leverage was 0.85x. None of that looks alarming. Software at roughly a tenth of the book is meaningful given the AI disruption fears, but it is not the concentration we see in the tech-focused funds that have been gated hardest.

Then comes the yield. The fund's Class I shares generated a 9.06% annualized distribution rate in September. That is a fund marketing a yield. It is not a net-of-loss return. A high distribution rate on a model-marked book, held in a vehicle that limits withdrawals, is not risk-adjusted return. It is compensation for illiquidity and for marks nobody has yet tested in a downturn.

So the read on Apollo is this. The headline default number is genuinely low, the book looks conservatively levered, and the one number that reveals stress, the inclusive measure, is double the headline. Both can be true at once.

That gap leads to the next question. If the stress is showing up in definitions, where will it show up in value? The answer is recovery marks, and here the LCD survey gives us a pointed fact. Respondents say roughly 70% of lender takeovers stem from 2021 and 2022 transactions.

Think about what that means. Those vintages were underwritten at peak valuations and tight spreads, often with aggressive add-backs. When lenders take the keys on those loans, the recovery is the real test of the asset class. The current default rate does not measure it. The marks on those 2021 and 2022 loans do. If private valuations are lagging reality, the lag is most likely to be there.

The so-what is that the next test is not payment defaults. It is what lenders actually recover, and whether the marks at the funds were right.

Now to the retail wrapper that sits underneath all of this, because the per-fund texture got more interesting this month.

KKR's K-FITS is a fund with net assets of about $996 million. For the quarter ended September 29th, it received redemption requests of 5.06%, up from 3.43% in the previous quarter. Its cap is 5%. KKR chose to pay every request, in the fund's words a small exception to its own cap.

Contrast that with Blue Owl. Blue Owl limited redemptions from two of its private credit funds at 5% for another quarter. Reporting puts the requests at 39 percent for its Technology Income Corp and 17 percent for its Credit Income Corp. Those two figures come from secondary coverage, so treat them as reported rather than confirmed by the filings.

Two different playbooks. KKR is protecting its franchise by paying out. Blue Owl is prorating and letting the queue build. But be careful about reading KKR as strength. At under a billion dollars, paying an extra few hundredths of a percent is cheap insurance. It does not show the structure can handle a real run, and it does not commit KKR to doing the same next quarter.

The more important point is the Blue Owl tech fund. Requests there are the highest in the group, and the fund is concentrated where AI and software fears bite hardest. Stress is concentrated, not spread evenly.

Put it together and the liquidity mismatch looks structural. Proration is becoming a feature of the semi-liquid BDC wrapper. If that holds, growth will keep shifting toward insurance-funded and asset-based vehicles. Those do not face retail run dynamics. In a downturn, their problems surface later and more quietly. That is not safer. It is just slower to be seen.

Let me pull the threads together.

Where does the cycle actually stand? This is a grind in flows and definitions, not yet a turn in payment defaults. The headline default rates are low. The inclusive measures are roughly double. The aggregate flow data has turned negative. Reported yields are high, but they are gross of anything the unmarked book may still owe.

The biggest unresolved risk is valuation. Specifically, recovery and NAV marks on the 2021 and 2022 vintages, where most lender takeovers originate, held in vehicles that cannot easily be redeemed. That is where mark-to-model and mark-to-reality could diverge.

What would confirm or refute this read? The catalysts are close. Ares Capital reports tomorrow, October 27th. Ares Management follows on the 29th. Apollo's asset-level marks come October 30th, with earnings on November 3rd. Blackstone's BXSL reports November 4th. Watch non-accruals and payment-in-kind share first, because that is where the gap between headline and inclusive defaults would show up in fund-level data. Watch NAV marks on the older vintages second.

The Federal Reserve meets on October 28th. I will not guess at the outcome, but for floating-rate borrowers the path of rates matters directly to coverage. And further out, the Dallas and New York Fed banks plan a pilot survey of private credit, with findings expected in the first quarter of 2027.

If non-accruals stay quiet and marks hold on the old vintages, the benign reading survives. If they do not, the flow data we got today will look like an early warning.
