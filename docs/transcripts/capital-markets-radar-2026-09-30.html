Capital Markets Cross-Asset Radar

Good morning, it's Wednesday, September 30th, and this is Cautious Optimism Briefings.

Last week this desk tracked the ten-year Treasury from about four-point-nine-six percent to a five-point-one-seven close. We called term premium a hypothesis, not a finding. On Tuesday, the thirty-year yield touched above five-point-six percent intraday, per Bloomberg. That's its highest level since June of 2002. Just five days earlier, Bloomberg had the thirty-year at its highest since 2004. That's two multi-decade landmarks in one week. Keep the intraday touch separate from the close. Trading Economics has Tuesday's closes at five-point-two-five percent on the ten-year and five-point-five-seven on the thirty-year. The hypothesis has a new data point, and for the first time, a named cause.

So what's driving it? Bloomberg's attribution has two parts: inflationary angst, and in its words, hefty corporate-debt supply. The inflation leg runs through oil. Bloomberg describes the seven-month war with Iran as still weighing on energy prices, even as the US and Iran hold separate talks with mediators. Brent was at about 105 dollars a barrel on Tuesday, per Trading Economics. Last Thursday this desk had it closing near 106. So oil is an elevated input, not a fresh spike. That matters, because the thirty-year kept climbing without a new oil shock behind it. On Saturday we noted yields twice ignoring a falling oil price. Now they're rising with oil roughly flat. Oil looks like the backdrop, not the marginal driver.

Now the curve check, because the label matters. This is not a clean bear-steepener. The two-year closed Tuesday at four-point-eight-nine percent, against four-point-eight-one on Friday. That's up about eight basis points. The ten-year rose about the same amount, from five-one-seven to five-two-five. So the two-to-ten spread held roughly flat, near thirty-six basis points. That's my own arithmetic from the quoted closes, not a published spread. When the front end moves as much as the ten-year, the whole curve is repricing together. So what: whatever is pushing yields up is not confined to long-dated inflation risk. The policy-sensitive end is moving too.

That raises the obvious question. Is this still just a Fed trade? The anchor is the September sixteenth hike to a range of three-and-three-quarters to four percent. The live question is October. Trading Economics, citing CME FedWatch, reports roughly a forty-five percent chance of another hike at the October meeting. Handle that number with care. The quote is undated. Last Wednesday, CNBC had CME odds at seventy-three percent. And other aggregators, which we could not verify, show readings much closer to that earlier level. So the gauges disagree, and we can't tell you that October odds have fallen. On Sunday this desk warned against building a trend out of two different sources, and that warning still holds.

What we can say is narrower. The two-year prices the whole hiking path into 2027, not one meeting, and it rose eight basis points regardless. The thirty-year's move also has a named driver that has nothing to do with the October date. So the honest read is this: the long end is not waiting on a single FOMC meeting to justify itself. The October decision lands on the twenty-eighth, and nothing about it is settled.

So who is supplying the duration? Part of Bloomberg's answer is corporate America. US investment-grade bond sales set a third straight monthly record in August, per Bloomberg, with the AI buildout fueling the borrowing. Barclays forecasts full-year net issuance up about thirty percent, to 945 billion dollars. That's a forecast, not realized supply. It also flips a point we made on Sunday. We argued the AI buildout's funding cost is mostly the Treasury base rate, so higher yields make each new deal more expensive. Tuesday's attribution suggests the causation runs the other way too. The debt wave is part of the supply pressing on the long end. That's a feedback loop. Issuance lifts yields, and higher yields raise the cost of the next issuance. If issuers keep borrowing anyway because the capex plans are already committed, the pressure compounds.

Now the skeptic's case, and it's a real one. Corporate bonds are not Treasuries. They compete for the same duration buyers, and big deals come with rate hedging around them. But that's an indirect channel, not new Treasury float. We couldn't find a dated figure for September issuance, so we can't size the effect. It may be a small factor next to inflation risk. So call supply an attributed driver, not a proven one. Here's where the market may be under-reading, though. If supply is part of the story, a Hormuz deal or a soft data print won't fix it. So what: the AI buildout may now be partly financing its own higher discount rate.

And that discount rate lands on AI-exposed stocks that already have an earnings problem coming. Here's the earnings side. Goldman Sachs projects that depreciation on the AI buildout will subtract roughly five points from S&P earnings growth in 2027. All that hardware spending eventually runs through income statements as a cost. Now add the rates side. A thirty-year above five-point-six percent pressures the multiple on long-duration cash flows. So those names face a squeeze from both directions: slower earnings growth, and a lower multiple on those earnings. The rates half of that squeeze is arriving now.

Which brings us to the assets that were supposed to hedge this, and aren't. Last Thursday this desk had gold at 4,250 dollars an ounce, and called its failure to rally on a war headline a pattern break. On Monday, gold fell more than three percent, per CNBC. The stated reasons were higher Treasury yields, a firmer dollar, and the outlook for more Fed hikes. The dollar index sat near 101.4 on Tuesday, per Trading Economics. Fortune had gold at 4,158 on Tuesday. So you have a live war, a hiking Fed, and inflation the Fed itself calls elevated. The asset built to price that combination is losing to the discount-rate math instead.

Now pair that with bonds. On Saturday we said long duration had stopped hedging a geopolitical oil shock. This week extends the point. If the long end rises on supply and inflation risk, whatever oil or the Fed does, the bond sleeve of a sixty-forty portfolio isn't protecting the equity sleeve. Duration and gold, the two classic inflation-and-war hedges, are losing money at the same time.

Is gold's move a regime break, or a positioning flush? Crowded macro longs unwinding into rising real yields would look exactly like this. We don't have futures positioning or ETF holdings data to tell the two apart. And as of last Thursday, gold was still up about thirteen percent year over year. So this may be a drawdown inside an uptrend, not a verdict on gold as a hedge.

The contrast is copper. Copper futures traded near 6.70 dollars a pound, per Trading Economics. That's just under the record 6.80 set on September eighth. The stated drivers are supply setbacks and electrification demand, and we can't weight one against the other. But copper is holding its bid in the same tape that's breaking gold. So what: in this market, physical scarcity is getting paid, and rate-sensitive stores of value aren't.

Two quick reads on risk appetite before the calendar. First, equities. The VIX closed Monday at 16.07. That's up only modestly from the 15.67 this desk cited last Thursday, in a week of multi-decade highs on the long bond. Equity investors are hedging, not panicking. Second, bitcoin. It opened Tuesday at 83,488 dollars, per Yahoo Finance, down about one percent from Monday's open. Late last week this desk had it near 85,200. That slide came right after the biggest weekly spot ETF inflow since last October. On Sunday we read that inflow as risk appetite intact. The flow showed up, but it hasn't held the price. One daily open is thin evidence, so treat it as a test, not a verdict.

Now the dates, with no outcomes asserted. August PCE is due today, and we have no print. Payrolls land Friday, October second. CPI follows on the fourteenth, and the Fed decides on the twenty-eighth. Third-quarter earnings season will also show whether companies stretch the useful lives of their servers to soften that 2027 depreciation hit. Here's where the market may be over-reading: pinning the long end on oil, or on the Fed alone. The real test is whether the thirty-year keeps climbing on data that should calm it. If it does, this stops being a Fed story. It becomes a supply-and-duration story that no single print can fix.

That's the radar for September 30th. Trade carefully, and I'll see you next time.
