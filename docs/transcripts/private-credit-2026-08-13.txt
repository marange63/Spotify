Good morning, it's Thursday, August 13th. This is Cautious Optimism Briefings.

The multi-year story in direct lending has been borrower-friendly pricing, funded by an oversupply of dry powder. This quarter that story broke. New data shows origination contracting and spreads widening at the same time, which is not a repricing cycle -- it's the signature of a lending pullback. Let's get into the mechanics.

Start with PitchBook LCD's tracker of the twelve largest publicly traded business development companies, covering the quarter that ended June 30th. Gross fundings across those twelve BDCs totaled five point one billion dollars in the second quarter. That's down from five point seven billion in the first quarter, and down hard from eight billion a year earlier. Meanwhile repayments and sales stayed elevated at seven point five billion. New money coming in, at five point one billion, was outrun by money going out. Every one of the twelve posted a net portfolio outflow for the quarter. That's not one weak lender -- that's the whole cohort.

The pricing data confirms it's not just volume. In the first quarter, fifty-three percent of loans to private-equity-backed borrowers priced tighter than SOFR plus 500 basis points. In the second quarter, that share fell to twenty-seven percent -- roughly half. Bain Capital Specialty Finance told investors its typical first-lien spread climbed to 570 basis points, up from 550 the prior quarter. Contracting volume and widening spreads together mean lenders are pulling back credit, not simply adjusting price for the same risk appetite. That's the trend break: the compression that defined this asset class for years has reversed inside a single quarter.

So why is origination actually collapsing? Because a large share of what BDCs call "origination" each quarter isn't new lending at all -- it's refinancing and extension of existing portfolio companies, typically fifty to seventy-five percent of quarterly activity. And that pipeline is drying up because borrowers are taking it elsewhere. We flagged this bank-refinancing reversal earlier in the week; today's data is its quantitative counterpart.

Bloomberg's Credit Weekly reports that, through mid-July, companies with private debt were refinancing into the syndicated loan market roughly three times more often than the reverse was happening. In dollar terms the flows were closer to balanced -- about nine point two billion of bank-led deals pulling business away from private credit, against roughly nine billion moving from syndicated back to direct lenders. That balance in dollars, alongside a three-to-one skew in frequency, tells you the traffic is now genuinely two-way, which breaks the one-way migration narrative private credit has told about itself for years. Insurance underwriter Fidelis Partnership is refinancing about two billion dollars of direct-lender debt through a Morgan Stanley-led syndicated loan. KKR-backed Accuris was pricing a seven-hundred-million-dollar leveraged loan to take out its direct lenders. Those are illustrations of the mechanism, not new news this week -- but they're exactly the kind of borrower banks are winning back: investment-grade-adjacent, easily syndicated credits.

That has a second-order implication worth sitting with. If the most refinanceable borrowers are the ones leaving for banks first, the book that stays behind in private credit skews toward lower-rated, less liquid names. Headline portfolio metrics can look stable while the average credit quality underneath is quietly deteriorating.

Now the front end of underwriting tells a related but genuinely ambiguous story. Lincoln International's data, cited in the Wall Street Journal, shows only thirteen and a half percent of new private credit loans in the second quarter carried a payment-in-kind provision, down from twenty-five percent at the end of last year. Lincoln's Brian Garfield called it a durable shift, not a one-quarter blip -- "the pendulum is shifting," in his words. Here's the tension: you can read that as underwriting discipline finally returning after years of borrower-friendly terms, which is the bullish case. Or you can read it as lenders retrenching across every dimension of a loan at once -- price, structure, and cash-pay terms together -- which is the bearish case. The data doesn't arbitrate between those two readings, and I won't pretend it does. What it does tell you is that this only touches new originations. The PIK-heavy back book written over the past several years is still there, still a legacy risk, and this pullback does nothing to fix loans already on the books.

On the spread-widening claim specifically, it's worth being precise about how far the evidence travels. Morgan Stanley Direct Lending Fund's own second-quarter earnings call said its weighted-average spread on closed deals was unchanged from the first quarter, with pricing generally stabilizing around SOFR plus 500. The fund did note "modest tightening" toward roughly 475 basis points, but only for a narrow pocket of high-quality, non-software assets facing unusually strong competition. That's not a contradiction of the LCD data -- it's a segmentation effect. One lender's best-quality niche can stay tight on strong demand even as the broader market of PE-backed loans reprices wider around it. But be clear about what hasn't been confirmed: no market-wide dataset beyond this twelve-BDC sample and this one lender's commentary was found to prove the widening holds across the full, roughly one-point-three-trillion-dollar direct-lending market. Treat the spread-widening read as strong within the public-BDC sample and directionally likely beyond it, not as a confirmed universal repricing.

Regulators are starting to notice this market exists as something worth measuring. The New York and Dallas Federal Reserve Banks announced on August 5th the first-ever joint pilot survey of the U.S. direct-lending market, targeting firms with at least fifty million dollars in dedicated private-credit assets under management. The Fed's own framing is telling: direct lending is now estimated at more than one point three trillion dollars, comparable in size to either the high-yield bond market or the broadly syndicated loan market. That's a market that's grown large enough to matter for monetary transmission and financial stability while remaining largely invisible in public data. The survey is voluntary and explicitly non-supervisory for now, with aggregate findings not expected until the first quarter of 2027. Don't overstate the near-term bite -- but the infrastructure being built now previews where a future systemic-risk debate gets its numbers from.

Close it out with the funding side, because that's where the next test of these private marks likely shows up first. Combined CLO issuance -- broadly syndicated and middle-market private-credit CLOs together -- ran near one hundred twenty-six and a half billion dollars in the second quarter alone, putting the first half of 2026 near two hundred thirty billion, on pace for a possible full-year record. And retail is showing up here too: CLO-focused exchange-traded funds have grown from essentially nothing five years ago to over thirty billion dollars in assets, per Dechert's mid-year note. So you've got record structured-credit formation, increasingly retail-accessible, sitting on top of a corporate loan book that just posted its clearest quarter of stress yet. If that tension resolves badly, CLO equity and mezzanine tranches -- and now retail ETF holders sitting on top of them -- are where mark-to-market discipline first tests marks that a real downturn hasn't tested yet.

So where does the cycle actually stand? Origination is contracting, spreads are widening within the sample that's been measured, PIK usage on new loans is falling, and banks are winning back the easiest credits. That's a lending pullback dressed in mixed signals about whether it's healthy discipline or late-cycle retrenchment. Watch three things into the next quarter: whether the refinancing reversal toward banks holds or fades, whether spread widening shows up beyond the twelve largest BDCs, and what the Fed's new survey eventually tells us once its findings land in early 2027. Until then, the honest read is neither crisis nor all-clear -- just a cycle turning at the margin faster than the marketing suggests.
