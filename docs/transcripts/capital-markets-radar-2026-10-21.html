Cautious Optimism Briefings

Capital Markets Cross-Asset Radar

Transcript · October 21, 2026

Good morning, it's Wednesday, October 21st, and this is your Capital Markets Radar.

Here's the frame. Through Tuesday, two markets that had been flashing stress all month went quiet. Equity volatility relaxed, and so did the long end of the Treasury curve. Credit, on the latest data through Monday, did not. That divergence is today's story.

Start with the credit ladder this desk drew last week. CoreWeave sits on the bottom rung, the neocloud whose bonds first traded like distressed debt. Oracle sits higher up. Last Thursday, we reported Oracle's five-year credit default swap spread at a record 288 basis points. One honest note before going further. Some reports since put that CDS spread wider still, but our follow-up research could not verify any newer print. So hold 288 as the last confirmed record, not the current level. What we can confirm is the rung Oracle stands on. S&P Global Ratings cut Oracle's long-term issuer rating to BBB-minus on July 9th. That's one notch above speculative grade, with a stable outlook. It's three months old, not this week's news. But it's the reason Oracle matters more than CoreWeave.

Here's what is genuinely new. The widening has now reached Oracle's own tier of the bond market. On the Fed's FRED data, the ICE BofA BBB spread rose from 1.03 percent to 1.08 percent in the week to Monday the 19th. Set that against what this desk reported on Saturday. Over the previous four weeks, BBB paper had widened only about 10 basis points. It has now added roughly half that again in a single week. The broad investment-grade index moved the same way, up about four basis points.

Keep the scale honest. These are four- and five-basis-point moves in a market that is still historically tight. Call it drift, not a break. But direction matters more than size here, because of fallen-angel mechanics. Oracle is one notch above junk. A further downgrade would push its bonds out of investment-grade mandates. They would land in a high-yield market that, as we'll see in a moment, is itself widening. That's forced selling stacked on top of a repricing. So the so-what: we can't update Oracle's single-name CDS print, but the ladder has climbed a rung on the index Oracle lives in.

There's a fair skeptical read on Oracle's CDS record, and it deserves airtime. A credit default swap spread near 300 on a BBB-minus name is not a default signal. Part of the widening is probably hedging demand. Lenders and counterparties with AI-project exposure buy protection on the most liquid proxy they can find. That's the same label-versus-level caution this desk applied to the hyperscalers last Friday. The competing reads are sharp. One investor quoted by Reuters called this a turning point in the pricing, availability, and structure of credit supporting the AI boom. CoreWeave's chief executive pushed back. He argued no one questions whether demand for its services is real, since it has contracts with all the big hyperscalers. Both can be true, and that's the point. The market isn't doubting demand for compute. It's repricing who finances it, and at what cost.

Is the stress spreading beyond the AI names? On Saturday, this desk said the validating signal would be higher-rated spreads starting to move. We now have a partial yes, and it runs through the junk index too. FRED's broad high-yield spread went from 3.16 percent to 3.29 percent over the same week, about 13 basis points. Monday was the equity rebound day. So broad high yield kept widening while stocks bounced. Yesterday we said credit wasn't confirming the rebound, but that rested on CoreWeave alone. Now it holds for the whole index. That's still a tight level, so again, drift rather than a break. But spreads that tight leave little cushion. A small move from a low base matters more for pricing new issuance than its size suggests.

The transmission into private markets runs through the listed vehicles. Business development companies, or BDCs, are listed funds that hold private loans. Their shares reprice every trading day. The loans inside them are valued by the managers, and those marks move far more slowly. The Boston Fed has found that BDC equities fell roughly 20 percent relative to the broad market from early 2025 through mid-2026. One plain reading of that gap: public investors value those loans below where the books carry them. Now public high yield is drifting wider as well. If that continues, the reported private marks look more exposed, because they haven't absorbed what the listed market is already pricing. That's inference, and I'll label it that way. Fitch puts the trailing private-credit default rate at a record 6 percent through the second quarter. That's background, not this week's news, but it points the same direction. The so-what: the middle of the credit stack has started to move, slowly, and the private book sits downstream of it.

Now the options market, which is telling a much calmer story. The VIX closed at 17.31 on Tuesday, down about 6 percent on the day. On Monday, this desk reported last Friday's roughly 30 percent spike, triggered by an unconfirmed rumor that an AI-focused hedge fund had blown up. We said then that a spike fading without a confirmed event might be over-reading a rumor. It is fading, and nothing has been confirmed either way. So you have a calm index sitting on top of nervous credit. Credit has the better recent record, since it refused to confirm Monday's equity rebound. But neither market has been proven right yet. The so-what: pick your indicator, and you get a very different read on how worried to be.

So why can volatility relax while credit can't? Part of the answer is in rates. The ten-year Treasury yield eased to 5.12 percent on Tuesday, a small step back from Monday. Earlier this month it touched levels last seen in 2002. The more useful detail is what drove that climb. As of early-October analysis, higher expected short-term real rates, not a rising term premium, account for most of the rise. That makes the long end mostly a referendum on the Fed's path, not a fiscal-supply story. And that concentrates the binary risk on October 28th.

Futures price a hold as the clear base case, with about a 15 percent probability on another quarter-point hike. That meeting hasn't happened, and I'm not predicting it. Here's the second-order point the headline misses. A real-rate-driven rise cuts both ways. If the Fed holds, real-rate expectations can unwind faster than a term-premium sell-off would. So duration carries more upside to a dovish surprise than a 2002-high headline implies. December is a different matter. Sources directly contradict each other, with some pricing leaning toward a cut and some leaning hawkish. We've lacked a reliable December probability for days now, and we still do.

Real rates show up across the other assets too, starting with the dollar. The dollar index sat at 101.77 on Tuesday, down slightly on the day. That's below the 101.99 we cited on Saturday, after last week's run of eighteen-month highs. So call it consolidation, not a fresh breakout. The same real-rate channel presses on non-yielding metals. Silver trades near $57.62 an ounce, per Trading Economics. That's more than 50 percent below its January all-time high, which was above $121.

A drawdown that deep invites an industrial-demand story. Copper argues against it. Cash copper settled at a record $14,822 a tonne on October 13th, per Mining.com. That was the same day the AI-credit rout began, though the record is now more than a week old. Part of copper's strength is tariffs and positioning, not just power demand. So treat it as consistent with physical demand holding, not as proof. The more plausible read on silver is a speculative January spike unwinding under real-rate pressure. The so-what ties back to the lead: it's the financing of the buildout that's repricing, not the physical demand inside it.

The speculative wrappers lean the same way as credit. Spot Ether ETFs saw roughly $1.06 billion of outflows in October, including $103 million on the 19th alone. That was part of a four-day outflow streak, and it ran straight through Monday's equity rebound. Four days of outflows is de-risking at the margin, not capitulation. But wrapper holders sold into the bounce rather than buying it. That lines up with credit's read of the week, not the equity tape's.

Pulling it together. Credit is the market refusing the relief that volatility and rates have offered. Two things to watch into the 28th. First, does broad high yield keep drifting wider while the VIX stays low? Second, does the BBB tier keep moving, putting Oracle's fallen-angel risk squarely on the table? The Fed hasn't met yet, so treat all of this as positioning, not a forecast.

That's the radar for today. Stay cautious, stay optimistic, and I'll see you tomorrow.
