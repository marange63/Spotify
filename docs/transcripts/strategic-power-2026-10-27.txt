Good morning. It's Tuesday, October 27th, and here's where power is actually shifting.

A cluster of mid-November deadlines is forming around Shenzhen and Abu Dhabi. But the paper underneath those deadlines is disputed, unconfirmed, or narrower than the headlines suggest. And underneath the diplomacy, the physical layer, electricity, is where announced plans are getting marked down hardest. Let's start with a correction.

On Monday, this desk said the US-China rare-earth truce now runs to January 10th. That was unsupported, and it's worth being precise about why. China's Ministry of Commerce issued two separate suspensions last November, not one. Announcement 70 paused six October 2025 directives. Those include the extraterritorial rule that reaches foreign-made products containing Chinese rare-earth content. A legal newsletter quoting that notice directly dates its suspension "until 10 November 2026."

Announcement 72 is a different instrument. It lifted an extra layer of US-only licensing on four minerals. Law firm Clark Hill, quoting the text, dates that suspension "until Nov. 27, 2026." So that's two instruments, lapsing eleven days apart, not one countdown.

What about January 10th? Beijing has published no numbered announcement naming it as a rare-earth date. The figure traces to a broader US-China trade statement, and multiple secondary accounts say that statement doesn't name rare earths at all. One caveat on our own sourcing: neither November date comes from the Chinese-language primary text. Both rest on legal analysts quoting it. So the honest framing is this. Beijing has not published an extension, full stop.

That ambiguity is itself the leverage. Both dates fall before the APEC summit in Shenzhen next month. Beijing can let one suspension lapse, extend the other, or re-escalate selectively. It can do any of that without technically breaking a published commitment.

Now the scope question, which matters more. A common reading of this truce is that gallium, germanium, antimony, and graphite stayed under Washington-specific licensing throughout. On that reading, even a clean rollover would leave heavy friction in place. Clark Hill's analysis says otherwise. Those are the four minerals the November 27th suspension covers. In Clark Hill's words, it ended up "restoring exports of these materials to China's standard licensing framework." So those four still need ordinary Chinese licenses, but they are not under a Washington-only regime right now.

Two layers, though, have never moved. The first is the outright ban on dual-use exports to the US military. Per Clark Hill, the underlying 2024 rule "still categorically prohibits exports of dual-use items to U.S. military end users." The second is the licensing China added last year on seven medium and heavy rare earths. Clark Hill says that regime "has not been suspended."

So the real map is three layers on three clocks. November 10th covers the extraterritorial rule on foreign-made products with Chinese content. November 27th covers the US-only licensing on gallium, germanium, antimony, and graphite. And the military end-use ban and the heavy rare-earth licensing were never suspended at all.

Here's what that means for positioning. A lapse on November 10th would re-tighten licensing on foreign-made products with Chinese content. That would hit non-Chinese chipmakers, EV and magnet supply chains, and defense primes at once. A lapse on November 27th would put those four minerals back under Washington-specific licensing. Gallium and germanium run straight into chip supply chains, so that lapse would land on semiconductors a second time. A clean rollover at Shenzhen is a relief trade, but only a partial one. Defense-adjacent supply chains stay constrained under any realistic outcome, because the military end-use ban and the heavy rare-earth licensing sit outside both suspensions.

Washington's partial hedge, the Pentagon's stake in MP Materials from July of last year, is background, not new capacity. The upshot is that markets pricing "the rare-earth truce" as a single on-off switch are pricing a deadline that doesn't exist as one thing.

And the venue where Beijing could pull any of these levers is Shenzhen, where Moscow now wants a seat at the table. Kremlin aide Yuri Ushakov said, per the Kyiv Post, that preparations have begun for a Putin-Trump-Xi trilateral on the sidelines of the APEC summit. Trump has said he's open to it, with reporting framing it around denuclearization. That agenda is undefined. Nobody has said whether it means strategic arms control or broader nonproliferation. Beijing's response has been a shrug. Foreign ministry spokeswoman Mao Ning said she had no information about a possible meeting.

The obvious read is a historic three-way format. The sharper read is that Moscow is the only party announcing it. That suits Russia's interest in bundling arms control, trade, and Ukraine into one bargaining table where it has a seat. Beijing has the opposite incentive. It wants to keep the rare-earth and trade negotiation strictly bilateral with Washington, and not let Moscow attach itself to that leverage. So the trilateral is a trial balloon, and Moscow is its clearest beneficiary so far.

The Ukraine track shows what Moscow's version of diplomacy looks like on the ground. This desk has tracked Washington's posture toward Russia loosening in steps. First came a sixty-day US waiver in mid-October that eased Russian energy sanctions. Then came talks over selling the oil company Lukoil's foreign assets, with the Kremlin openly at the table. Now there's a negotiating structure.

Russia and Ukraine agreed this week to a localized humanitarian ceasefire in the occupied town of Oleshky. The White House attributes it to First Lady Melania Trump's direct diplomacy. The important line came from Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov. He said, quote, "the decision to resume trilateral talks does not imply any restrictions on combat operations." That sentence is the whole story. Moscow is explicitly decoupling the negotiating process from the war itself.

The structural news is elsewhere. Ukrainian President Zelensky said negotiators from Ukraine, Russia, and the US agreed to technical-level meetings in the first half of November in Abu Dhabi. That's technical talks, with no leaders' meeting scheduled. It's the lowest rung of the ladder. So this is diplomacy generating process, not de-escalation. There's no basis yet for repricing war-risk premia in European gas or defense names. The trigger to watch is whether Abu Dhabi produces an actual framework, not the one-town truce.

Strip the diplomacy away entirely, and the base layer is telling a more sobering story. In a July report, BloombergNEF put US data-center power capacity at about 194 gigawatts by 2035. That was an 83 percent jump over its December forecast of 106 gigawatts. That's the demand headline, and it's months old.

Here's the fresh data cutting against it. South Korea's government said this week it will trim its own AI data-center power forecast. According to Seoul Economic Daily, only 10 of 522 projects that applied for grid connections have secured power supply so far. That's under 2 percent.

There's a fair counter-reading. Korea's trim may reflect a Korea-specific grid constraint, not a global demand signal, and one country shouldn't be overgeneralized. But it rhymes with what this desk reported last week from PJM, the largest US grid operator. There, the independent market monitor found a large share of forecast data-center load canceled or delayed. That's two grids, in two very different markets, marking down announced load. So a forecast that nearly doubles in seven months says more about how loose project pipelines are than about how much load actually gets built. Interconnection, not appetite, looks like the binding constraint.

Nvidia and German utility PreussenElektra illustrate the gap between announcement and capacity. Their review of former German nuclear sites is, as Tom's Hardware describes it, "a feasibility study of land, grid, and infrastructure, not a reactor restart, and no site has been chosen."

Contrast that with financed megawatts. In September, the Department of Energy closed a $1.9 billion loan to restart Iowa's 615-megawatt Duane Arnold plant, contracted to Google. Even that restart is still years out. So real, financed capacity arrives late this decade, while announced demand updates every few months.

The market implication follows. A widening gap between announced and interconnected load favors incumbents. That means grid-connected firm generation, nuclear operators, and the makers of transformers and turbines. It disfavors greenfield sites with no secured power. Whoever already holds the interconnection holds the scarce asset.

Pulling it together, this is a week where the documents keep failing to match the physical inputs. The rare-earth suspension has two dates, not one, and a different scope than the common reading. The Shenzhen trilateral has one party announcing it. The Oleshky truce comes with Moscow saying, on the record, that it changes nothing on the battlefield. And announced data-center demand is running into grids that can't connect it.

Keep watching four things. The November 10th and November 27th suspension dates. The Abu Dhabi talks in the first half of next month. The Shenzhen APEC summit. And Wednesday's Federal Reserve decision, still pending, as the backdrop for what capital-heavy grid and generation projects cost to finance.

That's the briefing. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
