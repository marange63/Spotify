Cautious Optimism Briefings

Capital Markets Cross-Asset Radar

Transcript · July 31, 2026

Good morning, it's Friday, July 31st, and this week's setup should have produced a textbook outcome. It didn't.

Start with the dollar, because it's the most counterintuitive print of the week. The Fed leaned hawkish on Wednesday. That should support the dollar through rate differentials. Instead, the Dollar Index slid to roughly 99.87 on Thursday, its lowest level since June 17th. Traders are attributing the move to suspected intervention by the Bank of Japan and the Ministry of Finance to prop up the yen. No Japanese authority has confirmed that officially, so treat it as suspected, not established. But the yen move was large and directional enough that the market is pricing it as real. Alongside that dollar slide, gold pushed toward $4,100 an ounce, its second straight session of gains. That's not a record — gold traded higher earlier this year — but it's a meaningful multi-session rally, and it's happening at the same time as long-end yields sit at a 19-year high. For weeks this show has argued that real yields dominate gold's direction. That relationship is now bending. Gold rising while yields rise too tells you investors are hedging something other than the standard rates trade — call it fiscal, currency, or geopolitical tail risk. So what does this mean. When a hawkish central bank fails to buy its currency support, and gold rallies into rising yields, the market is telling you it doesn't fully trust the rate signal. That's a credibility question, not just a positioning one.

There are two cross-asset tails worth naming off that dollar move. First, a weaker dollar eases global dollar-funding conditions at the margin — a modest tailwind for emerging-market assets and for commodities priced in dollars. Second, and more dangerous, a sustained yen rally unwinds the carry trades funded in cheap yen. If that persists, it forces deleveraging in exactly the leveraged, high-beta positions that have run hardest this year. Frame it as conditional — the yen move's durability is unconfirmed — but that's the channel through which a currency print becomes an equity problem.

The strangeness deepens on the rates side. The long end kept selling off. The 30-year Treasury yield hit a 19-year high near 5.21 percent this week, continuing a bear-steepening move that's been building since Monday. The 10-year rose too, to about 4.66 percent, but the long end moved more. That's the tell of a bear-steepening: the market is repricing term premium at the far end, not just the path of policy. Remember, Wednesday's Fed hold came with three hawkish dissents — Hammack, Kashkari, and Logan all wanted a hike instead. That vote reset the market's read on the next move, away from cuts and toward a possible hike. But there was no dot plot attached to that meeting, so the hawkish read rests entirely on dissents and language, not formal guidance. The market may be over-reading three votes into a durable shift in the Fed's reaction function. Either way, the practical effect is a rising discount rate on anything with long duration. And nothing has more duration in this market right now than the AI capex cycle.

That's where the discount-rate story runs straight into this week's earnings verdict. Microsoft and Meta already split the market's judgment on capex quality — Microsoft rewarded for Azure's acceleration and its 84 percent jump in backlog, Meta punished for raising its spending floor without new revenue proof. Thursday completed that split. Amazon reported AWS growth accelerating, and its stock jumped roughly 9 to 10 percent in after-hours trading. The number behind that reward is now quantified: AWS operating income hit $16.6 billion this quarter, up from $10.2 billion a year earlier, at a 39.4 percent operating margin. That's a hard profitability anchor, not just a growth headline, and it's the clearest monetization proof the market has seen yet in this earnings cycle. For allocators, the signal is a rotation inside the AI trade itself — toward the monetized cloud names with revenue proof and away from pure model-and-app-layer spend. Apple went the other way. Shares fell more than 6 percent after hours despite beating on both revenue and earnings per share. The driver wasn't capex discipline — it was guidance. Management projected September-quarter revenue growth of just 9 to 11 percent, missing the roughly 12 percent consensus, with services revenue coming in at $30.74 billion against a $31.22 billion estimate. Executives pointed to supply constraints and rising DRAM and NAND memory costs pressuring Mac and iPad margins. So Apple's selloff sits off the capex axis entirely — it's a supply-chain and memory-cost story, not a verdict on AI spending. And notice the read-through in that memory-cost complaint: the same AI-driven surge in DRAM and NAND demand that lifts the memory suppliers is now showing up as a margin headwind for the hardware makers who buy those chips. The AI build-out is starting to tax its own downstream. The discriminator thesis still holds for the true hyperscalers: monetized infrastructure gets rewarded, unmonetized spending gets punished. But treat the four-for-four pattern as strong, not clean, because Apple isn't playing the same game. The broader point: with the 30-year at a 19-year high, the hurdle rate on the roughly $725 billion in combined 2026 hyperscaler capex — itself up 77 percent year over year — just got more expensive, even as the spending accelerates. The rates story and the earnings story are pulling in opposite directions.

Now the inflation input that's keeping the Fed hawkish in the first place. Oil spiked after the US struck Iran on Wednesday, with Brent jumping to $90.74 a barrel. That move partially retraced Thursday after Saudi Arabia proposed a naval coalition to protect shipping, with Brent easing back to roughly $89. Keep that in perspective — this is a partial unwind of a risk premium, not an all-clear, and Brent near $89 to $90 keeps headline inflation risk alive. You can see the same hedge in the rates market: inflation breakevens firm and demand for inflation-protected Treasuries picks up when energy pushes into the $90s. That's the same input feeding the Fed's "elevated inflation" language and, by extension, the long end's selloff.

Last, the risk sitting underneath all of this that public markets still aren't pricing. High-yield credit spreads remain historically tight, around 269 basis points as of mid-July. Private credit tells a different story. BDC-reported stress keeps building, and software is now the largest single source of it, at 26 percent of stressed fair value, up from 19 percent at the end of last year. Step back to the first-quarter data for scale: the number of BDC-held companies under some credit pressure rose 15 percent to 538, and the first-lien and unitranche exposure under strain jumped 44 percent to $35.4 billion. Treat those as trend context, not a fresh shock — but the direction is one way. That's not a coincidence next to this week's AI-capex verdict — it's the same disruption thesis playing out on the other side of the balance sheet, public AI winners financed on top, private software borrowers under pressure underneath. And with the long end at a 19-year high, refinancing costs across that stressed private-credit book are rising exactly as the underlying stress builds.

So here's the week in one line. Cross-asset markets aren't trading a clean rates cycle right now — they're hedging tail risk on multiple fronts at once, from currency credibility to duration to AI monetization to private credit, and the cushion underneath all of it just got a little thinner.

That's the radar for today. Have a good weekend, and I'll see you at the next briefing.
