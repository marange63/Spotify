Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Equity & the Deal Economy

Transcript · October 26, 2026

Good morning. This is the Cautious Optimism private-equity briefing for Monday, October 26th, 2026.

On Friday we told you one manager's realization line fell twenty percent in a quarter. Today the number belongs to the whole industry, and it is worse than one bad quarter. Moody's, citing PitchBook data reported by CNBC on October 16th, says private equity distributions have fallen from more than thirty percent of starting net asset value in 2022 to about twelve percent today. No source pins down the exact window behind "today," so treat it as a current snapshot, not a precise trailing period. But the direction is not in doubt. The cash-back rate is well under half what it was four years ago.

Behind that thin flow sits a large stock of unsold assets. PitchBook data cited by the accounting firm CBH puts roughly three point eight trillion dollars tied up in about thirty-two thousand unsold companies globally. Remember that is a mark-based value, not cash. That's the inventory problem in one number: a warehouse of holdings with no buyer lined up.

Now, be careful about what the twelve percent actually measures. It is a ratio, and the denominator is net asset value. This show has spent the month tracking marks that keep rising while cash lags. A generous denominator pushes the ratio down. So on harder marks, the distribution rate would look less dire. But that is not good news for limited partners. It would only mean the book is worth less than stated. The numerator is mixed as well. Continuation-vehicle proceeds count as distributions, even though the sponsor never handed the company to a new owner. So twelve percent is not a clean read on arm's-length exits in either direction.

One thing the twelve percent is not, right now, is a recap binge. You might expect managers to borrow their way to distributions. The data reportedly runs the other way. PitchBook-LCD figures show syndicated-loan dividend recaps down about thirty-nine percent this year through early September, compared with the same stretch of 2025. That figure comes via secondary reporting, so hold it loosely. But if it holds, the easiest leverage lever is being pulled less, not more. Either managers are leaning harder on real sales than the headline suggests, or they are running out of cheap ways to manufacture cash. Neither answer is comfortable.

The deal data tells a concentration story that fits. PwC's midyear 2026 outlook shows private equity deal volume down thirty-four percent in the first half from a year earlier. Over the same period, average deal size rose nearly fourfold. Be careful with that average. A handful of megadeals can drag it up without the typical deal getting any bigger. Either way, it is not a market reopening. It is capital piling into fewer, larger checks. Think of the contested Qiagen take-private as the template: the exception that gets financed, not the rule. Fewer deals per dollar deployed means fewer future exits, and it squeezes mid-market managers who cannot compete for that shrinking pool.

PwC's own language on the consequence is blunt. GPs approaching the market without credible DPI, it says, will face an "existential fundraising challenge." PwC adds that the denominator effect has subsided. But years without meaningful distributions have, in its words, "permanently shifted LP expectations." So the binding constraint on this industry has moved from allocation math to cash. That is a harder problem to fix, because no rebalancing solves it.

So where does cash come from first? At the biggest manager, increasingly, it comes from the stock market. On its third-quarter call on October 22nd, Blackstone put its net accrued performance receivable at seven point three billion dollars, up twelve percent from a year ago. That figure is firm-wide. The new detail is inside the corporate private equity piece. Nearly a third of that receivable, management said, is already in public and liquid stock. Management also guided that fourth-quarter realizations are, quote, "expected to surpass the total net realization levels for the firm from the third quarter." That's guidance, not a result.

Hold two readings side by side. The bullish read is optionality. With a third of the corporate private equity receivable already listed, Blackstone can sell blocks into strength whenever it chooses. Peers with all-private books don't have that lever. The bearish read is about where the cash really comes from. Selling a listed stake returns real money, but that asset already exited through an IPO in an earlier period. It is the tail of an old exit, not proof the private sale window is reopening. And turning it into cash still depends on the public tape. That is the same tape that, as our Throughline desk reported on Sunday, saw hedge funds cut tech exposure by the largest proportion in Goldman's prime-brokerage records. If equities wobble, even Blackstone's fastest route to realized carry slows down. That is public-market risk feeding into private returns in close to real time.

Listed stock can clear. The asset class's biggest sector is finding out that it can't, at least not as easily. Evercore's first-half 2026 secondary-market review, published in July, shows software-focused continuation vehicles falling to ten percent of total secondary volume. A year earlier they were eighteen percent. Evercore ties the drop to buyers reassessing how exposed software companies are to AI disruption. Be precise about what moved. That is a falling share of a secondary market that set a record in the half. It is not proof that software dollar volume collapsed outright.

There are two different stories behind that shift. Either buyers are refusing to pay GP asking prices for software assets. Or GPs are pulling those assets off the market rather than crystallize a discount. The second is worse for limited partners. It leaves software companies sitting inside that pile of thirty-two thousand unsold companies, at marks nobody has validated. The pressure behind it showed up across our other desks last week. Enterprise Technology documented incumbents giving AI away free to defend renewals. Frontier Labs covered Moonshot listing Kimi K3.1 Pro at half the top labs' price. Both squeeze the software cash flows that sponsors actually own. So the software-heavy vintages, built on the leveraged software take-privates of recent years, now face the longest distribution drought and the stalest marks in the portfolio.

Not every limited partner is pulling back, though. CalSTRS's semi-annual report, as of June 30th, shows private equity at thirteen point six percent of the portfolio, against a fourteen percent target. That is essentially fully allocated, not retreating. Two cautions apply. A four-tenths-of-a-point gap is small, so don't read it as a buying spree. And being under target can itself be a symptom of slow capital calls and thin distributions. Still, the largest public pensions are not fleeing. Pair that with PwC's DPI warning, and the strain lands on mid-tier and first-time managers, while brand names keep getting re-ups. That bifurcation is what feeds manager consolidation.

So here's where the cycle stands. Deployment is concentrated in fewer, bigger deals. Distributions are thin, and they are now selective by asset type. Public stock and non-software assets can clear. Software increasingly can't. The biggest open risk is that long tail of unsold companies, software above all, held at marks nobody has tested. Three catalysts will confirm or refute that read. First, Ares reports on October 29th. Its book is weighted to credit, so treat it as only a partial read on buyout exits. Second, KKR reports on November 9th, which is the cleaner private equity test. Third, when Blackstone's fourth-quarter realizations land, check whether they come from new private sales or from selling down stock it already owns.

That's the briefing. Have a good Monday.
