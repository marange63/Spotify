Good morning. It's September 16th, 2026, and this is your briefing on AI in biopharma R&D.

This beat has spent the past week arguing that nearly every headline number out of an AI-native drug company measures an input, not an outcome. Today there's a new input worth taking seriously, and one real outcome number that turns out to be smaller than it sounds. Take the input first.

On September 11th, Tempus AI announced a research platform built around whole-genome sequencing linked to longitudinal clinical outcomes. The release commits to a specific number. Tempus says it will assemble a hundred thousand whole genomes linked to longitudinal clinical information over the next several years. Once that dataset is complete, the company says it plans to expand the effort toward a goal of one million genomes.

Now the timeline, because it's the strategic tell. General availability is planned for mid-2027. Nothing outside Tempus trains on this dataset for the better part of a year. Today the only route in is what the company calls its Early Adopter Program, where an initial dataset is already available. Founder and chief executive Eric Lefkofsky framed the point plainly. Tempus, he said, has spent the last decade building the infrastructure to connect diagnostics, multimodal clinical data and AI at scale. Adding whole-genome data linked to longitudinal outcomes, he said, gives researchers a richer foundation on which to build AI models. That is the company's own characterization of its strategy, not an outside assessment of what the data will actually do.

Here's why that framing is the right read on where this field's constraint sits. Three days ago on this beat, the argument was that the durable moat in AI for pharma is multimodal patient data that frontier labs cannot simply crawl off the internet. The company making that claim then was Owkin, the AI-biotech that licenses research agents to large pharma, and its version of the claim was asserted by the vendor and never measured by anyone else. Tempus is the first company to attach a stated scale and a delivery date to the same asset class. Model capability is no longer the scarce input here. Supervision is — labeled, linked, outcome-annotated patient data. That is the entire logic of productizing data as a standalone asset, separate from the diagnostics business it was originally a byproduct of.

Be precise about which technology this actually feeds, because the word AI is doing the work of three different stacks this morning. Genome-plus-outcome linkage is training material for sequence and multi-omics models. Those are specialized architectures, including the open-weight genomic foundation models. It is not training material for a language model. The agent layer that pharma has been licensing all week, for literature synthesis and competitive intelligence, is a separate stack. And the regulated-submission layer, where a model has to survive an FDA reviewer, is a third one again. A dataset that helps the first of those does nothing directly for the other two.

Now hold the skepticism right next to the enthusiasm. A genome count is still an upstream metric. It is the same species of number as Recursion's compound count on Monday, or Xaira's parameter count from earlier in the week. None of them tells you whether a drug works.

And ask whether a hundred thousand genomes is actually large. On its own, it isn't. The Wellcome Sanger Institute, working with deCODE in Iceland, says it has already read and assembled the whole genome sequences of five hundred thousand UK Biobank volunteers. That is five times Tempus's initial target. So the novelty claim here cannot rest on scale. It has to rest on design — disease populations, multimodal linkage, clinical outcomes attached — rather than a general-population biobank. Whether that design trains a model that predicts anything prospectively is untested. The historical track record of large linked cohorts is retrospective association studies, not foundation models.

Two absences in that release matter as much as the numbers in it. It names no Early Adopter Program partners. And it discloses no pricing, no licensing, and no exclusivity terms anywhere in the text. Those missing terms are what decide whether this widens access to AI-driven discovery or simply hands another advantage to whichever pharma or AI lab gets there first, and the primary document leaves it genuinely unresolved. The second absence is legal. Genomic data linked to detailed longitudinal clinical records at this scale strains what HIPAA-style de-identification was designed to cover, because a genome is in principle re-identifiable. That is not a legal prediction — no regulator has said anything here. It is the structural tension this asset class creates.

Follow that where it lands commercially, because it does land on somebody. Suppose linked genomic-and-outcome data becomes the priced input for model training. Then the oncology real-world-data holders — Flatiron Health, Foundation Medicine, and their peers — are being valued as diagnostics and data-services businesses. The scarcer asset they actually hold is training supervision. Expect competitive pressure on them to match multimodal genome-plus-outcome scale. Then push it one step further down the chain. If the binding constraint is labeled patient data rather than assay throughput, value accrues to whoever holds the consent relationships with health systems, not to whoever runs the instruments. That is the same conclusion this beat reached about pharma renting AI components piecemeal, arriving now from the data side rather than the software side.

So if the constraint has moved upstream to data, what does the evidence look like once a company finally gets downstream? That brings us to Recursion Pharmaceuticals, whose balance sheet and compound-count claims this beat covered on Monday. Today, the asset those claims are supposed to produce.

Start with the denominator, because it is the whole story. Six patients. In an open-label Phase 2 study of REC-4881, a MEK1/2 inhibitor in familial adenomatous polyposis, Recursion reports a preliminary median forty-three percent reduction in polyp burden at the week-thirteen assessment. Five of those six patients showed reductions, ranging from thirty-one to eighty-two percent. Recursion's own investor materials say the company has initiated discussions with the FDA on a potential registrational study design, with an update expected in the second half of this year. That is an opened conversation, not an agreed pathway, and that status is the company's characterization of it.

Here is the honest judgment on that number. It is the strongest clinical data an AI-native platform has produced. It is also six patients, single-arm, with no control, on a surrogate endpoint — polyp burden, not a hard clinical outcome. And MEK1/2 inhibition in a genetically defined disease like FAP is well-precedented pharmacology. So it reads more like competent target selection than proof that a phenomics-driven discovery engine found something a human team could not.

The second-order point is about the cohort, not the company. If a registrational path in FAP actually lands, it would be the first genuine derisking event for any AI-native platform. That would matter far more for how the whole group of AI drug-discovery names gets valued than for the size of the FAP market itself. But that is a forward event, not today's.

One more figure, from the regulatory side, and it complicates the picture. In its January 2025 announcement of a credibility framework for AI models, the FDA said it had reviewed more than five hundred drug and biological product submissions with AI components since 2016. That is a cumulative count over nine years, not a current run rate. It also says nothing about whether a model was load-bearing in any given submission or merely touched the process somewhere. Set it next to the survey finding that fewer than fifteen percent of the AI tools sponsors say they use have ever appeared in an actual regulatory submission, and the two are fully compatible. A large cumulative count of AI-touched filings can coexist with very few where AI carries the argument.

Pull the threads together. Capital and effort this week bought a bigger, better-labeled input — a hundred thousand genomes, with a ship date still about ten months out. The evidence that grades whether any of this changes clinical success rates is still six patients wide, on a surrogate endpoint, in well-precedented pharmacology. Nothing that happened this week moves the probability-of-success needle for AI-designed drugs. The two events that actually could — a generally available dataset in mid-2027, and a registrational-path decision in the second half of this year — are both still ahead of us.

That's the briefing for today. Talk to you next time.
