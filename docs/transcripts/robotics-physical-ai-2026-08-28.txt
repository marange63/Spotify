Good morning. It's Friday, August 28th, and this is your briefing on robotics and physical AI.

For days this beat has had one open question. Who checks these companies' numbers? Humanoid makers publish their own package counts, their own uptime claims, their own offering documents. This week an outside check finally arrived. It did not come from a company deciding to open its books. It came from a workers' compensation filing.

TechCrunch reviewed OSHA data and found that Waymo and Zoox test and safety drivers logged more than two dozen injuries in 2024 and 2025 from hard braking or sudden autonomous maneuvers. Transdev, which employs and manages Waymo's test drivers, reported sixteen of those injuries across depots in three cities. Sprains, strains, whiplash. Zoox reported up to eight worker injuries of its own tied to hard braking.

One case makes the pattern concrete. A Phoenix filing from September 2025 describes what it calls an exaggerated braking event with no obstruction. That single incident cost the worker 157 days away from the job. Neither the injury tallies nor that 157-day figure came from a press release. Both came from a mandatory federal filing, and that is the entire reason we can see them at all.

Now discipline that number the way this show disciplines everyone else's. Start with what the report is. It is fresh reporting about 2024 and 2025 operating data. That is a new window onto old miles, not new miles. Next, who got hurt. These are test and safety drivers, employed to sit in the vehicle and take over. They are not paying passengers, and they are not members of the public. A follow-up dig turned up the clearest description of the job, in the drivers' own words. The car enacts what they call a nogo at 45 miles an hour, and you need to be able to take over quickly. That is a human physically present and alert during supervised testing. And there is no denominator anywhere in this reporting. No injuries per mile. No comparison against a human-driven commercial fleet, which would plausibly look worse on the same measure. So treat this as a frequency signal, not as proof the stack is unsafe.

But here is what sharpens the story rather than softening it. Zoox's actual driverless robotaxis, the purpose-built ones carrying real passengers, are structurally excluded from this OSHA dataset. They either run unoccupied or carry passengers who mostly aren't employees. Any injury there simply never gets reported to the agency. That means the count TechCrunch found is a lower bound on hard-braking harm in the commercial system, not a comprehensive one. And it means something uncomfortable about the future of this data. As both companies convert more of their fleets to fully driverless operation, this public reporting channel will show fewer incidents even if real harm to passengers is rising. That is not a safety improvement. It is a transparency cliff, and it is the next thing to watch.

Both companies responded on the record. Waymo said the safety of its riders, road users and team is of paramount importance. It said it has driven tens of millions of miles with human operators behind the wheel as part of its validation process. It also declined to answer direct questions about the specific injuries. Zoox called hard-braking incidents sometimes unavoidable, and said the injuries represent a small percentage of the millions of miles its test fleet has traveled. Neither company attached a number to that percentage.

So what is left is an engineering question the evidence cannot yet settle. Hard braking with no obstruction might be a conservative driving policy working exactly as designed. On that reading the system is trading a jerky ride for public-safety margin, and the cost lands on the person in the seat. Or it might be a perception failure dressed up as caution. Nobody outside these companies has the data to distinguish those two, and that is the point. The only reason we know the injury count at all is that OSHA compels it.

Hold that next to something this show covered on Monday. Nevada's Clark County authorized commercial robotaxi service across Tesla, Uber and Waymo, with permits totaling up to eight thousand vehicles over the next twelve months. Waymo's own slice is capped at a thousand vehicles, and Uber was approved for another thousand through its robotaxi partners. That is regulatory headroom, not a deployment plan. But it is headroom that a human safety monitor still sits inside of today.

Which gives you the second-order effect worth banking. The labor-displacement thesis for autonomy is not arithmetic you can run yet. Staffing has partly moved from driver to monitor, and that monitor now carries a workers-compensation cost line that no cost-per-mile estimate includes. If injuries scale with fleet hours, insurance and OSHA exposure become a real unit-economics input as Clark County's authorized capacity comes online. And here is the useful part for anyone trying to track this sector from the outside. That cost is measurable from public filings even while cost per mile stays undisclosed.

Now pivot to the other side of the evidence question, the side where nobody is required to report anything at all. Figure AI came out of stealth this week with Index. It is a consumer app that pays people to upload video of themselves doing everyday tasks, feeding Figure's Helix vision-language-action model. The numbers are all Figure's own. Two hundred sixty-four thousand downloads. More than sixteen million uploaded videos. That pace, by Figure's own framing, is the equivalent of 4.9 years of human work uploaded every day. Figure has paid contributors fifteen million dollars so far, and has committed more than a billion dollars to data and compute over the next twelve months.

Take the thesis seriously before you discount it. Figure is betting that the binding constraint on a general-purpose robot brain is not compute and not model architecture. It is diverse, real-world manipulation data. And it is trying to buy that data at consumer-app scale rather than through an expensive teleoperation fleet. If that diagnosis is right, it devalues the teleop-fleet moat that Tesla, 1X and Physical Intelligence have been building.

But look at what is actually being measured. Every one of those figures is an input metric. Downloads, uploads, minutes ingested, dollars spent. None of them is an outcome metric. Human first-person video is not the same thing as an on-robot trajectory. Turning one into the other requires cross-embodiment transfer, which Figure asserts and has not shown in any published result comparing an Index-trained Helix checkpoint against the prior one. Raw uploads are also not training data. Figure itself describes a multi-stage filtering and fraud-review pipeline, which is a quiet admission that sixteen million overstates the clean, usable material by an undisclosed margin.

Then sit with Figure's own diversity statistic. Per thousand hours collected, Index contains 373 unique tasks. That is one new task for roughly every three hours of footage, so the marginal hour is mostly repetition of tasks already in the set. That is the signature of a dataset approaching saturation rather than one still compounding. And this is the second large self-counted number Figure has put out in three days, after the package-completion figure we covered yesterday. The pattern is consistent. Figure publishes a procurement metric and calls it a capability metric.

The balance sheet sharpens it further. A billion dollars over twelve months is a pure cash outflow. Figure has stated capacity and a capability claim, but it has not named a paying buyer. Compare that with a hardware maker selling machines against a signed order book, which gets paid on delivery and funds the next unit out of revenue. Figure is paying up front, before anyone has shown the constraint is what they say it is. That is a visible price tag on buying your way past the data problem.

So set the two stories against each other. The autonomous-vehicle side is generating involuntary, externally recorded operating data, because it employs people and runs on public roads that regulators watch. The humanoid side is generating voluntary, self-counted metrics, because nobody is required to check it. That gap in evidence quality tracks regulatory surface area, not engineering maturity. The humanoid makers are not necessarily further from the truth. They are just further from anyone who can force them to disclose it.

Two things would settle each side. For the robotaxi fleets, it is an injury or intervention figure with an actual mileage denominator attached, which neither company has published. For Figure, it is a benchmark showing Helix's measured performance before and after Index training. Until one of those arrives, price both stories as claims, not proof.

That's the briefing. Have a good weekend.
