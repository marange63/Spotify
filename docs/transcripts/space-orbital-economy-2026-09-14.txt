Good morning. It's September 14th, and this is your briefing on the space and orbital economy.

This beat usually asks you to grade a reservation. Most weeks the news is an announced target measured against what has actually flown, and the gap between those two is the story. Today, for the first time in five straight editions of this show, one of those gaps closed completely.

On September 13th, SpaceX flew the 700th mission of the Falcon family. It was a Falcon 9 out of Cape Canaveral, carrying the eleventh, twelfth and thirteenth O3b mPOWER satellites for SES. The reporting around the launch states in running text, not just in a headline, that this completed SES's second-generation medium-Earth-orbit constellation. The fleet now stands at thirteen spacecraft. That is a finished deployment, not a milestone toward one. The first stage came back down on the droneship "A Shortfall of Gravitas" a little under nine minutes after liftoff.

Put the cadence next to it, carefully. This was also SpaceX's 107th orbital flight of 2026 so far. Across all of last year the company flew roughly 165 Falcon missions. Do not turn that into an annualized projection. The 107 is a year-to-date count as of Sunday, the two counts are not strictly like-for-like, and secondary sources do not even agree on last year's total — one says 165, another says 167. The defensible read is that 2026 is running in the same band as last year's record, not visibly above it.

Now line that completed constellation up against the last five days of this show. Amazon's Kuiper has been sitting at 396 satellites against a required 1,618. China's Qianfan constellation is around 238 against a state-approved target of 15,000. AST SpaceMobile, which we graded on Sunday, needs 45 satellites in orbit for continuous coverage and has thirteen launched. Every one of those is a deployment still in progress. O3b mPOWER is the first constellation in that run that actually finished — and notice that the finished one belongs to the customer of the launch provider, not to any of the operators we keep grading.

Don't over-read the number 700, though. A flight count proves that the hardware survives repeated reuse and that a manifest clears on schedule. It says nothing about refurbishment cost or margin per launch. SpaceX has never disclosed either, and that is the same caution this show applied yesterday to a reflown national-security booster. Durability is not a cost disclosure.

There is also a less comfortable read on the customer side. Finishing SES's constellation also ends SES's capital cycle on it. A launch cadence built partly on completing other operators' deployments needs a continuous supply of new constellations starting up behind it. The two largest Western starts left in that pipeline are Kuiper, which is behind its own regulatory clock, and the next generation of Starlink, which depends on a vehicle that has not reached orbit yet. And the revenue on SES's side lags the hardware. Reporting around the mission points to full commercial service across all thirteen satellites only in early 2027 — treat that as a manufacturer's forward statement, since no SES financial disclosure putting revenue, backlog or capacity on the completed constellation could be found. Deployment finishing and monetization starting are months apart.

That 700-flight record is now a number the public market prices every day, because SpaceX is a listed company. It went public on Nasdaq in June at 135 dollars a share and closed its first day at 160.95, a 19 percent pop that valued the company at 2.1 trillion dollars. What is newly useful is the segment split underneath that number, and it inverts the way this beat usually frames the company.

Starlink generated 11.4 billion dollars of 2025 revenue. That is about 61 percent of the whole company, and it carried 4.4 billion dollars of operating income at a 39 percent margin. The consolidated company, meanwhile, posted an estimated 1.9 billion dollar net loss, dragged down by Starship development and xAI infrastructure spending. Read that plainly. The constellation is the profit center. The rockets are the cost center that makes the constellation possible. One provenance note, because this show owes you them: those figures come from an aggregator's summary of SpaceX's IPO filing, and they are full-year 2025 numbers disclosed before the listing. They are not audited post-listing segment reporting.

Now resist the obvious conclusion, which is that a 39 percent margin validates constellations as a business model. It does not. That margin is earned on launch priced internally by the one operator with 700 missions of amortization behind it. No other constellation operator buys launch at anything close to that price. So the read-across is narrower and much less flattering. Vertical integration is what produces the margin, not the constellation business by itself.

That changes the right question to ask about every standalone operator on this beat. For AST SpaceMobile, for Kuiper, for China's megaconstellation programs, the valuation question is not subscriber growth. It is whether any of them can ever buy launch cheaply enough to reach a comparable margin at all. On the evidence in front of us, none of them can, because the cheapest launch input in the sector is not for sale — it is consumed internally by the company that owns it. There is a second-order effect on disclosure, too. As Starship and AI-infrastructure spending scale inside one consolidated listing, the market's visibility into Starlink's standalone economics may get worse after the IPO rather than better. That is the opposite of what a listing is assumed to deliver.

That same vertical integration is exactly what everyone without it is now trying to buy their way around. Europe just tried, at the first International Space Summit, held in Paris on September 9th and 10th. Nearly 120 delegations attended. The summit produced more than fifty deals worth roughly 20 billion euros, about 22 billion dollars, framed explicitly around European space sovereignty and security.

The sharper fact is what happened before it. One outlet reports that the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy called SpaceX, Blue Origin, Stoke Space and Starcloud in late August, and suggested that attending could be read as endorsing European positions at odds with American interests. That account is single-sourced and could not be corroborated against any primary US government release, so hold it loosely. But if it is accurate, it means Washington is now treating an allied sovereignty push as a strategic-alignment question rather than an industrial-policy one.

The channel to watch there is not the deal count. It is export control. A government willing to discourage attendance at an allied summit is a government for which ITAR posture toward European primes becomes a live variable — which would raise the cost of exactly the supply-chain independence the summit was convened to fund.

And do not mistake 20 billion euros of commitments for capacity. These are deals and pledges announced at a summit, not appropriated spend, and not deployed hardware. Europe's binding constraint has never been capital willingness. It has been Ariane 6 launch cadence, and money does not buy cadence on a short clock. None of that summit's commitments has flown a kilogram yet.

One more item remains purely unflown: Starship's Flight 14, which SpaceX has set up as an actual orbital attempt rather than another suborbital test profile. No confirmed date exists. One filing-derived account points to September 15th, other reporting says the 18th or 19th, and this show is not characterizing an outcome that has not happened.

So here is where things actually stand. The parts of this economy that have genuinely inflected are the ones you can count from the ground: a 700th Falcon mission, a finished thirteen-satellite constellation, and a disclosed 39 percent operating margin on Starlink specifically. The parts still sitting as reservations are Europe's 20 billion euros and Starship's next attempt at orbit. Watch three things this week. Whether Starship flies and holds an orbit. Whether SpaceX, as a public filer, ever reports Starlink as its own disclosed segment. And whether Europe's commitments start converting into manifested Ariane 6 launches rather than announced ones.

That's your briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
