Cautious Optimism Briefings

Semiconductors & Compute Infrastructure

Good morning. It's Friday, October 9th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

Today's theme is easy to state and hard to trade around. Scarcity across the chip supply chain is now priced in. The open question is no longer how much gets shipped. It is who sets the price, and for how long.

Start with TSMC. On October 8th the company reported preliminary third-quarter revenue of NT$1.49 trillion, up 51 percent year over year. That beat the analyst consensus of NT$1.46 trillion, a margin of roughly two percent. The headline sounds dramatic. The more telling comparison is against TSMC's own guidance, not the Street's.

Back in July, TSMC guided this quarter to between 44.6 and 45.8 billion US dollars, assuming 32 Taiwan dollars to the dollar. By our own arithmetic, at that same exchange rate, the print converts to roughly 46.6 billion dollars. That is above the top of TSMC's own range, not just above consensus.

The sequential picture tells the same story. Against second-quarter revenue of NT$1.27 trillion, again by our arithmetic, this quarter grew about 17 percent. In the second quarter, sequential growth was 12 percent. So growth accelerated, and it beat a company that already expected a strong quarter.

Here is the catch. Nobody can yet split that overshoot between price and volume. TrendForce, relayed by GuruFocus, reports TSMC is raising 3-nanometer prices by up to 15 percent in the second half of this year. And on the July call, CEO C.C. Wei said packaging capacity is now so tight it is limiting customers' growth. Those are real signals that pricing power is shifting toward TSMC. But no source we have splits the 51 percent into more wafers versus higher prices on the same wafers. That matters. In a capacity-rationed foundry, revenue growth increasingly reflects price and mix, and that is a different kind of growth for margins to absorb.

The real test lands October 15th, when TSMC reports full results. July's guidance put gross margin at 65 to 67 percent. If price is doing the work, margin should land at or above the top of that band. If ramp costs eat the pricing gains, it won't. That is what the call will reveal.

One market detail fits the thesis. The stock hit a record of $487.47 on October 5th, three days before the print, then drifted lower into the release. The market had priced the scarcity before the number arrived. And this is the second supply-constrained Asian chipmaker in two days to show that pattern. Samsung's record profit guidance, which we covered yesterday, landed in line with consensus to a modest beat. So TSMC's leverage right now sits in its price list, not in its revenue beat. The surprise has migrated from volume to pricing terms.

If scarcity is already priced, the next question is how long it lasts. In memory, that is now openly disputed, on the record, by executives with opposing incentives.

On the seller side, Micron CEO Sanjay Mehrotra says memory supply will not balance until 2028. That comes from Startup Fortune's account of his remarks, not a transcript. He sees DRAM and NAND prices climbing into 2027. That fits the contract book we discussed on October 2nd, with roughly three-quarters of Micron's 2027 output already spoken for.

On the buyer side, Acer CEO Jason Chen expects new Chinese DRAM and NAND capacity to start easing prices in late 2027. He called talk of a shortage lasting to 2030 "impossible." So the two forecasts sit about a year apart.

Both men are talking their own book. Micron is a seller renewing long-term contracts, so a long shortage serves it. Acer is a buyer facing rising costs, so a short one serves it. And Chen's argument rests on commodity DDR4 and DDR5 supply, not on the high-bandwidth memory that feeds AI accelerators. He could be right about PC memory and wrong about the AI memory squeeze at the same time.

The duration question also re-scores a trade we described on October 2nd. Micron sold ceiling upside in its banded contracts to buy floors that run out to 2030. SK hynix, per a July TrendForce report, dropped the price cap and kept its upside exposed. If Chen's late-2027 easing proves right, Micron's floors become the cushion, and SK hynix's uncapped exposure becomes the risk. The winner of that contracting trade depends entirely on the end date.

What is genuinely new is the pass-through to buyers. Dell, Lenovo, HP, Asus and Acer have all confirmed price hikes of up to 8 percent on memory costs alone. Separately, Acer's CEO puts fourth-quarter PC price increases at 5 to 20 percent. Treat those as two different claims, not one number.

Here is the second-order effect. AI datacenter demand is constrained by availability. Buyers would take more if they could get it. PC demand is constrained by economics, and price increases on that scale risk unit-demand destruction. So the AI buildout's memory reallocation is now being paid for, in part, by ordinary PC buyers. That is evidence the reallocation is real. It is not yet proof that it is permanent.

SK hynix's report, expected around October 27th or 28th, is the next test of whether sellers still believe the shortage holds past 2027.

Duration bets do not just set prices. They decide who is willing to build capacity before customers sign. That is exactly Intel's position right now.

Intel raised 15 billion dollars in a stock offering in August, diluting existing shareholders by roughly 3 percent. According to Tom's Hardware, the purpose was to fund 14A capacity ahead of customer commitments materializing at scale. That sits in tension with Chief Financial Officer Dave Zinsner's own stated discipline. He has said it does not make sense to build significant 14A capacity until Intel knows it has the customers.

There is a benign reading that reconciles the two. The raise may fund tooling and readiness, not full-scale capacity. That would keep Intel inside Zinsner's rule while shortening the lead time if a customer signs. But the market is still paying for an option before the order exists.

A correction is in order here. On yesterday's briefing, we said Intel's 14A had no named, committed anchor customer. Tom's Hardware reports that, as of Intel's August offering, Tesla was the only publicly confirmed external 14A customer. That ties back to the Terafab project we covered yesterday. So there is a named customer after all, and we are revising our line.

But a publicly confirmed customer is not committed anchor volume. The reporting does not say how much Tesla has agreed to buy. One strategically unusual, single-buyer relationship is not a diversified fabless customer base. A rationed TSMC makes Intel's second-source pitch more attractive, since customers squeezed by TSMC have a reason to look elsewhere. But only signed volume turns that pitch into a business, before equity-funded capacity sits idle.

So here is the scorecard by dimension. On foundry pricing power, TSMC is rising and using it. On memory pricing power, the sellers hold it, but for a duration nobody agrees on. On downstream economics, PC buyers are absorbing the cost today, before any AI company feels it directly. And on foundry second-sourcing, Intel is funded but thinly committed.

The broader pattern this week is capital committed ahead of demand being proven. A foundry is raising billions in equity for capacity it cannot yet fully book. Memory makers are locking long-term contracts before anyone agrees on when the shortage ends. That is a bet that AI infrastructure demand stays structurally tight. It is not a confirmation.

Watch two dates. TSMC's full results on October 15th should show, through gross margin, whether this quarter's growth was price or volume. SK hynix's report around October 27th or 28th will test how long sellers themselves think this shortage runs.

That's the state of the chip supply chain heading into the weekend. Have a good one, and I'll talk to you next week.
