Good morning, it's Monday, August 3rd, and this is your private equity briefing.

We're leading cold on a live one. Right now, in London, a three-month bidding war for easyJet is at its first hard deadline. Castlelake, the Minneapolis alternative manager, has been raising its offer for the airline since late May, and by early July had pushed it five times to £6.90 a share — a bid the easyJet board was initially minded to recommend. Then, in early July, Apollo Global Management came in over the top with a superseding cash proposal of £7.15 a share, valuing the company at about five-point-seven billion pounds. The board flipped its support to Apollo and told Castlelake it was no longer minded to recommend its terms. Under UK Takeover Code rules, that set two separate "put up or shut up" deadlines: Castlelake's falls today, August 3rd, at 5pm London time — either announce a firm intention to bid, or formally withdraw. Apollo's own deadline follows four days later, on August 7th.

Here's what we don't know, and I want to be precise about it: as of this recording, no outcome has been reported. And there's a documented reason to treat "deadline" loosely here. Back in late June, in this same contest, the Takeover Panel already granted Castlelake a nine-day extension — specifically so it could come back with a higher number, after the board had unanimously rejected an earlier £6.50 proposal as substantially undervaluing the company. So this process has a track record of extending rather than snapping shut. Today could bring a firm Castlelake bid, a walk-away, or another extension request. Don't assume resolution just because the calendar says so.

What we can say with confidence is the shape of the contest itself, and that's the more interesting story anyway. Castlelake retains the right to proceed with its own offer if the Apollo deal falls apart, which means even a firm Apollo bid on paper doesn't fully close this out. And on financing, be skeptical of what you're reading elsewhere: reporting describes Apollo's package as arranged through Barclays and expected to combine fund equity with debt facilities, but there is no confirmed, committed financing language on the record yet, and Apollo hasn't crossed into formal "firm offer" status under the Takeover Code. Treat this as a live process, not a done deal.

Step back, and here's the "so what." This is a public-to-private LBO of a listed European airline — cyclical, capital-intensive, exactly the kind of asset that got expensive to finance when rates moved and stayed hard to finance since. That financing backdrop is the point. An airline is precisely the sort of credit lenders priced punitively when rates first moved, so the fact that two sponsors are willing to underwrite a multi-round contest for one now says something about how far leveraged-buyout financing has loosened — at least for scaled buyers who can bring their own fund equity to the table. Europe has lagged the US on public-to-private volume this cycle. A contested cash fight for a London-listed carrier is exactly the kind of marginal signal that the gap may be starting to close. But notice what this deal is not. It is dry powder going out the door, not cash coming back in. The industry's stated problem, the one we've flagged repeatedly this cycle, is the exit and distribution logjam — limited partners waiting on realized cash, not paper marks. A cleared easyJet deal, however it resolves, does nothing to fix that. It's the mirror image of the exit problem: proof the entry side of the machine still works at scale, sitting right next to proof the exit side still doesn't. And if Castlelake ultimately walks rather than matches, it's worth asking whether newer, less-scaled entrants can realistically go the distance against a manager with Apollo's balance sheet once a bidding war runs multiple rounds — a live question for how competitive future European contests actually are.

Now, if the entry side of the market is buyers competing to own assets, there's a second, different bet being placed right now on the manager side itself — and Blue Owl's second-quarter print, out July 30th, is the cleanest read on it. Blue Owl reported assets under management of three hundred nineteen billion dollars, up twelve percent year over year. Fee-related earnings came in at three hundred ninety-two-point-two million dollars, and distributable earnings at three hundred fifty-one-point-two million — both up nine percent from a year earlier. That's the GP-stakes and permanent-capital model doing what it's designed to do: compound fee income in a way that's structurally insulated from the redemption and fundraising-timing pressure that's hitting ordinary drawdown funds right now.

Here's why that matters beyond Blue Owl itself. Sophisticated capital appears to be bifurcating. One kind of investor is betting on fund-level equity — the traditional LP stake, a claim on realized carry once assets sell. That's the trade currently stuck behind the exit logjam. The other kind is betting on manager-level equity — buying a slice of the fee stream itself, largely indifferent to whether any individual fund's carry ever gets realized on a normal timeline. There's a reason that second bet looks attractive right now. Manager-level equity gets paid on committed and fee-paying capital, much of it locked up on multi-year terms, so it keeps accruing whether or not any given portfolio company finds a buyer this year. Fund-level equity only pays when assets actually sell — and that is precisely the gate that has jammed. So an allocator worried about the timing of realizations, not their eventual size, rationally prefers the claim that compounds on the way in. Blue Owl's growth says allocators increasingly like that second bet better right now, because it pays out on AUM and fee accrual rather than on asset sales that keep getting pushed out.

But hold the skepticism here too. Fee-related earnings and distributable earnings are fee-machine optics — they tell you the manager is collecting, not that underlying portfolio companies are exiting at good marks, or exiting at all. And there's a concentration point worth naming: Blue Owl's AUM has grown roughly five-fold since its public listing, largely by buying stakes in other alternative managers' management companies. That means Blue Owl's own fortunes are increasingly a levered bet on the same large managers whose realized carry is the thing not clearing right now. If the industry-wide distribution drought persists long enough to dent those managers' own franchise value — not just this year's fee take, but their fundraising power three or five years out — that eventually shows up in what Blue Owl owns, even if this quarter's FRE print looks clean.

So pull the two threads together. The entry side of the machine is wide open — a contested, multi-round cash bid for a listed airline, and a GP-stakes franchise still compounding fee income at double-digit growth. Neither of those is the same as solving the thing that actually defines where this cycle stands, which is realized cash back to limited partners. That constraint hasn't moved. The near-term test that would actually move it: Apollo and Carlyle both report second-quarter earnings tomorrow, August 4th — the first chance in a while to see whether realized-carry figures, not just fee-related earnings, are showing any real thaw. Watch that, and watch whether Castlelake's deadline today produces a firm bid, a walkaway, or another extension. Either way, that resolves the entry-side question. The exit-side question is still open.

That's the briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
