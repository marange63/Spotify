The story this week isn't a new capability. It's that the price of capability is being rewritten from both ends at once, and pricing power is landing in some unexpected hands.

Start with the inference price war, which just went overt. On July 30, OpenAI cut API prices on two of its three GPT-5.6 tiers. The workhorse tier, Luna, dropped eighty percent. The mid tier, Terra, dropped twenty percent. The flagship, Sol, stayed exactly where it was. OpenAI's own developer pricing page now shows the concrete numbers. Luna runs twenty cents per million input tokens and a dollar twenty per million output tokens. Terra runs two dollars in and twelve dollars out. That's a ten-times gap between Luna and Terra, on both input and output. Put plainly, OpenAI's cheapest capable model now costs a tenth of the tier sitting one rung above it. And this lands just three weeks after GPT-5.6 launched on July 9th, an unusually fast repricing cycle for a frontier lab.

OpenAI says the cuts come from efficiency gains. In its own words, from improving the models, the inference systems that run them, and the agentic harness that connects them to tools and context. That's the official line, and it's worth taking seriously. But the timing tells a different story to most of the reporting around it.

Three days before OpenAI's cut, Moonshot AI released the full open weights of Kimi K3, a 2.8-trillion-parameter model, on Hugging Face. Yesterday that was still flagged as unconfirmed. It's now verified directly. Moonshot's own model card says it released the full weights under an open license, and the repository has logged nearly half a million downloads in the past month. This isn't a promise anymore. It's a shipped, actively-used, frontier-class open model that anyone can download and run. And it's not a toy. Kimi K3 scores fifty-seven on Artificial Analysis's Intelligence Index, which places it fourth among the 189 models that service tracks. For a company running sensitive workloads, that means near-frontier output without sending a single token to a US API. That's a real option now, not a roadmap item.

Add DeepSeek's V4, which reached general availability on July 20th with a novel dynamic pricing scheme that lowers cost during off-peak hours. That's two distinct low-cost vectors arriving within ten days of each other: one free to own, one dynamically discounted. Put them together and you have two real Chinese competitors putting live downward pressure on price. So the honest read is contested. OpenAI calls it efficiency. The market is reading it as a forced response to free and cheap alternatives suddenly sitting at the frontier. Both things can be true. But the second one is the more important story, because it means frontier pricing in the US is increasingly being set by the competitive field, not just by any one lab's own cost curve.

That competitive read has a run-over-run signature worth naming. We covered Anthropic's Sonnet 5 launching at introductory pricing on the 29th, and Opus 5 landing flat, at half its flagship's cost, on the 31st. Now OpenAI cuts GPT-5.6 three weeks after shipping it. That's three straight releases repriced downward inside a single week. And here's the second-order effect to watch. A three-week post-launch repricing cadence sets a new expectation, and it pressures Anthropic and Google to match or preempt with cuts of their own. That would compress margins across the industry. And this part is analysis rather than a measured figure: likely faster than the underlying compute costs are actually falling.

There's one more change in the same OpenAI update worth a developer's attention. OpenAI renamed its Priority Processing tier to Fast mode, which now delivers up to two-and-a-half times the speed of standard processing, at twice the price. So the menu is getting more explicit: cheaper tokens if you can wait, a speed premium if you can't. It's a small change, but it's the shape of a maturing market, letting developers pay for latency and cost separately rather than bundled together.

Now the so-what on the price cut itself, which is concrete. An eighty percent cut to a mid-tier model, down to a dollar twenty per million output tokens, lowers the cost floor for the always-on, high-volume agent workloads. Think customer support, monitoring, background processing. Those are the loops that run continuously, and they were previously too expensive to leave on even at mid-tier pricing. That's a product unlock, not just a spreadsheet change.

That's the price of a token collapsing. The other pricing revolution underway is about how you're charged at all.

Salesforce has shipped outcome-based pricing for its prebuilt Agentforce Help Agent. You pay a flat rate only when the agent autonomously resolves an issue. If the customer escalates to a human, or walks away unhappy, there's no charge. Your cost is tied to outcomes, not activity, as Salesforce puts it. That's a genuine departure from the seat-based or consumption-based pricing that's dominated enterprise software. And it directly answers the skepticism that's dogged enterprise AI agents generally: does this thing actually work well enough to trust. By shifting the deployment risk from the buyer to the vendor, Salesforce is betting its own numbers back up the claim. On its own help site, Agentforce has handled 4.3 million inquiries and resolved seventy percent of them autonomously.

Two honest caveats there. That resolution figure is Salesforce's own self-reported number, not independently audited. And there's no data yet on how customers are actually taking to the pay-per-resolution model itself, as opposed to Salesforce's existing consumption-based option. This is also not a brand-new announcement. The pricing was unveiled earlier and reached general availability over the past month, so the novelty here is the model, not the calendar. Still, the direction matters. If outcome pricing catches on, expect pressure on other enterprise-agent vendors, the ServiceNows and Zendesks of the world, and even on Salesforce's own legacy per-seat licensing, to justify themselves against a model where you only pay for value actually delivered.

Put those two developments together and they point the same direction. At the bottom of the stack, the price of raw model access is collapsing under open-weight and low-cost competition. At the top, the pricing model itself is shifting from tokens consumed to outcomes delivered. Both moves push value and margin away from simply selling access to a capable model, and toward whoever can convert that capability into resolved work. The middle of that trade, selling a good-enough model at a fat margin, is getting squeezed from both sides at once.
