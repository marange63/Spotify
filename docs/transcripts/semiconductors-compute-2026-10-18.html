Good morning, it's Sunday, October 18th, 2026, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

Start with the backdrop, in brief. Upstream suppliers still describe a shortage. ASML says its planned tool supply won't fully meet demand in 2027 or 2028, and TSMC still calls the AI supply gap quite big. But the two newest signals in this market complicate the boom for reasons that have nothing to do with supply. One is legal, out of Beijing. The other is cyclical, out of Seoul's equity tape. Let's take them in order.

On October 16th, China's Ministry of Commerce and Ministry of Science and Technology published a draft catalog adding forty technologies to export controls. Twenty-three of the forty are AI. Fourteen would be banned from export outright, and the rest restricted. Public comment is open through November 15th, so this is a draft, not law.

The first question for this audience is scope. Is this about model licensing, or does it touch silicon? The answer is that it touches silicon. Our source here is an English translation of the announcement by the newsletter Geopolitechs, which quotes the annexes directly. The Taipei Times confirms the broad outline. We haven't seen the original Chinese text, so treat the exact wording as a translation. With that caveat, the draft defines a controlled "large-scale intelligent computing cluster" as one with more than ten thousand AI accelerator cards. It lists AI processor chip design technologies as their own controlled category. It does the same for memory chip design technologies. And the large-model items explicitly reach weight parameters, checkpoints, source code, and architecture design. One thing we can't yet pin down is which of these items sit in the banned tier and which sit in the restricted tier.

So the reach is hardware-adjacent. Chip design know-how and big compute clusters are named targets, not just trained models changing hands. That widens the population of exposed deals. Any Chinese team co-developing an accelerator or memory design with an overseas partner now sits inside the same regime as an AI lab licensing a model.

Now the part that bites today. The catalog is a draft, but a temporary registration requirement took effect the same day, October 16th. In the translated text, it applies to "technology exports that have already commenced but have not yet been completed." Exporters must register through a Ministry of Commerce online system before proceeding. Covered transactions include ownership transfers, intellectual property transfers, and licensing for use overseas. So deals already in flight face a compliance gate now, before any final rule exists. That's how commercial chilling starts. Counterparties don't wait for the final text to slow down.

There's also a notable carve-out. Technology already public through open-source release or academic publication is excluded. That gives Chinese developers a reason to keep publishing openly, because publication is a way to stay outside the controls.

So how should we read Beijing's intent? A Ministry of Commerce spokesperson said the measures are "not directed at any particular country, region, or entity." The ministry framed them as solidifying a security baseline and preventing misuse. That's unverifiable either way, so don't take it at face value, and don't dismiss it either. The consensus read will be retaliation. The structure certainly mirrors Washington's playbook: a list, banned and restricted tiers, and review before transfer. But an equally plausible reading is defensive. Beijing may simply want to keep domestic AI know-how from leaking out as Chinese labs commercialize abroad. Both readings survive the evidence we have.

So who actually loses ground? Start with the direction of the controls. They cover technology leaving China, not chips entering it. That means the exposed parties are Chinese developers who license or transfer AI and chip-design know-how abroad, plus the overseas partners on the other side of those deals. Our sources don't name a single company or deal that has hit the registration gate yet. And nothing in the catalog touches accelerator demand, memory pricing, or foundry utilization. For now, this is a story about deal flow and leverage, not about the economics of the boom. The tell to watch is whether any chip company outside China discloses a stalled co-development program with a Chinese partner.

Here's the inference worth carrying forward. If the catalog is finalized, access to Chinese AI and chip-design technology becomes something Beijing can trade. That looks like a government stacking leverage, not settling a relationship.

And that updates this show's own framework. On Friday, we said China's binding AI ceiling was a policy ceiling, set in Washington through chip export controls. As of that same day, Beijing started building a ceiling of its own. Washington gates chips going in. Beijing now gates know-how going out. The bifurcation of the AI stack has gates on both sides.

If Beijing is fencing off the stack with law, Seoul's investors are discounting the boom with arithmetic.

Samsung shares are still more than twenty-five percent below their June peak, according to the Seoul Economic Daily, despite record earnings. A Morgan Stanley note dated October 13th put Samsung and SK hynix at roughly three to four times forward earnings. It warned the rally leaves limited room for further gains. The same report says Asian memory stocks have risen roughly ninefold since September of last year. Both figures reach us through the financial site Invezz, not from Morgan Stanley directly, and the ninefold number may be Invezz's own.

To see what peak memory earnings look like in dollars, take Micron. Micron isn't part of the Morgan Stanley note. It's here only as the clearest picture of scale. Its SEC filing shows fiscal 2026 revenue of $133.19 billion, against $37.38 billion the year before. That's more than triple in a single year. GAAP net income was $84.97 billion. That's the kind of earnings power the market in Seoul is now pricing as a peak.

Now the mechanism. TrendForce's forecast from August had conventional DRAM contract prices rising another ten to fifteen percent in the fourth quarter. That compares with a roughly sixty percent jump in the second quarter. So prices are still forecast to go up. What's decelerating is the rate of increase, not the price level. Equities trade on that rate of change. A three-to-four-times multiple after a ninefold run looks like the market treating next year's earnings as a cyclical peak, not a new baseline.

But be careful with that multiple. A low forward multiple is ambiguous. It can mean investors expect earnings to fall. It can also mean consensus estimates simply haven't caught up with prices yet. The note, as reported, doesn't say which. So the cyclical-peak reading is the most natural one, but it isn't proven.

That's a partial answer to the question we left open yesterday. Memory fell hardest this week, and we laid out three explanations: counterparty risk, rates and crowding, and competition inside HBM. The Morgan Stanley evidence supports the rates-and-crowding explanation. Investors piled into a historic rally and are now repricing the rate of change. It doesn't rule out counterparty risk or HBM competition, so treat it as one piece of evidence, not the verdict.

The bull case against the cyclical reading is structural. High-bandwidth memory is sold on multi-year contracts. Bulls argue that changes the cycle, because past DRAM booms ended in the kind of oversupply those contracts are meant to prevent. If that's right, today's multiples are investors projecting an old cyclical pattern onto a different business. It's a plausible argument. But nothing in today's evidence quantifies it, so it stays a thesis rather than a fact.

There's also a split outcome, and mark this as inference. Slower contract-price inflation is relief for hyperscalers and device makers, whose bills of materials have been absorbing memory costs. The same number caps the equity upside for the memory makers. One data point reads as good news for buyers and bad news for sellers.
