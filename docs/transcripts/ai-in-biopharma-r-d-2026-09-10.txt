Good morning. It's Thursday, September 10th, and this is your briefing on artificial intelligence in biopharma R&D.

We owe you a correction before anything else. On September 6th, and again on September 8th, this briefing described Insilico Medicine's Phase III trial of rentosertib as already running. It was not. Insilico dosed the first patient in that trial yesterday, September 9th. We took a sponsor's trial-design announcement as evidence the trial was underway, which is the exact failure mode this beat has spent the past week diagnosing in other people's coverage. So we correct it, and we lead with it, because the event underneath the error is the most important thing that happened on this beat this week.

The trial is GENESIS-IPF-3, and the first patient was dosed at Peking Union Medical College Hospital. Rentosertib is a TNIK inhibitor for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis that Insilico says its generative-AI platform designed end to end. Insilico's release also says TNIK had never previously been linked to fibrosis before this program — that's the company's characterization, not an independent one. On Sunday, this briefing named the test to watch for the whole AI-native drug discovery category: the arrival of a disclosed date that the sponsor itself cannot quietly move. Three days later, that test resolved. The registry record for this trial, NCT07687459, now lists an estimated completion date of October 30th, 2029. That is a real clock. It runs for roughly three more years, across 320 patients and 47 centers, with a once-daily dose over 52 weeks and a primary endpoint of the annual rate of decline in forced vital capacity.

Now the skeptical turn, because a trial start is the cheapest milestone in drug development. The sponsor controls when it happens and how it's described. Insilico's press release calls this the world's first Phase III trial of a generative-AI-driven innovative drug. No industry body adjudicates that claim — it's Insilico's own language, in its own release. And on this beat's own reporting from Sunday, Generate Biomedicines' GB-0895 is already in global Phase 3 for severe asthma. So the "first" framing doesn't hold up cleanly. What actually changed yesterday isn't the probability that rentosertib succeeds. It's the observability of the answer.

The best data the program has is still the Phase IIa result, and it's worth restating what that number can and can't carry. Insilico's release of its Nature Medicine publication reported that the 60-milligram once-daily arm showed a mean FVC increase of 98.4 milliliters at 12 weeks, against a mean decline of 20.3 milliliters on placebo. Hold that against what the Phase III is powered on. The Phase IIa figure is an absolute change over 12 weeks. The Phase III endpoint is an annualized rate of decline over 52 weeks. Those are different quantities in a disease that worsens continuously, and a favorable three-month snapshot is a weak predictor of a full-year slope. So the honest read is that a real early signal now has to survive a much harder test, on a clock nobody controls.

And notice what the dosing does not change. A 52-week annualized decline endpoint in 320 patients is a conventional, expensive, unhurried registrational study. It is priced almost identically to one designed without any AI in it at all. Whatever the platform saved upstream gets spent back here at full freight. That's why the trial venue matters more than it looks. Every listed site sits in mainland China, which buys fast enrollment and low per-patient cost. It also defers a question rather than answering it: whether an NMPA data package built entirely on Chinese patients supports a filing at the FDA or the EMA without a bridging study. If it doesn't, the 2029 date is the start of a second, more expensive clock, not the end of the first one. The CRO demand and the regulatory-strategy consequence both sit in that gap.

That's the one end of the pipeline where somebody else eventually does the scoring. Now the other end, where the scoring is free and so is the raw material.

Google DeepMind this week published the AlphaGenome Atlas: a precomputed dataset scoring the predicted molecular effect of all 9 billion possible single-letter DNA substitutions across the human genome, covering both the 2 percent that codes for protein and the 98 percent that doesn't. It's roughly a petabyte, more than 30 times the size of the AlphaFold Database, and it's free for academic use through a web portal and an API. Alongside it, DeepMind introduced a new ranking metric, the AlphaGenome Variant Impact score, which fuses its regulatory-effect model with its coding-variant model, AlphaMissense, into a single number. DeepMind's own example: researchers used the score to prioritize overlooked variants in a rare-disease cohort and surfaced one affecting a gene called DNM1.

Be clear about what this is and isn't. It's a dataset and a scoring metric, not a new model architecture — the underlying model shipped last year. The evidentiary base for the new score, in the public record, is that single DNM1 case, described in DeepMind's own blog post, with no independent wet-lab benchmark yet. That's the same self-scoring pattern that's shown up everywhere on this beat this week. And the size comparison to the AlphaFold Database is a storage comparison, not a capability comparison. A petabyte doesn't mean thirty times the scientific value.

Here's the argument that actually matters. This attacks the wrong end of the funnel for the field's real problem. Making variant-to-mechanism hypotheses free doesn't move the transition rate through Phase 2, because Phase 2 failures are failures of target validity confirmed in humans, not a shortage of hypotheses. Widening the top of a funnel whose narrow end is unchanged produces more shots on goal, not a better hit rate. What it does do is run the AlphaFold Database playbook a second time: give away the precomputed map instead of the model, and let ecosystem adoption become the moat. The commercial cost lands on whoever currently charges for variant interpretation, not on DeepMind. And the paid layer of genomics gets pushed back toward curation, wet-lab functional follow-up, and cohort quality — the same unglamorous conclusion the real-world-data thread on this beat reached from the other direction last week. Differentiation keeps migrating to the part of the stack nobody wants to fund.

When the input goes free, the question becomes what anyone in this field can still charge for. Schrödinger answered that this week by spinning out Tectora Therapeutics with NEA and RA Capital, contributing two AI-discovered small-molecule programs, SDGR-4594 and SDGR-8139, in exchange for equity, milestones, and royalties. Tectora closed a 55-million-dollar Series A alongside the deal. Terms of Schrödinger's stake weren't disclosed, so the transaction can't actually be sized. But the shape of it is the point: two programs that have never been in a human being are being converted into equity on someone else's balance sheet, not licensing revenue on Schrödinger's own. That's what you sell when you don't yet have evidence to sell — optionality instead of proof, which keeps development cost off your books while keeping upside on your slide deck. If that structure becomes the template, the number worth tracking on these companies stops being partnership revenue and becomes the size and the dilution of the stakes they hold in other people's startups.

One more clock, briefly, for contrast. Isomorphic Labs, the DeepMind and Alphabet spinout, raised 2.1 billion dollars in a Series B back in May. Its CEO, Demis Hassabis, said at Davos in January that first clinical trials — already slipped once from an end-of-2025 target — would now come by the end of this year. That's a little under four months away, with no update on a dosed patient found anywhere in this run. The deadline hasn't expired, so don't call it missed. But unlike rentosertib's, it's still a date Isomorphic can quietly move.

So pull the threads together. The field's evidence base still sits almost entirely upstream of the one stage where the thesis actually gets graded. A target hypothesis, a molecule design, a spun-out program are all cheap to generate and getting cheaper. R&D spending in this sector keeps reallocating toward that cheap end rather than shrinking overall. And when a graded result finally arrives, the repricing will be uneven: Insilico and Generate own assets and get marked on outcomes, while the closed private platforms carry marks that no rival's readout touches. The single thing worth watching now isn't a conference keynote or a press release. It's a registry date: October 30th, 2029.

That's the briefing for today. Talk to you tomorrow.
