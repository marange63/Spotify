Good morning. It's Monday, July 27th, and this is your frontier AI labs briefing.

The competition this week is splitting into two separate battles. One is over compute, and the money there is scaling up. The other is over model capability, and the price there is collapsing. Today's news gives you the cleanest evidence yet of both, on the same week, from the same industry.

Start with compute. On July 25th, Nvidia and South Korea's SK Group signed letters of intent for a partnership worth more than 500 billion dollars, covering AI data centers, memory supply, and next-generation computing infrastructure. SK Telecom will build a 2-gigawatt AI data center running Nvidia's Vera Rubin platform, with the first phase targeted for 2027. Separately, Nvidia and SK Hynix agreed to deepen their collaboration on HBM4, the high-bandwidth memory that next-generation AI training depends on.

Two caveats matter here, and our own deep-dive checked them directly against SK Hynix's newsroom release and three independent write-ups. First, this is a letter of intent, not a signed contract — both companies' own language says so, and neither discloses a near-term cash schedule. Second, nobody, not even SK Hynix itself, put a number on the HBM4 volume or dollar value involved. The memory piece is described only as a "stable supply" and "long-term partnership." So treat 500 billion as a multi-year, non-binding aggregate, not a check that's been cut. Still, the signal is real: two of the industry's biggest players just committed to the largest single AI infrastructure package disclosed to date, with real geography, real megawatts, and a real 2027 target.

Why does the timing matter? Because it lands just over a week after the market spent a session violently second-guessing whether AI capex is worth it at all. That's the hinge story, and I'll get to it in a moment. For now, the headline is: Nvidia's biggest customers just voted with 500 billion dollars that the compute buildout is not slowing down.

The same doubling-down shows up on a different balance sheet. Alphabet reported second-quarter earnings on July 22nd, with revenue up 24 percent year over year to 119.8 billion dollars and cloud revenue surging 82 percent to 24.8 billion, backlog running to 514 billion. Alphabet raised its full-year 2026 capex guidance to a range of 195 to 205 billion dollars, up from a prior 180 to 190 billion. That's roughly 15 billion dollars more committed to data centers and compute, announced in the same window that Nvidia's own customers were re-pricing whether that spending pays off. Alphabet's stock actually fell about 5 percent on the print, purely on the capex number — investors rewarded the growth and punished the spend, which tells you the market's patience for open-ended AI capex is thinner than the headline beat suggests. Worth noting: Alphabet didn't break out how much of that new capex goes to Gemini training versus TPU production versus general cloud buildout, so don't read this as a direct bet on model supremacy — it's a bet on owning the infrastructure underneath it, TPU included.

Now to the hinge. On July 16th, China's Moonshot AI released Kimi K3, a 2.8 trillion parameter open-weight model it billed as the largest open-source release ever, with pricing at 15 dollars per million output tokens against Anthropic's Fable 5 at 50 dollars — a roughly 70 percent discount. The very next day, Nvidia's stock fell nearly 17 percent in a single session, a 589 billion dollar market-cap loss, the largest one-day decline for any company in market history, with the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index down 9 percent. That selloff is exactly what this week's infrastructure commitments are answering. The market asked, in effect, whether a Chinese lab undercutting frontier pricing by 70 percent means the entire Western capex thesis is broken. Nvidia's biggest customers just responded, within days, by committing the largest infrastructure package on record. Capital has, for now, voted against the capex-bubble read — though it's worth remembering the original DeepSeek shock in January 2025 also triggered a steep Nvidia selloff that mostly recovered, so this is a live argument, not a settled one.

Here's the piece that keeps the K3 story honest: nobody has independently verified that Kimi K3 actually performs anywhere near Fable 5's capability level. Every comparative claim traces back to Moonshot's own materials or single-source aggregator reporting. This briefing flagged July 27 — today — as the date K3's open weights would make independent testing possible. As of this recording, no independent benchmark has surfaced. So the capability premise underneath a 589 billion dollar market move is still, technically, unconfirmed. That's not a small caveat — it's the whole question.

What is confirmed, and quoted directly from source materials, is the price floor K3 and its peers are setting. DeepSeek's V4-Pro reportedly prices as low as 87 cents per million output tokens. At 87 cents against Fable's 50 dollars, that's nearly sixty times cheaper on output — not a rounding error, but a gap on the exact axis that decides most high-volume production workloads. That kind of pricing pressure is real regardless of whether K3 matches Fable 5 on raw intelligence, because enterprise buyers make cost decisions on a spectrum of "good enough," not just leaderboard position.

Which brings us to how the US closed labs are answering that pressure — not with a price cut, but by holding price flat while closing the capability gap. Anthropic shipped Claude Opus 5 on July 24th at unchanged Opus pricing, 5 dollars per million input tokens and 25 dollars per million output, the same as its predecessor. What changed is the ceiling: Opus 5 posted a big jump on FrontierBench, roughly doubling the prior Opus score and edging past both Anthropic's own flagship Fable 5 and OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Sol on that benchmark, alongside strong gains on computer-use and coding evaluations. The strategic move here isn't the benchmark table — it's that Anthropic chose not to raise price while closing the gap to its own top-tier model. That's a direct response to a market where Chinese open weights are compressing prices from below. The caveat stands: those figures are all vendor-reported by Anthropic, with no independent benchmark run confirming them yet, and there's still no public usage data showing developer traffic is actually shifting toward Opus 5 as a result. Flat pricing is a signal of intent, not yet proof of adoption.

So step back and look at the shape of the whole week. The infrastructure layer — Nvidia, SK Group, Alphabet — is consolidating and scaling capital harder, not softer, even after a 589 billion dollar scare. The model layer — Kimi K3, DeepSeek, Opus 5 — is commoditizing on price, with capability leaders now competing on price-per-token rather than raw ceiling. These are two different competitions with two different winners. If that pattern holds, expect blended API margins to compress industry-wide even as compute and memory supply stays tight — a squeeze that favors labs with their own low-cost captive compute, like Google on TPUs or Anthropic on AWS Trainium, over pure API resellers riding someone else's chips. The HBM4 lock-in between Nvidia and SK Hynix is the mechanical reason: the hardware moat is hardening in the exact same weeks the model moat is eroding.

That's the briefing. Have a good day.
