Strategic Power, Energy, and Institutional Change

Good morning. It's Wednesday, September 2nd, and this is your strategic power briefing.

Two American energy instruments moved in opposite directions over the past week. One is restrictive. It's a sanctions bill meant to punish the buyers of Russian oil. The other is permissive. It's the legal machinery that clears US export capacity to get built. This week the restrictive one stayed stuck, and the permissive one actually resolved something. That contrast is the spine of today's briefing.

Start with the sanctions bill, because we flagged on Tuesday that it had passed in early August and then sat all month in a House on recess. Today we have a piece of the reason. House Democratic Leader Hakeem Jeffries said on August 31st that his caucus has not taken a position on the bill. Note what he did not say. He did not say his caucus opposes sanctioning Russia. The reported sticking point is the scope of the authority the bill hands the White House.

Here is what that authority looks like. The measure, formally the Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026, would let the President impose tariffs of up to 100 percent on any country that buys Russian oil or gas, or that helps Moscow evade sanctions. It is aimed at India and China. But it is written broadly enough to reach almost anyone. It cleared the Senate on a lopsided bipartisan margin in early August, and it has not moved an inch in the House since.

Be precise about the mechanics, because this is where the easy narrative goes wrong. A minority caucus does not block a floor vote on its own. The House was in recess, and no leadership floor date is public. So the honest statement is narrower than "Democrats killed it." Nobody has scheduled it, and the loudest recorded objection is about presidential discretion rather than about Russia.

That distinction carries the durable consequence, and the consequence is domestic. Open-ended power to tariff third countries at 100 percent is not confined by the Russia rationale that justified it. Once written, it becomes a template. Any future administration inherits a tool for trade coercion against targets nobody has named yet. A bill this popular does not stall over its stated target. It stalls over what it lets a President do to everyone else.

Now look at how the actual target is behaving, because that's the scoreboard. In June, India's purchases of Russian crude climbed 17 percent month over month to a record 2.6 million barrels a day. That lifted Moscow's share of India's crude basket to 46 percent. By July, roughly 55 percent on a different tracker, so read the trend rather than the exact step.

The direction is the point. India's Russian-oil exposure was climbing before the Senate vote and it kept climbing after it. Unexercised authority is not leverage. Indian refiners buying discounted barrels while an unpassed bill sits in a recessed chamber are behaving rationally, not defiantly. And the honest caveat runs both ways. Nobody knows when the House takes this up. Nobody knows whether a President who has spent the year courting India as a counterweight to China would actually pull the trigger, even if the authority became law.

That's the instrument that's stalled. Now the instrument that's working, which adds your own supply rather than restricting someone else's.

On August 25th, the DC Circuit Court of Appeals upheld FERC's approval of Venture Global's CP2 LNG export terminal in Louisiana, along with its associated pipeline. Environmental groups had brought eleven separate claims of legal error under the Natural Gas Act and federal environmental law. Senior Judge Douglas Ginsburg, writing for the court, said the challengers' arguments "lack merit," and dismissed all eleven. The opinion leaned on a deliberately high bar. The Natural Gas Act, the court held, sets a demanding standard for finding that an LNG terminal is not in the public interest, and FERC's approval cleared it.

Ginsburg then refused to hand future challengers a shortcut. Anyone hoping for what he called "something akin to a bright-line rule" for when a terminal fails that standard is, in his words, out of luck. That refusal is the part that outlives this one case. It tells every other pending LNG docket what standard of review to expect. Price that as a discount-rate effect rather than as new volume. The legal risk premium just came down across the whole Gulf Coast export build-out, which helps the terminal developers and the gas-directed producers and midstream operators who feed them.

Hold one thing against that. A deferential standard cuts both ways. The same low bar that protects CP2 from challengers today protects whatever a future FERC decides to approve or to deny. What the court blessed is the agency's discretion, not the project's merits.

Put this against the run of stories this beat has tracked all week. Conditional Pentagon loan commitments to critical-minerals projects. A Chinese State Council reactor approval still on paper. An American reactor construction contract with no site attached to it. Those are all instruments that remain promises. CP2 is the first one this week that actually resolved. But clearing the legal risk still hasn't shipped a molecule. Cargoes are targeted for 2027, and a target is not a schedule. The turbine, transformer and labor constraints that slow every large energy project in America don't disappear because a court sided with a developer. What did disappear is the litigation overhang, which is a real if narrower form of progress.

Export capacity is one leg of the American energy-abundance case. The nuclear leg has an import problem, and this week's data makes it precise.

The Energy Information Administration's uranium marketing annual report shows that US-origin uranium was only 7 percent of US uranium deliveries in 2025. Canada supplied 32 percent. Kazakhstan supplied 28 percent, and Australia 15 percent. So the fuel underneath America's nuclear-for-AI-power strategy is the one link in the chain the country barely supplies itself.

The service that turns that fuel into reactor fuel is also getting more expensive. The average 2025 price for enrichment services came in at 108 dollars and 70 cents per separative work unit. That is up 11 percent from the year before. Read that carefully. An 11 percent rise is consistent with a tightening market, but it does not by itself prove a structural shortage. Ordinary utility restocking and post-Rosatom re-contracting would print the same number.

Now the strategic read. If domestic origin is 7 percent, then 93 percent of the fuel is imported. Just under half of that total, Canada plus Australia together, comes from allies. That part is not the same risk as dependence on a rival. But Kazakhstan's 28 percent sits closer to the fault lines this beat tracks every week, and the honest description is concentration risk rather than friendly-neighbor comfort. The sharper point is about price. With domestic origin that small, and the enrichment price set abroad, the marginal cost of the nuclear leg of the AI power trade is set outside the United States. Washington has talked for years about billions of dollars in new domestic enrichment capacity. That remains a funding commitment. It is not yet a plant, and it will not become one inside this data-center cycle.

So here's where the week leaves the abundance thesis. The restrictive lever, sanctions-by-tariff, is stalled in the chamber that has to pass it, and the recorded objection is about the President's discretion rather than about Russia. The permissive lever, export capacity, just cleared its biggest legal hurdle of the year. That is real progress, and it is still not gas in a ship. And the leg meant to make nuclear the reliable base layer for data centers runs through a fuel cycle the United States barely touches.

Watch two things from here. Whether the House takes any position on the sanctions bill once it returns from recess. And whether CP2's challengers seek further review. Both tell you whether this week's instruments were durable, or just the first move.
