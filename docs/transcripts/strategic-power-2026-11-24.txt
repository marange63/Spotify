The lever this week is the license, not the supply. Beijing runs one on minerals. Washington just built one for compute. And on the grid, from PJM's footprint to Spain, the right to connect is becoming a permit with conditions attached. Three stories this morning, one mechanism.

Start with the story that's new to this desk, because it has a deadline three days out. Beijing's Ministry of Commerce suspended its ban on exporting gallium, germanium, antimony and superhard materials to the United States in November of last year. That suspension runs out this Friday, November 27th. Together these metals feed semiconductors, optical fiber, munitions and night-vision systems. As of this recording, we have found no official Ministry of Commerce notice on what happens Friday. A tracker monitoring Beijing's own announcements reported in early November that it had found no notice amending the suspension. One report does claim an extension to January 10th. But that may well refer to Beijing's separate rare-earth suspension, and we could not corroborate it. So whether this one lapses or rolls over, nobody outside Beijing knows yet.

Here's the point that matters more than that binary. Even while the suspension has been in force, these metals have stayed on China's dual-use export control list. Exporters still have to apply for licenses for any overseas sale. A headline extension on Friday would not mean free flow. It would mean the license desk keeps deciding, shipment by shipment, who gets material. A formal lapse, meanwhile, would mostly be a political signal on top of a control that already exists. The second-order effect is our inference. Western chip-packaging, optical-fiber and defense-optics supply chains carry discretionary supply risk that no extension removes. That's a durable incentive to source outside China, even in the good outcome. The ban is the headline. The license desk is the lever.

There's a second data point here, and it needs care. Washington says China has met only about two-thirds of its rare-earth commitments under last year's Busan trade truce. Beijing disputes that assessment. The figure traces to unnamed US officials, reported by the Financial Times, not to a named official or an agency report. It covers rare earths, not the three metals expiring Friday. And a shortfall could reflect licensing friction, logistics or demand, not deliberate throttling. But it fits the same reading: a truce that holds on paper while the license desk sets the pace underneath.

On Sunday we called this truce a managed detente, reversible on Beijing's schedule. We pointed to January 10th as the date to watch. That's when Beijing's separate rare-earth suspension is reported to come up for renewal. Friday is an earlier, separate test of that framework, one we hadn't flagged. And if Beijing says nothing at all, don't read silence as calm. Silence just means the license desk keeps working.

One more piece before we leave geopolitics. At the APEC summit in Shenzhen, Trump and Xi agreed that AI must not independently make major military decisions. It's the first partial readout we've had from that room. But it's a norm, not a mechanism. Nothing verifies it, and it costs neither side anything. No chip deal came out of the meeting. So each side's hard levers, Beijing's mineral desk and Washington's compute desk, keep running on their own clocks. And Washington's version of that license desk just moved offshore.

Commerce's Bureau of Industry and Security has issued a rule on remote access to advanced chips. We're working from law-firm and trade-press summaries, not the Federal Register text. The rule requires companies to get licenses to export, reexport or transfer AI chips and servers to data centers that restricted-country firms could tap for remote compute. The trigger is over 12 million in total processing performance, or TPP. The rule also adds license requirements for shipments into jurisdictions judged to present a higher-than-normal risk of diversion. Structurally, that pushes the compliance burden past chip sellers, onto data-center operators and their financiers in third countries. Host compute in a friendly-looking jurisdiction for customers who couldn't buy the chips outright, and you are now the licensee.

Offshore AI hubs pitched as neutral ground now grow only as fast as US licenses allow. On Sunday, our private-credit desk reported that a Malaysian data-center loan with no reported hyperscaler tenant stalled mid-syndication. Nothing ties that loan to this rule. But it's consistent with lenders pricing exactly this kind of risk.

Here's the part that hasn't landed yet. Operators already running these facilities get 90 days from the rule's effective date to secure their licenses. On our arithmetic, counting from the November 19th publication date, that lands around mid-February. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent told CNBC last week that the proper chip restrictions are in place. He added, quote, "the remote access has been curtailed." That's premature at best. A license requirement is also only as tight as the approval practice behind it. And this desk's look at the licensing record on Saturday found that practice to be generous. Whether the rule bites on facilities already running is a question for mid-February, not for today.

The same permission logic is now arriving at the grid, at home and in Europe.

The International Energy Agency's new World Energy Outlook, as reported by Bloomberg, sharpens a point this desk has been circling. The announced AI data-center pipeline overstates what grids can actually connect. The IEA says planned developments would exceed current peak power demand in some major markets, if every proposed project got built. That's a ceiling scenario, not a forecast. But in those markets, part of the pipeline cannot exist as announced. Fewer than 100 major AI-focused projects could together need about 50 gigawatts. That's comparable to Great Britain's entire peak demand. And it's a requirement on paper, not connected load.

Then the money. Global data-center investment is projected to reach about 4 trillion dollars by 2030, up from 565 billion in 2025. On our arithmetic, that's roughly a seven-fold increase in five years. Much of that capital is being underwritten against grid connections that haven't been granted yet.

Two readings compete here, and both deserve weight. Reading one: much of that pipeline is speculative or duplicative, and simply won't get built as announced. That's a realization risk for the 4 trillion dollar trajectory, and for the suppliers pricing it. Reading two: it does get built, just off the grid. Think behind-the-meter, on-site generation rather than grid draw. That doesn't shrink the demand. It relocates it. The IEA's satellite monitoring of 93 major AI sites found a tenfold increase in capacity in just two years. That argues against writing the pipeline off as vapor. The question is whether it connects to the grid or routes around it.

Two smaller data points show where that question is heading. First, PJM, the largest US grid operator. Its independent market monitor, as reported by Bloomberg, says data-center load has raised capacity costs for all PJM customers by 29.4 billion dollars to date. That's the watchdog's counterfactual, not an audited bill, and it's cumulative, not annual. But it's the number that turns an abstract pipeline into a ratepayer bill. That's why grid access is becoming a political fight, not just an engineering one.

Second, Spain. A royal decree approved on November 20th requires large data centers to show that 80 percent of their electricity is renewable, matched hour by hour. Only then do they get the right to connect to the grid. It's a license condition on electrons, the same species of control as Washington's on compute and Beijing's on minerals. Hourly matching is far harder than annual offsets, and our reporting doesn't say how the decree will be enforced. It may simply push large loads toward looser jurisdictions rather than add clean supply.

The market read is our inference. Conditional, politically priced connection rights raise realization risk on capex timelines. They favor developers who bring their own firm or clean supply, and the independent power producers and on-site generation vendors who sell it. They also favor utilities and projects in jurisdictions with spare capacity. And they penalize any business case that assumed a grid connection on demand.

So pull the thread. Three license desks, in three different arenas. Beijing's decides who gets gallium and germanium. Washington's decides which offshore data center gets to run a restricted chip. And on the grid, from PJM's footprint to Madrid, the right to plug in is turning into a conditional permit. These are permission stories more than supply stories. Two dates to watch. Friday, when we learn whether Beijing says anything at all about the minerals suspension. And mid-February, on our arithmetic, when the 90-day clock runs out for offshore data centers already running restricted chips.
