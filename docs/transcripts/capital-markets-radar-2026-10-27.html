Good morning. It's Tuesday, October 27th, and this is Capital Markets Radar.

The through-line today: the AI financing test has climbed into public equity. And it's landing in a market that is paying up everywhere to avoid being first in line for a loss.

Start with the lead. Anthropic filed its S-1 with the SEC yesterday, October 26th. It intends to list Class A shares on the Nasdaq Global Select Market under the ticker A-N-T. A disclosure, because it matters here: Anthropic makes the model that powers this show, so weigh what follows with that in mind. This catalyst has now gone from press reports, to a leaked draft this show covered on Sunday, to a public filing. A rumor became paper.

The revenue line isn't news. The leaked draft already showed it. The filing confirms first-half 2026 revenue of 16.3 billion dollars, an annualized pace about seven times last year's 4.6 billion. The roadshow still has to show the quarterly trajectory.

What's new is the loss line, and it's the number that will dominate the roadshow. The filing shows a net loss of 80.7 billion dollars through June. That compares with a 42 billion dollar loss for all of 2025. The company says the vast majority of the first-half loss is accounting charges.

That phrase cuts both ways, and the market shouldn't resolve it yet. The bullish reading is that the cash loss is far smaller than the headline, and the business is closer to self-funding than the headline implies. The bearish reading is that accounting charges in a pre-IPO company often reflect fair-value remeasurement or dilution, and public holders inherit that. The filing as reported doesn't tell us which. You'll see confident breakdowns of that loss circulating this week. The ones we checked don't add up to the reported total, so treat any precise split with suspicion until it can be sourced to the filing itself.

Two more limits. The price range and share count have not been set. And the valuation you'll hear, 1.5 to 2 trillion dollars, is press reporting, not a filing figure. It's a talking point, not a market price.

Here's why this is the lead and not just another IPO story. This desk has spent the week climbing a financing ladder for the AI buildout, one layer of the capital stack at a time. It started with public debt, in CoreWeave's bonds. It moved to the price of credit protection, in Oracle's and Meta's default swaps. Then it reached private lenders, with Blackstone's money. Anthropic's S-1 is the first time a frontier lab asks public equity holders to fund it directly. Equity is the layer that absorbs losses first. So pricing this deal is a direct test of how much loss-absorbing risk capital the market will actually supply.

The second-order effect is the benchmark. Wherever Anthropic prices becomes the reference mark for private AI-lab valuations and secondary trades across the stack. That includes OpenAI, which this show has noted is raising privately and can keep its own numbers out of view. Anthropic's numbers are now public, and every rival will be marked against them. The so-what: public equity is now being asked to underwrite the cash and dilution profile behind the losses, not just the growth rate.

So what kind of market is this supply walking into? That's the options market's job to tell us, and Cboe's own read for the week of October 19th is blunt. The VIX rose five points week over week, to 20. Cboe ties the move to hotter-than-expected September inflation reports, both CPI and PPI. In Cboe's words, those reports revived Fed rate-hike fears. That's Cboe's attribution, and we don't have a clean futures read today to size how much hike risk is actually priced. So take it as the options market's narrative, not a measured repricing.

The more telling number is skew. One-month skew on the S&P 500 steepened from the 15th percentile to the 82nd. Cboe says the move was driven primarily by demand for downside convexity.

And the activity was heavy, not just pricey. Friday's SPX and XSP options volume ranked fourth- and fifth-highest on record. That distinction matters, and it's where the market can over-read. A VIX of 20 is elevated, not stressed. What got extreme was the price of the downside wing relative to at-the-money options. That's a market buying insurance, not a market in panic.

Now the inference, and it's ours, not a reported forecast. An IPO of this scale is a draw on equity risk budgets, and those budgets are currently tied up in hedges. If the Fed and the hyperscaler earnings pass this week without real damage, those hedges run off unused. That frees risk budget just ahead of a roadshow, and unused hedges tend to fuel relief rallies. If either catalyst disappoints, the deal meets a market that has already pre-paid for the downside. The so-what: the options tape says investors want equity exposure, but only with a floor under it.

The same instinct shows up in credit. Bank of America, in an October 22nd note reported by Investing.com, says US high-yield spreads widened through October. That pushed yields back to levels last seen in late 2023. BofA's advice to clients is to own exposures that do not take the first loss. It sees value in shorter-dated secondary double-B paper, at roughly 9.5 percent.

Tie that back to the lead. An IPO of a loss-making lab is, by construction, a first-loss security. If credit investors can earn about 9.5 percent without taking the first loss, that raises the hurdle for anyone asking the market to fund losses through equity instead. Rising credit yields lift the effective discount rate on long-duration, loss-making growth, before the Fed says a word.

And there's a slower channel underneath. On Sunday, this desk argued that AI credit risk is migrating into private loans that mark slowly and disclose little. Public high yield widening is the part of that story you can see daily. The private book reprices later, and usually in lumps. Options and credit are running the same trade in different clothes. Investors are paying up, or moving up the capital structure, to avoid being first in line for a loss.

Now a quick read on the fast money, because it's telling the same story from the sidelines. US spot Bitcoin ETFs took in 110.34 million dollars on October 26th, ending two sessions of outflows. That followed a 137.94 million dollar outflow on October 23rd. It's a thin number next to the six-hundred-million-dollar-plus outflow day this desk reported on Sunday. And the inflow came entirely through one fund, BlackRock's IBIT, the same fund that led those exits. One fund is a narrow base for a turn. The so-what: the marginal crypto buyer is waiting on Wednesday's Fed decision, not leading into it.

Which brings us to rates, and the catalyst everyone is waiting on is still pending. Start with the front end. Investrade's market review described Monday's 69 billion dollar two-year Treasury auction as soft. That review gave no tail or bid-to-cover, so treat "soft" as one source's characterization, not a measured result. And even taken at face value, one soft auction ahead of a Fed meeting is often just concession, not a verdict. The so-what: if that read holds, the market isn't pricing near-term relief on front-end funding costs. That's a quiet headwind for leveraged carry and for anyone refinancing, though it shouldn't be overstated.

The long end is calmer. The ten-year yield ended Monday essentially flat, down one basis point at 4.978 percent. That leaves it just under the 5 percent line this desk has tracked all month. So whatever rate pressure exists today sits at the front end, not in long duration.

The Fed's decision lands Wednesday at two p.m. Eastern. Microsoft and Alphabet report the same afternoon, with Amazon on Thursday. None of that has happened yet. No rate outcome, no dot plot, and no earnings print should be treated as fact until it prints.

So here's what to watch. First, whether Anthropic amends its S-1 with an actual price range. Second, whether that options skew bleeds off or holds through Wednesday's double catalyst. Third, whether two-year yields' reaction to the Fed confirms the soft-auction read or pushes back on it. All three ask the same question from different angles. How much will the market pay for growth that still needs funding, and how much does it want to be paid to take the first loss?

That's Capital Markets Radar for October 27th. Stay disciplined out there, and we'll see you tomorrow.
