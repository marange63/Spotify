Private Credit & the Corporate Credit Cycle

Transcript · August 20, 2026

Good morning, it's Thursday, August 20th, 2026, and this is your private credit briefing.

For four straight days this show has stayed on the liability side of the ledger — redemption gates at Blue Owl, Ares, Apollo, and BlackRock, and a wealth channel that PIMCO's Christian Stracke says simply will not sell direct-lending retail vehicles anymore. That story hasn't reversed. But it isn't the marginal news this morning. The marginal news is where the capital that's still deploying is actually going, and yesterday it went to Hubbard, Texas.

Eagle Point Credit Management is putting roughly one point three billion dollars of mezzanine debt into an AI data-center campus there, anchored by Anthropic as the lead tenant, inside a financing package reported at roughly sixteen billion dollars in total. Read that against the prediction this show made on Sunday: that deployment pressure — banks re-pricing the best middle-market credits away from direct lenders — would push private credit into AI infrastructure to defend returns. That's what's happening. The disruptor and the disrupted are increasingly sitting inside the same portfolios.

Now, what exactly is Eagle Point underwriting? Follow-up reporting fills in a capital stack the initial headline didn't. The senior piece of the Nexus package is reported at roughly fifteen billion dollars, led by a bank consortium under Morgan Stanley, sitting ahead of Eagle Point's one point three billion mezzanine slice. And the tenant risk is more structured than a raw bet on Anthropic's credit. Google is reported to be guaranteeing billions of dollars of Anthropic's obligations across four specific data-center leases and their matching power-purchase agreements, in exchange for roughly a twenty percent equity stake in the combined project. So the honest framing is that Eagle Point's junior capital is less a direct bet on a startup lab and more a bet on the scope and durability of Google's guarantee. That's a materially better-collateralized risk than "private lender underwrites one AI tenant" — but it is still a bet on a guarantee that, as of this week, is reported and consistent across multiple outlets but has not been confirmed by Google or Anthropic themselves as formally closed. Treat it as advanced, not settled.

And notice who is actually sitting inside this so-called private stack. The fifteen-billion-dollar senior tranche is a bank consortium. Google is on the hook for the guarantee. So the label "private credit" is doing a lot of work here — banks are supplying the senior layer and a hyperscaler is supplying the backstop around what gets counted as non-bank lending. That is exactly the interconnectedness the Financial Stability Board flagged back in May, when it warned that links between private credit funds, banks, and insurers are deepening and that the asset class has never been tested through a severe downturn. And the channel is scaling into that risk, not out of it: Moody's expects corporate private-credit assets to pass two trillion dollars this year and head toward four trillion by the end of the decade. The point is uncomfortable but simple. Growing this channel doesn't move risk out of the banking system. It wires the banking system into a newer, more opaque corner of it. There's a second risk buried in the collateral itself. The Hubbard campus runs on its own behind-the-meter power generation, so Eagle Point isn't only underwriting a tenant's credit — it's underwriting power availability directly. That's a departure from lending against pure digital infrastructure, and it's a risk private lenders have very little history pricing.

One thing to flag and then set aside: it's tempting to fold this into the retail-exposure story we've been tracking all week. Don't. Eagle Point runs this deal through an infrastructure-credit strategy launched last year, distinct from its listed, retail-accessible closed-end fund. Nothing in the reporting confirms retail money sits behind this specific tranche, so that claim doesn't make it into today's briefing — a useful discipline given how loosely "private credit exposure" gets asserted elsewhere.

Zoom out and this deal is one data point in a channel Morgan Stanley says has gone from near zero to over two hundred billion dollars of AI-related private credit outstanding in a few years, with another eight hundred billion projected over the next two years. That's Morgan Stanley's forecast, reaching us through secondary aggregation rather than a note I've read directly, so hold it as directional scale, not gospel. But it's the same shop making the other call worth pairing with it: Morgan Stanley expects private-credit direct-lending defaults could hit eight percent annually between the second half of this year and the first half of next, versus a historical average of two to two and a half percent, driven by software exposure that runs around twenty-six percent of direct-lending books and is vulnerable to AI-driven obsolescence. So AI is simultaneously the largest new thing private credit is financing and, per this same forecast, the biggest threat to the sector it already owns the most of. Morgan Stanley itself calls an eight percent print "significant but not systemic," pointing to lower leverage among funds and BDCs than banks carried into 2008 — a fair counter to keep this from tipping into panic, but not a reason to wave off the concentration.

And the existing book is already showing it's not the "zero-loss" asset class it was marketed as. Octus's cross-BDC dataset — a hundred seventy-four funds — found total nonaccrual debt at cost jumped forty percent in a single quarter, from seven point one two billion dollars to nine point nine eight billion, after sitting in a narrow one-point-three to one-point-five percent band through all of last year. Separately, and less rigorously sourced, the median non-accrual ratio across the twenty largest BDCs is reported at two point eight percent for the second quarter, up from two percent — the highest since 2017. Those two figures come from different samples and don't stitch into one clean line, so don't hear them as one accelerating series. But they point the same direction, and non-accruals are the cleanest hard signal available in an asset class that otherwise marks its own homework. Much of what's still reported current is likely being kept there by amend-and-extend and PIK toggles, so the real stress rate is probably running ahead of the headline.

So where does the cycle actually stand? Not a broad credit event — underwriting standards are still repricing rather than breaking, and the senior-secured, guarantee-backed structure of the Eagle Point deal shows lenders adapting rather than chasing yield blindly. The unresolved risk is concentration and opacity compounding at the same time: junior AI-infrastructure paper priced on power and GPU-cycle assumptions that have never been through a downturn, arriving just as the existing software-heavy book is turning, and increasingly wired back into bank balance sheets through the senior and guarantee layers around it. Watch three things into the fall — whether Google's guarantee formally closes, whether more direct lenders start disclosing specific AI-infrastructure tranche exposure the way Eagle Point just did, and whether Q3 non-accrual and PIK prints keep accelerating toward Morgan Stanley's eight percent trajectory or plateau where they sit today.
