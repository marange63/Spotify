Good morning. It's September 11th, and this is your space and orbital economy briefing.

For five straight sessions on this beat, we've been grading operators against a rate they've never actually hit. China's constellation targets against its launch cadence. AST SpaceMobile's revenue against its cash burn. Amazon's deployment clock against its own FCC deadline. Today, for the first time in that run, one company gets graded on what it already does rather than on what it says it will do. That company is Planet Labs.

Planet reported second-quarter fiscal 2027 revenue of 116 million dollars, up roughly 58 percent year over year. The company's own release calls it a record. Adjusted EBITDA more than doubled, to 13.9 million dollars from 6.4 million a year earlier. That is the number that matters more than the top line, because it is the first evidence that the growth is carrying operating leverage with it rather than buying it.

The engine underneath is government. Defense and intelligence revenue grew 90 percent year over year. Part of that came from an 8 million dollar National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency contract where, on the company's own account, Planet was the only vendor considered. Management raised full-year guidance to a range of 430 to 441 million dollars, which would be 40 to 43 percent growth. Treat that as guidance, not as booked revenue.

Here's why this business can post numbers like that while nearly every launch-dependent name on this beat is still spending ahead of revenue. Planet isn't selling capacity that has to be built and then deployed. It's selling analytics on imagery from satellites that are already in orbit. The marginal cost of the next government contract is software and account management, not another rocket. That's a fundamentally different unit-economics story than Starship, than Neutron, than any constellation still filling out its shell. The point to bank is simple. In this sector, the asset that pays is the one already flying.

That matters beyond one ticker. Earth observation has spent a decade arguing that the analytics layer, not the sensor, is where the margin lives, without a public name demonstrating it at scale. Planet has now put a breakeven marker on the board at roughly 116 million dollars of quarterly revenue. Iceye, BlackSky and Capella get underwritten against a demonstrated number now instead of a projected one. That cuts both ways: it validates the model, and it tells you how much revenue you need before the model works.

Now the bear case, stated as plainly as the bull case. Planet's own numbers show bookings are not keeping pace with billings. Backlog was 814.9 million dollars, up 11 percent year over year. That is roughly a fifth the rate of revenue growth. Remaining performance obligations, a tighter measure of contracted future work, stood at 753.1 million dollars, with only about 46 percent of that applying to the next twelve months.

When revenue grows more than five times faster than the book behind it, some of this quarter's profitability inflection is being harvested out of an existing pipeline rather than manufactured by a widening one. The company's counter is that current backlog alone implies more than 400 million dollars of revenue over the next four quarters. That's a real floor. It is also, by construction, a floor built from contracts already signed.

The profit headline needs a precise caveat too. Planet's first positive non-GAAP earnings per share, reported by the company at two cents, is not GAAP profit. The actual GAAP net loss was 9.4 million dollars. That is narrower than the 22.6 million dollar loss a year ago, which is real progress, but it is still a loss. Management touts a pipeline of over 4 billion dollars in identified opportunities. It calls roughly 1 billion of that near-term. But chief executive Will Marshall defined near-term only as quarters, not years, and never said what makes an opportunity qualified at all. Treat that as a company characterization, not an audited metric.

And the 90 percent growth in defense and intelligence is a concentration risk as much as a validation. A sole-considered-vendor award is a moat while appropriations grow. It is a single point of failure when they don't, because the same procurement behavior that hands you the contract without a competition can hand the next one to somebody else without a competition.

That's what a claim on the future looks like once it's actually been collected and turned into an invoice. Now the other end of the spectrum, where the claim hasn't been collected at all.

China has filed with the International Telecommunication Union for something on the order of 244,000 satellite orbital slots. Two filings alone account for nearly 200,000 of those slots. China's actual operational fleet is estimated at somewhere between 1,300 and 1,900 spacecraft. That puts the paper reservation at roughly 128 times the hardware actually in orbit. Both of those figures come from secondary reporting of ITU filings, not from documents we could read, so hold the precision loosely and the order of magnitude firmly.

This is standing context, not news from the past few days. The filings date to late December of 2025. But it is the sharpest available test of a thesis this show ran on Monday and then questioned on Wednesday: that the sector's real currency is deployed satellites holding spectrum priority. The arithmetic here argues the other way. If a filing can reserve a shell without a single satellite in it, the filing is the cheap asset and the deployment is the expensive afterthought.

Don't take that as decided, though. The ITU's bringing-into-use rules exist precisely to expire paper claims that never get built, and nothing in the available reporting establishes that these filings will survive their own milestone schedule. The more defensible read is that Beijing bought a very cheap option. Its value depends entirely on whether regulators enforce the deadlines. That puts the FCC in an uncomfortable position. It can license the next tranche of Kuiper or Starlink capacity while a rival holds paper priority over the same slots. Or it can start challenging filings that far outrun any credible buildout. Either way, part of this contest is now fought at filing desks rather than launch pads, and that is a very different cost structure to compete in.

It's also worth underwriting China's paper claims and its hardware claims as two separate things. Secondary analysis puts each Hainan launch pad near sixteen launches a year. If that's the right order of magnitude, the binding constraint on Chinese megaconstellations is pad cadence, not capital and not satellite design — and no amount of filing activity relieves it.

Briefly, a callback. We told you twice this week to watch whether Rocket Lab's Neutron rocket physically reaches the pad inside the fourth quarter. The first evidence against that has arrived. A Stage 1 tank test failure has pushed the debut further into the quarter and narrowed the year-end window. Note the sourcing: that comes from a single secondary financial outlet, with no primary confirmation from Rocket Lab of the event or its root cause, so don't build a schedule conclusion on it. Rocket Lab's record 2.36 billion dollar backlog is prior-quarter data we already covered. Planet's book at least turns into invoices. Rocket Lab's hasn't turned into anything yet. A record book does not de-risk a rocket that hasn't flown.

So where does this economy actually stand. It's genuinely inflecting in government-facing analytics running on sensors that already exist. Planet just proved that case with an invoice rather than a rendering. Everywhere else it is still a reservation: paper orbital slots without hardware, a medium-lift rocket without a flight, and a Starship orbital attempt pencilled in for September 15th, which we still cannot confirm - if it flies, it is the biggest cost-per-kilogram datapoint of the quarter. Watch three things. Whether Planet's backlog growth accelerates to match its billings next quarter. Whether any regulator moves against China's paper filings. And whether Neutron hardware shows up at the pad before the year closes.
