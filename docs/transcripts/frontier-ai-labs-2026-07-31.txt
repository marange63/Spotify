Good morning. It's Friday, July 31st, and this is your frontier AI labs briefing.

This week's constraint has been compute. We've tracked the story that frontier capacity is effectively sold out through 2027, at something like fifty billion dollars a gigawatt. Today's news is how one lab answers that constraint: not by building more, but by locking down more suppliers.

Anthropic and AMD announced a strategic partnership on July 22nd to deploy up to two gigawatts of AMD's Instinct MI450-series chips in AMD's Helios rack-scale systems, with the first gigawatt coming online in the first half of 2027. Alongside the compute commitment, AMD said it will make a strategic equity investment of up to five billion dollars in Anthropic. That's the AMD press release, verbatim.

Now, the instinct here is to read this as Anthropic adding a clean third vendor to a two-vendor base. That's not quite right, and it's worth getting right because it changes what the deal actually proves. Anthropic's own site describes its hardware mix as "a range of AI hardware — AWS Trainium, Google TPUs, and NVIDIA GPUs" — three families already, before this announcement. And AMD's own release says this new commitment "builds on Anthropic's existing use of AMD Instinct MI355X GPUs." AMD was already in the stack. So the real story is an existing, smaller AMD relationship being scaled up to two gigawatts and formalized with equity — not a scarcity-driven pivot to a brand-new supplier. That's a more mature, multi-year hedge than a reactive scramble, and arguably a more credible signal, since AMD didn't have to win the account cold.

Size it against what we already know. Anthropic's April expansion with Amazon secured up to five gigawatts of new Trainium capacity, with Anthropic saying it already runs "over one million Trainium2 chips" to train and serve Claude, and AWS remains, in Anthropic's words, its "primary training and cloud provider for mission-critical workloads." Its separate deal with Google and Broadcom is measured in "multiple gigawatts" of next-generation TPU capacity starting in 2027. Two gigawatts of AMD silicon sits below the Amazon number and roughly in the same range as the Google one. So what this buys Anthropic is optionality, not primacy — a hedge against Nvidia allocation and against any single cloud partner's politics, layered onto commitments it already had.

For AMD, the value is validation, not just revenue. A marquee frontier lab scaling its Instinct footprint, plus a joint engineering loop where Anthropic tunes Claude to optimize workloads on Instinct chips and speed up AMD's ROCm software, is the clearest evidence yet that the GPU market might bifurcate rather than stay a near-monopoly for Nvidia. But be skeptical of how far that goes. Both headline numbers — the two gigawatts and the five billion dollars — are ceilings, not commitments. Nothing serves customers until the first gigawatt lands in 2027. And AMD's own disclosure states the equity investment is "up to five billion dollars... in the future," with no valuation, share count, or firm schedule attached. This is a supply hedge and a validation signal. It is not yet a measured dent in Nvidia's share of frontier spend.

Step back, though, and the deal terms point at something larger. When compute is sold out through 2027, capacity stops being something you simply buy and becomes something you have to be financed into. AMD's up-to-five-billion-dollar stake is one instrument for exactly that. Secondary analysis of the deal describes the money releasing in tranches as Anthropic hits performance and commercial milestones, rather than as a single check up front — capacity extended on equity-linked, milestone-gated terms. Treat that mechanism as reported analysis, not primary disclosure, because AMD's own release says only "up to five billion dollars... in the future" and describes no schedule. But the direction is the point. If the physical build is the binding constraint, then which labs keep scaling is increasingly decided by which ones can pull chip and cloud partners into equity- and milestone-linked capacity deals. Model competition starts to track balance-sheet access, not just research talent.

That same logic — cheaper, more diverse compute — is what funds the other move Anthropic made this month. On July 24th, Anthropic shipped Claude Opus 5, its fourth frontier-tier model in eight weeks, priced identically to the prior Opus 4.8: five dollars per million input tokens, twenty-five dollars per million output tokens. That's half the price of its own top-tier Fable 5. On CursorBench, Anthropic's agentic coding benchmark, Opus 5 lands "within 0.5% of Fable 5's peak score, but at half the cost per task." So think of this as compute diversification funding a pricing weapon: once you're not paying a Nvidia-only premium and you've got a cheaper AMD lane opening up, you can afford to sell near-frontier capability at half price and still protect margin.

The message to the rest of the industry is blunt. Anthropic is competing on cost per task, not just leaderboard position, and that puts direct pressure on OpenAI's mid-tier pricing and on whatever Google prices its coming Gemini 3.5 Pro at. And the direction of that pressure is one-way. Every time a lab holds near-frontier performance at half its own flagship price, it drags the whole industry's blended API margin down with it. The labs get squeezed from two sides at once: input costs that a sold-out compute market keeps high, and output prices that competitive discounting keeps pulling lower. But keep the skepticism proportionate. These benchmark numbers — the price comparison, the coding score — come from Anthropic itself or from lab-selected leaderboards. No neutral, third-party head-to-head has confirmed them, and there's no public data yet showing developer traffic actually shifting to Opus 5. A price cut and a benchmark claim are a signal of intent. They are not evidence of adoption.

Which brings us to the pressure from below that's been building on this whole compute-and-pricing story all week: Moonshot AI's Kimi K3. The full weights for that two-point-eight-trillion-parameter open model have been public on Hugging Face since July 27th. Four days on, there is still no independent, neutral benchmark run against those released weights. The claim that priced in a real US equity selloff — that a free Chinese download trails only the very top US frontier models — remains unverified by anyone outside Moonshot or the press coverage repeating Moonshot's framing. So hold that one loosely: it's real pressure on Western pricing power, but it's pressure from a claim, not yet from a measured result.

Put it together and the shape of the competition is clearer than any single benchmark table. It's no longer just about who tops a leaderboard. It's about who can secure diverse, non-Nvidia compute on workable terms, and who can turn that into a price a customer will actually pay for near-frontier performance. Anthropic answered the compute question this week by scaling a relationship it already had with AMD and dressing it in equity. It answered the pricing question by cutting Opus 5 to half of its own flagship. Both moves are real. Neither is proven yet at the scale the headlines suggest. That gap between the announcement and the evidence is exactly where a sophisticated buyer should be watching next.
