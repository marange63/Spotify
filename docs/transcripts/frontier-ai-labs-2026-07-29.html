Cautious Optimism Briefings

Frontier AI Lab Competition

Transcript · July 29, 2026

Good morning, it's July 29th, and this is your frontier AI labs briefing.

The story this week is not who has the best model. It's that the frontier race has split into separate contests, and this week each camp showed its hand on a different one. Model quality, price, distribution, compute access, safety posture — these used to move together on a single leaderboard. They've come apart. So track them separately, because the labs now are.

Start with governance. Yesterday, 1,178 employees across the top AI labs signed a letter called "Pacing the Frontier." Signatories include Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei, OpenAI's chief scientist Jakub Pachocki and chief research officer Mark Chen, Meta AI's chief scientist Shengjia Zhao, and Google DeepMind's VP of AI safety Anca Dragan. The count was still climbing at publication — an earlier snapshot had it at 1,134 — so read it as more than eleven hundred and moving. The ask is narrow: not to halt AI development now, but to get governments building the technical and governance tools to slow it later if needed. OpenAI and Anthropic both endorsed the letter as companies within hours. That's rare — rival labs coordinating publicly on anything.

But hold that against what the Future of Life Institute's own Summer 2026 Safety Index says about the same labs, which we flagged here on the 25th for walking back prior pause pledges. Anthropic scored highest of any lab graded — a C-plus, 2.66 out of four. OpenAI came in at a plain C, 2.28. Google DeepMind also a C, at 2.01. xAI failed outright, a flat F. And FLI's own report states that Anthropic, OpenAI, Google DeepMind, and Meta have all weakened or voided their earlier pledges to pause unilaterally if red lines were approached — language FLI's review panel itself calls "moving the goalposts." So the letter reads less like a safety breakthrough and more like branding. The same labs criticized for softening their own commitments are now the loudest voices calling for someone else to build the brakes. No government has responded or committed to anything yet. This is an ask, not an outcome.

There's a deeper problem the letter can't escape, and it points straight at the next camp. A governance moat built on closed-lab restraint means less every month, because frontier-class capability is increasingly downloadable for free. You cannot pace a model whose weights are already on the open internet. So treat the letter as a competitive signal — governance restraint as a marketing line, and an implicit contrast with the Chinese open-weight alternative — not a policy shift.

Now the other side of that trade. While US labs compete on safety branding, Chinese labs are competing on price and openness — and this week that story got a wrinkle. DeepSeek, which has led the price war all month, rolled out V4 this month in two versions. V4-Pro is a 1.6-trillion-parameter model with 49 billion active per token, aimed at heavy reasoning. V4-Flash is a smaller 284-billion-parameter model for cheaper, faster serving. Both carry million-token context windows. DeepSeek also retired its old chat and reasoner API names on the 24th, folding everything into the V4 line.

Here's the wrinkle. Alongside the launch, DeepSeek introduced something new for a Chinese lab: peak and off-peak pricing. During Beijing business hours — nine to noon and two to six — output token pricing roughly doubles. V4-Pro output runs about six yuan per million tokens off-peak and twelve at peak, which works out to roughly 87 cents versus a dollar seventy-four in dollar terms.

That matters more than the sticker price. Surge pricing is a tacit admission that even DeepSeek's inference capacity is constrained. This is the lab that's spent the year undercutting Western API pricing — and it's now rationing demand by the clock. That complicates the story this feed has been running all week, that Chinese compute is effectively infinite and cheap. It isn't. Compute scarcity binds on both sides of the Pacific now. And it changes the read on the Western pricing response. Anthropic answered this same price pressure last week by holding Opus 5 at the same five and twenty-five dollars per million tokens as the model it replaced, while claiming near-top-tier quality at about half the cost per task. If Chinese pricing is now drifting up at peak rather than only down, then the downward pressure on Western API prices may be gentler than the bear case assumes, and blended inference margins may not compress as fast as a pure price-war reading implies.

A third camp is answering a completely different question: not safety, not price, but who owns the pipes. SpaceX, merged with xAI since February, has formalized its $60 billion all-stock acquisition of the AI coding startup Cursor, a deal expected to close in the third quarter. The logic is vertical integration. Pair xAI's Colossus training cluster in Memphis — described by the companies as roughly a million H100-equivalent GPUs, a figure no outside party has audited — with Cursor's existing base of paying developers. xAI isn't trying to out-benchmark Claude or GPT this cycle. It's trying to own compute and distribution at the same time, the way SpaceX owns rockets and Starlink owns satellites.

You can see the same instinct in xAI's own model line. Grok 4.5, launched earlier this month, competed on token efficiency rather than peak benchmark scores. xAI reported resolving coding tasks on the SWE-Bench Pro benchmark using about 16,000 output tokens, where Anthropic's Opus 4.8 burned roughly 67,000 — a better-than-four-times gap. Those are xAI's own published charts, not independently reproduced, so discount them accordingly. But the direction is consistent: cheaper to run, wrapped in a product people already pay for. If the Cursor deal closes, expect it to squeeze standalone coding tools like Cognition's Devin or Replit, which don't have a hyperscaler-scale compute backer behind them. That's a fundamentally different bet than the OpenAI-Microsoft or Anthropic-Google-and-Amazon model of API plus cloud partnership.

So three camps, three axes: governance branding, price and openness, vertical integration. All of them are spending against the same ceiling. Alphabet, Microsoft, Meta, and Amazon are guiding to roughly $725 billion in combined 2026 AI capex, up about 77% from last year's $410 billion. Microsoft and Meta report results today, and the market isn't watching this quarter's numbers — it's watching what they say about 2027 spending. That guidance is what decides which of these three strategies stays fundable. A safety letter costs nothing to sign. A surge-pricing scheme buys time on limited chips. Buying a coding startup for $60 billion in stock requires a capex line that keeps growing.

And the hardest question sits underneath all of it. Even the largest open-weight model ever shipped, Moonshot's Kimi K3, went out with full weights on the 27th, and there's still no independent benchmark on those released weights. So nobody can yet price how much of this spending buys durable advantage versus a capability that diffuses for free. Watch today's prints for the first sign of whether that ceiling is starting to bend.
