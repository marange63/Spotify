Cautious Optimism Briefings

Strategic Power, Energy, and Institutional Change

Transcript · November 16, 2026

Good morning. It's Monday, November 16th, 2026, and this is Strategic Power.

This week the loudest shocks finally got measured, and the measurements complicate the stories more than they confirm them.

Start with oil. A United Nations Security Council briefing said Iran has, in its words, "all but closed" the Strait of Hormuz. Before the war, that strait carried a fifth of the world's oil. Yet the same briefing put Gulf oil exports, excluding Oman, at 18.4 million barrels a day last month. That is only 26 percent below pre-war levels.

Those two claims sit uneasily together. If the strait were truly shut, three-quarters of normal Gulf flow should not still be getting out. Either more oil is moving through or around Hormuz than the closure language suggests. Or last month's export number is a lagging snapshot, and it flatters what is moving now. We have no transit count to settle it, so we hold both readings.

The forecasts add a second puzzle. The International Energy Agency's November report puts fourth-quarter global oil supply 1.7 million barrels a day above forecast demand. That is a sized surplus, not just a headline. Meanwhile, the US Energy Information Administration expects Brent to stay near 100 dollars a barrel this quarter. One agency sees oversupply. The other holds its price forecast near triple digits.

Here is our reconciling inference, and it is ours, not either agency's. A market can run a surplus and still price high if that surplus is refilling stockpiles drawn down earlier in the war. In effect, the calm is borrowed against future inventory, and repaying that loan would keep prices firm. A second reading is simpler: the EIA's path carries a risk premium for the strait itself. Even the EIA sees Brent averaging 86 dollars next year, so its own forecast assumes that premium fades.

That premium hinges on escalation, and here the gap between claim and confirmation matters. Iran's Revolutionary Guard claims three tankers struck mines in the strait. The UK Maritime Trade Operations agency reports something narrower. One caveat on sourcing: we have that report only as relayed by Iran's Mehr news agency. It says an oil tanker was attacked near Hormuz. The crew's status, the damage, and any environmental impact remain unknown. That report names no mines, and it describes one vessel, not three.

Our read is that the forecasters are pricing the verified record, not Tehran's messaging. We have no verified Brent print from this week to test that against the market itself. The risk is that one independent confirmation closes the gap between the claim and the record.

Underneath sits a slower effect, also our inference. If a quarter of Gulf exports can go missing while the world still runs a surplus, the chokepoint's coercive value is eroding, even while the strait stays contested. The winners are non-Gulf barrels and the routes around the strait. That is energy abundance working as strategic defense. So the shock is real and so far absorbed, but it sits one confirmed escalation away from a repricing.

Iran's leverage is measured in barrels. Beijing's is measured in dates.

The US-China trade détente was extended again this month. Its main package of tariff relief and paused controls runs to January 10th. What's new is a nearer date. A separate suspension covering gallium, germanium, antimony and graphite export items runs only to November 27th. That is eleven days from now. It is also narrower than the main package.

Our inference is that the truce is not one clock. It is a staggered calendar of separate levers, and each one can lapse on its own. With no signal yet on renewal, treat the 27th as a live expiry, not a predicted lapse.

The more structural shift is in semiconductors. The familiar story is Washington holding the chip-export door to China barely open. Nvidia's quarterly filing, as reported, shows Beijing holding that door too. It says Beijing restricted H200 sales, and that Nvidia has been unable to sell all the H200 units it is licensed to ship. Those licenses also require a US inspection before shipment.

One caution on timing. That filing covers the quarter that ended in late July. The disclosure is newly surfaced, not news about the current quarter.

The chip chokepoint now runs in both directions. Washington licenses and inspects. Beijing decides whether its own companies may buy. Why Beijing is doing this is genuinely open. One reading is protection for its domestic accelerators. Another is leverage it can trade inside the truce. Both are our inference.

Either way, Nvidia's China upside is now set by Beijing's policy, not only Washington's. Nvidia reports tomorrow. Listen for whether its China commentary describes a door Beijing means to keep shut, or one it is holding as a bargaining chip.

Those are chokepoints held by rivals. The costliest constraint on America's AI build this week is homegrown.

PJM's independent market monitor made a blunt demand in its November 12th report. It wants December's capacity auction postponed outright. In its words, PJM "should postpone the December auction until it can remove all forecast data center load from the auction."

Behind that demand sits 29.4 billion dollars in data-center-driven capacity costs across PJM's last four auctions. That total dates to the Monitor's October 30th report. What's new is the label. The Monitor now calls it "an irreversible increase."

The more useful number answers a question we raised yesterday. Were slow new supply and plant retirements doing more of the work than the Monitor's attribution allows? The Monitor's own counterfactual now speaks to that. It finds the data-center capacity effect added 9.8 percent to wholesale costs over the first nine months of this year. That is just under ten dollars a megawatt-hour.

Now strip that adder out. On our arithmetic, wholesale costs would still be up about 28 percent from a year earlier. So data centers are a real adder, but a minority of the jump.

Two caveats keep that honest. First, the counterfactual removes only the capacity-market channel. Any effect of data-center load on energy prices isn't in it, so the data-center share is a floor, not a ceiling. Second, last year's base already carried some data-center-affected costs, so treat the 28 percent as directional. Even so, the finding undercuts the Monitor's own framing. And it undercuts the "AI broke the grid" headline just as much.

The Monitor's sharper charge is institutional. It says "PJM has failed to implement the broad public policy mandate" that data centers pay their own way. PJM, per reporting, disputes the Monitor's role on cost allocation. So the fight has moved past the size of the bill. It is now about whether forecasts get to set prices at all.

The market read is our inference. If PJM strips forecast load from the auction, clearing prices fall. That would cut capacity revenue for independent power producers in the footprint, such as Constellation, Vistra and Talen. The postponement call alone injects revenue uncertainty three weeks out. And delay isn't costless, because it also delays the price signal meant to call forth new capacity.

One step further, also inference. If the mandate fight pushes states to make data centers bring their own generation, that favors behind-the-meter gas and nuclear developers over pure grid-draw siting.

And the supply response to that bill is still mostly paper.

Per the Daily Caller, a Utah county has approved a land-use permit for Valar Atomics' reactor campus. Alongside it sits a separate, larger proposal called Project Beehive. That one targets about 9.6 gigawatts of small reactors and data centers, nearly matching Utah's statewide summer peak of about 10 gigawatts. That figure belongs to the larger proposal, not the permit.

And the permit is land use only. The reactors still need a Nuclear Regulatory Commission license or a Department of Energy authorization. Valar's own chief executive calls the full build-out "many many years from now and uncertain." A permit is not a license. The siting ambition is real. The megawatts are not, yet.

Three dates to watch. Tomorrow, Nvidia's earnings and its China commentary. November 27th, when the gallium, germanium, antimony and graphite suspension expires. And whether PJM answers the Monitor's postponement call before the December 9th through 15th auction window opens.

Each of this week's shocks now has a measurement, and each one points to a nearer date than the narrative did.

That's Strategic Power for November 16th. Stay cautiously optimistic.
