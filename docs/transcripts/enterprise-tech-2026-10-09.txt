Good morning. It's Friday, October 9th, 2026. The fight for the enterprise AI budget has moved up a level, above the application itself, and this week's evidence says nobody has actually won that layer yet.

Start with OpenAI, because it just changed its own role in enterprise software. At its DevDay event at the end of September, OpenAI launched what it calls Marketplace. The launch roster has thirty-two partner vendors, spanning cybersecurity, customer experience, developer tools, legal tech and creative software. The names include Salesforce, ServiceNow, Adobe, CrowdStrike and Palo Alto Networks. The headline pitch is that OpenAI now sits between the enterprise's AI budget and the software vendors who want a piece of it.

The mechanics undercut the boldest version of that story. According to Channel Insider's reporting, the customer doesn't buy through OpenAI at all. The customer signs directly with the partner, and the partner sends its own invoice. OpenAI then credits that eligible spend against the customer's existing OpenAI commitment. So this isn't a new transaction engine. My read is that it's a tool to help big customers burn down AI spend they have already committed. That matters more for OpenAI's retention and utilization than for net new demand. And the terms are blank. As of September 30th, OpenAI had not published discount terms, a cap on how much commitment can be redirected, or how usage is metered. Enrollment mechanics are also described inconsistently across outlets, which looks like a program still in rollout. Treat Marketplace as an announced channel, not proven volume.

There is still a real strategic cost for the partners, even at this stage. On Wednesday we covered SAP's Joule Work, a work layer above SAP's own applications, which we read partly as SAP guarding against being reduced to a back-end database. Marketplace raises the same disintermediation risk, one level higher. A vendor listed inside OpenAI's commercial relationship gains a new route into AI budgets. In exchange, the model vendor gets to stand between that software vendor and its own customer. My read is that the thirty-two vendors accepted that trade easily because, for now, a listing costs them almost nothing. The real question is what it costs once money actually moves through the channel.

The same ambiguity shows up in OpenAI's other big move this week, an expanded partnership with Atlassian announced October 6th. OpenAI's own post says its frontier models will power agents across Atlassian's platform and Rovo, and it names GPT-6 Astra. An OpenAI representative, speaking on background to VentureBeat, called the arrangement "effectively a spend commitment." That means Atlassian is committing dollars to OpenAI, not the other way around. The same representative declined to share specifics. So there is no dollar figure, no term, and no stated exclusivity.

Atlassian's side tells a noticeably different story. Atlassian has said GPT-6 Astra won't be the default. Its own materials highlight its work with Anthropic as well as OpenAI. And Rovo runs an internal AI gateway that routes dynamically across OpenAI and other providers, balancing capability, speed and cost per task. Two companies are describing one deal with very different specificity. That gap is the finding. Treat "spend commitment" as OpenAI's characterization, not a jointly confirmed fact.

One callback for context. Yesterday we covered Atlassian's self-hosted Data Center exploit and that product line's 2029 end of life. Today's news is the cloud half of the same strategy: Rovo, tied to a model vendor's spend commitment while keeping its options open. A spend commitment without exclusivity isn't lock-in. It's buyer leverage dressed up as loyalty. My inference is that if more SaaS platforms copy Rovo's per-task router, model vendors lose pricing power at the platform level. Spend commitments then start to look like volume discounts rather than moats.

Rovo itself has real scale behind it. Atlassian's latest shareholder letter, dated August 6th, says over eighty percent of Fortune 500 companies now use Rovo. It also says Rovo adopters grow their recurring revenue more than twice as fast as non-adopters. That is production penetration, not a pilot story. But read the second number carefully. It's a relative comparison with no base disclosed, and faster-growing customers may simply be the ones that adopt first. Atlassian still discloses no standalone Rovo revenue. Its April letter said only that it continues to add "millions" of monthly active users, with no hard count. So usage at this scale still hasn't produced a disclosed monetization number.

If a model vendor wants to sit above the applications, the application incumbents are building their own top layer too. Salesforce just supplied the first real adoption numbers behind that race. Following Dreamforce, Salesforce introduced AIforce, which it positions as an interface layer above Agentforce and its data platforms. It also introduced Koa, its first CRM reasoning model. Press commentary notes that Salesforce names no benchmark or comparator behind Koa's three-times performance claim. Until it does, that claim is marketing, not evidence.

The useful number comes from a third party. An Oppenheimer survey found that around eighty-five percent of surveyed Salesforce customers have tested or deployed Agentforce. That sounds like a landslide. But nearly half of them are still testing or running pilots. Caveat that properly: it's one survey, and its sample size and respondent seniority weren't disclosed. Still, it's the first outside adoption read in a week where SAP and ServiceNow announced agent products with no production data at all. There is also a fair counter-reading. Long pilots are normal when a CRM workflow changes, and many of them may convert. The bear case is a stall, not a failure.

Salesforce also touts seven billion "Agentic Work Units" processed across Agentforce and Slack, three point two billion of them in the second quarter alone. That's a unit Salesforce defined itself. It has no standard definition, it can't be compared with anyone else's metric, and it has no mapping to revenue. It tells you activity is happening, not that it's being paid for. So the budget everyone claims to be fighting over is still, in large part, pilot money. The real test for Salesforce is Agentforce's contribution to growth, not trial counts.

There is one AI-adjacent budget line the sell-side insists will grow regardless, and that's security. Two of OpenAI's Marketplace partners, CrowdStrike and Palo Alto Networks, sit squarely inside it. Yesterday we called Tuesday's agentic-security accelerator, and the stock pop that came with it, narrative rather than fundamentals. This week Morgan Stanley wrote that narrative into a model. According to press summaries of the note, it forecasts corporate cybersecurity software spending growing twenty-three percent a year through 2028. That could accelerate to thirty-three percent if major cyberattacks spur new government mandates. Notice what drives the upside case. It's an outside shock plus regulation, not enterprise pull.

The number worth sitting with is a smaller one. Morgan Stanley estimates that only about one and a half percent of enterprise AI investment currently goes to AI security. Read that as headroom if you're bullish. Read it as evidence that buyers haven't yet decided agent security deserves its own line item if you're not. Remember too that the real attack this week, the Atlassian exploit, was a conventional file read on self-hosted software, not an agent compromise. And no vendor has yet reported a standalone AI-security revenue figure. Until one does, this is a valuation argument, not a demand signal.

Pull the three together and the picture is consistent. OpenAI wants to be the channel above the applications. Salesforce wants to own that layer itself. The security platforms want to be the toll on it. Buyer behavior says none of them has captured it yet: Rovo routes across models, nearly half of Agentforce customers are still piloting, and AI-security spend exists mainly in forecasts. Watch two things. First, any disclosed dollar size or take rate on OpenAI Marketplace or the Atlassian commitment. Second, whether Salesforce's next earnings call shows paid production customers rather than trial counts.

That's the briefing. Have a good one.
