Good morning. It's Sunday, August 16th, and this is your strategic power briefing.

This week we've been tracking the gap between announced power and delivered power. Thursday it was a forecast: only a fraction of the 1,066 gigawatts sitting in America's interconnection queues is likely to actually get built. Today that debate stopped being a forecast. PJM, the 13-state grid operator that runs the largest wholesale power market in the country, held its annual capacity auction on July 14th for the 2028-2029 delivery year. It procured 138,318 megawatts of capacity, clearing at $325 per megawatt-day, the price cap set by federal regulators. That's a $16.4 billion market. And it still wasn't enough. PJM's own release states the resources it procured fell short of its reliability requirement by 6,831 megawatts — the first time in the market's history the entire RTO has missed its reliability target. PJM's CEO put it plainly: demand for electricity is growing faster than electricity supply. The release describes data centers as a continued driver of that load growth, though PJM has not attached a specific megawatt figure to their contribution in this particular auction, so don't take any number you hear elsewhere as confirmed.

Now notice what that $325 cap is hiding. It isn't a market price. It's a ceiling federal regulators imposed to protect ratepayers. Strip the ceiling away and the scarcity underneath is obvious. PJM's own prior-year auction would have cleared near $530 a megawatt-day — roughly 60 percent higher — if that temporary cap hadn't been in place. So read today's result correctly. The headline isn't that prices came in a hair below last year's cap. The headline is that the market pressed right up against an artificial ceiling and still could not clear enough capacity to keep the lights on. Price told you this market was tight. The reliability miss tells you it actually broke.

Step back, because this is the moment the week's arc turned. All week this show has drawn one distinction: announced capacity versus delivered capacity. Thursday it was Wood Mackenzie's projection that most of that 1,066-gigawatt queue is phantom — a claim about what won't get built. Today is different in kind. This is not a forecast about the future. It's the capacity market itself, the mechanism designed specifically to procure enough power, coming up short in real time. The debate moved from what might not get built to what already didn't clear.

Here's the mechanism that follows. A shortfall this size triggers mandatory backstop procurement. PJM has asked federal regulators to approve a Reliability Backstop Procurement targeted to start September 30th. Under it, PJM can sign contracts for up to 15 years, at a maximum willingness to pay averaging $555 per megawatt-day — well above the auction cap, and the clearest signal of what firm capacity is really worth right now. And critically, PJM itself says the states, not the grid operator, decide how those backstop costs get allocated to utilities and end-use customers. That turns an abstract capacity shortfall into a live political fight across 13 state capitals over whether ordinary ratepayers or the data centers driving the load pay the backstop bill.

Follow the money from there. The beneficiaries are the independent power producers sitting on existing, dispatchable capacity — Constellation, Vistra, Talen, NRG — who get paid at cap-level or higher prices regardless of who built what. The backstop mechanics likely favor them further, since incumbent gas and nuclear plants have the fastest path to actually delivering megawatts on PJM's timeline. The losers are the data center developers, for whom power procurement now looks less like a utility bill and more like a scarce, biddable input to AI capex. That's exactly why hyperscalers keep chasing behind-the-meter and dedicated nuclear deals to bypass the interconnection queue entirely — the same routing this show described yesterday. And it sharpens a question the whole AI buildout has been dodging: if power is the binding constraint, then data-center siting starts migrating toward whichever grid operators can actually clear their queues, and toward states willing to let developers self-supply.

The supply-side answer to that domestic constraint is energy abundance, and the US has real, physical evidence of it. The Energy Information Administration now forecasts full-year 2026 LNG exports at a record 17 billion cubic feet a day, up from the prior record of about 15 set just last year — and driven by new export trains actually coming online, not permits on paper. This is the base layer of the whole story: the one strategic input the US has in genuine surplus, even as it runs short of grid capacity at home. And that same abundance is now doing double duty as a geopolitical weapon abroad — the base layer working at home and against Russia at once.

The Senate passed the Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act 86 to 11 on August 7th, granting the president discretionary authority to impose tariffs of up to 100 percent on countries still buying Russian oil and gas — aimed implicitly at China and India. Be precise about its status. This is Senate passage only. House action and a presidential signature haven't been confirmed, so treat it as a weapon now sitting on the shelf, not one that's been fired. And it lands as Russian exports have already collapsed on their own. Crude loadings fell to 4.7 million tonnes in July, the lowest on record and under half last July's volume. Diesel exports had been squeezed to 187,000 barrels a day by early July before Moscow redirected them to its domestic market. That decline is the work of existing sanctions and infrastructure attrition. It predates this bill, and the honest read is that the physical channel, not the new legislation, is what's doing the damage.

China holds the countervailing chokepoint. Its one-year suspension of last October's sweeping rare-earth export controls expires November 10th. That's now the next hard date on this show's geopolitical calendar, alongside the Lukoil license cliff and the Fed's Cook fight we flagged earlier this week. If Beijing lets the suspension lapse, the October regime snaps back on top of controls that were never suspended in the first place — China's April 2025 restrictions on seven heavy rare earths, including dysprosium and terbium, which have stayed in force throughout. And China has already shown it knows exactly where to press. In June it added MP Materials, the flagship US rare-earth producer central to the Pentagon's onshoring strategy, to its own export-control entity list. For defense primes and the magnet and motor supply chains feeding them, that's a renewed input-cost and availability risk arriving right into the fourth quarter.

Chip supply is diversifying against that same backdrop. TSMC announced on July 16th an additional $100 billion for its Arizona campus, lifting its total commitment there to $265 billion across ten fabs, two advanced-packaging facilities, and an R&D center — up from a $165 billion, six-fab plan, a $100 billion jump in a single announcement. The company and Arizona officials project it eventually reaching a fifth of world leading-edge output and 80,000 jobs; treat those as targets, not achieved capacity. But here's the loop back to today's lead. Those 2-nanometer fabs will draw power and water from the same constrained Southwest grid that hyperscaler data centers are already competing over. Diversifying chip production out of Taiwan solves one strategic vulnerability while running straight into the one we opened with. There's only so much reliable capacity to go around — and the market that's supposed to price it just failed, for the first time in its history, to clear enough.

That's your briefing. Have a good Sunday.
