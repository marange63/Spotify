Cautious Optimism Briefings

Strategic Power, Energy, and Institutional Change

Transcript · November 10, 2026

Good morning. It's Tuesday, November 10th, 2026, and this is your Strategic Power briefing.

This week both capitals finally put their levers on paper. Washington moved to make data centers pay for the grid they strain. Beijing signed a document saying how long it will hold back its rare-earth leverage. Both instruments turn out to be narrower and more conditional than the headlines suggest.

Start in Washington. Last night the Senate voted seventy-three to twenty-five to invoke cloture and begin debate on its permitting bill. Its data-center provisions rewrite who pays for the grid that AI load is straining. That vote cuts against our read from yesterday. We relayed TD Cowen's odds of roughly one in four on this bill. And we said FERC's own proceeding on how new load connects to the grid was the live federal lever instead. So we're qualifying yesterday's call, not erasing it. A vote to begin debate is not passage.

The path from here is long. The substitute text and the bill itself each still face their own cloture votes before final Senate passage. The vehicle is awkward, too. The Senate is carrying its permitting language on a House-passed tax bill, used as a shell. That means the House has not acted on this substantive text at all. House Democratic leadership has signaled it will not, in its words, "rubber-stamp legislation without more constructive involvement with our Caucus." And the White House isn't locked in either. On the day of the vote, President Trump said, "Well, the bill hasn't been fully negotiated." In the same exchange, he said, "We are behind it 100 percent." So treat last night as momentum, not a done deal.

Here's what the bill actually does, if it survives intact. The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission would get ninety days to rewrite transmission pricing, so data centers pay for their share of the grid. Data centers would have to post financial guarantees before a utility builds to serve them. That protects other customers against stranded assets if the load never shows up. Utilities would be barred from charging other customers the added cost of serving new data centers of twenty megawatts or more. And data centers that accept flexible or interruptible service would pay less. Senator Heinrich cited a Brattle Group estimate that the bill makes data centers pay about thirty-five billion dollars more over a decade than under current law. Attribute that number carefully. It comes via the bill's own advocate, not an independent score.

Now set that thirty-five billion against the problem it's answering. PJM's independent market monitor found that data centers drove twenty-nine point four billion dollars of capacity charges across the grid operator's last four base auctions. That's forty-six percent of the total. It's older analysis, from July, but it's the evidence behind the political pressure now showing up in this bill. Notice the time frames. The bill's figure is spread across ten years. The monitor's figure covers just four auctions in one market.

Then there's a category question worth sitting with. PJM's number is capacity charges, the price of keeping enough generation on call. The bill's FERC rewrite is about transmission pricing, which is a different cost line. The twenty-megawatt ban sounds broader. But nothing reported so far says whether it reaches charges set in PJM's capacity auction. So it's genuinely unclear whether this bill touches the largest documented data-center cost shift. If it doesn't, that fight comes back, at FERC or in a second bill.

Step back and look at the arc. Large-load cost allocation started as a voluntary corporate pledge, OpenAI's National Community Compact. It moved to campaign platforms, with governors-elect in Texas, Ohio and Georgia running on data-center moratoriums and pauses. Now it's a federal bill with seventy-three votes to start debate. What OpenAI offered voluntarily last week could become a federal floor.

And notice what the bill pairs with that cost. It exempts upgrades to interstate gas pipelines within existing corridors from NEPA review. This is not an anti-data-center bill. It loads shared-grid costs onto data centers while easing the path for gas supply. A plausible net effect is more developers self-supplying with gas, rather than building less. Pipeline operators and fast-deploy gas generation are relative winners. Independent power producers that live on PJM scarcity rents gain little from a transmission fix.

The guarantee requirement also draws a line between borrowers. Balance-sheet hyperscalers can post collateral easily. Debt-funded neoclouds are a different story. Our Capital Markets Radar desk reported Sunday that one CoreWeave bond now yields above sixteen percent. Collateral up front is more expensive for exactly those builders. So Congress is starting to write the price of local consent into law. Whether that price reaches the biggest cost on the table is still an open question.

Washington put its lever on paper last night. So, finally, did Beijing. China's commerce ministry and customs authority issued Announcement No. 58 of 2026. It keeps suspended the rare-earth export controls Beijing announced in October 2025. Those cover equipment, medium and heavy rare earths, overseas-origin items, and rare-earth technologies. The suspension now runs from November 11th through January 10th, 2027.

That resolves the decision tree we laid out on Sunday and Monday. Beijing didn't snap the controls back, and it didn't slide into case-by-case licensing. It took the extension branch. It also closes an asymmetry. On Sunday we said one government had a document and the other had only a word. Now both have documents.

But look at the length. The original pause followed the November 2025 Xi-Trump summit and ran close to a year, to November 10th. The renewal is two months. A shorter leash is not de-escalation. It's leverage that comes back up for review every two months instead of once a year.

And the dates now line up. As we reported Sunday, the Commerce Department is again suspending its own export-control measure, known as the Affiliates Rule. That pause also runs to January 10th. Per trade-compliance reporting we cited then, the rule could take effect in January if the two sides can't reach a permanent agreement. So both governments' concessions now expire together, and that date becomes a joint cliff rather than a one-sided one. For defense primes and EV-motor makers, that one date now sits inside their 2027 cost assumptions.

The near-term effect is relief. The extension removes an immediate supply shock for magnet-dependent sectors. That's also exactly when urgency drains out of the American and allied push to build rare-earth refining at home. No price or volume data came with this announcement, so don't read it as a market event. Read it as a reshoring risk, because announced capacity tends to stall the moment the threat recedes.

Both governments are rationing a shared system they depend on. In Washington's case it's the grid. In Beijing's, it's magnets. One Chinese AI campus shows a third way, which is to skip the shared system altogether. SemiAnalysis visited Envision's Galaxy campus in Inner Mongolia in September. The first phase, a hundred and twenty megawatts, was reportedly delivered in August, in under a year. The plan is up to eight hundred megawatts of IT capacity by the end of 2027. Envision aims to get more than sixty percent of the first two phases' power from its own wind farms, and it is building battery storage too.

Keep the caveats in view. Only the first phase is reported delivered. The eight hundred megawatts is a plan. The sixty percent is a target, not measured operating data. Wind in Inner Mongolia is intermittent, and the remainder still leans on the grid.

But the contrast is the point. In America, self-generation escapes the interconnection queue and then runs into a local ballot. This desk called the voter the third gatekeeper last week. In China, self-generation escapes both the queue and any equivalent public vote. This is one campus, not a national count of power additions. What it illustrates is a build path with fewer gates in Beijing's system right now. If America holds the stronger lever in this contest, it looks like chips rather than power. That's Washington's grip on where Nvidia's chips can go, which our semiconductors desk tracked all last week.

A short watch list. First, whether the Senate's permitting bill clears its next cloture votes, and whether the House engages with this text at all. Second, the Abu Dhabi talks between Russia and Ukraine, which open today and run through tomorrow, with no outcome yet to report. Third, January 10th, 2027, which now sits on two calendars at once: Beijing's rare-earth suspension and Washington's Affiliates Rule pause. Separately, October's CPI print lands this morning. We're not getting ahead of that number here.

That's the briefing. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
