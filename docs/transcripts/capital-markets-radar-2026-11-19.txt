Good morning. It's Thursday, November 19th, 2026, and this is Capital Markets Radar.

Here's the thesis for today. On Wednesday, the market absorbed a record $81.3 billion of brand-new AI equity, priced above its range. On the same day, it kept selling the AI equity it already owns. The S&P 500 closed lower for a fifth straight session. And the rates move that's supposed to explain the selling didn't intensify. The long end actually eased. So the discount-rate story this desk has leaned on all week is no longer sufficient on its own.

Start with the new supply. Anthropic priced its IPO at $83 a share. A standing disclosure, stated once: Anthropic makes the model behind this show. On Tuesday, this desk reported a marketed range of $70 to $78, for 825 million shares. That was a deal of more than $64 billion at the top of the range. By Wednesday evening, every term had moved higher, into a falling tape. Anthropic upsized to about 980 million shares and raised $81.3 billion. Per CNBC, that's the largest IPO on record, surpassing SpaceX's deal in June. CNBC puts the fully diluted market cap at around $1.5 trillion. That's a fully diluted number, not a float-adjusted one, and the gap matters for what comes next.

The strategic backing escalated too. On Tuesday, the anchor was Google's contingent option to buy up to $10 billion of stock. Now a group including Google, Microsoft, Nvidia and AMD has agreed to collectively invest $26 billion at the IPO price, per CNBC. What we have doesn't say how that relates to Google's earlier option. It also doesn't settle whether the $26 billion sits inside the $81.3 billion raise or on top of it. Secondary reports lean toward a separate placement on top. None we've seen is primary-sourced, so treat it as open. Either way, by our own arithmetic, the strategic commitment equals roughly a third of the headline raise. That's a large share of the demand coming from a handful of corporate partners.

And look at who those partners are. Nvidia and AMD are among Anthropic's compute suppliers. So part of this book is circular, with vendors financing a customer. That makes the clearing price a weaker read on independent institutional demand than the headline implies. The float compounds it. The $81.3 billion raise is about a twentieth of a $1.5 trillion company, again by our arithmetic. If the strategic tranche sits inside that figure, the free float is thinner still. Scarce float plus a strategic backstop can clear a price that a full float might not.

The counter-argument deserves equal weight, though. Pricing above range and upsizing during a five-day equity selloff is real evidence that capital for AI isn't rationed. Reading this book as an all-clear on AI valuations would be an over-read. Dismissing it as purely circular would be an under-read. Trading begins today under the ticker ANT. No first-day price is known yet, so the debut is the next test, not this one.

Now the other side of that same day. Yesterday, this desk had Nvidia's shares higher after hours on its earnings beat. We said the verdict on the rest of the chip group was still pending. Wednesday's regular session answered, and not in Nvidia's favor. Nvidia closed down 2.89 percent, at its lowest close since June of last year, per a closing market wrap. That came after a quarter with data-center revenue up 110 percent from a year ago. The S&P 500, by contrast, slipped less than a quarter of a percent.

That gap is the point. By our arithmetic, Nvidia fell more than ten times as much as the index. A broad discount-rate shock should press on the whole market. This one landed hardest on the AI leader. So a beat that size failing to hold is the secondary-market half of today's divergence. The market bought $81 billion of new AI stock and sold the old AI trade on the same day.

So is it still the discount rate doing this? That's the claim this desk has made all week, and this week's rates data says it isn't enough on its own. Start with the front end. CME FedWatch, as reported by 24/7 Wall St, put the odds of a quarter-point December hike at about 96 percent as of Wednesday. The rest sits on a half point. That resolves yesterday's open question. We said then that our source didn't tell us whether the leftover five percent was hold odds or the half-point tail. It's the tail.

And that tail has shrunk. Late last week, a different cut of FedWatch, via CNBC, put roughly one-in-seven odds on a half point. Now it's closer to one in twenty-five. The two cuts aren't perfectly comparable, but the direction is clear. The distribution is collapsing onto a quarter point. That's not the market turning more hawkish. A hike was already effectively fully priced last week. And this is still market pricing, not an outcome.

The long end tells the same story. Per CNBC, the 10-year Treasury yield fell almost 4 basis points on Wednesday, to about 5.06 percent. On Tuesday, this desk had it holding around 5.15 percent through Monday's selloff. The 2-year, meanwhile, rose just over a basis point. CNBC tied the long-end move to Fed Governor Christopher Waller, who called for aggressive action against inflation, and to the Fed's minutes. So a louder hawkish signal flattened the curve.

Our read is that this is the classic flattener. The long end can read a hawkish Fed as credibility on inflation, or as future damage to growth. Either way, the yield that matters most for long-duration AI multiples fell on Wednesday. And AI stocks fell anyway. A few basis points is not a duration bid, so the claim here is narrow. The rates impulse did not intensify, and the selling did.

That leaves three competing explanations for buying the new AI stock while selling the old. I'll name all three rather than crown a winner. The first is supply absorption. Institutional desks may be funding their Anthropic allocations by trimming the AI names they already hold. The second is plain de-grossing, a positioning unwind after a crowded run, independent of any one company's fundamentals. The third is genuine valuation doubt about AI capex exposure broadly. A scarce-float, strategically anchored IPO can sidestep that doubt in a way an index heavyweight can't.

We don't have the flow data to prove any of them. But each one makes a different prediction. If it's supply absorption, pressure on the incumbents should ease once allocations settle and the debut trades. If it's de-grossing, the selling should hit crowded winners whether or not they're AI names. If it's valuation doubt, the debut itself should struggle once the strategic backstop is no longer the marginal buyer. So the next few sessions can start to separate them.

Now the second-order effects, and there are three worth tracking. First, private marks. The $83 print, roughly $1.5 trillion fully diluted, is now the public comp for every private frontier-lab valuation. If today's debut trades below issue, that comp moves against every fund marking a stake in a rival lab.

Second, the supply overhang. The scarcity that helped clear this price is temporary. Roughly nineteen-twentieths of the company isn't in the float yet, on our arithmetic. As lock-ups expire, that float expands, and the market will have to absorb far more AI supply than it did on Wednesday.

Third, the vendor-financing loop, and this is our inference. When compute suppliers like Nvidia and AMD invest at a customer's IPO price, part of their future data-center revenue is effectively recycled from their own balance sheets. That's worth remembering the next time Nvidia reports what looks like purely external demand.

Put those together and the risk in AI exposure looks like it's migrating. It's moving away from duration and toward supply and positioning. If that's right, the signals to watch are flows and issuance, not FedWatch.

On the calendar, without assuming any outcomes. Anthropic's first trade happens today. Bids in the ING data-center loan auction are also due today. That's the last of the three AI-risk tests this desk has flagged since last week, and the only one priced in credit rather than equity. After that comes the PCE inflation report on November 25th, the jobs report on December 4th, and the Fed's decision on December 9th. November's CPI doesn't print until December 10th, the day after the Fed decides.
