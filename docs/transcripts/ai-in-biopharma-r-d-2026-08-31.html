Cautious Optimism Briefings

AI in Biopharma R&D

Transcript · August 31, 2026

Good morning. It's August 31st, and this is your briefing on artificial intelligence across the biopharma value chain.

Start from the two facts that frame everything else. No AI-designed medicine has been approved by any regulator in any major market. And no layer of the stack has yet moved the failure rate that actually costs money, which is Phase II. Today the first AI-native drug company to turn a real profit reported its numbers. The shape of how it made that money is the story, and it does not break that pattern so much as explain it.

Insilico Medicine's interim results, released August 26th, show about 106 million dollars of revenue for the first half of 2026. That is nearly four times last year, at a ninety percent gross margin, with a real net profit and positive operating cash flow. For a sector whose defining characteristic has been burning cash against a distant option, that is genuinely new. One note on the number itself. These are exchange-filed interim results, which is a better class of evidence than a vendor slide or a conference poster, but they are not audited full-year accounts.

Now read what sits underneath the top line. The company discloses two segments. Drug discovery and pipeline development brought in 103 million dollars. Software brought in under three million. So essentially all of it is licensing and milestone income, and the named driver is the March out-licensing and research collaboration with Eli Lilly, valued at up to 2.75 billion dollars with 115 million paid upfront. A ninety percent gross margin on a revenue line dominated by licensing is not a pharma margin. It is a platform-optionality margin. Partners are paying Insilico for the right to develop AI-derived assets. Nobody is paying for drugs that are selling, because there aren't any.

The clinical half of the release matters just as much. Rentosertib, Insilico's AI-designed TNIK inhibitor for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, has formally moved into a large Phase III trial in China. The design is 320 patients. The primary endpoint is the annual rate of decline in forced vital capacity over 52 weeks, with time to first disease-progression event as the key secondary. This beat flagged that program on August 27th as a pending catalyst and told you to treat it as nothing more until it arrived. Four days later the company has stepped past its Phase II readout and straight into a pivotal trial.

And rentosertib is not entering Phase III on company claims alone. Its Phase 2a proof-of-concept data, from the GENESIS-IPF trial, was published in Nature Medicine in June of 2025, more than a year before this Phase III start. In 71 patients in China, the 60-milligram dose produced a mean lung-function gain of 98.4 milliliters in forced vital capacity, against a mean decline of 20.3 milliliters on placebo. That is a peer-reviewed efficacy signal, not a marketing claim, and it is the single genuine crack in the claim I opened with. Two limits on it. It is a Phase 2a signal, not an approval. And the published account gives the mean changes without a p-value for that comparison, so treat it as directionally strong rather than statistically pinned down.

The rest of the caveat is narrower than the field's usual complaint, but it is not small. This is a China-only Phase III, which is faster and cheaper to run, and a positive result there does not by itself open a US or European registration path. It also relocates the evidence base. The one company earning real money on AI drug discovery is Hong Kong-listed and running its pivotal trial in China, which means the regulator most likely to rule first on an AI-originated medicine may not be the FDA. That matters for how the sector gets priced, because a Chinese approval is a weaker read-across to US pipeline economics than the headline suggests. Separately, garutadustat, Insilico's inflammatory bowel disease candidate, dosed its first Phase IIa patient in the same window. Early, but forward motion on a second program.

So if partners are paying real money for de-risked early assets, the question becomes what that de-risking is worth across the whole field. That is where a cross-industry dataset presented at ASCO becomes the analytical engine of today's briefing. Across 117 tracked AI-enabled clinical assets, AI-designed candidates are clearing Phase I at 80 to 90 percent. The historical industry range is 40 to 65 percent. That is a large jump, and the plausible mechanism is better tolerability and pharmacokinetic triage before a molecule ever reaches a patient. But that mechanism is an inference, not something the data establishes. Phase II is a different story. Success there sits at roughly 40 percent, statistically indistinguishable from the baseline the industry has lived with for decades.

Three caveats belong here. First, this comes secondhand, from a write-up of an ASCO presentation rather than the underlying analysis, so treat it as a directional number. Second, resist the flattering explanation for the Phase I jump. AI-native companies are small and capital-constrained, so they tend to pick tractable targets, well-characterized chemotypes, and permissive first-in-human designs. A higher Phase I pass rate may be measuring what they chose to run as much as what the models produced. Third, hold the definition in view. AI-enabled spans any machine-learning touchpoint anywhere in a program, so 117 is not a census, and competing counts circulate for exactly that reason. Put it plainly: AI is buying down the cheap kind of failure. It has not yet touched the expensive kind.

There is a structural reason that gap will close slowly, if it closes at all. Of those 117 assets, only 8 have cleared Phase II at all. The population capable of generating the missing efficacy evidence is tiny, and it arrives one readout at a time, over years. Rentosertib's Phase III is one of a handful of live tests that can convert platform optionality into proof. Meanwhile there is a cheaper, faster signal nobody has published. If the Phase I effect is real and durable, it should show up as lower per-program IND-enabling spend at AI-native platforms than at conventional peers. That is a testable, capital-markets-visible number, and its absence is conspicuous. Demand it on the next earnings call.

That raises the obvious question. If the models are not moving Phase II, what is stopping them? A 2026 survey of pharma and biotech technology leaders found 68 percent cite poor data quality and governance, not model capability, as the reason AI initiatives stall. That is the mechanism behind everything above it. And the industry's answer to that constraint is architectural rather than legal. The workaround is federated: the model goes to the records instead of the records going to a central store, with schemas harmonized on standards like FHIR and governance built around HIPAA expert-determination rules. HIPAA has not been carved out. It has been designed around.

The ownership of that access is consolidating. Roche now owns both Flatiron Health and TriNetX, which gives it a privileged position on curated real-world oncology data and a federated multi-site network at the same time. Smaller biotechs and academic labs cannot buy their way around that. Say the missing number plainly, though. Nobody has quantified how much any of this raises the odds of clinical success or lowers R&D cost. That causal claim is vendor-asserted, not demonstrated.

One more note from the model layer, because it cuts against the intuition. John Jumper, who shared the 2024 Nobel Prize for AlphaFold, left Google DeepMind for Anthropic in June, and reporting since indicates much of the original AlphaFold team has been reassigned. So the frontier structure layer is getting less open, not more, which matters less than it sounds. If 68 percent of the industry is stalled on data governance, the binding scarcity is access to records, not access to weights.

Pull the threads together. The one AI-native company making real money on drug discovery earns it at licensing margins on early-stage optionality, and rentosertib's Phase III, grounded in a genuine peer-reviewed Phase 2a signal, is one of the only live tests that can turn that optionality into proof. The cohort data says AI has solved the cheap, early kind of failure and left the expensive, efficacy-defining kind untouched. And the binding constraint on closing that gap is data governance, not frontier model capability, which is exactly what Roche is paying to own. For R&D spend broadly, that still points to reallocation toward earlier, cheaper failure rather than an outright reduction. Watch two things next: rentosertib's Phase III readout, and anyone willing to publish per-program IND-enabling spend that shows the Phase I effect in actual dollars.

That's the briefing for August 31st. Talk to you tomorrow.
