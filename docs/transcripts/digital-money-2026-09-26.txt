Good morning, it's Saturday, September 26th, and this is your digital money briefing.

Yesterday this desk asked whether the shrinking bitcoin ETF inflow streak would flip to outflows or steady. The answer is that it steadied, and kept shrinking. US spot bitcoin ETFs took in $134.5 million on Friday, according to aggregator data relayed by KuCoin. That made seven straight sessions above $100 million, for a cumulative $2.98 billion. But Friday's print was the smallest of the seven. So the bid persisted through a real-yield shock and a Fed still guiding toward another hike. It just persisted at declining intensity. Call it neither a breakdown nor a re-acceleration.

The more important number is the year-to-date baseline, because it reframes the whole week. In mid-July, these same funds were down $5.8 billion for the year. As of Friday, CoinDesk has them back to nearly $800 million of net inflows. That reads like institutions piling back in. The scale says otherwise. Full-year inflows were $35.2 billion two years ago, and $21.4 billion last year.

Now run the arithmetic on this year. The year-to-date total is under four percent of last year's full-year figure. And strip out the last seven sessions, and 2026 would still be roughly $2.2 billion in the red. So this year's institutional story is a round trip, not a new wave. Anyone citing this week's streak as proof of fresh demand is measuring against the wrong baseline.

There's a fragility point layered on top. Nearly $4 billion of the recovery has arrived since a single Treasury announcement in August, per CoinDesk. A bid anchored to one policy signal is more fragile than a broad allocator bid. It can unwind as fast as the narrative that built it. Breadth inside the streak looks thin as well. On Thursday, BlackRock's IBIT took about 85 percent of the day's net inflow, with Fidelity's FBTC a distant second. That is one session, and no issuer breakdown is sourced for the other days. But at least part of this bid is a single-issuer phenomenon, not diversified allocator demand.

That has a business read-through. Issuer asset and fee models built on the pace of the last two years will undershoot if this year's bid is a refill rather than a new leg. Asset managers' crypto revenue lines are exposed to that gap. Now the bull case, stated plainly. Seven sessions above $100 million, straight into a real-yield shock, is a genuine persistence signal. The bid didn't break under pressure. It just isn't broad, and it isn't big by the standard this market set for itself.

Ether ETFs are telling a parallel but smaller story. They logged a sixth straight day of inflows on Friday, taking in $86.9 million, led by BlackRock's ETHA and Bitwise's ETHB. No cumulative total for that run is sourced, so bank the streak length rather than a size. The read is the same as bitcoin's: persistence, with no evidence yet of scale.

So if the flow data is weaker than the headlines, where is the strongest evidence for a floor? It's on-chain, not in the ETF tape. Glassnode's latest weekly report found that bitcoin's June low held above its realized price. None of the bear markets on Glassnode's chart since 2017 managed that. Net unrealized profit and loss, across all coins, never turned negative this cycle either. As of that report, bitcoin traded above Glassnode's True Market Mean of $77,000, and above the short-term holder cost basis.

Here is the part that connects to this desk's own arc. At the start of this week, we called the long-term holder band in the low-to-mid eighty thousands the overhead supply that any breakout had to clear. Glassnode now puts the largest long-term holder cluster at $84,000 to $85,000, just below price. Its read is that the band has flipped from resistance to support.

Treat that with care, for three reasons. First, Glassnode's report predates part of this week's trading. Wednesday's intraday low of $83,500, which this desk covered yesterday, dipped briefly under that band. CoinDesk's Friday piece has bitcoin back around $85,000. So the band has had one intraday test, not a sustained one. Second, "resistance flipped to support" is Glassnode's interpretation of positioning. It isn't proven until price holds through a real test. Third, a realized price that held may partly reflect a cost basis lifted by ETF-era buying, not stronger conviction among holders.

Still, the tension is worth stating plainly rather than resolving. On-chain holder behavior looks structurally firm. The marginal ETF flow looks historically weak. So who is the marginal buyer this cycle, if it isn't the ETF allocator at the old scale? That stays an open question. And the bear setup follows directly. If the $84,000 cluster fails as support, the overhead-supply problem returns, this time paired with a thinning ETF bid. The takeaway is that the floor looks firmer than the bid, and that band is now the line in the sand.

The same gap between headline and substance shows up in the dollar layer. Stablecoin supply on Solana hit a record $17.3 billion on Friday. That's about $600 million above its August high, according to a Solana-ecosystem outlet relaying SolanaFloor and DefiLlama counts. Look closer, and it's mostly one event. Supply rose $1.26 billion in the preceding twenty-four hours, as Circle and Tether minted fresh USDC and USDT directly on Solana. That implies Solana's supply was roughly $16 billion just before the mint. In other words, it was sitting below its August record the day before. So the "all-time high" is largely a single day's issuance, not sustained accumulation. The purpose of the mint is undisclosed. And minting isn't circulating demand. Fresh supply is often pre-positioned inventory that can be redeemed within days, and there's no follow-on redemption data yet.

There's a venue wrinkle too. On Solana, Circle's USDC holds 72.3 percent of stablecoin supply, against 15.7 percent for Tether's USDT. That's a Solana venue story. It says nothing about USDC displacing USDT globally. Scale matters as well. Solana is still only the third-largest stablecoin network, and Ethereum alone holds $145.9 billion, more than eight times as much.

That brings us to the question the record actually raises. Earlier this week, this desk described the aggregate stablecoin base as flat. So is stablecoin supply growing in aggregate, or just moving between chains? The distinction matters for Treasury-bill demand from issuer reserves. Genuine aggregate growth adds to that bid. Redistribution across chains adds nothing. A chain-level record can't tell you which one you're looking at.

Put the three legs together, and the pattern rhymes. In each case, the headline reads bigger than the evidence underneath it. The ETF bid is real, but it's refilling a hole, not building something new. The strongest floor evidence is on-chain, not in the flows investors are watching. And a stablecoin record turns out to be one day's minting.

One housekeeping note. Friday's quarterly options expiry, flagged here all week, has now passed. But no verified settlement snapshot was available in the sources this desk checked, so we're not describing how it went.

Three things to watch. First, whether the ETF streak holds above $100 million or finally breaks. Second, whether bitcoin holds the $84,000 to $85,000 band through a real test. Third, whether Solana's new float sticks around or gets redeemed within days.

That's the digital money briefing. Have a good weekend.
