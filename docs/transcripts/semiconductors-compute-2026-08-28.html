Cautious Optimism Briefings

Semiconductors & Compute Infrastructure

Transcript · August 28, 2026

Good morning. It's August 28th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

Yesterday this show covered NVIDIA's print in full — the revenue, the data-center number, the guidance. The open question we left on the table was value capture. Demand is clearly still accelerating. Who keeps the money from this buildout was not resolved. Today the call detail underneath that print starts answering a different and more interesting question. Not how much is being bought, but who can actually ship, and at what cost.

Start with Vera Rubin. NVIDIA said on the call that it commenced production shipments of Vera Rubin earlier this month, and called it the fastest product ramp in company history. That matters because of what it isn't. It isn't a roadmap slide. On this show two days ago, we noted SemiAnalysis's point that Rubin, not Blackwell, was the fair architectural comparison for OpenAI's Jalapeno chip. We also noted that Jalapeno was reportedly still at engineering-sample stage while claiming an efficiency win. That gap has now resolved on NVIDIA's own record. Rubin is in production. Jalapeno, as far as anyone has shown, is not.

Be precise about what NVIDIA actually disclosed, though. Production shipments have commenced. That is not the same as volume shipped, and it is not the same as revenue recognized. The company put no unit count, no dollar contribution, and no share of the coming quarter behind the Rubin ramp. Purchase orders from every major hyperscaler is a company assertion with no volume or timing attached to it.

NVIDIA also put a number on the efficiency gap itself. It said Vera Rubin delivers thirty times higher throughput per megawatt and three-and-a-half times lower token costs than Grace Blackwell Ultra, its own predecessor. Treat that carefully. It is NVIDIA's own figure, from an earnings call, with no stated workload, no configuration detail, and no independent benchmark behind it. That is the same self-published structure as OpenAI's one-and-a-half to one-point-nine times claim for Jalapeno, which we covered on Wednesday. A thirty-times gain in one generation almost certainly bundles software and precision-format changes along with the silicon. It is not a clean hardware result, so don't launder either number into physics. What separates these two companies right now isn't whose number is more believable. Neither is audited. It's that one of them is shipping and one of them isn't. Shipping is the evidence that outranks the benchmark.

There's a quieter line in the same results worth pulling out. NVIDIA's networking business grew eighteen percent sequentially, with Spectrum-X Ethernet up two-point-six times year over year. Since Monday, this show has been reading hyperscaler custom-silicon wins — Marvell's warrant tied to Google's TPU ecosystem in particular — as erosion of NVIDIA's position at the rack level. The networking number complicates that story. Hyperscalers buying custom accelerators, and even custom networking silicon from vendors like Marvell, are apparently still buying NVIDIA's switching fabric in volume. The accelerator socket is genuinely contestable. The fabric attaching everything to it is proving a lot stickier. That's a distinction worth holding onto the next time a custom-ASIC design win gets read as a straight loss for NVIDIA. It may cost the company a socket without costing it the network.

Now the cost side, which NVIDIA cannot design its way out of. On the call, CFO Colette Kress named the cause of the company's own gross-margin compression directly. It is rising memory component costs. Gross margin was seventy-five percent this quarter, and NVIDIA guided the current quarter to seventy-four. Both of those come straight from the company's release. Secondary reporting of the call has margin troughing a couple of points below that before recovering in fiscal 2028 — treat that trough as call reporting, not a primary disclosure. Kress said the magnitude of the memory price increase has already exceeded the company's prior expectations. She said it is headed higher into next year. And she framed the scarcity as a byproduct of the AI buildout itself. That is NVIDIA's own demand feeding back into NVIDIA's own cost base.

That statement lines up with a harder number from the memory market. TrendForce puts HBM3e contract pricing up roughly twenty percent quarter over quarter, as SK hynix, Samsung, and Micron allocate 2026 capacity against already-booked accelerator demand. Hold two things at once here. A genuine per-megawatt efficiency gain, if it is directionally real, means fewer memory parts per unit of useful work. That favors the buyer. A supplier-set contract increase, landing while those same three suppliers are adding capacity, looks more like allocation power than intensifying physical scarcity. That favors the supplier, not the chip designer. NVIDIA can improve throughput per part. It cannot design away what three memory makers charge per part. And the companies that feel this hardest aren't NVIDIA, which can absorb compression from a seventy-five-point base. It's the accelerator vendors without contracted memory supply lined up — which is every entrant, including the lab-designed ASICs.

That's the input-cost side of the buildout. The tooling side has its own story, and it's a geography story. Applied Materials reported record results this week: revenue of nine-point-one billion dollars, up twenty-five percent year over year, with record non-GAAP earnings per share, both ahead of consensus. The stock sold off anyway. The reason was mix. China accounted for about twenty-eight percent of Applied Materials' total sales in the quarter, down from roughly thirty-five percent a year earlier.

Separate two questions there. Is total wafer-fab-equipment demand rising? Yes. Lam Research has raised its calendar-2026 spending outlook for the whole industry to the low one-hundred-fifty-billion-dollar range, up from a prior one hundred forty billion. That is a company forecast of its own end market, not orders in hand, but the direction is clear. The access question is different: who is allowed to buy that equipment. Be careful with the causation, though. The link between China's declining share and any specific new export-control rule is reporting inference, not confirmed regulatory text. And that twenty-eight percent is a share of a denominator that grew twenty-five percent, so China revenue did not fall as fast as the share drop implies. The honest read is that a beat can still get sold off when the market is pricing access rather than aggregate demand.

Last stop: who else can actually manufacture at the leading edge, because that's the constraint underneath all of this equipment spending. Yesterday this show argued that a credible second leading-edge source changes foundry pricing power before it changes market share, using Samsung's roughly sixty percent yield on two-nanometer. Intel Foundry is the other candidate. Its second-quarter numbers, reported back on August 11th, separate the two things cleanly. The foundry operating loss narrowed to two-point-one billion dollars from three-point-two billion a year earlier, on genuinely improving yields and cycle times as 18A ramps. That's manufacturing execution getting better. But external, third-party foundry and assembly-test revenue was two hundred ninety-three million dollars for the quarter. That is a rounding error next to the loss it is supposed to offset. Manufacturing execution and customer traction are two different competitive dimensions, and Intel Foundry is improving on one while essentially flat on the other. Intel's own language is that it has increasing momentum on customer engagements; the characterization that no external customer is committed at volume is a secondary read, not Intel's. Either way, 14A, the node that would actually attract a marquee external customer, is still targeted for 2028. On this evidence, a real second source to TSMC is a 2028 question, not a 2026 one — which extends TSMC's pricing leverage further than the 18A headlines suggest.

So here's where that leaves the buildout. The seller's own efficiency numbers, from NVIDIA and from OpenAI alike, remain self-published and unaudited. What's real is the shipment. And the numbers that will actually decide who keeps the money from all this aren't in either company's benchmark slide. They're in memory contract prices, in equipment-maker revenue geography, and in a foundry customer list that's still nearly empty. All of that rests today on one earnings call's vendor claims, one contract-price tracker, and one three-week-old foundry segment report. Worth remembering as more of it gets confirmed, or doesn't, in the quarters ahead.

That's the briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
