Good morning, it's Sunday, August 2nd, and this is your capital markets radar.

For a week this show has traced the AI-capex verdict as it moved through the market's plumbing: first equity multiples, then earnings monetization. Today it reaches the debt that funds the build, and the picture there is not comfortable.

Start with the divergence. High-yield spreads closed July 30th at 284 basis points over Treasuries, according to Convex's tracking of the option-adjusted spread — that puts junk debt in the richest decile of its own history against a roughly 450-basis-point long-run median. Investment-grade spreads tell the same story from the other end: 80 basis points as of that same July 30th close. Put together, public credit is charging almost nothing for default risk right now. Private credit disagrees. Fitch reported private-credit defaults hit a record 6% last quarter, up from 5.7% the quarter before, with industrials borrowers defaulting at 10.4%. Take the 6% as a headline read — the exact cohort behind it isn't broken out — but the direction is unambiguous. That is not noise sitting on top of a healthy market. That is a fundamentals gap public spreads are simply not pricing.

The clearest evidence the gap is now showing up specifically in AI-infrastructure financing is a single loan. CoreWeave went to raise $2.6 billion tied to its Anthropic computing buildout, and had to sweeten the terms twice over. The spread widened by as much as 125 basis points, to SOFR plus 5.5 percentage points, dated July 29th. And the original issue discount — the price lenders actually pay for the debt — widened too, from 99 cents on the dollar to 97 cents. Two separate concessions on one deal, both moving the same direction, both dated the same week. So the capex-quality debate that started in equity multiples has now reached the cost of capital funding the compute build itself.

Treat that as one marquee deal, not yet a market-wide trend — that's the honest caveat. But the second-order read matters regardless: if lenders are demanding more yield and a steeper discount to fund AI datacenter capacity, the effective cost of capital for the entire buildout thesis is rising at the margin, even as equity investors keep rewarding the same capex story. That's the tension to watch heading into the next round of hyperscaler earnings — the market that actually prices default risk on this debt is charging more than it was two weeks ago, while the market that prices growth keeps paying up.

There's a catch-up risk sitting underneath this too. If private-credit defaults keep climbing while public high-yield stays parked near historic tights, that gap doesn't close quietly — it closes with a repricing shock, most likely triggered by a marquee business-development company posting a marked NAV writedown or getting forced to sell into an illiquid market. Blue Owl is already showing the strain on the funding side, not the default side: its second-quarter fundraising fell to $7.6 billion, down from $12.1 billion a year earlier, even as redemption pressure eases. And the pricing pressure looks US-specific. European private loans are now reportedly pricing about 4 basis points tighter than US loans — a reversal from a historical gap where US paper priced some 22 basis points tighter. That's a single-source read, so hold it loosely, but it points the same way: the stress is concentrated in US direct-lending books, not the asset class everywhere. Slower capital formation into a market that's supposed to be absorbing more AI-infrastructure lending, not less, is worth watching as a leading indicator.

Now, why is private credit cracking now specifically. The mechanism is the long end of the Treasury curve, and this part of the story you've heard from this desk all week, so treat it as backdrop, not news. The Fed held its target range at 3.5 to 3.75% on July 29th, but three regional presidents — Cleveland's Beth Hammack, Minneapolis's Neel Kashkari, and Dallas's Lorie Logan — dissented in favor of a hike, the first unified-direction three-way dissent since September 2016. Chair Kevin Warsh stripped the statement of the forward-guidance language markets had gotten used to under his predecessor. The 30-year Treasury yield has kept backing up since, printing a fresh 19-year high. It's the same real-rate gravity pulling on gold, which now sits about 28% below its January record even with the Fed hawkish — real yields, not the old inflation-hedge story, are setting that price. That's the through-line: floating-rate private credit and freshly-priced AI-infrastructure loans get repriced against a long end that keeps ratcheting higher, with no forward guidance from the Fed to anchor expectations either way. The hawkish read here rests on three dissents and a shorter statement, not a dot plot — the market may be over-reading a one-meeting reaction into a durable policy shift, so hold that loosely.

One of the inputs keeping that long end elevated is oil. Brent crude was trading near $86 a barrel on July 31st, still tracking a monthly gain of more than 20% for July, on renewed fighting between the US and Iran that's raising fresh doubts about supply through the Strait of Hormuz. That resurgence in oil is being cited directly as a driver of the Treasury selloff, feeding straight into inflation expectations. The premium is easing off its peak, but it's still live, and it's still one of the reasons the Fed's inflation language isn't softening as fast as growth data might otherwise suggest.

Switching to crypto for a moment, where the flow data is telling a differentiation story rather than a single-asset-class story. US spot Bitcoin ETFs are on track for their weakest monthly inflow ever, roughly $205 million in July, a barely-there recovery after $2.43 billion left in May and $4.52 billion left in June. Ethereum ETFs had the opposite month: $365 million of July inflows, their strongest of the year, led by BlackRock's product. Don't read that as confirmed rotation out of Bitcoin into Ethereum specifically — no source establishes that causally, it could just as easily be a broader pullback from crypto beta during a volatile bond market. What it does suggest is that institutional allocators are starting to treat digital assets as distinct exposures rather than one basket, which matters for how desks size crypto risk going forward. On stablecoins, briefly: the market has grown past $310 billion under the GENIUS Act's backing requirements, a structural rather than cyclical tailwind for short-dated Treasury bill demand, since compliant issuers have to hold Treasuries against their float.

Last, the forward catalyst. The whole rate-path debate pivots on the next labor print. June's confirmed number was weak — payrolls up just 57,000, unemployment at 4.2%. The July report is pending, and sources disagree on both its release date and its likely level, so treat any specific July figure you see this week as unconfirmed until it's actually printed. The stakes are straightforward either way: a strong number validates the Fed's hawkish dissenters and pushes the long end higher still, tightening the vise on exactly the private-credit and AI-infrastructure borrowers already conceding pricing today. A weak number reopens the case for a cut and could unwind a good chunk of this month's Treasury selloff in a hurry.

That's the radar for August 2nd. Watch the spread between public and private credit — it's the gap this show will be tracking all week. Have a good one.
