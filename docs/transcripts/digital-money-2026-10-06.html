Digital Money, Crypto, and Market Structure

Transcript · October 6, 2026

Good morning, and welcome to Cautious Optimism Briefings for Tuesday, October 6th, 2026.

The lead today is Bitcoin, and the question this desk has carried since late September: who is actually the marginal buyer? This week's answer is not the one the narrative prefers.

Start with price. Bitcoin traded at about 86,200 dollars when Cointelegraph published on October 5th, up 3.7 percent over seven days. FXStreet has it heading into a fourth straight week of gains. That puts it above the 84 to 85 thousand dollar band where Glassnode places the heaviest cluster of long-term-holder supply. That band capped price through quarter-end and into last Friday.

One caveat. The 86,200 figure is a publication-time quote, not a daily close. So Bitcoin is trading above the band, but a reclaim on a closing basis is still unconfirmed.

Now the ETF tape, because that is where the narrative breaks. Cointelegraph reports that spot Bitcoin ETFs took in 241.1 million dollars last week, a third straight inflow week. But September as a whole brought 2.65 billion dollars of net inflows, according to FXStreet. So last week's bid ran at well under half of September's weekly pace, even as price climbed.

Compare that with the pattern we tracked through October 1st. Then, flows chased price. They were biggest on the squeeze, faded as price stalled, and flipped negative at quarter-end. This week price rose without the flows. The ETF allocator was not the marginal buyer.

The longer view makes the same point. Cointelegraph puts year-to-date net inflows at only around 1.2 billion dollars. That is less than September alone. Our arithmetic, then, is that the first eight months of the year were net outflows, and September's buying mostly filled that hole. So 2026 ETF demand is marginal, and any story built on a standing ETF bid is overstating it.

There is also a selectivity signal. Ether ETFs recorded 138 million dollars of weekly net outflows, after taking in 690 million the week before. That is selectivity inside crypto, not broad appetite for the asset class.

So if not the ETFs, who? Two pieces of evidence, both thin. First, leverage. Glassnode's weekly report, relayed by CryptoSlate, shows futures open interest falling from 38 billion dollars to 36.6 billion. Price rose over the same stretch, so our inference is that the drop in coin terms was larger still.

That grades the warning we gave on October 2nd. Then, shrinking open interest alongside rising long funding looked like a one-sided book at risk of a forced unwind. Instead, open interest kept falling while price rose. That is de-leveraging into strength, not a liquidation cascade.

Second, spot buying. Glassnode's spot cumulative volume delta swung from negative 102.8 million dollars to positive 33.2 million. That is the right direction. But a swing of about 136 million dollars is small next to the 1.4 billion that came out of open interest. Call it a modest improvement in net spot buying, not a large spot bid.

FXStreet also reports whale holdings up 75,000 Bitcoin over 30 days. Treat that as suggestive, because custodial reshuffles, including at ETF custodians, can look like whale buying.

Here is the bull case, stated fairly. Positioning is the cleanest it has been this quarter, and Bitcoin climbed while leverage came out. Rallies built that way tend to be more durable. Now the bear case. A de-levered rally on thin ETF demand can simply be a short-covering drift with no new sponsor. Lower leverage cuts cascade risk in both directions, and it also removes the fuel for a breakout.

One cross-asset note. Our Capital Markets Radar desk saw the ten-year yield rise even on Friday's weak payrolls, and read that as term premium, not the Fed path, driving long rates. Rising long yields should weigh on Bitcoin, and this week they did not. September CPI on October 14th and the FOMC on October 28th are the next tests of whether rates cap this move. Neither has happened yet.

So the so-what on Bitcoin. Price is up, leverage is down, and the ETF bid is small. The next leg needs either ETF demand to return or the non-ETF spot buyer to persist.

That same question, who is actually paying, shows up in the crypto equities. According to The Market Periodical, Circle's market capitalization reached 25.7 billion dollars at a recent Friday close. That made it the best-performing crypto-related equity of 2026, up roughly 30 percent. Coinbase trailed the group at down 10 percent. It is a low-tier source, so treat the gap as directional.

The direction still tells us something. On October 2nd we framed Circle's earnings as float times yield, minus what Circle pays venues to hold that float. Binance is the live example. Its 100 million dollar Circle stake came with a deal under which Circle pays Binance a monthly fee on USDC held in its wallets. The market is paying for the first half of the equation. It is valuing stablecoin float and short rates over trading volume, which is Coinbase's engine.

Now the misunderstanding. At the last firm reading, the float the market is paying for was not growing. Reap's compilation shows total stablecoin supply peaked at 322.4 billion dollars on May 17th. By August 13th it stood 4.5 percent below that peak. We have no verified early-October total, and no current split between Tether and USDC. So we cannot say the plateau persists today.

Still, the strongest real-usage pillar of the crypto story stopped growing in spring, and the most visible equity tied to it rallied anyway. The alternative is that USDC is gaining share inside a flat total, which would partly justify Circle's re-rating. We have no quoted series to test that, so it is the open question, not a conclusion.

The second-order effects matter. If the plateau has held, stablecoins have added no incremental Treasury bill demand since May. In that case issuer revenue holds up on rate, not growth, and Circle is partly a duration bet. If rate expectations fall, its earnings power falls even if the float holds. Legislation is the other exposure. Circle fell about 10 percent on September 15th, the day the CLARITY Act failed its Senate cloture vote. Part of its valuation rides on law that agency rules can only partly replace.

So the so-what. The equity market has crowned the stablecoin float, and that float, at last reading, was waiting for a new source of demand.

The bull case's candidate for that demand is tokenization. On September 17th the SEC issued a temporary innovation exemption for trading tokenized stocks. It expires five years after publication in the Federal Register. And the SEC says tokenized NMS stocks on these venues face limits on the number of symbols and volume traded.

The bull logic is clear. If tokenized equities trade around the clock on-chain, stablecoins become settlement collateral for a new asset class. That would be a large demand channel not tied to crypto trading. But nothing in hand measures how much demand it creates. The caps limit it near term, and an exemption is not a statute. This is an option on future flow, not a flow.

That brings us to the rules, which are being written on a short clock. Treasury's GENIUS Act proposal has comments due October 19th. The SEC's proposed Regulation Crypto Assets is open for comment until October 20th. And on September 17th, the CFTC sent a market-structure rulemaking to White House review, so its contents are not yet public.

All of this is the fallback after that failed CLARITY vote, 49 to 50 by Orrick's count, well short of the 60 needed. We see no reporting of Senate movement since the chamber returned on October 5th.

The fragility point is simple. A future SEC or CFTC can rewrite agency rules and exemptive orders. A statute is much harder to undo. The bull reading is that agencies are moving faster than Congress ever could. Both are true. Every business model priced off this regime, from Circle's float to tokenized stock venues, carries that durability risk until a law arrives.

Pulling it together. This week's crypto gains were paid for by buyers other than the ones the narrative credits. Bitcoin climbed on a shrinking ETF bid and falling leverage. Equity investors crowned a stablecoin float that, at the last firm reading, had not grown since May. And the rules governing that float are still drafts.

Three things to watch. Whether Bitcoin holds the band on a closing basis, and whether ETF money returns. The next dated stablecoin supply reading against the May peak. And the October 19th and 20th comment deadlines, with September CPI on October 14th as the next macro test.

That is the briefing for today. Thanks for listening, and stay cautiously optimistic.
