One standing disclosure before we start. Anthropic makes the model behind this show, and it's also today's lead story, so listen with that in mind.

Here's the thesis. Anthropic's amended IPO filing puts its annualized revenue run rate at 81 billion dollars. The same filing discloses nearly 900 billion dollars in compute commitments. By our arithmetic, that's roughly eleven times the run rate. So this week's listing is less a referendum on AI demand than a test. Can revenue that customers can dial down in a month carry obligations an order of magnitude larger?

Start with the revenue side. Per the filing, as reported by TradingView, the run rate reached 81 billion dollars at October 31st. That's up from 71 billion just a month earlier. But a run rate isn't recognized revenue.

Anthropic's actual third-quarter revenue was 16.96 billion dollars. By our arithmetic, that annualizes to roughly 68 billion. So the gap up to 81 billion tells you the exit month ran well above the quarter's average. The growth is real, and it's concentrated in the latest month.

The filing's own definition explains why that matters. The run rate annualizes consumption fees from a trailing 28-day window, and subscription fees from a single month. It assumes that pace holds for a full year. That's a snapshot, not a forecast. Last Friday, this desk noted that most of Anthropic's revenue is usage-billed. On a metered base, a snapshot can fall as fast as it rose, without a single customer cancelling anything.

One timing note. This October snapshot predates Monday's default-on rollout of Anthropic's unified Chat and Cowork experience. This desk called that switch distribution, not adoption. It can't have inflated today's number. The next disclosed run rate will include it, so read that one with the switch in mind.

Now the other side of that ledger. The roughly 900 billion dollars in compute commitments runs to partners including Amazon and Google. The headline total varies a little by outlet and filing amendment, so treat it as approximate. What matters more is how firm it is, and the follow-up reporting on that is new.

Futuriom's analysis of the filing describes the deals with Amazon, Google, and Microsoft as firm and non-cancelable, with terms running as late as 2036. Its words are blunt: "None of these deals is cancelable."

The analyses differ on how big that firm portion is. Futuriom puts the three hyperscaler deals at 807 billion dollars combined. Venture Magazine counts about 437 billion of non-cancelable obligations across those three plus Broadcom. Even the lower figure is more than five times the run rate, by our arithmetic. And Venture Magazine says only a fraction of the commitments let Anthropic avoid paying if capacity goes unused.

That closes off the more comfortable reading. You could imagine these commitments as capacity reservations, the way hyperscalers pre-buy supply in a tight market. On that reading, they'd be more option than obligation. The evidence doesn't support that for the bulk of this figure. The exceptions look small. At least one arrangement becomes cancelable on ninety days' notice once its initial window ends in early 2027. For most of the rest, the reporting says Anthropic pays whether or not it uses the compute. So what: these behave like fixed costs, set against a revenue line that flexes month to month.

Put a near-term number on it, because a multi-year total is abstract. Venture Magazine estimates nearly 70 billion dollars in contractual payments come due by September 2027. Against that, Anthropic holds 95.6 billion dollars in cash today.

The offering would add more. By our arithmetic on the share count and price range, it comes to roughly 58 to 64 billion dollars before fees. That assumes the company receives all of it, which the reporting we have doesn't break out. Either way, cash coverage of the near-term bill looks workable. The multi-year total is the harder question. It depends on usage growing into commitments that don't flex.

That connects to a pattern this desk tracked over the past week. We discounted vendor backlogs at Oracle, CoreWeave, and Dell, on the logic that a backlog is only as firm as the customer behind it. Here, for once, we see that customer from its own side. Anthropic's commitments to Amazon and Google are the kind of obligation that fills a cloud provider's order book. That's our inference, since we don't have the clouds' own backlog figures for these deals.

And the loop runs both ways. Venture Magazine reports that resale through the cloud marketplaces accounted for roughly 44 percent of Anthropic's nine-month sales. It also reports that no single end customer exceeds ten percent of revenue. So the clouds are both Anthropic's major suppliers and its channel for close to half its sales. Our inference is that part of the clouds' AI backlog now rests on metered revenue that flows partly through their own storefronts. That's the circularity this desk flagged last week, when Google and Microsoft were reported in Anthropic's placement. For anyone reading cloud backlog as a demand signal, that's a concentration worth discounting.

What does this mean for the enterprise buyer? A lab carrying commitments of that size has every incentive to maximize consumption, not just land logos. Expect more default-on features and more usage-expanding bundles. Expect more deals built around drawing down pre-committed spend, like the Claude Marketplace rail this desk covered on November 10th. That's inference, but the incentive is plain. For buyers, the leverage sits in committed-spend minimums and drawdown terms negotiated up front, not in headline pricing.

What's still missing is the customer side of the ledger. Last Friday, this desk asked whether the filing would disclose customer minimum commitments. The reporting we have still shows none, and no remaining performance obligations either. So there's nothing contracted from customers to set against the commitments Anthropic owes its suppliers. That remains the single most important open item.

On valuation, just this. At 70 to 78 dollars a share, Anthropic would be valued at up to 1.4 trillion dollars fully diluted. The same shortening of revenue visibility shows up one layer up, at the application layer.
