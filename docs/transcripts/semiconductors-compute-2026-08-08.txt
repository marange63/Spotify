Good morning, it's August 8th, and this is Semiconductors and Compute Infrastructure.

We open with the clearest test yet of a thesis we flagged last week: the market has stopped rewarding growth alone in this cycle, and it now demands growth, a raise, and a credible reason the growth accelerates from here. On August 5th, SanDisk reported fiscal fourth-quarter revenue of $8.965 billion, up 372 percent year over year, comfortably ahead of the roughly $8.4 billion consensus. Non-GAAP gross margin hit a record 84.6 percent, up almost 60 points from a year earlier. Adjusted earnings per share came in at $39.25 against a $34.45 estimate, a beat of about 14 percent, according to TradingKey's read of the release. Guidance for the next quarter came in at $10.3 to $10.8 billion in revenue and $44 to $46 in adjusted earnings, both above what the Street was modeling. By any normal standard, that is a flawless quarter. SanDisk shares still fell roughly 8 percent in after-hours trading, on top of a 5.4 percent decline in the regular session.

Western Digital told almost the same story the same day. Fourth-quarter revenue of $3.75 billion beat the $3.69 billion consensus, adjusted earnings of $3.56 beat the $3.31 estimate, and next-quarter guidance of $4.0 to $4.2 billion in revenue and $3.85 to $4.15 in earnings again came in above what analysts expected. Western Digital shares still dropped double digits in the reaction, with reported declines ranging from roughly 10 to 14 percent depending on the source and the exact window measured. The selloff didn't stay contained to the two companies that reported. SK hynix fell about 10 percent in Korea and Samsung Electronics fell nearly 6 percent, dragging the Kospi down with them, per Yahoo Finance's coverage of the move.

Here's the important distinction, because it's easy to get this wrong: this is not a guidance miss. Both companies raised, and raised above consensus, on revenue and on earnings. What happened is a valuation event, not a fundamentals break. Expectations for AI-driven memory demand had already run so far ahead of even genuinely excellent results that beating consensus wasn't enough — investors wanted to see a credible path to further acceleration, and a beat-and-raise quarter, however record-setting, didn't supply it. So the bar in this sector has now moved from "beat and raise" to "beat, raise, and prove growth speeds up from here." That bar just claimed two companies posting the best numbers in their history, and it dragged the rest of the memory complex down with them. Worth watching whether Micron's next print, and SK hynix's and Samsung's own memory results, get held to the same standard — if they do, the financing window for the next leg of HBM and NAND capacity expansion could tighten even while physical demand for that capacity stays genuinely constrained.

That's the valuation story. The technology roadmap underneath it is moving the opposite direction — accelerating, not stalling. At the Future of Memory and Storage conference on August 4th, Samsung unveiled a concept it's calling zHBM: instead of placing high-bandwidth memory beside the AI accelerator, as today's designs do, zHBM stacks the memory directly on top of it, shortening the distance data has to travel. Samsung's own projections claim a next-generation interface built around zHBM would deliver roughly eight times the performance of HBM5, more than ten times the memory density, three times the energy efficiency, and less than half the thermal resistance, according to Samsung's newsroom announcement. Read this as Samsung trying to reset the competitive narrative after ceding HBM market share to SK hynix. But treat it exactly as the announcement-versus-delivery gap this sector keeps testing: those multipliers are Samsung's own numbers for a concept-stage architecture, not an independently benchmarked result, TrendForce's reporting notes there's no announced production timeline, and there's no confirmed compatibility with Nvidia's or AMD's accelerators. So the memory roadmap keeps advancing on paper regardless of what the tape did to SanDisk and Western Digital this week — the two stories are running on separate tracks, one about pricing today's delivered results, the other about staking a claim on tomorrow's architecture.

All of that memory and compute capacity assumes one open question underneath it: who's actually allowed to buy it. That brings us to the sharpest policy development of the week. Bloomberg reported on August 7th that the enforcement arm of the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security has begun reviewing the legal channels Chinese AI firms use to reach advanced Nvidia chips by renting compute located outside China, rather than importing the chips directly. This is explicitly a review, not new rulemaking — the reporting describes BIS's enforcement team as "reviewing how Chinese AI firms reach Nvidia hardware overseas," and notes the review followed shortly after Moonshot AI's Kimi K3 model scored close to the latest American frontier systems on benchmarks.

The scale here isn't hypothetical. Bloomberg's reporting, via Yahoo Finance, puts Moonshot's compute behind Kimi K3 at roughly 20,000 Nvidia H200 chips, accessed through a computing agreement with Alibaba. Separately, the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy has accused Moonshot of illegally acquiring and using Nvidia's newer, more tightly restricted Blackwell accelerators to train that same model, suspecting they were obtained through a third party in Thailand. Tom's Hardware, drawing on the same reporting, documents two further cases. Megaspeed, a Singapore-linked firm, purchased nearly $2 billion in advanced Nvidia chips through its Malaysian subsidiary, shipped to data centers in Malaysia and Indonesia that appeared to be remotely serving Chinese clients. And a Shanghai-based firm, INF Tech, allegedly gained access to 2,300 banned Nvidia GPUs — 32 GB200 servers worth roughly $100 million — by renting capacity from an Indonesian telecom company.

None of that adds up to a total figure for how big this offshore-rental channel is; these are individual documented cases, not a market size. But they establish this is a real, billion-dollar-scale pattern rather than a theoretical loophole. BIS is reportedly building two separate tracking lists as part of its review — one for countries where restricted chips are physically smuggled into China, another for countries where Chinese firms tap the chips remotely — though the specific countries named on each list weren't independently confirmed in the reporting, so hold that detail loosely rather than as settled fact.

What sharpens the stakes is that Congress is already ahead of BIS on this. The House passed the Remote Access Security Act, H.R. 2683, by a vote of 369 to 22 back in January, a bill that would extend export-control jurisdiction to remote and cloud access of controlled chips — precisely the loophole BIS is now investigating administratively. It's currently sitting in the Senate Banking Committee with a companion bill from Senators McCormick and Wyden. So there are two independent paths toward closing this channel, one administrative and one legislative, running in parallel rather than as a single sequence — and that dual-track pressure makes some restriction landing within the next year meaningfully more likely than a lone BIS review would suggest on its own. If either path closes the channel, Southeast Asian data-center capacity in Malaysia, Thailand, and Indonesia — capacity Nvidia's regional channel partners currently depend on — faces new licensing friction, which is exactly the market Nvidia's own public comments have been warning not to lose after already losing most of its direct access to China.

Put it together and the picture for this sector is a split screen. The fundamentals and the technology roadmap are both still booming — record memory margins, accelerating architecture claims — but the two constraints that now actually matter aren't compute demand or manufacturing capacity. They're valuation discipline in the public markets and export policy in Washington. Both got sharper this week, and both are worth watching closely into the next earnings cycle.
