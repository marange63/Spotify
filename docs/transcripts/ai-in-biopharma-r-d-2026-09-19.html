AI in Biopharma R&D

Transcript · September 19, 2026

Good morning, and welcome to Cautious Optimism Briefings for Saturday, September 19th, 2026. This is the AI in biopharma R&D briefing.

Capital in this field has been pricing AI capability rather than proof, and stacking the money upstream at discovery and design. Today one sponsor bought both halves of the capability trade in forty-eight hours. And for the first time, a large institutional check landed on the plumbing instead of the models.

Start with Novo Nordisk. On September 16th, Novo and Anthropic announced a collaboration to put Claude models and the Claude Science workbench into Novo's R&D workflows. The next day, Novo signed a multi-target oral macrocycle discovery deal with Orbis Medicines. Read together, they draw the line between the two kinds of AI in this field.

Take Anthropic first, and the obvious disclosure — Anthropic makes the model that writes this briefing, and it gets no discount for it. Claude Science, in Anthropic's own words, is "not itself a new model." It runs existing Claude models over more than sixty scientific databases. That is an integration and distribution advance. It is not a biology model, and nothing in the announcement says molecule quality improves. The release says the work will address drug discovery challenges identified by Novo's own scientists, and that it builds on Novo's existing AI initiatives with other technology partners. It names no program, no target and no financial terms. The entire value claim is the chief executive's framing. Mike Doustdar said AI can help Novo "increase productivity in R&D and compress the path from research to marketed product."

The only quantified customer result Anthropic has published for Claude Science is a set of germline genomics workups at UCSF done in roughly one-tenth the time they previously took. That is a vendor-reported workflow speed-up, not a discovery outcome. So this is a productivity bet on literature, data and coding work — exactly where large language models are the workhorse today, and nowhere near the molecule.

Novo's own release on April 14th said it would integrate OpenAI's capabilities "globally from drug discovery to commercial operations," with pilots across R&D, manufacturing and commercial, and full integration targeted by the end of this year. An Amazon blog post on August 10th made AWS Novo's preferred cloud provider and strategic AI partner. Anthropic now makes three such relationships in five months. The OpenAI and AWS arrangements are enterprise-wide. The Anthropic release is scoped to R&D workflows. So the labs are competing for specific workflow slots inside one buyer. That favors connectors, data governance and integration over raw model capability, and it caps the labs' pricing power over pharma. Novo's internal renewal decisions, not any public number, will be the real grader.

Orbis is the other layer. Its nGen platform combines generative AI with high-throughput synthesis, aimed at oral cardiometabolic macrocycles. That is the specialized-architecture bet, with a wet-lab loop feeding the design model. Orbis says the platform can design, synthesize and analyze thousands of distinct synthetic macrocycles in weeks. That is a throughput claim about the machine, not a result from it.

Then the money. The release says Orbis is eligible for up to 1.4 billion dollars in "upfront and potential development and commercial milestone payments," plus tiered royalties. That is one blended number. The release gives no upfront amount, and it describes Novo's strategic investment only qualitatively. So the cash at signing is genuinely unknown. Treat the headline as biobucks. The tail risk is that realized cash never approaches it while the platform's valuation gets marked to it.

One buyer purchased two layers in two days, and they are graded on completely different clocks. The workflow layer is graded by whether Novo's scientists keep using it. The design engine is graded by a molecule years away. Neither deal disclosed a number that settles either question. Novo's Capital Markets Day falls on September 21st, after this briefing, and nothing about its content is known here.

If you are buying both layers, the next question is what you refuse to hand over. Sponsors are not refusing frontier models. They are changing where training happens. Several large pharmas have trained a shared co-folding model on the open OpenFold3 architecture using federated learning, so each contributes proprietary structures without sharing raw data. Pfizer licensed Chai's antibody design model in June and will receive a custom model trained on its own data. Those details come from secondary summaries, so treat them as directional.

The unresolved issue is whether any of it protects the advantage. Federated training shields the raw data. But a consortium model trained on several sponsors' structures diffuses the knowledge each sponsor thought it was keeping. Open-weight architectures are the common base, and proprietary data is the only differentiator left.

Regulation reinforces where the value sits. The FDA's draft guidance on AI credibility, issued in January 2025, still is not final. The FDA and EMA added joint guiding principles this January. Unfinalized guidance is not a neutral state. It keeps measurable AI value in workflows no reviewer has to credit — value that shows up in operating margin, not in pipeline economics.

Which brings us to where the money actually moved this week. On September 15th, Neuberger and KKR agreed to buy what the announcement calls a significant minority stake in Datavant, with New Mountain retaining control. Terms and valuation are undisclosed, and closing is expected in the fourth quarter. The stated use of proceeds is data-linking rails, tokenization infrastructure, and data provisioning for clinical AI training and real-world evidence.

Until this week, nothing was landing anywhere but upstream, at discovery and design, and nothing in the parts of the value chain that actually move a molecule to market. This is the first large check that isn't upstream. Datavant's own scale figures are company marketing: it says it touches data representing ninety percent of the U.S. population.

That bet only pays if the data underneath is usable, so ask whether AI can cross the balkanized formats that followed HITECH. Mostly it does not, and that is not the point. The balkanization problem is being solved by tokenization and linkage layers, and by scaled data owners. Language models are the extraction layer over unstructured notes. The moat is contractual access and linkage. HIPAA shapes that architecture more than it blocks the work. De-identification standards and business associate agreements push you toward tokenization, enclaves and locally deployed open-weight models. No HIPAA rulemaking happened this week, so that is structural analysis, not news. What is news is that capital is pricing data access as a durable asset. Tempus, which owns oncology multimodal data, reported a thirty-six percent increase in data licensing revenue in its second-quarter release back in July.

Two second-order effects follow. Tokenization becomes a chokepoint. Consolidation, including Datavant's absorption of the analytics firm Aetion last year, gives pricing power over the sponsors and diagnostics companies that must pass through it. And eligibility and indication work should compress screening failure and enrollment cost, which are real, bookable savings. But there is no measured improvement in trial success rates or R&D cost from any of this, only vendor and preprint accuracy claims. It lowers cost and cycle time. It does not touch Phase 2 biology risk.

That leaves the one asset in this field capable of grading any of it. Insilico's rentosertib, a TNIK inhibitor for pulmonary fibrosis where both target and molecule came from generative AI, dosed its first patient in the China Phase 3 on September 10th. The pivotal has moved from announced to running.

Fortune reports Insilico earned 35.5 million dollars in net profit in the first half of 2026. That profit is not product revenue. It is other companies' willingness to pay for capability, booked as earnings — an input metric dressed as an outcome.

The same caution applies to the science around it. In Nature Biotechnology, Insilico analyzed forty-two fibrosis patients with six proteomic aging clocks and reported a reversal in biological age. That is a company-reported effect from an exploratory analysis, and critics note the clocks cannot separate aging from disease-specific effects. Then on September 17th, Insilico launched LongevityBench, a benchmark it wrote itself, and reported that its own smaller aging-trained models beat several frontier systems on it.

So where does that leave us? The most useful capital this week went to the least glamorous layer. That is the right rotation, because plumbing and operations are where the savings are demonstrable. But demonstrable savings are not improved odds. Nothing this week moves the probability of clinical success. The readout that could is a China-only 52-week lung-function endpoint that will not print before 2027. Until it does, better clinical success rates remain an assumption sitting inside the valuations, and the likely near-term result is that pharma R&D spend falls faster than success rates rise.
