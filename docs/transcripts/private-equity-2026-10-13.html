Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Equity & the Deal Economy

Transcript · October 13, 2026

Good morning. It's Tuesday, October 13th, and this is the private equity briefing.

All week this show has asked one question. Who, if anyone, is pricing private equity at arm's length? The secondaries market has now answered, and the answer splits by asset quality.

Start with the split itself. One caveat up front: this is first-half 2026 data, surfacing in recent secondaries coverage, not an October print. Single-asset continuation vehicles are the deals where a sponsor rolls one trophy holding into a new vehicle. According to Evercore, they hit $34 billion of volume in the first half, up 88% from a year earlier. That makes them 53% of all GP-led secondary volume. Those deals have generally cleared near or above the sponsor's own carrying value. Multi-asset continuation vehicles bundle several companies into one vehicle, and they did not get that treatment. Industry data reported in secondaries coverage puts the share of multi-asset continuation vehicles that traded below net asset value at 71%. That's a share of deals, not a depth of discount. A one-percent markdown and a twenty-five percent markdown both count toward that number. No tracker we could verify has published the average. So don't read it as a uniform haircut.

This lands exactly where the show has been stuck all week. On the 8th, we called near-par pricing on single-asset deals close to circular, because the seller is also the one setting the mark being tested. On Sunday, we flagged Coller EQT's refusal of a 25% carry on Lightspeed's planned multi-asset fund as the only visible instance of buyer discipline. Today's number generalizes that refusal across the diversified side of the market. For the first time this week, there's a price signal that didn't come from the seller.

There are two honest readings of the split, and both deserve airtime. The first is valuation opacity. Diversified, fund-level marks are running ahead of what the market will actually pay. The second is adverse selection. Sponsors route their best, most marketable assets into single-asset vehicles and leave the weaker holdings in multi-asset ones. On that reading, a below-NAV multi-asset price reflects which assets got chosen, not whether marks across the industry are inflated. One test would separate the two. Do the sponsors who sold a discounted multi-asset strip then mark their remaining, similar holdings down to match? Nobody has shown that yet.

That test points to the second-order problem, which is mark transmission. If a diversified strip clears below NAV, the fund that sold it still holds comparable companies at the old marks. So do the limited partners who chose to roll rather than sell. Their paper marks now sit above a price the market just declined to pay. The large managers' third-quarter results are the first place to see whether marks follow the clearing price or hold their ground. If they hold, the gap between reported NAV and realizable value becomes a choice, not an accident.

There's a paradox in all of this. A below-NAV secondary sale is real cash, at a price set by someone with no stake in the old mark. That's unlike a NAV loan that funds a distribution. It's also unlike a near-par continuation vehicle anchored by the seller. So the most honest liquidity in this market sits in the deals the industry advertises least. Their pricing deserves more weight than the record volume headline.

Why can secondaries buyers afford to be that selective? Follow the capital. Per With Intelligence data cited by S&P Global, dedicated secondaries fundraising fell to $46.6 billion in the first half of this year. That compares with $92.9 billion a year earlier, so it roughly halved. Over the same stretch, one source puts secondary transaction volume at a record. Here's this show's analysis, not a reported fact. When closed-end buyer capital is scarcer, buyers can ration it. They point it at trophy single assets and demand discounts to take on anything else.

One caveat matters here. Fundraising measures new commitments, not total buyer firepower. Some of the first half's record volume is likely funded from dry powder raised in earlier vintages. Evergreen and semi-liquid secondaries vehicles may also be supplying capital that closed-end fundraising tallies don't capture. We couldn't verify a figure for either pool, so treat the scarcity story as directional, not precise.

What it implies, though, is that bargaining power now splits by asset quality. On the best single assets, sponsors are extracting rich terms, which is Sunday's super-carry story. On the diversified tails, buyers are extracting discounts. So the upside in this cycle concentrates with GPs holding the best assets. The downside gets crystallized for the limited partners who sell the rest.

Why do sellers accept a discount at all, instead of just waiting? Because the alternative is a paper mark with no cash behind it. S&P Global data shows the median ratio of distributions to paid-in capital remains below 1x for every private equity vintage going back to 2018. Some of that is unremarkable. A 2023 or 2024 vintage fund hasn't had time to return capital yet. That's ordinary J-curve arithmetic. But the 2018, 2019, and 2020 vintages should be well past the point of returning what investors put in. By that data, the median fund in those vintages hasn't. That's the telling part of the number, and it's the seller's real motive. Cash below the mark beats a paper mark that stays a paper mark.

The same tail-asset logic shows up in public markets, too. Bumble is reportedly in advanced talks to go private with Ithaca Holdings, at a valuation of roughly $1 billion. That's according to the Wall Street Journal, which reported a deal could arrive as soon as this week. Nothing is signed. There's no disclosed offer price, so there's no premium to calculate. Treat it the way we treated the CCC Intelligent Solutions talks with GTCR and Elliott. We flagged those on Sunday as unsigned, and we have no update since. Both are reported processes, not transactions. On our read, they share a pattern. A listed company exits through a private buyer at whatever price clears today, rather than waiting for public markets to re-rate it. That's the public-market version of the discounting logic in multi-asset secondaries.

Pulling it together: this cycle is liquid at the top of the quality stack and discounted below it. The best single assets trade near their marks, and sponsors keep pricing power over them. Everything else is being tested by buyers who appear short on fresh dedicated capital and are pricing accordingly. Sellers take those discounts because, at the median, distributions still trail paid-in capital all the way back to the 2018 vintage. The unresolved risk is the gap between diversified-portfolio marks and what buyers will actually pay. If that gap widens, realized cash falls further behind paper NAV, even as headline secondaries volume keeps setting records.

Watch three things. First, whether the Bumble talks produce a signed deal this week. Second, the large managers' third-quarter results, for realized carry against fee income and for any markdowns on diversified holdings. Third, second-half secondaries data, for whether the multi-asset discount widens or narrows.

That's the private equity briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
