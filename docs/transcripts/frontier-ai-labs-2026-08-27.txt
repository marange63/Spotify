Good morning. It's Thursday, August 27th, and this is your frontier AI labs briefing.

The number this show deferred to twice this week has landed. And the interesting part isn't the beat. It's the story Nvidia chose to tell around it.

Nvidia reported fiscal second-quarter results on Wednesday, for the quarter ended July 26th. Total revenue was 96.2 billion dollars, up 18 percent from the prior quarter. Data center revenue was 89 billion dollars, up 117 percent from a year ago. Guidance for the current quarter is 108 billion dollars, plus or minus two percent. Those are the figures from Nvidia's own release, and they are not in dispute.

The framing around them is. A year ago Jensen Huang's story was that one lab was driving the entire buildout. His line this week is different. He described a golden age of new AI labs and startups, with multiple frontier labs scaling in parallel, a thriving open-model ecosystem, and physical AI coming online. Strip the adjectives and the claim is simple. Nvidia wants you to believe demand has broadened past any single customer.

Take that claim seriously, then interrogate it. It is the seller's line, in the seller's own press release. And it happens to be exactly the line that dissolves the single-customer-concentration risk hanging over Nvidia's stock. So what would settle it? The customer-concentration disclosure — the percentage of revenue coming from Nvidia's largest direct buyers, this quarter against a year ago. That figure does not live in an earnings release. It lives in a footnote to the quarterly filing, and that filing typically posts one to three weeks after the call. It is not out yet.

That timing matters more than it sounds. The narrative gets three weeks of unopposed air before the one number that could contradict it arrives. Until then, the honest position is that the record does not show whether demand really has broadened, or whether it is still concentrated and Huang is narrating around it.

Now assume, for argument, that the broadening is real. It does not make concentration risk disappear. It changes the shape of it. One enormous customer is replaced by a long tail of smaller labs. And a hyperscaler cuts capex slowly, over quarters, against depreciation schedules and board approvals. A venture-funded lab's compute budget disappears the month a round fails to close. That is a more procyclical buyer base, not a safer one. So the broadening story is not the risk-off read the framing implies. It swaps a visible, single-name risk for a diffuse one that only shows up when private financing tightens.

There is a piece of counter-evidence sitting in this show's own coverage. On Tuesday we described Nvidia financing a new OpenAI data center campus and taking an equity stake in the developer that will own the site and collect the rent. That is not what a supplier does when its buyer base is broad and interchangeable. That is what a supplier does when one buyer is structurally central to the demand curve. Both things can be true — many labs buying, one buyer that has to keep buying — but only one of them is in the press release.

Note the shape of the whole picture, too. This show has opened on a frozen capability leaderboard for more than a week. Spending is now broadening across labs while the capability rankings sit still. That is not what a winner-take-most market looks like on its way to consolidation.

There is a second data point buried in the same release. Nvidia stated plainly that it is not assuming any data-center compute revenue from China in its outlook. That is a guidance choice, not an observation about demand. A prudent company zeroes out revenue it cannot forecast. But it corroborates, from the supplier's own outlook, what we flagged on Monday: the binding constraint on Chinese labs' access to Nvidia-class compute now looks like it comes from Beijing at least as much as from Washington's export rules. It also creates an asymmetry worth holding. If that constraint ever loosens, none of it is in the guide. Any China revenue is pure upside to a number the market has already priced.

Now the other side of that same invoice. Nvidia's largest customer has spent this week publishing evidence that it wants out of the dependency altogether.

OpenAI released its first public benchmarks for Jalapeño, the inference-only chip it has built with Broadcom. OpenAI claims 1.5 to 1.9 times more AI work per watt at peak throughput than Nvidia's Blackwell and Rubin systems. It also claims 1.7 to 3.6 times lower end-to-end latency than the best commercially available systems. Handle those as what they are. They are OpenAI's own numbers, on a benchmark suite OpenAI selected, with the analysis firm SemiAnalysis verifying only some of the runs on-site. No rival lab or chipmaker has published a counter-benchmark. Which generation is even the fair comparator — the older Blackwell or the current Rubin — is itself contested.

The decisive fact here isn't the ratio. It's the shipping status. Jalapeño is still at the engineering-sample stage. Nvidia's Rubin systems are already going to customers. A chip that beats a shipping product on a bench does not serve a single token to a paying user.

So separate two channels that get blurred together. The cost channel is unquantifiable today. Nobody has published anything that lets you estimate what Jalapeño would do to ChatGPT's or the API's real unit economics, and OpenAI has not tried. The leverage channel is live right now. Every future purchase negotiation between OpenAI and Nvidia from this point forward happens in the shadow of a credible in-house alternative. That has value before a single unit ships, and it costs OpenAI nothing to keep publishing benchmarks.

There is a third party who gains here who isn't in the headline. Broadcom now has a second marquee custom-silicon customer story alongside its Google work. The load-bearing assumption under Nvidia's multiple is that it captures most of the inference-silicon economics indefinitely. Two named hyperscale-class buyers designing around that is the beginning of an argument against it.

One more detail inside that benchmark tells you where the industry's center of gravity has moved. OpenAI ran Jalapeño against three workloads: its own open-weight GPT-OSS, DeepSeek's R1, and Moonshot's Kimi K2.5. Two of the three are Chinese open-weight models, used as reference workloads for measuring American inference silicon. That is a diffusion win that shows up in no revenue line anywhere. Those architectures are becoming the default target hardware gets optimized against, and that compounds on its own, independent of what anyone charges per token.

While compute and cost get fought out in public benchmarks, capability oversight is moving the opposite way. On August 4th the White House met with OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, Microsoft and Nvidia to review a voluntary AI safety-testing framework. The framework would let the government take up to thirty days of early access to certain frontier models before release. It explicitly cannot be used to create mandatory licensing or preclearance. And the details of the framework itself are being kept confidential. No lab has been established as having opted in.

A confidential standard isn't really a standard. It lets a lab say it went through government review without anyone outside the room being able to check what was tested or what was found. Set that against the outside-grading trend we covered on Tuesday, where a third party scored the labs on what they had published. Those two mechanisms push disclosure in opposite directions, and it is worth saying which one wins. The confidential one does. Outside graders can only score what is already public, so their pressure is real but bounded. Government review confers the strongest credential available — reviewed by the federal government — at a disclosure cost of zero. When the highest-value badge is also the quietest one, the rational lab publishes less, not more.

So here's the pattern across all three stories. Everyone is publishing the claim that flatters them. The seller says demand is broad. The buyer says it can out-engineer its supplier. The government says it is watching, but won't show its notes. None of the three is independently established. Two things to watch, both checkable. Whether Nvidia's quarterly filing, when it posts, shows customer concentration falling or holding. And whether Jalapeño moves off engineering samples into an actual serving fleet.
