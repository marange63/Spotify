Good morning. It's Thursday, November 12th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

On Saturday, this desk called CoreWeave the weakest link in AI demand. On Tuesday, CoreWeave raised its 2026 revenue range and grew its backlog sharply in a single quarter. On Wednesday, Nvidia rallied on that print. Today we work through what that bounce tells us, what it doesn't, and why the part it doesn't explain just got harder to price.

Start with CoreWeave's guidance. The company lifted the bottom of its 2026 revenue range from 12.4 to 13.0 billion dollars. The top of the range barely moved. So the headline raise is mostly a higher floor, and the midpoint shifts only modestly.

The real signal is backlog. CoreWeave reported 132.5 billion dollars of backlog at the end of September, against 104 billion at the end of June. That's growth of roughly 27 percent in three months. And it's contracted commitment, not recognized revenue, at the most leveraged buyer in the AI-cloud stack.

So what kind of demand is this? On the evidence in hand, it is demand constrained by compute availability, not by economics. Commitments are piling up far faster than revenue guidance is rising. That makes conversion the gating factor. Conversion depends on delivering data-center capacity, and that depends on financing CoreWeave can actually raise. So the weak-link thesis from Saturday isn't confirming on the demand side. Whether it holds on the financing side is still unresolved.

There's a second-order point in how the market used this print. Investors took a leveraged neocloud's results as their read-through on Nvidia's demand. That ties Nvidia's sentiment, at least this week, to the credit health of its most financing-sensitive customers. It's a helpful anchor while backlog grows. It becomes a fragile one if neocloud credit tightens. That's our inference, not a market consensus.

That explains the bounce. It doesn't explain what's left on Nvidia.

Per The Coin Republic, Nvidia was still down about 14 percent for November even after Wednesday's gain. Decrypt reported that by Tuesday afternoon, the decline had reached 18.42 percent, erasing all of the stock's gains for the year. You can't cleanly split that residual into one cause. It mixes three things. There's the sector-wide selloff after the midterms. There's ordinary positioning. And there's the governance overhang around Jensen Huang and the Megaspeed chip-smuggling probe, which this desk has tracked all week. A one-day pop on a customer's print clears some of the demand fear. It doesn't clear the rest.

And the rest just got a new data point. Bloomberg reported that Nvidia's general counsel, Tim Teter, called Commerce Under Secretary Jeffrey Kessler in late summer of 2025. The subject was the investigation into suspected AI-chip smuggling by Megaspeed, the Singapore-based reseller. Bloomberg's account rests on people familiar with the matter, not on a filing or an on-record statement.

Follow-up reporting relaying Bloomberg sharpens what the call was about. Teter reportedly asked whether the Bureau of Industry and Security was involved in Singapore's detention of a Megaspeed executive named Alice Huang. Alice Huang is the woman reportedly at the center of Jensen Huang's alleged personal relationship. Despite the shared surname, nothing in the reporting suggests the two are related. Bloomberg's sources said the bureau was not involved in her detention. Nvidia's on-record account to the New York Times describes its regulatory contacts only in general compliance terms.

Why does that matter? Until now, the risk ran on two separate tracks. One was Jensen Huang's alleged relationship with Alice Huang. The other was Nvidia's compliance posture toward resellers like Megaspeed. Teter's call puts the company's top lawyer on the phone with Commerce about Alice Huang's detention. So the personal story and the corporate story are no longer separable.

Hold the caveats firmly. Nvidia reportedly says Teter did not know of the relationship when he called. Calling a regulator about an enforcement matter is not improper on its face. We don't know what, if anything, the call changed. Nothing here is a finding of wrongdoing.

The timing matters too. On Saturday, this desk reported Nvidia's new conditions on cloud resellers and its stepped-up chip tracking in Southeast Asia. One reading was defensive. Nvidia was protecting its export licences ahead of scrutiny. The other reading was remediation. Nvidia was tightening a channel it already knew was under a microscope. Teter's call came more than a year before those moves, and that pushes toward remediation. That's this desk's inference, and it still doesn't show wrongdoing at the company.

Now the calendar. House Foreign Affairs Chairman Brian Mast gave Jensen Huang until November 11th to respond to a request for a closed-door briefing, or face a subpoena. As of this briefing, we have no confirmed report of how that deadline resolved. Treat it as genuinely open.

Then the fixed dates. Nvidia reports third-quarter earnings on November 17th, one day before the requested briefing. A strong revenue print can address the demand half of November's discount. It does nothing for the governance half. And that half now includes a question about the company's own conduct, not just its chief executive's.

If demand is real, it eventually has to show up in the tools that build capacity two years out. That's where the equipment makers come in. Lam Research has forecast wafer-fab-equipment spending in the mid-150-billion-dollar range for 2026. It sees that rising to more than 200 billion dollars in 2027.

That's a step-up of roughly 30 percent in a single year, which is aggressive. It's also Lam's own forecast, relayed by 24/7 Wall St. An equipment maker forecasting equipment demand is an interested seller. This is a bull case on record, not order data from customers.

The stocks have moved ahead of the evidence. Equipment names sold off through October, then rebounded hard on Wednesday. Applied Materials rose 8 percent, with Lam and KLA close behind. That came ahead of Applied's fiscal fourth-quarter report, due later today.

Lam also announced a 25-billion-dollar share buyback authorization on November 5th. That cuts both ways. It signals confidence in cash generation. But Lam's October outlook was reported as capped by supply constraints. A company in that position returning capital, rather than adding capacity, invites the question of how durable management thinks the step-up really is.

So Applied's print is the first hard test, and it lands after this briefing goes out. The bar is Applied's prior quarter, a record 9.12 billion dollars of revenue, up about 25 percent year on year. A guide consistent with a 2027 step-up would support the view that Wednesday's rally reflects real demand, held back only by supply bottlenecks. Caution on orders would undercut it. We don't know which yet, and nothing here is a prediction. Rallying into a print is positioning, not confirmation.

Most of that tool spending, if it materializes, lands at TSMC. The question is whether anyone else earns a share of it. That's where Intel's own manufacturing numbers come in. Per 24/7 Wall St.'s reading of Intel's disclosures, costs on the 18A node are down about 40 percent since the first quarter.

Adoption is moving just as fast. Chips built on that node have gone from under 5 percent of Intel's client CPU revenue to nearly a third. That's genuine manufacturing execution. Cost progress isn't the same as yield parity with TSMC's leading node, and we have no yield figure. But the curve is moving the right way.

The catch is that Intel is mostly selling to itself. Intel Foundry, the business trying to sell that capacity to outside customers, posted a 1.85-billion-dollar quarterly operating loss. So score Intel on two separate axes. The process is improving, and that helps Intel's own product margins first. The foundry business still hasn't converted it into external revenue. Only an outside leading-edge customer turns Intel's cost curve into a share of the coming tool cycle. Until then, a bigger equipment upcycle mostly funds TSMC's existing lead.

Pulling it together. The demand fear in Nvidia's November selloff is starting to ease, on contracted backlog rather than headline guidance. The governance risk is deepening, and its personal and corporate threads have now converged. The tool cycle gets its first hard test today. Watch three things: Applied's results later today, Nvidia's earnings on November 17th, and the congressional briefing requested for November 18th.

That's the briefing for November 12th. Thanks for listening, and I'll catch you next time.
