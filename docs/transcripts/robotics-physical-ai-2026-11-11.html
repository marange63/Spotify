Good morning, it's Wednesday, November 11th, and this is your robotics and physical AI briefing. The theme this week is that robot autonomy is coming loose from the bodies and the data it was built on. But almost every number we have today measures whether a system can do something at all, not whether it can do it the ten-thousandth time.

Start with Nvidia's GR00T 2, the robot foundation model from the CoRL conference in Austin, which we called GR00T N2 yesterday. Yesterday Nvidia gave only a relative claim. Now it has published absolute numbers. They are Nvidia's own evaluation results, published on its research page and reported by The Robot Report, with no independent replication. The test set is fifty tasks that appear in other robots' data or in human video, but never in data from the target robot itself. With zero demonstrations on that robot, GR00T 2 succeeded at least once on forty of the fifty. That's a lenient bar. On the stricter measure, average success across repeated trials, one demonstration gets seventy-one percent. Five demonstrations, under three minutes of robot data, get ninety-five percent.

Here's the skeptical read. Forty of fifty at least once tells you whether the robot can ever do the task, not how often. That's exactly the gap between doing something once and doing it reliably that we keep coming back to. And ninety-five percent at five demos sounds strong until you read Nvidia's next line. Thirty-seven of the fifty tasks succeeded on every single trial. Work the arithmetic the other way, and thirteen of fifty tasks still failed at least once, even with five demonstrations and a fine-tune. A warehouse station or a factory cell runs thousands of cycles, not a handful of trials per task.

There's a second wrinkle, and it cuts deeper. According to the analysis blog Pebblous, reading Nvidia's whitepaper, the fifty tasks were selected from tasks the model could already learn with five episodes at eighty percent success or better. That's a secondary account, not something we've checked against the paper itself. But if it holds, the benchmark was drawn from tasks expected to do well at five demos, not sampled at random. Nvidia also hasn't disclosed the task categories, whether the evaluation ran on a real robot or in simulation, or how many rollouts sat behind each number. None of this makes the result fake. It's real progress on sample efficiency.

Now the unit-economics question, and here the honest answer is a gap. Our inference is that five demonstrations per task, instead of programming a conventional cell step by step, points at a real cut in integration labor. But Nvidia disclosed no cost, integration-hour, or uptime figure. That's the number that would turn sample efficiency into payback, and it's the one to watch. Until it shows up, this is a lab result, not evidence of fielded reliability.

The more important disclosure, though, is what the model learned from. As reported by Unite.AI, GR00T 2 is a two-billion-parameter transformer built on Nvidia's Cosmos video model. Its training data includes one hundred thousand hours of human video. That data has been released under an open CC BY 4.0 license. This matters beyond one launch. As we reported Monday, Chinese regulators have held up SoftBank's purchase of ABB's robotics unit, reportedly over concern about robot data leaving China. That makes owned fleet data a geopolitical liability as well as a cost. Across Monday and Tuesday we also covered synthetic-data efforts, Google DeepMind's Genie 4 and Nvidia's Isaac Lab, meant to reduce the need for owned fleet data. Human video pretraining is a third way around that same problem. It's a corpus nobody needs a robot fleet, or a Chinese factory floor, to collect.

What follows is our inference, not something Nvidia states. If a capable robot brain can be built on public human video and then adapted with a handful of demonstrations, the competitive edge stops being who owns the biggest teleoperation fleet. It starts being who does the best last-mile demonstrations, and who runs the most reliable deployment on the ground. The bull case is that the brain becomes a commodity input, and value shifts to hardware and service integration. That's good news for the Chinese humanoid cohort and the industrial-arm vendors, who never built their own foundation models. The bear case is that five demonstrations, multiplied across a long tail of real-world tasks, is still a real data bill. And human video carries no force or torque signal. That's precisely where contact-rich manipulation tends to break down, and contact-rich hand-work is what actually sets humanoid unit economics. So the moat in robot brains is migrating, from owning the data toward owning the last mile.

The other way around the data problem this week is simulation. Boston Dynamics released video of its Atlas humanoid fitted with wheels, spinning, dashing sideways and balancing on a single wheel. According to Korea JoongAng Daily's account, Boston Dynamics engineer Andy Wu says the system learned all of these behaviors in simulation and executed them in real life with surprising accuracy. No transfer metric came with that claim. The same account says the wheeled Atlas is an experimental research platform and will not be commercialized. Our read is that it's a clean data point, but a narrow one. Whole-body dynamic locomotion is the regime where simulation-to-reality transfer has worked well for years. It tells you very little about dexterous hand-work, which is the harder and more economically relevant problem.

Now, if the brain is becoming portable across robot bodies, the cleanest test of that idea this week came from the air, not the floor. Per Defense News, Shield AI announced on November 9th that its Hivemind autonomy software guided two of Anduril's Fury Collaborative Combat Aircraft through beyond-visual-range combat maneuvers on October 26th. Todd Wesley, Shield's vice president of Hivemind engineering, said the combined Hivemind and aircraft software release process is, quote, "nearing the point where the Air Force can fly a sortie." We've watched Shield AI all week on the capital side. It matched a Navy contract with its own money, agreed to buy radar maker Echodyne, and raised fresh equity at a twenty-eight-point-eight-billion-dollar valuation. This flight is the first evidence on the flight-line side of that ledger, rather than the balance-sheet side.

Set that against the money. Of the Air Force's roughly two-point-four-billion-dollar fiscal 2027 request for the program, per Defense News, nearly sixty percent is still research and development rather than procurement. Our reading of that split is that the program is still weighted toward development rather than volume buying. Autonomy that flies in a test is still a long way from autonomy the Air Force buys in bulk.

So be precise about what this flight was and wasn't. It was two aircraft, in a company-disclosed test exercise. No engagement outcomes, intervention counts, or independent Air Force assessment were released. Wesley's own phrase, nearing the point, concedes this was not an operational sortie. And structurally, Shield AI's autonomy software is flying on an airframe built by Anduril, a company that also competes with Shield in defense autonomy. Here's our inference on how that cuts. The bull read is that being the autonomy layer across many airframes is more durable than any single airframe contract. The bear read is that the airframe owner brings autonomy in-house and squeezes the software vendor out. That's the defense version of the same question running through GR00T 2. Once the brain is portable, does the brain's maker or the body's owner capture the value?

Pulling it together. Where embodied AI is genuinely inflecting is sample efficiency, and the early portability of autonomy software, across both robot bodies and airframes. Where it's still a demo is reliability at volume, whether in a factory cell, a warehouse station, or a combat sortie. The open questions are what Nvidia's fifty tasks actually are, whether the five-demo result holds on contact-rich manipulation, and who captures the value once autonomy becomes a portable layer rather than a proprietary moat.

For catalysts, watch three things. First, Nvidia's research page lists GR00T 2 code as available in December, though it doesn't say the same for model weights or give license terms. Second, the Air Force's first real sortie with Shield AI's software would turn a test into an operation. Third, Agility Robotics, the Digit humanoid maker that began trading on Nasdaq last week, still owes its first quarterly filing as a public company. The listing materials we've seen disclosed no unit cost, uptime, or cycle counts. So that filing is the first chance to see whether a humanoid maker puts anything like the reliability-at-volume number missing today next to its revenue.
