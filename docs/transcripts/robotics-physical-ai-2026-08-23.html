Robotics & Physical AI

Transcript · August 23, 2026

Good morning. It's Sunday, August 23rd, 2026, and this is your Cautious Optimism briefing on robotics and physical AI.

This show has spent the past week pricing physical AI off private marks and a retail IPO tape. The freshest disclosures this week sort differently. They line up almost exactly by who had to check the number before you heard it. That's the spine of today's briefing.

Start with Tesla. Following meetings at Fremont, JPMorgan's newly appointed auto analyst relayed Tesla's own pitch on August 20th. FSD version 15 is a step-change, and roughly 40 percent of its core technologies are already running in the Austin robotaxi fleet. That's the company's characterization relayed by a sell-side analyst after a company-hosted visit, not an independent technical assessment. Underneath it sits a number Tesla gave on its July 22nd earnings call. AI software vice president Ashok Elluswamy said the company's robotaxis had logged more than 380,000 unsupervised miles across six cities in Texas and Florida with, in his words, zero notable incidents. Set that against Waymo, which has logged more than 220 million rider-only miles across roughly 3,000 driverless vehicles and completes 500,000 paid trips a week. That's a gap of about 580 times on miles. Tesla's answer to the gap isn't more miles. It's a cost target: roughly thirty cents a mile at scale, with fleet expansion accelerating into next year. Cost per mile is a claim about utilization and depreciation. It is not a claim about safety, and the two shouldn't blur just because they arrive in the same meeting.

And the safety claim needs a harder look than self-reported. A public, regulator-facing dataset does exist for this exact fleet. Tesla files its own crash reports into NHTSA's Standing General Order database, and as of mid-January this year that filing carried 14 crashes on the Austin robotaxi operation. Against an outside estimate of roughly 800,000 fleet miles by that date, Electrek put the rate at one crash every 57,000 miles — by its arithmetic, about four times Tesla's own stated human-driver benchmark, and about eight times NHTSA's broader police-reported average. By March the cumulative tally reached 17 unique incidents, spanning the service's entire life back to July of last year. Now the caveat that keeps this honest. That dataset covers the whole fleet's operating history, safety-monitored miles included, and it ends in March. It does not map onto the narrower unsupervised-only subset Tesla cited in July, so it is not a refutation on the same denominator. It's something more awkward. The company's own regulatory filings for this fleet already contain a reported crash record, and its investor framing didn't mention it.

Now the Optimus side, which closes an arc this show has been tracking. Three days running last week, we noted that Optimus production still hadn't started at Fremont. The same August 20th readout turns that into guidance. Gen 3's design and supply chain are, in Tesla's words, essentially locked in. But external commercial sales begin only as early as the second half of 2027 — target language, not a shipping date. And the capacity figures Tesla floats, a million units at Fremont and ten million in Texas, carry no date at all. So the humanoid line contributes nothing inside the current forecast window. Whatever humanoid premium sits in Tesla's equity is being carried on capacity targets rather than shipments, which is how this whole capex cycle is being underwritten right now.

One more piece from Fremont, because it tells you where the bottleneck isn't. Tesla told JPMorgan its current AI4 computer is enough to run version 15 and unsupervised FSD, with no fleet retrofit flagged. A refreshed AI4.5 adds about 10 percent more compute and roughly double the memory, and the next-generation AI5 chip has slipped to mid-2027. So the near-term robotaxi ramp is decoupled from next-generation silicon. Through 2027 the constraint is deployment and regulatory approval, not compute.

Which is the bridge, because the other American robotaxi story this month didn't need a mileage claim. It needed a permission slip. Amazon-owned Zoox began charging fares in Las Vegas on August 10th, in a purpose-built vehicle with no steering wheel and no pedals, after NHTSA granted what CEO Aicha Evans called the first-ever commercial exemption for a purpose-built robotaxi. That exemption waives eight federal motor vehicle safety standards and is capped at up to 2,500 vehicles a year for two years. An exemption is not a rule change. It's temporary, it's capped, it's granted to one company, and it waives the standards rather than certifying the vehicle met them. Read the cap for what it is: the regulator's ceiling on what commercial is allowed to mean right now, not a fleet count. Zoox has disclosed no Las Vegas fleet size and no ride volume, so we don't know how much of that ceiling it's using.

The second-order effect is the one nobody models. Because this is an individually granted exemption rather than a rulemaking, first-mover regulatory position becomes a durable asset. Every wheel-free entrant has to negotiate its own, and the holder of the first one spends two years accumulating exactly the operating record the regulator will judge the next applicant against. Tesla's Cybercab is also wheel-and-pedal-free, and it will have to walk through this same door. No amount of FSD mileage substitutes for it. Regulatory sequencing, not model capability, is the binding constraint on wheel-free robotaxis.

From a regulator capping units, to a customer capping the pace of installation. Warehouse automation produced the sector's most recent filed, GAAP income statement this month, and it's worth pausing on precisely because nobody has been looking at it. Symbotic reported fiscal third-quarter results on August 5th: revenue of 721 million dollars, up 22 percent year over year, and GAAP net income of 55 million dollars against a 21-million-dollar loss a year earlier. Shares slipped anyway.

Here's why. The company carries a contracted backlog of 22.5 billion dollars, and guided fourth-quarter revenue to 760 to 780 million. Do that arithmetic on the company's own two numbers and the order book is roughly seven years of revenue at the guided run-rate. The live number underneath it is the deployment split: 77 systems in deployment, only 56 of them operational. That 21-system gap is where the years actually go, and it isn't a robot-supply problem. It's commissioning: skilled integration labor and customer site downtime. That input doesn't scale with a factory ramp, and it's the one humanoid vendors have least of. So apply the discipline. If a proven, contracted rail-and-shuttle system takes this long to turn contracted demand into installed revenue, any humanoid fleet forecast assuming a faster ramp is unserious.

Paired with that print, the negative control. AutoStore and Amazon signed what they call a global strategic supply agreement on August 13th, and AutoStore's own press release says plainly it does not contain any purchasing commitments at this time. No financial terms, no volume expectations, so the revenue impact is unknowable from current disclosure. That's the clean illustration of a headline that reads like a contract and isn't one.

Which makes the week's loudest number the one nobody outside the building had to accept. The World Humanoid Robot Games opened in Beijing yesterday, and when we spoke there were only entry counts. Now there are results. Tiangong Ultra won its 100-meter heat in 9.39 seconds, faster than Usain Bolt's human world record of 9.58, against 21.50 seconds for the winning time at last year's inaugural Games. That is a real 2.2-times improvement in twelve months, and it reflects genuine gains in actuators and control. But a 100-meter heat is a straight track with a known start, and no source establishes whether the machines that ran it are the platforms these vendors actually sell or one-off competition rigs. Credit the engineering delta. Don't read it as evidence about the unstructured long tail.

So rank this week's four numbers by who checked them. Zoox's came from a federal regulator. Symbotic's came from a filed, GAAP income statement. Tesla's came from the company itself on an earnings call, and the one regulator-facing dataset that does exist for its fleet tells a less flattering story than zero notable incidents. Beijing's came from a stopwatch on a closed track. The open question into the fall is whether any dataset someone outside the company had to accept ever lands squarely on that robotaxi safety claim. Three things to watch: an NHTSA tally current enough to cover the unsupervised-mile window, Symbotic's fourth quarter against its own 760-to-780-million guide, and the Beijing Games closing out on the 26th.

That's it for today. Stay cautiously optimistic.
