Good morning. It's Friday, August 28th, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

Eight days ago, a report that Anthropic was preparing a share sale knocked MongoDB, Cloudflare, Okta and Zscaler down four to five percent in a single afternoon, on the theory that the frontier lab was about to eat the per-seat revenue those vendors live on. This week, Salesforce reported a two-point-six-billion-dollar mark-to-market gain on its stake in that same lab, sitting inside Salesforce's GAAP net income. And now Salesforce is putting that lab's model inside its own product. Supplier, balance-sheet asset, and alleged displacer, all in one counterparty, inside eight days. The market has no clean framework for pricing that, and neither did this show until today.

The new fact is Claudeforce. Salesforce and Anthropic announced a plugin that embeds Claude's reasoning directly into Salesforce's CRM data, workflows and governance layer, shipping with thirty-seven prebuilt sales skills, in open beta starting in September. Be precise about what that is and isn't. It is an open beta. There is no disclosed pricing, no named customer, and no revenue attached to it. Treat it as an announcement, not a deployment.

The strategic read matters regardless of timing. For two years, Salesforce sold Einstein as its first-party model differentiator. Claudeforce is Salesforce conceding the reasoning layer to a third party and rebuilding its moat one level down, around CRM data, workflow and governance instead of the model itself. That is a narrower claim than the one Salesforce has been selling investors, even if it is the more honest one. It also sets a template. If Claudeforce works commercially, HubSpot, Microsoft Dynamics and SAP face the same choice: embed a rival lab's model, or keep defending a first-party stack nobody asked for. The vendors with the weakest own-model stacks move first, because they have the least to give up.

Now the balance-sheet wrinkle. That two-point-six-billion-dollar gain is a valuation mark on a private stake, not cash from customers, and it reverses if Anthropic's next round prices flat or down. There is a second-order effect worth naming here that goes well past Salesforce. An enterprise software company now carries a frontier lab's private valuation inside its GAAP net income. If other incumbents take similar stakes as the price of preferential model access, the sector acquires a shared, correlated, non-operating swing factor. A single down round at a single lab would then hit several income statements in the same quarter, for reasons that have nothing to do with anyone's software. That is a new kind of exposure for a category investors buy for its predictability.

Separate that from the operating story, which is solid and unspectacular. Salesforce's second-quarter revenue was eleven-point-three-five billion dollars, up eleven percent year over year. Current remaining performance obligation reached thirty-three-point-five billion, up fourteen percent, against consensus near thirty-three-point-two. Earnings per share of five-ninety matched estimates rather than beating them, so the contract-obligation strength and the guidance raise, up two hundred million dollars to a range of forty-six-point-one to forty-six-point-four billion, are the corroborated signal, not the EPS line some wires framed as a beat. Combined Agentforce and Data Cloud annualized run-rate is nearly three-point-nine billion, up more than two hundred percent, with Agentforce alone above one-point-five billion. Useful context, but it is a vendor-defined run-rate that Salesforce does not decompose into production agent usage versus bundled or promotional attach. Do not read it as recognized revenue.

That gap, a vendor defining its own AI number in a unit nobody else reports, is exactly what this show flagged as the open question yesterday. So look at the one vendor that actually publishes a comparable line. ServiceNow's AI annual contract value crossed one billion dollars in the quarter reported July 22nd, with net-new AI contract value accelerating more than forty percent sequentially, against subscription revenue growth of twenty-four-and-a-half percent. That is a five-week-old print resurfacing in this week's comparative coverage, not fresh news on its own. But it answers what nobody else discloses.

And on that earnings call, chief executive Bill McDermott went further. Asked directly by an analyst about seat compression, he said fifty percent of ServiceNow's net-new business is already non-seat-based. That is management's own words under a direct challenge, not a secondary aggregator's estimate, which is stronger evidence than this show had going in. The deal shape corroborates it. ServiceNow logged a hundred twenty-three transactions above a million dollars of net-new contract value in the quarter, up nearly forty percent year over year, and ended with six hundred fifty-eight customers above five million dollars. Those are not seat renewals creeping upward. They are large multi-product commitments, which is what non-seat pricing looks like on the way in. Chief financial officer Gina Mastantuono also disclosed a ninety-eight percent renewal rate the same quarter, though she did not state it as specific to the AI or non-seat cohort, so do not read it as proof those particular contracts stick.

The question that remains unresolved, even in the full call transcript, is whether that AI contract value is incremental new spend or existing workflow contracts relabeled at renewal. ServiceNow discloses the target and the product bundle. It does not disclose the accounting mechanics that would settle it either way. That matters for how you read every vendor in this group, because a durable shift to non-seat pricing compresses near-term revenue recognition even where it improves long-term price-to-value alignment. Contract value and remaining performance obligation become the metrics that carry the information. Headline subscription growth becomes less informative than it was, which is convenient for management and inconvenient for anyone trying to check them.

That is the same escape from the seat, executed with a contract vehicle instead of a price list. CrowdStrike is running a third version of it, through a committed consumption envelope. This show already covered CrowdStrike's beat and its two-point-two-nine-billion-dollar Falcon Flex run-rate. What is new is management's forward answer. CrowdStrike raised full-year net-new annual recurring revenue guidance by a hundred sixteen million dollars, to roughly one-point-three-five billion, and added nine hundred thirty-five new Flex accounts in the quarter, more than the prior three quarters combined. Chief executive George Kurtz called it an arms race, arguing AI is driving both more attacks and more security spending.

But CrowdStrike discloses no AI revenue attribution at all. What it does disclose is bundling, customers consolidating point products from rivals like SentinelOne and Palo Alto into one committed contract. That is displacement of competitors' budgets, not necessarily new security spend, and consolidation land-grabs exhaust. Nine hundred thirty-five adds in one quarter is a land-grab pace, not a steady state. Note the comparison as well: the year-over-year acceleration in net-new commitments runs against a prior-year quarter still depressed by the 2024 outage's sales disruption, so part of that growth rate is an easier base rather than pure demand.

So three vendors, one underlying move. ServiceNow bills AI as contract value instead of licenses. CrowdStrike bills a consumption envelope that customers fill by swapping tools. Salesforce bills an annualized agent run-rate it defines itself. The market's fear was that AI would eat per-seat software revenue. The vendors' actual response has been to stop selling seats before that happens.

That cuts both ways for the buyer. Outcome and consumption pricing align price to value, which is the honest version of the pitch. But it also strips out the one cost lever a chief information officer could always pull, which is headcount, and replaces it with usage the vendor meters. When the bill scales with agent activity rather than employees, a successful deployment is indistinguishable from a budget overrun until the invoice arrives. That is the question to ask about Agentforce and Falcon Flex alike, and neither vendor has published the unit economics to answer it.

What would prove this durable is next quarter's renewal cohort on Falcon Flex holding up, and ServiceNow finally stating whether its AI dollars are incremental. What would prove it cosmetic is AI-labelled contract value climbing while total subscription growth flattens underneath it. Watch the buckets, not the branding. And watch what happens to the losers, SentinelOne's and Palo Alto's own numbers, not just the winner's.
