On Thursday it looked like the market had started pricing AI by who carries the balance-sheet risk. Public money moved out of debt-financed AI infrastructure and into platform software. By Friday, that move had reversed. The reversal is the real story, because it shows how little a single session can prove. After that, we'll look at Oracle's balance sheet, and at a private-market deal that points the other way.

Start with Thursday, October 15th. Enterprise software names rallied together. According to Invezz, Salesforce gained almost four percent, Adobe rose more than three percent, and Oracle climbed two-point-eight percent. Invezz tied the move explicitly to a semiconductor sell-off and to concerns about heavily indebted artificial intelligence companies. Benzinga put it more bluntly, describing money flowing from debt-laden AI names into enterprise software. ServiceNow was the headline name in that coverage. A day earlier, TD Cowen had raised its ServiceNow price target to one-sixty-five from one-forty.

The read at the time was a cost-of-capital argument, not a demand argument. Investors seemed to be separating AI capacity funded with debt from AI sold as a feature on top of recurring subscriptions. That fit the arc this show has tracked all week. Banks struggled to offload Oracle-linked construction debt. CoreWeave's bonds traded near a fifteen percent yield. Oracle's credit default swaps hit a record. On Thursday, it looked like that credit stress had finally reached equity allocation.

It didn't last. On Friday, October 16th, the same names gave the move back. According to 24/7 Wall Street, ServiceNow was down five percent, to one-forty-one-sixty-eight. Salesforce was down four percent, to two-thirty-eight-oh-six. Palantir slid by a similar amount, and the IGV software ETF fell four percent as well. Here's the part that matters. The press attributed Friday's decline to the same explanation it used for Thursday's rally: doubts about how AI infrastructure spending gets financed. When one explanation covers opposite moves on consecutive days, it's being fitted after the fact.

Adobe adds one more wrinkle. Wells Fargo downgraded it on October 12th, and the stock fell. Three days later it rallied more than three percent with the group. A stock that goes from downgrade casualty to rotation beneficiary in three sessions is trading on flows, not on news about its business.

So the honest read is that Thursday was one volatile session inside a choppy pattern, not a re-rating. Suppose the split between borrowed capacity and subscription software does persist. Then it would raise the relative cost of capital for Oracle and the neoclouds, and favor vendors that monetize AI through existing contracts. That's an inference, and the evidence for it so far is one day long.

Oracle deserves its own look, because it sat in both baskets on Thursday. One outlet, journalarta, had Oracle down seven percent by ten past ten in the morning, on renewed concern about financing its Project Jupiter data center. Yet the rotation coverage had it climbing. Those two reports aren't a sourcing conflict. TradingKey reports that Oracle recovered on institutional dip-buying and closed the session up three-point-oh-seven percent. So the seven percent drop was the morning trough, and the rally figure was a later read on the same session.

The balance sheet behind that swing is worth sitting with. These figures come from that same secondary report, not from an Oracle filing. It puts Oracle's cash at thirty-seven-point-one billion dollars, against a debt load of a hundred-sixty-nine-point-one billion. Annual cash burn runs near twenty-nine billion, close to four-fifths of that cash pile in a single year. And OpenAI, the anchor customer, isn't required to make payments on the data center before 2027.

That's the real mechanism. Oracle's problem is duration. Cash goes out now to build capacity, and the biggest contracted revenue stream arrives later. Someone has to finance that gap. The bull rebuttal comes from Mizuho's analyst, Panigrahi, who pointed to continued construction progress on Project Jupiter. It's a fair point. A contract with a large counterparty is exactly what you're supposed to finance capex against. Note, too, that Oracle's downgrade to one notch above junk happened back in July. It's the backdrop everyone keeps citing, but it isn't this week's news.

So the live question for Oracle isn't whether demand is real. It's whether lenders keep extending credit on schedule until OpenAI's payments start landing. That makes lender appetite, not customer demand, the binding variable. And as we said on Thursday, enterprise buyers signing multi-year cloud commitments should weigh the provider's balance sheet alongside price and delivery dates.

While public markets argued over who should carry AI infrastructure debt, private markets kept paying up for the software layer that runs on that capacity. Modal, an AI infrastructure startup, announced it crossed one billion dollars in annualized run rate. It raised a seven-hundred-fifty-million-dollar Series D at a fifteen-point-seven-five-billion-dollar post-money valuation. That's roughly sixteen times run rate. The company says revenue grew more than fifteen times year over year.

The most interesting line is Sandboxes, Modal's isolated code-execution product for AI agents. On its own, it runs at a three-hundred-million-dollar annualized rate, with close to three billion sandboxes spun up so far. If that holds up, agent execution is becoming its own budget line rather than a feature inside someone else's platform. That would be a startup creating a category, not an incumbent absorbing one.

Now the caveats, because every one of those figures is self-reported in Modal's own funding announcement. There's no disclosed gross margin, no customer concentration, and no churn. A run rate is an annualized snapshot, not recognized revenue. And we don't know whether that growth rests on a handful of AI-native customers or a broad enterprise base. One more contrast with Oracle. Modal's announcement says nothing about debt. The market may be distinguishing capacity owners from capacity software, but nothing disclosed proves Modal is capital-light.

There's also an obvious competitive risk. With sandboxes now a visible category, the hyperscalers have every reason to build their own agent-execution services rather than treat Modal as a complementary vendor. That's an inference, but it's the first thing a sixteen-times multiple has to survive.

Now put that multiple next to what an incumbent is earning from its own agents. Salesforce's most recent disclosed figure, from its second-quarter fiscal twenty-twenty-seven results in late summer, put Agentforce annual recurring revenue above one-point-five billion dollars. That was up more than two hundred forty percent year over year. So Salesforce's flagship agent business is about one and a half times the run rate of a single infrastructure startup. Both metrics are vendor-defined, so treat the comparison as directional, not like-for-like.

If Thursday's rotation was about buying clearer AI monetization inside existing platforms, that's the scale of monetization on offer. It's real, but it's small relative to the capital being deployed around it. The rotation was buying a story about AI revenue more than a proven quantity of it.
