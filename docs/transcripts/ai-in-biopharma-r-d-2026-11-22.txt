Good morning, this is your AI in biopharma R&D briefing for November 22, 2026.

Isomorphic Labs announced the largest venture round in biotech history five days ago. Three days later, a lower-profile rival claimed to match Isomorphic's flagship design engine on potency prediction. Then it gave its training data away. If the model isn't the hard part, where does the money actually go? Today we follow that question from a benchmark claim to the cash pharma is paying out.

Start with the challenge. On November 20, Genesis Molecular AI announced a potency-prediction model called Ruby. Ruby is built on Genesis's own cofolding foundation model, Pearl. It predicts potency without generating an explicit binding pose for each compound. Genesis says Ruby posted the highest average correlation of any method it evaluated across four public potency benchmarks. That field included Isomorphic's design engine, IsoDDE, and physics-based free-energy methods. Listen to Genesis's own verbs. It says Ruby "edges out" IsoDDE, while "significantly outperforming" other models such as Boltz-2. At its lowest setting, Genesis says, Ruby scores a ligand in 25 milliseconds. Treat that as a floor on latency, not a promise of full accuracy at that speed.

The timing is pointed. Genesis spoke one day after Isomorphic signed an oncology discovery deal with Merck, built around IsoDDE. It spoke three days after Isomorphic's five-billion-dollar Series C, which took its disclosed venture funding to seven point seven billion dollars. And Genesis is not a garage project. Its release lists backers including Andreessen Horowitz, NVIDIA, T. Rowe Price and Fidelity. Andreessen Horowitz also joined Isomorphic's Series C. So at least one investor now sits on both sides of this benchmark fight.

Now the caveat that changes what you can take from this. Go looking for the number behind "edges out," and there isn't one. On the four public benchmarks, Genesis publishes no correlation values for any method, Ruby included. It also doesn't say whether it ran IsoDDE itself or relied on Isomorphic's own reporting. The one hard figure sits elsewhere, a zero-shot average Spearman correlation of 0.61, across eight assays from five programs run with Incyte. Genesis gives no IsoDDE number on those assays, so don't hear 0.61 as Ruby's margin over Isomorphic. Those Incyte assays are proprietary partner data, and nobody outside can rerun them. And all of this is retrospective. Rank correlation against potency data that already exists doesn't show Ruby makes better calls in a live program. On leakage, Genesis describes only a training cutoff before January 2023, which it never ties to the test sets. IsoDDE's own benchmark record is self-reported too. So this is one vendor's scorecard set against another's. The honest headline is rough parity, and with no independent evaluation, even parity is unverified.

That makes this the third vendor-graded chemistry scorecard this show has covered in four days. We covered NVIDIA's LitmusBench on the 19th, and OpenAI's MedChemBench yesterday. Genesis partly breaks the pattern. Alongside Ruby, it released a curated training set from the public potency literature, called GenesisPotency-2026. It's under a Creative Commons share-alike license. Outside groups can now train competing models on the same data. What they still can't do is rerun the head-to-head.

Why does this matter commercially? The free-energy comparison has more teeth than the IsoDDE one. Free-energy calculations are the compute-heavy standard for ranking potency in lead optimization. Genesis's claim there is narrower, though. It says Ruby equals or exceeds free-energy methods on two of the four benchmarks, OpenFE and FEP plus 4. Suppose a pose-free model really matches that accuracy at millisecond speed. Then the throughput economics of a core workflow change. Vendors of physics-based free-energy software would face pricing pressure. So would the computational-chemistry services that contract research organizations sell. That's our inference, and it hangs on outside validation that doesn't exist yet.

There's a second pressure point, and it lands on the closed platforms. A credible parity claim raises the cost of staying unaudited. Isomorphic and its peers may need independent or blinded benchmarks, or watch their state-of-the-art claims get discounted. Our read is that this could shape how the next private AI-design rounds get marked.

Now the IP angle. Genesis gives away the curated data but keeps Pearl and Ruby closed. Our read is that it's commoditizing the complement. Curating public potency data stops being a moat. The defensible assets become the model, and proprietary assay data like Incyte's. One open question sits underneath. How share-alike terms apply to a model trained on that data is legally unsettled. Here's the takeaway. If the parity claim survives outside testing, the closed engine behind seven point seven billion dollars of funding isn't uniquely capable at this task. The model looks like a thinner moat than the money implies.

If the model isn't the moat, follow the cash. Recursion Pharmaceuticals supplied the clearest cash number this week. At the Jefferies healthcare conference on November 18, Recursion's chief executive said the company had just crossed about five hundred thirty-five million dollars in partnership cash inflows. The summary doesn't say over what period that total built up, so don't hear it as an eighteen-month figure. What he did tie to roughly the last eighteen months is the milestone count, nine to ten milestones. That's conference commentary relayed through a transcript summary, not a filing. Still, it's a real, quoted figure for what pharma pays an AI-native discovery company. Set it against Merck and Isomorphic, whose financial terms are undisclosed.

Recursion's Sanofi deal adds a contingent layer. Sanofi optioned a Recursion molecule that could bring in about three hundred forty-three million dollars in milestones, plus double-digit royalties. That's a ceiling, not cash received. Recursion also says its Roche and Genentech collaboration began with target identification from its biology maps. It is now expanding into molecule design.

That forces a correction to our own read. Yesterday, this show suggested that buyers like Novartis and Novo Nordisk were replicating design platforms in-house. Sanofi complicates that. On Friday it talked up its in-house AI gains, yet it is still optioning a Recursion molecule. Roche signed Chai Discovery for design work, as we covered on the 20th. Now Roche has Recursion doing design too. So pharma is building and buying at once, and spreading design across vendors rather than anointing one. Our inference is that pharma isn't mainly paying for any particular model. It pays for proprietary biology output, and for options on the molecules that biology produces.

Now to the clinic, where Recursion also has news. It disclosed an early signal for REC-1245, a candidate targeting RBM39. Early monotherapy testing produced three partial responses, two of them confirmed. There's no disclosed denominator, so no response rate can be computed. It's a signal, not a rate. And early oncology monotherapy responses often fail to hold up in expansion. The real test is an endometrial cancer expansion cohort, due to read out in the second half of 2027. One more discipline point. Recursion's remarks, as reported, don't say what role AI played in finding REC-1245, so don't file it as AI-designed.

Isomorphic, by contrast, still has no program in human trials. Its year-end target for first clinical trials is a company goal, not a filed IND. About five to six weeks remain on that clock.

So, pulling the threads together. The biggest open question is whether anyone outside Genesis runs Ruby against IsoDDE on blinded data. But even a verified win would sharpen chemistry against a target someone already chose. On the 20th, this show argued that much of Phase 2 attrition comes from the wrong mechanism or the wrong disease, not the wrong chemistry. That's our standing judgment, not a sourced statistic. If it holds, the realistic lift to clinical success rates from this chemistry layer is modest. Any bigger gain would most likely come from selection, meaning picking the right target and the right disease. The clearer effect is on spend. As we read it, R&D budgets tilt toward generating proprietary data and buying options on real assets, not toward licensing models. Three things to watch. First, any independent or blinded evaluation of Ruby or IsoDDE. Second, whether Isomorphic files an IND before year-end. Third, whether REC-1245 gets a denominator, and survives its expansion cohort.

That's today's briefing. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
