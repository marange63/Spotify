Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Credit & the Corporate Credit Cycle

Transcript · August 17, 2026

Good morning. It's Monday, August 17th, and this is your private credit briefing. The through-line today: the cycle is turning at the margin, and the mechanisms that would let you see it clearly are loosening at the exact same moment.

Start with the Wall Street Journal's analysis of the big listed direct-lending vehicles, published last Wednesday. It found nonperforming rates at Ares, Blackstone, Blue Owl, Golub and KKR funds at their highest levels since at least 2021 — worse, on the Journal's read, than the stress of the 2022-2023 hiking cycle. The one hard number in that analysis is Blue Owl's: its flagship fund's non-performing loan rate hit 2.8% in the second quarter, a five-year high. Watchlists at Ares, Golub and KKR funds swelled to levels the Journal says haven't been seen since the last hiking cycle. And this lands against a direct, on-the-record denial: Blue Owl's co-CEO Marc Lipschultz told analysts that "across our direct lending strategy, credit health remains strong," with "no meaningful change" in the watchlist versus a year ago. That's the marketed number sitting next to the harder number, and this week they don't agree.

But before you take the deterioration as uniform, a primary-source check complicates it. Ares Capital's own second-quarter release shows non-accruals at cost rising only slightly, to 2.4% from 2.1%, still below the firm's roughly 3% average since the financial crisis. Blackstone Secured Lending's non-accruals actually improved, to 1.8% of fair value from 3.1% the prior quarter — the opposite of a five-year peak. So Blue Owl's number is real and it is a five-year high, but the "five of the largest lenders" framing doesn't survive contact with the filings for Ares and Blackstone specifically — those two books look closer to normal, or improving. Treat the Journal's read as directionally suggestive of stress, not as five confirmed data points. And no one has disaggregated how much of any of this is genuine payment default versus PIK toggles and amend-and-extend keeping loans technically current — the very distinction that separates a real credit-cycle turn from a flattered one.

Now, a related but distinct question: even where the marks aren't obviously deteriorating, are they trustworthy at all? FS KKR Capital's second-quarter numbers, out earlier this month, give you a clean test. Net asset value fell again, to $18.30 per share as of June 30th from $18.83 at the end of March — continuing a slide from $20.89 at year-end. Non-accruals actually eased, to 3.8% of fair value from 4.2%, and leverage came down, net debt-to-equity to 122% from 131%. On paper, a mixed-to-improving picture. But look at what management is doing with its own capital: under a $300 million buyback program, FSK repurchased about $40 million of stock through August 5th at a weighted average price of $10.73 a share — against a reported NAV of $18.30. That's a board buying its own credit book at roughly 40% below what it's telling shareholders that book is worth. When the people with the best information available transact at a discount that large to their own official mark, that's the market's verdict on mark-to-model reality, delivered by management itself.

That valuation-opacity problem isn't confined to BDCs — it shows up in its most serious form inside the insurance-funded lending model. Delaware Life and Clear Spring, insurers controlled by Dodgers owner Mark Walter and tied to Guggenheim, disclosed in June 26th regulatory filings that they'd failed to report billions of dollars of private-credit holdings that actually backed other Walter-controlled ventures. The restatement is stark: Delaware Life's related-party investments jumped to at least $17 billion, or at least 39% of total invested assets, from a previously reported $1.4 billion, or 3%. A $16 billion gap between what was disclosed and what was real. Both companies received grand jury subpoenas back in February, and the SEC is running a parallel investigation. Delaware Life says it's now executing a remediation plan to cut affiliated exposure, cited in a recent S&P Global Ratings note. No charges have been filed — this is a disclosed investigation and a voluntary restatement, not an adjudicated fraud finding. But structurally, it's the sharpest available case of the insurance-balance-sheet model's core risk: capital that's supposed to be independently underwritten instead getting recycled into the sponsor's own deals, invisibly, until a filing forces it into the open.

That's exactly the kind of opacity you'd want regulators tightening up on right now. Instead, the SEC just loosened it, in a different corner of the market. Staff guidance issued August 10th exempts bonds backed by data-center contracts from the standard asset-backed-securities risk-retention rule — the requirement that an issuer keep at least 5% of the risk on its own books so it can't offload everything to investors. The SEC's reasoning is that data centers aren't "financial assets that liquidate over time" the way auto loans or mortgages are, so they don't need the same skin-in-the-game discipline. This is happening as data-center ABS issuance has grown from $2.4 billion in 2020 to $15.5 billion last year, and it's on pace for a record this year. Removing the mechanism that aligns issuer and investor incentives, right as volume scales fastest, is close to a textbook setup for underwriting quality to lag issuance.

And this is exactly the structure that channels capital toward the concentration risk this show has been tracking for days: Nvidia's push to mobilize more than $500 billion in third-party financing — from Apollo, Blackstone, BlackRock, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR — to fund hyperscaler and frontier-lab data-center buildouts, with Nvidia itself able to backstop up to $125 billion, or a quarter, of the resulting deals. Jensen Huang calls Nvidia's chips "an investable asset class." That's still marketing running ahead of structure: these are non-binding memoranda of understanding, with no deployed capital, pricing or final structure disclosed. The backstop is also worth a second look — a first-loss position that size could concentrate chip-obsolescence risk back onto Nvidia rather than spreading it, the opposite of what "investable asset class" implies. Whether any of that $500 billion actually gets drawn remains the open catalyst.

So where does the cycle actually stand? Turning at the margin, not yet in distress — Blue Owl's number is genuine, FSK's own buybacks say reported marks are running ahead of realizable value, and the Delaware Life probe shows the insurance-funded model's opacity risk is live, not theoretical. But the "five lenders at five-year highs" framing doesn't survive a primary-source check for Ares and Blackstone Secured Lending specifically — be skeptical of both the managers' reassurance and the loudest version of the deterioration story. The binding risk right now is valuation opacity and loosening disclosure, not headline spread levels. Watch three things next: whether Ares and Golub's own next filings clarify the watchlist claims the Journal made, whether the Delaware Life probe widens beyond one insurer, and whether any real dollars move under Nvidia's compute-financing platform.

That's the briefing. Have a good week.
