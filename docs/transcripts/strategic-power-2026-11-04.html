Cautious Optimism Briefings

Strategic Power, Energy, and Institutional Change

Transcript · November 4, 2026

Good morning. It's Wednesday, November 4th, 2026, and this is Strategic Power.

The short version of today: the AI power shortage has stopped being a pricing story and become a rationing story. Grids can't serve everyone, so someone has to decide who waits, who leaves, and who builds their own power plant.

One note before we start. Results from yesterday's votes, including the local data-center ballot measures we flagged, were not in when this briefing was written. The same goes for the post-vote Iran decision we flagged on Monday. There's no new reporting on it yet. So nothing here assumes an outcome on either.

Start with the clearest capital-allocation signal. In October, Microsoft pulled out as anchor customer for Nscale's data center in Loughton, England. The reason was power. The local utility said it couldn't deliver enough until the early-to-mid 2030s. Compare that with Northern Virginia. The International Energy Agency says a 100-megawatt grid hookup there takes about seven years. Seven years is already a long wait. Loughton's date runs longer still, and Microsoft decided not to sit through it. Keep the scale honest. This is one site. It is not evidence that Microsoft is cutting capex. The better read is that Microsoft is moving capex toward places where the power date is closer. So the takeaway is that grid timing now decides not just when AI capacity comes online, but where it gets built at all.

Where the grid can serve some projects but not all of them, the state starts choosing. That's what is happening in Texas, in a fight that has been building since the summer. In an August 3rd letter, Governor Greg Abbott ordered a full audit of the data-center projects in ERCOT's interconnection queue. That queue holds an estimated 474 gigawatts of requests. Abbott put that in context himself. He said it is more than five times Texas's record peak electricity demand. And he said about 90 percent of the new requests are data centers. His instruction was blunt. Any data center project that fails to comply with the verification and audit process must be denied.

This isn't this week's news, but it frames everything else today. A queue five times the grid's record peak can't all be real demand. Much of it is speculative applications, filed just to hold a spot in line rather than to pour concrete. So the audit isn't a ban on data centers. It's a filter. The queue used to work on first come, first served. It's becoming verify or be denied. On October 29th, this desk said whoever already holds the interconnection holds the scarce asset. Texas is now deciding who gets to keep theirs. My read is that the winners are projects with real financing, land, and verified load, plus owners of generation already connected to the grid. The losers are speculative developers, and the flippers trading land and power rights behind the queue. There's a market implication too, and it's inference. Investors who treat announced hyperscaler capex as committed megawatts may be underpricing state-level gatekeeping risk. Texas has the most build-friendly grid in the country, and it's the one running the audit.

And where developers won't wait for the grid at all, they leave it. The workaround is on-site generation from converted jet engines, rebuilt to burn natural gas. TechCrunch reported yesterday that data centers are turning to these old engines as the wait for power grows. The marquee example sits inside Texas. Crusoe, which is building OpenAI's Stargate campus in Abilene, ordered 13 converted units from ProEnergy back in April. xAI has gone the same way. Citing the New York Times, TechCrunch reports that xAI has installed eight converted turbines in Mississippi to help power its Colossus 2 data center. These machines carry a real penalty. They're less efficient and pollute more than turbines built for conventional gas plants, and it takes more of them to make the same power. No one has published a reliable cost figure for that penalty, so treat it as real but unpriced.

Two second-order effects follow, both inference. First, this is demand for fast-deploy gas equipment. That favors remanufacturers like ProEnergy and the turbine makers behind them. It's also gas demand that grows independently of the LNG export story. Second, and more important, that gas burn sits behind the meter. It's outside the grid planning process the Texas audit is trying to fix. So the audit can ration the queue as hard as it likes. The largest players are increasingly not in the queue.

The permanent fix for all of this is on a calendar, not yet on a grid. Nuclear is the firm supply the hyperscalers are contracting for. The marquee deal is Google's with Constellation Energy, struck in early October. Per Bloomberg, it will add 890 megawatts of new reactor capacity to the PJM grid, with power arriving as soon as 2028. That's the one number to hold, and "as soon as" is the best case. Amazon's commitment from mid-October funds 12 small modular reactors in Indiana, 960 megawatts in all, and carries no in-service date. There's a fuel wrinkle too. On October 31st, this desk flagged that new US enrichment capacity is due by 2029. That's a year after the waivers allowing Russian imports expire. Reactor output arriving in 2028 inherits that timing risk. Call it a risk, not a guaranteed shortage.

The one fresh nuclear item is abroad. On November 3rd, South Korea's Export-Import Bank and Turkey Nuclear Energy signed a memorandum of understanding to explore financing for Turkish energy projects. Seoul wants a role building Turkey's planned second nuclear plant, at Sinop on the Black Sea coast. That's an exploratory step, not a construction award. But it shows allied reactor exporters competing to attach state finance to foreign projects. So here's the point to land. A signed power-purchase agreement is not a megawatt on the ground. The rationing in Texas and the gas workarounds in Abilene and Mississippi will run their course well before this nuclear capacity exists.

The same gap between scheduled and delivered runs through European security. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov told reporters that Russia, Ukraine, and the US will hold talks in Abu Dhabi on November 10th and 11th. Putin aide Vladimir Medinsky will lead Moscow's side. The core disagreement hasn't moved. Russia still demands that Ukraine cede all of the Donbas region, including territory Russia has not taken militarily. Kyiv rejects giving up any land it currently holds. Last month, the two sides reportedly agreed a local ceasefire in Oleshky, in Kherson. But that's a truce over one town, not a framework. Here's my inference on the Russian position. Demanding more territory than the battlefield has delivered suggests Moscow thinks time is on its side. It doesn't suggest readiness to settle. So treat Abu Dhabi as choreography until one side moves on territory.

That matters for European capital, and again this is inference. If the talks stall, which is the base case, Europe's current defense-spending and energy-security posture holds. On October 30th, we noted that Britain funded its defense plan by reprioritizing capital across Whitehall rather than borrowing. A real settlement would test whether pledges built that way survive a lower threat premium. It would also reopen arguments over sanctions relief, frozen Russian assets, and the risk premium in European gas. None of that has a number attached yet.

One calendar note. November 10th is also the day China's suspension of its expanded rare-earth export controls is due to lapse. So a single week carries both a trade-truce test and a war-diplomacy test.

Three things to watch. First, whether the Texas audit produces visible denials, or stays procedural. Second, whether another hyperscaler walks away from a power-constrained site, the way Microsoft did in Loughton. Third, Abu Dhabi on November 10th and 11th, landing the same week as Beijing's rare-earth date.

The tie across all of it: control over access to power is moving away from press releases and toward regulators and gas turbines.

That's Strategic Power for November 4th. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
