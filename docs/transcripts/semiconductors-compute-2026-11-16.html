Cautious Optimism Briefings

Semiconductors & Compute Infrastructure

Transcript · November 16, 2026

Good morning, it's Monday, November 16th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

On Thursday, Jensen Huang resigned as Nvidia's president, chief executive and board member. CFO Colette Kress is now interim CEO and keeps the CFO title, and Harvey Jones became the company's first-ever board chair. Those mechanics have been well aired. What's new today is what a full trading week says the market was actually pricing. And the capacity calendar gives us a way to test that price.

Start with the tape, and with a callback. Early last week, this desk tracked two straight sessions of Nvidia falling while AMD and Broadcom rose. On Saturday, we reported that pattern breaking on Friday. Now we have the full week, and per CNBC, Nvidia fell 11 percent across it.

Its rivals moved the other way. AMD gained 30 percent on the week, and Broadcom gained 24 percent. Both then gave back part of those gains on Friday, and outlets disagree on exactly how much. That disagreement is itself a sign of a repricing that hasn't settled.

The breadth of the move is the more telling part. CNBC reports TSMC rallied about 11 percent over the same stretch, a mirror image of Nvidia's loss. The bid also reached analog and industrial names. Texas Instruments climbed about 13 percent, with Analog Devices and Infineon close behind. Those companies don't compete with Nvidia for an accelerator socket. When they rally on Nvidia leadership news, it reads less like a verdict on silicon and more like capital rotating out of concentration risk. The so-what: this was a sentiment move, and no change in accelerator allocation or lost design socket has been reported.

So what was the market pricing? SemiAnalysis's Dylan Patel offered the sharpest framing, in comments to CNBC. In his words, "given they're only a year ahead of their biggest competitors, it's not like the gap is massive." His argument is that Nvidia's real edge is the supply chain and scale Huang built, not a durable technical moat. That's an analyst's judgment, and "a year ahead" is an opinion, not a measurement. Mizuho's Farzin Azam put the rivals' appeal more simply. He told CNBC, "they don't have this overhang of a management disruption."

Now separate the claim into its parts. Architecture leadership is one dimension, and nothing last week touched it. Supply-chain and allocation leverage is a second, and that's the one Patel says is exposed. Leadership continuity is a third, and it's the one an interim CEO puts in question. Software ecosystem lock-in is a fourth, and nothing in last week's news bears on it at all. So the tape moved on two dimensions, continuity and supply chain. It did not move on product.

Take the market's reading at its strongest first. If Huang personally held the supplier relationships, his exit weakens Nvidia's hand in every allocation negotiation. Patel's other line supports exactly that: "if Nvidia can't muster the supply chain, then technology is the only competitive advantage." The counter-reading is that the leverage lives in contracts, prepayments and multi-year commitments, and contracts don't resign. Nvidia's board leans that way. It says it expects "the company's operations, customer commitments and product roadmap to continue without interruption." But that's the company's claim about itself, not independent evidence. The way to arbitrate between the two readings is the capacity calendar.

If the lead is allocation of something scarce, the question is how long it stays scarce. Start with TSMC's advanced packaging. This is October context, not news from the last few days. Goldman Sachs, as relayed by TrendForce, estimates CoWoS capacity at about 1.275 million wafers for 2026. It sees that rising to 2.73 million in 2027, more than a doubling in a single year. Those are Goldman's estimates, not TSMC disclosures.

The pricing signal cuts in an interesting direction. TrendForce, citing Taiwanese sources, reports that TSMC is eyeing a roughly 20 percent CoWoS price increase. TSMC hasn't confirmed it. A price hike alongside a capacity doubling supports two readings. One is that demand is still outrunning even doubled supply, which keeps the allocation lever alive into 2027. The other is that TSMC is pricing scarcity at its peak, just before supply catches up. Either way, packaging scarcity is on a clock, and that clock reads 2027, not this week.

That dates a constraint this desk named on November 11th. We said then that a buyer can sign a second-source hedge this quarter. But the hedge only becomes deployed racks when packaging and memory slots open. Goldman's estimate now puts a rough date on the packaging half. Our inference is that this is when hyperscaler custom-ASIC programs gain real bargaining power, because allocation stops favoring the incumbent by default. At that point, Patel's one-year technical lead has to defend itself without scarcity behind it. That's Nvidia's genuine exposure, and it runs on a 2027 timetable, not on last week's headlines.

Meanwhile, TSMC collects rent whoever wins the socket. Our inference is that a higher packaging price either squeezes accelerator gross margins or gets passed to the hyperscalers buying the parts. That holds whichever logo is on the chip. TrendForce also reports that TSMC's 2027 and 2028 capex forecasts have been revised up. The most bullish brokerage estimate reaches 105 billion dollars for 2028, which is the high end, not consensus. Even so, it lengthens the runway for the tool cycle this desk said yesterday had passed its first test. The takeaway: TSMC is paid on nearly every outcome of the accelerator race.

But the gate doesn't disappear when packaging loosens. It moves to memory, and memory is already spoken for. This is late-October guidance, so treat it as backdrop. SK hynix says more than 50 percent of its 2027 and 2028 capacity is already committed under long-term agreements. Management says it "expects the structure in which demand exceeds supply to persist through 2030." Hold the obvious caveat. SK hynix is the largest HBM seller, and it's talking its own book. Committed capacity can also be renegotiated if accelerator demand slows.

Micron points the same way. On November 11th, this desk noted that Micron had signed agreements covering the vast majority of its 2027 high-bandwidth memory. Per Seoul Economic Daily, Micron has locked in contracts for most of that 2027 output at prices above this year's levels. The same outlet projects HBM4 contract prices roughly doubling next year. That's a press projection, not company guidance, so treat it as directional. And nothing in the reporting says which buyers hold those slots, so don't assume Nvidia holds the largest share.

Here's why the memory picture matters more than the CEO story. These are signed contracts, not personal relationships. Whoever signed them, 2027's accelerator output was largely decided by deals already on paper. That bounds how fast a leadership shock at Nvidia can turn into lost share. The memory to build a rival's racks isn't sitting open on the shelf. It also caps the custom-ASIC upside from a looser packaging market, because a hedge needs HBM as well as CoWoS. Through 2027, this is demand constrained by availability, not by economics. One more inference, kept to a sentence: as HBM absorbs contracted capacity, PCs, phones and cars face tighter conventional memory supply.

There's one more capacity lever on the supply map, and it belongs to Intel. On November 12th, this desk said Intel's 18A costs were falling fast, but that we had no yield figure. Now there's one, from a single analyst. GF Securities' Jeff Pu estimates 18A yield has climbed from about 65 percent in the second quarter to roughly 80 percent. Panther Lake chips on that node launched in January. That's not an Intel disclosure. The yield definition isn't stated either, so it can't be compared with any TSMC figure.

The catch is that Intel Foundry still has no confirmed external customer at the leading edge. Intel is still mostly building for itself. So an improving process doesn't yet change who can buy accelerator capacity in 2027. Call it progress on execution, not a new option on next year's supply map.

That sets up Tuesday, when Nvidia reports its fiscal third quarter. It will be Colette Kress's first call as interim chief executive. Those numbers don't exist yet, so there's no preview here. No timeline for a permanent CEO search has been disclosed either. So listen by dimension, not by digits. Does management speak the language of supply commitments and capacity allocation, the thing Patel calls the real moat? Does interim leadership sound fluent on supplier relationships, or does it lean entirely on the roadmap? And does anything said shift the packaging or memory calendars laid out today? Those are the tests. The CEO changed. The contracts, so far, haven't.

That's the briefing for November 16th. Thanks for listening, and I'll catch you next time.
