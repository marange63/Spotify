Good morning, it's Monday, September 28th, and this is your private equity briefing.

The most telling data point of the past couple of weeks isn't breaking news. It's an exit that looks nothing like the ones that have dominated recent coverage. H.I.G. Capital has signed a deal to sell General Datatech, an enterprise IT and networking provider it has owned since 2021, to the UK-listed reseller Softcat. The enterprise value is one point zero five billion dollars. The deal was signed on September 17th, and it has not closed. Softcat expects completion by the end of the first quarter of next year, subject to customary regulatory approvals.

Here's why this one is different. Look at the exit routes that have carried the story lately. Single-asset continuation vehicles are clearing close to the seller's own mark. An industry adviser tally of first-half deals puts the average discount to net asset value at under three percent, though that figure couldn't be pinned to a single primary report. NAV loans put borrowed money where a sale should be. And EQT closed its purchase of the secondaries specialist Coller Capital on August 31st. So EQT now earns fees from the exit shortage instead of resolving it. General Datatech breaks that pattern. Softcat is an unaffiliated, publicly listed buyer. It is paying a price negotiated at arm's length, and on completion that price arrives as cash. That's real price discovery, not a mark validated by an interested party.

There's a second layer worth naming. Softcat's own announcement spells out how it is paying. It is combining cash on hand and new bank debt with a three hundred fifty million pound equity placing. That placing is roughly a third of Softcat's total funding package. So a meaningful slice of the money comes fresh from UK public shareholders. Public equity investors are, in effect, underwriting part of this private exit. That's the private-to-public transmission line in concrete form, rather than as a theoretical channel.

Softcat also disclosed what it expects General Datatech to earn this calendar year. It projects about two hundred forty million dollars of gross profit, growing roughly thirty percent, and about eighty million dollars of EBITDA. Divide that EBITDA into the one-point-oh-five-billion-dollar price and you get roughly thirteen times. That's my arithmetic on two disclosed numbers, not a multiple Softcat stated, so treat it as implied, not reported.

Now the caveats, because this deal is easy to over-read. H.I.G.'s 2021 entry price isn't disclosed anywhere. So we have no idea what multiple on invested capital this exit represents. A clean exit price is not the same as a good return. And it's signed, not closed. No cash reaches H.I.G.'s limited partners, and nothing shows up in distributions to paid-in capital, until completion. One deal also isn't a trend. Nothing in the data shows strategic buyers taking a larger share of exits. So the lesson is narrow but real. This is what an actual exit looks like, priced by someone with no stake in flattering the seller. It's rare, and it's worth noticing precisely because it's rare.

It's rare because the usual buyer for a company like General Datatech has nearly disappeared. Foley and Lardner's September analysis of PitchBook and PwC data puts sponsor-to-sponsor sales at just ninety-four in the second quarter. That's the fewest in at least a decade. There are two ways to read that number. One is that the mid-market buyer of last resort has essentially vanished. The other is that assets which once would have traded sponsor-to-sponsor are being routed into continuation vehicles instead. There, the general partner keeps control and effectively sells to a vehicle it also manages. On that reading, the channel hasn't frozen so much as moved. What used to be an arm's-length sale has become a related-party transaction. The data can't tell us which story is right, and that ambiguity is itself the point. Either way, fewer exits carry a true third-party price.

Widen the lens and you see the stock-and-flow problem behind it. The same Foley analysis shows exit counts at midyear running well below 2025's pace. Deployment, meanwhile, has held up. Firms invested about five hundred forty-five billion dollars in the first half, on pace to match last year's one point one trillion. So capital is going into new deals as fast as last year, while deals coming back out run well behind. That flow feeds the stock. PitchBook data, reported by Bloomberg last week, put the number of unsold PE-backed companies at roughly thirty-three and a half thousand as of June. The stock keeps growing because the flow keeps pointing one way.

There's one more wrinkle, because the IPO market looks wide open. PwC counts about one hundred fourteen billion dollars raised in traditional IPOs through June, seven times the prior year's pace. But the same Bloomberg reporting found only about seventy PE-backed companies have gone public on US exchanges since 2022. So don't mistake a hot IPO tape for a thawed exit window. The aggregate proceeds are not reaching the broad sponsor-backed book. For most of those companies, that door is still closed.

Now to the entry side, because the forces shaping exits also shape what it costs to buy in. The exit door is narrow, and the entry door just got a little cheaper. Law360 reported this week that Delaware's Court of Chancery dismissed a shareholder suit over the Alteryx take-private. Insight Partners and Clearlake Capital bought Alteryx at forty-eight dollars and twenty-five cents a share, a total value of roughly four point four billion. The court held that the shareholder vote approving the deal wiped out the fiduciary-duty claims. That's the Corwin doctrine, which requires the vote to be fully informed and uncoerced. We're working from press reporting of the decision, not the opinion itself.

The practical effect is to shrink a tail risk sponsors price into take-privates, which is post-closing litigation. It also moves the burden onto disclosure quality, since a thin proxy forfeits the shield. But the ruling is narrower than it sounds. Alteryx was bought by outside sponsors, not insiders. So it doesn't reach management-led buyouts like the Priority Technology take-private announced last week. In the Priority deal, the special committee extracted a price increase of more than thirty percent over the buyer group's initial proposal. That's independent-committee negotiating leverage doing the work, not a Corwin shield. And a big bump over an insider's opening bid says as much about how low that bid started as about the committee's leverage.

Here's the so what on entry. A cheaper legal path for third-party take-privates supports more capital going into new buyouts. That means more future inventory entering a system whose exit channels are already narrow. Lower friction at entry doesn't relieve today's logjam. It adds to tomorrow's.

So here's where the cycle stands. The inventory of unrealized companies keeps growing, because deployment is matching last year's pace while exits run well behind it. The exits that do land come in two flavors. There are arm's-length strategic sales like General Datatech, which are genuinely clean but don't scale. And there are continuation vehicles and other related-party mechanisms, which scale but rest on marks the seller helped set. The biggest unresolved risk is still the exit-and-distribution logjam. Now there's a measurement problem layered on top of it, because more of the exit volume that does exist is priced by interested parties.

Watch three things from here. First, whether the General Datatech sale actually completes and turns into cash rather than a signed agreement. Second, the third-quarter sponsor-to-sponsor count, to see whether ninety-four was the floor or the start of a longer slide. Third, late October's alternative-manager earnings, and whether fee-related earnings again outrun realized carry.

That's the briefing. Have a good week.
