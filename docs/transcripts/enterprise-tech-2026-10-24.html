Good morning. It's Saturday, October 24th, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

We've already covered the headline earnings prints from SAP and IBM. Today is about what sits underneath those headlines: where the AI dollars actually land once you look past the top-line beat. The pattern across three stories this week is the same. Enterprise AI is redistributing revenue and margin inside incumbents. It is not yet adding much net-new money on top of them.

Start with IBM, because its numbers are the cleanest test of that idea. IBM's quarter was a modest beat at $17.1 billion. What's new is the mix underneath.

According to Futurum's summary of the results, IBM's consulting segment grew just two-tenths of a percent, to $5.34 billion. Essentially flat. But inside that flat segment, IBM says AI now makes up about 30 percent of consulting revenue. It also says AI is about 35 percent of consulting backlog. Backlog share running ahead of revenue share implies AI's slice of the business keeps rising. Put differently, AI work is taking over a bigger piece of a pie that isn't growing. That's substitution, not expansion. Clients appear to be redirecting existing systems-integration budget toward AI implementation rather than adding new money to the consulting line.

A caveat on that. The 30 and 35 percent figures are IBM's own classification, not independently verified. We don't know how broadly IBM defines "AI work." There's a real chance some of this is relabeling. Existing integration projects could be getting an AI tag because the client's broader initiative is AI-branded, even if the underlying work hasn't changed much. That's the competing reading, and it matters. It would mean the substitution story is partly taxonomic rather than real.

The likely second-order effect reaches beyond IBM. For integrators like Accenture, a flat top line with a rising AI share would point to a skills and headcount mix shift, not revenue growth. And if AI implementation work proves more automatable than legacy integration, pricing pressure follows.

What's not in doubt is where IBM's actual growth showed up: software. Software annual recurring revenue rose 8 percent, to $24.9 billion, far outpacing the flat consulting line. My read is that the durable economics sit with the platform owner, not the implementer. So the so-what: AI is changing what IBM's consultants do, not how much clients spend with them. Watch who owns the platform, because that's where AI is adding revenue rather than reshuffling it.

If services spend is being redirected rather than added, the next question is who pays for the AI itself. At SAP, the answer increasingly shows up on the margin line.

As we noted Friday, SAP's AI shows up in deal mix but not as a standalone revenue line. SAP's earnings call gives us the cost side of that bundling. CFO Dominik Asam said non-IFRS cloud gross margin came in at 74.3 percent, down seven-tenths of a point year over year. Management tied that to, quote, "investments to accelerate our AI transformation." CEO Christian Klein separately pointed to higher hardware prices as SAP invests in software and cloud. So if AI is free inside SAP's bundle, it isn't actually free. It's being paid for out of gross margin.

That margin squeeze isn't coming with a growth scare. SAP kept its 23-to-25 percent full-year cloud growth outlook and says it expects to land toward the top of that range, though Asam did note that one-off items added about a point to this quarter's cloud growth.

Now the skeptical read. A seven-tenths-of-a-point margin dip is small. It could reflect hardware mix or data-center transition costs as much as AI specifically. The AI attribution is management's framing, not a line-item cost breakdown. Still, the direction matters. On Friday we noted ServiceNow bundling its new Arc agent at no additional cost, and earlier in the week Okta did the same with Agent SSO. For every SaaS incumbent giving AI away inside existing seats, gross margin is the number that will reveal what the AI actually costs. Vendors that own their inference stack, like the hyperscalers, carry that cost differently than vendors who rent it. So the so-what for SAP: with bundled AI, watch gross margin, not attach-rate claims.

Every AI cost line at a software vendor is revenue for somebody running the infrastructure underneath it. This week, a good chunk of that was Oracle.

Oracle held its AI World conference in Las Vegas, and on Friday two things came out of it. First, Oracle committed $1 billion over the next four years to its partner ecosystem. That covers training, migration funding, and go-to-market support. It's the kind of channel investment Amazon and Microsoft have had for years, and Oracle is still building. The release gives no per-category breakdown, so we can't tell how much is net-new spend versus reallocated partner budget.

Second, and more interesting, a TikTok spokesperson told CNBC the same day that the platform, quote, "runs primarily on Oracle Cloud Infrastructure in the U.S. and Oracle will continue to be our primary cloud infrastructure provider." One source told CNBC that TikTok's U.S. entity is targeting about four gigawatts of computing capacity by the early 2030s.

So TikTok is an existing tenant. What's new is the reported ambition to grow it to roughly four gigawatts. If that happens, Oracle has a second very large counterparty alongside OpenAI. Treat the four-gigawatt number with real caution, though. It's attributed to a single unnamed source, and it's a target years out. Neither company has confirmed a contract, a price, or a deal value. It fits a pattern this show has flagged before: an announcement-shaped headline standing in for a signed document.

Here's why it still matters. Oracle's AI demand is enormous and concentrated in a small number of counterparties, rather than a broad base of mid-market customers. If that target turns into a contract, it raises capacity commitments further. That sharpens the financing question we raised on Thursday, about who ultimately stands behind Oracle's buildout. The billion-dollar partner program is Oracle's attempt to broaden that base. Oracle is paying for the integrator capacity that IBM's numbers say is being redirected to AI work. So the so-what: Oracle's AI-driven demand is real, but it keeps pooling into fewer, larger, and in this case still-unsigned hands.

One counterpoint worth closing on, because it answers a question we asked on Wednesday. We asked Salesforce for a resolution-quality number rather than another activity metric. Salesforce now says Tottenham Hotspur's fan-facing agent, built on Agentforce, is running in production. It reports an 86 percent engagement rate, with 20 percent of interactions resolved by the agent alone.

That's a real outcome number, but read both sides of it. It's a vendor-selected case study, not independently audited. And 20 percent resolved autonomously means roughly 80 percent still needed a human or went unresolved. Notice, too, what that agent sits on top of. Salesforce describes a roughly two-year transformation that replaced about thirty legacy systems, unifying more than 4.6 million fan accounts. The agent arrived at the end of a long, consulting-heavy integration project. That tail is exactly the cost of sale behind IBM's shifting consulting mix.

Pull the three threads together. Services spend is being redirected toward AI implementation rather than expanded. AI capability is increasingly paid for out of gross margin rather than billed as new revenue. And infrastructure demand for AI keeps concentrating in a small number of very large, not-yet-contracted customers. Next Wednesday, October 28th, ServiceNow reports; watch whether any vendor finally puts a hard revenue number on AI rather than a mix or margin story.
