Good morning, this is The Throughline for Saturday, September 12th, 2026. Today's headline: a one-tenth-of-a-point core inflation surprise pulled Wednesday's Fed decision sharply toward a hike. And it landed on a bond market where trend-following funds are already carrying their largest-ever short position. So the position most exposed to Wednesday isn't the hawkish case. It's the record short book that has nowhere left to grow.

Start with the macro backdrop. Strategic Power has SoftBank pricing a record one-trillion-yen retail bond, roughly six point three billion dollars, to fund its OpenAI commitments. That is a fixed coupon running through 2033, backing an equity stake with no market price, sold to Japanese retail households. The same briefing has Nvidia's five-year credit default swap up ninety percent this year, still a high-grade level but no longer an ignored one. Read together, the AI buildout's financing is starting to show up as priced, contestable risk rather than as a headline commitment.

Capital Markets Cross-Asset Radar has the print that moved everything else this week. August core consumer prices ran three tenths, a tenth hot, driven by gasoline rather than by demand. Hike odds for Wednesday's meeting jumped from roughly seventy percent to roughly ninety on CME FedWatch, though prediction markets read lower. The curve did the more interesting thing: two-to-ten compressed to thirty-three basis points while equities rallied anyway. And the number underneath all of it is positioning. UBS data show commodity trading advisors holding their largest-ever bearish bond position, which means a dovish surprise has far more fuel to unwind into than a hawkish one does.

That same September 16th date is the test Private Credit and the Corporate Credit Cycle is also watching. Its story is a contradiction of its own. Realized defaults on CLO collateral are running at roughly half last year's pace, on track to nearly match a four-year low, even as the share of leveraged loans trading below ninety cents on the dollar keeps climbing. The reconciliation is that a default is elective, deferred by refinancing while the window stays open. Wednesday's decision is what decides whether that window narrows.

Private Equity and the Deal Economy closes the macro leg with the one thing this beat has been missing: a real, competed clearing price. Two UK targets, Bodycote and Gamma Communications, were agreed to sponsors at premiums of thirty-four and fifty-three percent after multi-bidder contests. Neither has completed, so hold them as agreed deals rather than closed ones. That is still genuine price discovery on the buy side, and a reminder that entering at those premiums was never the industry's real constraint. Realizing gains still is, and the exit channel absorbing most of that volume, the GP-led continuation vehicle, prices itself rather than facing a rival bidder.

Now into the AI and compute stack, where documentation itself became this week's currency. Frontier AI Lab Competition has Anthropic's own threat-intelligence report. It describes a China-based actor allegedly using Claude to write a two-hundred-page naval weapons proposal. That is ordinary analytical work, not exploit code, on the same model surface every paying customer reaches. It is also self-published, unverified, and omits the one detail that would make it load-bearing: which model tier the actor used. The same briefing has xAI's Grok 4.7 missing its expected launch with no technical disclosure at all, the clearest holdout from an industry norm of publishing what a model does.

AI Products and Applications carries that same permission theme into consumer products. Meta's Muse agent connects to email, payments and shopping, with a human-approval step that so far rests on third-party reporting rather than on Meta's own published terms. The bigger move was pricing. DeepSeek's V4.1 Flash lists at roughly one-seventieth of OpenAI's frontier input rate, blowing open a cost spread this beat had been tracking all week. That forces orchestration platforms like GitHub's Copilot to justify themselves on quality rather than on savings.

Enterprise Technology found the same self-reported-versus-checkable line running through its own week. Oracle guided to ninety billion dollars of revenue against ninety to ninety-five billion of gross capital spending, funded partly by customer prepayments. Salesforce disclosed it paid three point six billion dollars for the customer-service AI firm Fin, which is a real number, while Fin's headline resolution rate remains an unaudited claim. For six straight editions, this beat noted, vendor metrics had drifted away from dollars. Today the numbers that finally showed up were costs, not revenue.

Into the applied frontier. Robotics and Physical AI got the same treatment from a source nobody controls: the SEC. Agility Robotics' merger filing shows 1.8 million dollars of net sales last year against a 140 million dollar operating loss, plus a going-concern warning tied to the deal closing. Unitree, the one humanoid maker with a real market price, has fallen nearly forty percent from its listing pop and still trades at roughly a hundred twenty-five times revenue. That is the strong case getting marked down hard, which makes the pre-revenue names harder to defend, not easier.

Space and the Orbital Economy closes the tour with Golden Dome's tracking layer finally getting a per-satellite price. Divide the disclosed contract split and you get roughly fifty-three million dollars a spacecraft from L3Harris and forty-four million from Sierra Space, for satellites not ready to launch until the end of 2028. The Pentagon is deliberately diversifying who builds the satellites while nearly the entire launch manifest still depends on one company. And Golden Dome's own cost estimate is disputed nearly sevenfold between the administration and the Congressional Budget Office.

One thread runs under almost every briefing today. Wherever an outside process forced a number into the open, the picture got harder to spin. An SEC filing, a competed UK bid, a disclosed contract split, a hot CPI print. Wherever the number stayed self-reported, it stayed convenient for whoever published it. A lab's own threat report, a vendor's own benchmark, a continuation vehicle's own mark, a bond whose final book and spread were never confirmed. Three of today's beats made versions of that complaint out loud. Today it arrived in nine unrelated markets at once.
