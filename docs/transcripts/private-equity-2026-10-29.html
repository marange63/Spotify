Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Equity & the Deal Economy

Transcript · October 29, 2026

Good morning, it's October 29th, 2026, and this is your private equity briefing.

Start with one number. Sponsor-to-sponsor sales fell to just 94 deals in the second quarter, the fewest in at least a decade. That count comes from the law firm Foley's midyear review of exit data. It counts deals, not dollars, and it covers a single quarter. Still, this is the industry's own exit valve, sponsors buying assets from other sponsors, and it's nearly shut. Monday's briefing covered how thin the cash coming back to limited partners is. Today is about who's still writing checks when the usual buyer, a fellow sponsor, has largely stopped showing up.

The clearest illustration landed yesterday. Nelson Peltz's Trian Fund Management submitted a non-binding proposal to take Wendy's private at $7.35 a share. That values the company at roughly $1.4 billion, a premium of about 20 percent over Wendy's October 27th close. Be precise about what this is. It's a non-binding proposal, not a signed deal. A special committee of Wendy's independent directors is evaluating it, advised by Morgan Stanley and Simpson Thacher. There is no assurance anything closes.

Here's why the buyer matters more than the price. Trian and its affiliates already own roughly 16.25 percent of Wendy's, which makes them the largest shareholder. The group intends to roll that stake into the deal rather than cash out. So this is an insider setting the price the independent committee has to beat, not an outside bidder discovering value. The consortium also includes Flynn Group, one of Wendy's largest franchisees, which runs more than 340 of its restaurants. The takeaway is that this is a tightly coordinated insider group. The committee is negotiating with a bidder who keeps its influence whatever the committee decides.

On financing, the buyer group says the deal needs no new debt and carries no financing condition. It also expects Wendy's existing securitization to stay in place after closing. That's a credibility signal. It removes the leveraged-loan uncertainty that sinks a lot of take-private talk before it gets serious. But don't read credibility as certainty. Wendy's stock traded around $7.01 on the morning the proposal became public. That's up about 14 percent, but still below the $7.35 offer, and the shares remain down about 12 percent for the year. The market is pricing real deal risk, not a done transaction. The next checkpoint is Wendy's November 5th earnings call, where the company says it will discuss its results and strategic plan.

Now weigh both sides. For an activist that already knows the company intimately, a 20 percent premium on a de-rated stock is a cheap, informed entry. A premium over a depressed price says little about whether the price is fair. For public holders stuck in a name that has lagged for years, though, it's a real floor under the price. There's also a competitive angle for sponsors. Activists with big existing stakes are now bidding for exactly what buyout funds want: cheap, de-rated consumer turnarounds. That narrows the pool of discounted entries that today's vintages need to make their return math work. And step back to the asset-class level. Like every take-private, this is capital going in. It returns nothing to private equity limited partners waiting on distributions.

Wendy's is one way around a thin sponsor bid. Healthcare shows another. Earlier this month, on October 8th, Clayton Dubilier and Rice and McKesson agreed to take Option Care Health private at a $5.8 billion valuation. That pairs a strategic co-owner with a sponsor rather than running a pure buyout. How the two split ownership isn't public, so treat what follows as reasoning, not disclosure. A strategic partner can offer something a pure sponsor deal lacks: a natural eventual buyer, or at least shared risk. That's a workaround for a sponsor-to-sponsor market that's gone quiet. Notice what's absent from both deals. It's software. Two deals is a small sample, so hold that loosely. But it fits what Evercore's data showed on Monday: secondary buyers are stepping back from software continuation vehicles over AI-disruption risk. The fresh entries are going where exits still look plausible.

So why has the sponsor-to-sponsor channel gone quiet? There are two readings, and they matter for different reasons. One is a simple bid-ask gap. Buyers won't pay sellers' marks, so deals don't clear. The other is worse for the asset class. Sponsors may prefer to recycle assets into their own continuation vehicles rather than sell to a peer, because a continuation vehicle keeps the fees and keeps the asset. Under that second reading, the arm's-length sale that actually disciplines private marks is disappearing by choice, not just by market conditions. Either way, assets end up marked against whoever is still bidding. That's an activist with an information edge, a strategic with synergies, or a continuation-vehicle buyer the manager picked. None of them gives a clean comparable. Meanwhile the advisory firm Kaden Wood counts roughly 13,500 US sponsor-owned companies still waiting to be sold. That's one firm's tally, not an industry standard. But the point holds. Supply is backed up, the channel that used to clear it is drying up, and mark opacity rises even while deal volume continues.

Much of that recycled volume has gone into continuation vehicles, and one of them is about to face an outside price check it didn't ask for. Back in August, Secondary Scoop and Benzinga reported that Coller Capital is the lead buyer on a roughly $600 million continuation vehicle for Lightspeed Venture Partners. Per that reporting, it holds a stake in OpenAI alongside a newly added Anthropic position. Standing disclosure: Anthropic makes the model behind this show. Frame this correctly. It's a venture and growth vehicle, not a buyout continuation fund. Normally a continuation-vehicle price is set inside a small circle: the selling manager, a lead secondary buyer the manager picked, and a fairness opinion. No public market ever checks it. This one is different. Anthropic filed publicly for an IPO this week, which puts that stake on a path toward a market price.

Be clear about what that test covers. It checks the Anthropic position only. It says nothing about OpenAI or the vehicle's other holdings. And the price Coller actually paid hasn't been disclosed, which is the number the AI-marks debate really needs. Still, the outcomes split cleanly. If the public price clears well above where the vehicle bought in, it validates secondaries buyers' appetite for AI exposure and likely pulls more venture GP-led deals forward. If it lands below, the first public-market test of a continuation-vehicle mark arrives on the most-watched asset in private markets. For scale, Coller reports GP-led secondaries volume hit a record 108 billion dollars in 2025, up from 77 billion the year before. Coller is an interested party in that market, so weight the figure accordingly. The so-what is that secondaries capital is now underwriting the most contested private marks there are.

Last stop on the list of who's still buying: retail. Titan Investors estimates that assets in evergreen and semi-liquid private-market vehicles have now passed 500 billion dollars. That spans credit, real estate and equity, not private equity alone. That wealth-channel money is becoming a buyer of last resort for illiquid exposure. The liquidity mismatch shifts from institutional limited partners to individual holders. And it hasn't yet faced a real redemption test.

Pull it together. Capital is still flowing into private equity through activists, strategic-sponsor hybrids, continuation vehicles, and evergreen funds. What's missing is the one channel that used to prove the price was real: a sponsor selling to another sponsor. The binding risk remains the exit-and-distribution logjam. Today's deal flow doesn't ease it. It reroutes around it, with weaker price discovery than before. Ares reports today, so hold judgment until those numbers are actually out. Then watch KKR on November 9th for the cleaner private equity read on realizations. Watch whether Blackstone delivers the fourth-quarter step-up in realizations it promised. Watch the Wendy's special committee's response. And watch where Anthropic's public price lands relative to that continuation vehicle's mark.

That's your briefing. Talk to you next time.
