Good morning, and welcome to Cautious Optimism Briefings for Wednesday, October 7, 2026.

Today's question is whether private-equity exits are actually unlocking. The short answer is yes, but narrowly. Preliminary PitchBook data show exit value surging on a handful of large sales, while the number of exits rose only modestly. And one reported deal this week hints at where the marginal exit may be heading, which is toward a strategic buyer seated at the table from day one.

Start with that deal, which is reported, not announced. The Financial Times reported on October 5 that McKesson and Clayton Dubilier and Rice are nearing a deal to acquire Option Care Health, an infusion-services provider. The report puts the transaction at more than five billion dollars including debt. It said a deal could be announced as soon as Tuesday, which was yesterday, although talks could still fall apart. As of our check late Tuesday evening, we had not seen a primary announcement. So treat this as pending.

Two cautions on the number. First, more than five billion including debt is an enterprise value. It is not the equity check, and it is not a premium. The report gives no per-share price, so there is no premium to discuss yet. Second, the ownership split between the sponsor and the strategic is not verified. Neither is whether McKesson would hold any option over the sponsor's stake. Those terms are the whole story, and they are still unknown.

Why does this matter for the exit problem? Last week's pattern on this show was exits clearing where a strategic buyer wanted the asset. This deal moves that logic to the front of the holding period. Here, a strategic is reportedly in the deal from the start. On our analysis, that means the sponsor has seated the most likely eventual buyer at the table before it owns anything. That is one way to pre-solve an exit. And if the final structure gives McKesson a path to the whole company, the exit would run through a corporate owner, not through the public market or another sponsor.

There is a competing reading, and it deserves equal weight. A strategic that brings in sponsor equity may simply be renting a balance sheet. On that reading, Clayton Dubilier is a financing and structuring partner, not a conviction buyer. Its upside is smaller than in a classic leveraged buyout. And its downside is shared with a corporate partner that has its own agenda. Which reading is right depends on terms we don't have yet.

Either way, the takeaway is this. If the structure holds, the strategic becomes the sponsor's exit plan, not just its counterparty. If that pattern spreads, private-equity returns depend more on corporate M&A appetite than on the IPO window or on sponsor-to-sponsor trading. That is a different cycle, with different people bearing the risk.

One second-order effect matters for valuation, and this is our analysis. Option Care is a listed company, so a deal would take another public name off the board. Every take-private removes a listed comparable. The pool of public marks that private holdings are benchmarked against shrinks at the margin. When that reference set thins, a private mark built off a handful of remaining peers can drift from reality with less to check it against.

So does one reported deal fit a broader thaw? PitchBook's third-quarter First Look is the first aggregate test. These are preliminary figures, and we have them as summarized in a publication from Foley and Company, not from PitchBook's own page. Global exits numbered 1,089 in the third quarter, up 9.3 percent from the second quarter. Exit value rose far faster, up 65 percent, to 481.6 billion dollars. And PitchBook attributes the gap to a few large sales that boosted value.

The United States tells the same story on a smaller scale. Per the same relay, US exit count rose 11 percent in the quarter, while US exit value jumped 51 percent. So the gap between dollars and deals is not a quirk of one region. Value is outrunning volume on both measures.

That breaks the read we gave yesterday, when we said the week had brought no fresh evidence that sponsor exits were clearing. At the aggregate level, they did clear, and far more in dollars than in deals. But the reconciliation is concentration, not breadth. A broad recovery would show up in the count, and the count rose only modestly. An exit-value surge built on a few large sales tells you about the biggest assets. It tells you little about the typical portfolio company waiting for a buyer.

Exit value is also not distributions. Proceeds reach limited partners with a lag, and only after the debt on the asset is repaid. So a strong exit quarter does not mean distributions-to-paid-in has recovered. The First Look gives us no fresh distribution metric. The latest ratio we have dates to the twelve months ended June 2025, which is too old to describe today. The honest statement is that we don't yet know how much of this exit value has reached LP accounts.

The same data corrects the usual fundraising story. Per the same PitchBook relay, the global fund count is 523, pacing down 30.5 percent year over year. But capital raised is pacing up 3.1 percent. So fewer funds are closing, while the dollars are holding up. That is not a general retreat from private equity. It is fewer, larger closes.

There are two readings, and the data alone cannot separate them. The first is lumpiness. A few large flagship closes landing in the window can lift the dollar total while the typical manager still struggles. The second is structural. Limited partners are consolidating commitments into the largest franchises, and mid-sized managers are losing share of a pool that isn't shrinking. Our lean is toward the structural reading, because it matches the exit data. Capital and realizations are both pooling at the top. What would separate the two? Watch whether the next flagship closes come in on time and above target, and whether mid-sized funds can raise at all over the next two quarters.

Put the two halves together, and dispersion becomes the central question. If exits and capital are both concentrating, managers will diverge sharply. Those holding large, saleable assets will show realizations. Those holding software-era vintages may not. The stuck software holdings this show has been tracking are still stuck. A strong aggregate quarter gives no relief to a fund whose assets have no obvious buyer, whether strategic, sponsor, or public market. On our read, strategic demand reaches scarce healthcare and physical assets far more easily than it reaches a mid-cap software company bought in 2021.

So, pulling the threads together. The private-equity cycle is reopening, but selectively. The reopening runs through a handful of large exits, and possibly through strategic buyers coming in at the front of the deal, if Option Care is signed on those lines. Fund counts are falling while dollars hold up, so capital is concentrating, not leaving. The biggest unresolved risk is still the distribution logjam in software-era vintages, which strategic demand does not obviously reach. Until cash reaches limited partners in size, this recovery is as much a paper story as a cash story.

Three catalysts will confirm or refute this read. First, whether Option Care is signed, and on what terms. The ownership split, any call right on the sponsor's stake, and the premium will tell us whether Clayton Dubilier is a conviction buyer or a financing partner. Second, the alternative managers' third-quarter results in the coming weeks. The question there is whether realized carry is finally contributing, or whether fee-related earnings are still doing all the work. Third, PitchBook's fuller third-quarter data, which should confirm or revise these preliminary exit figures, and ideally bring a current distribution metric.
