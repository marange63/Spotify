Good morning. It's Friday, August 14th, and this is your robotics and physical AI briefing.

We're going to spend today's briefing on one question: who actually owns the humanoid robot business, and what does "owning" it even mean once you separate shipped hardware from paying work. The freshest data point of the week answers the first half of that question with a number, and complicates the second half considerably.

Start with the number. Smart Analytics Global, the research firm tracking this market, put out a report this week showing global humanoid robot shipments hit roughly 19,100 units in the first half of 2026, up 272% year over year. Chinese vendors supplied more than 97% of that volume, and China represented more than 85% of global demand. Inside that number is a leadership change worth dwelling on. AgiBot, the Shanghai robot maker, shipped about 8,400 units, up 562% year over year, which lifted its global share from 25% to 44%. Unitree, last year's leader, shipped about 5,900 units, up a comparatively pedestrian 170%, for a 31% share. AgiBot didn't just edge past Unitree. It nearly doubled its share while Unitree's slipped.

Here's why that lands awkwardly this week specifically. This show has spent the last several days tracking Unitree's public listing — the IPO pricing, then the eye-watering oversubscription that priced the stock at roughly 219 times last year's earnings, effectively crowning it China's national humanoid champion in the eyes of the market. The shipment ledger just named a different champion. The company the market bid up as the volume leader isn't the volume leader. That's not necessarily a verdict on Unitree's technology or its long-term prospects, but it is a live overhang on a valuation that was built, in part, on a leadership story the underlying data doesn't support.

Now, before we treat 19,100 units as proof that humanoid robots are suddenly working at scale, we need to be skeptical, because shipped is not deployed. A unit sold isn't necessarily a unit doing real, revenue-generating work. Smart Analytics Global's own report gives us the best proxy we have on this, and it's worth stating precisely because it cuts both ways. Industrial and commercial applications rose to more than 70% of shipments in the first half of 2026, up from about 50% a year earlier. That's a genuine shift toward higher-value use, and it should temper the reflexive skepticism that these are all research toys. But the same report specifically flags that research, education, and performance-related use — think demonstrations, not factory floors — still make up, in its words, a meaningful portion of Unitree's own shipments. So the application mix is improving, but it isn't resolved, and "industrial and commercial" as a shipment category still isn't the same thing as a paying customer running a robot on a line every day. Nobody has published that number yet, for anyone.

There's also a sourcing caveat that matters more than usual here. This entire dataset traces back to one research firm. It got picked up nearly verbatim by roughly a dozen outlets — Bloomberg, TechNode, the South China Morning Post, and others — within about 48 hours. That's wide press pickup, not independent corroboration. No established research house like IDC has published its own competing count. So treat the order of magnitude and the AgiBot-over-Unitree reversal as the real story, and hold the exact digits a little loosely until a second firm checks the math.

What's driving the 97% concentration in the first place is worth a beat of its own, because it's a supply-chain fact more than a capability verdict. China's dominance in shipped units reflects its lock on actuators, motors, batteries, and rare earths — the bill-of-materials layer underneath every humanoid chassis, Chinese or otherwise. That matters for the reshoring and export-control debate on this side of the Pacific, because American and allied humanoid makers, Figure and Apptronik among them, are still largely sourcing those same components from the same supply base even as they build finished robots domestically. A tripled Chinese shipment market doesn't just win units. It deepens the requalification burden anyone outside that supply chain has to absorb to build at comparable cost. One footnote on AgiBot specifically: Tencent has joined its latest investment round, per the South China Morning Post, which gives the new volume leader a consumer-tech platform investor with real distribution reach — a plausible piece of why its growth rate so badly outpaced Unitree's.

Now turn west, because the contrast with America's highest-profile humanoid program is stark. Tesla's Optimus line at Fremont still has not started production. The company's July shareholder letter pushed output to "later this year," missing the late-July, early-August start Musk had guided to on the first-quarter call. Musk himself has said the initial rate is, in his words, literally impossible to predict, given that Optimus is built from roughly ten thousand unique parts on an entirely new line. A larger, higher-volume facility at Giga Texas isn't targeted until around summer 2027. So while Chinese makers are shipping thousands of units a quarter, the most heavily capitalized Western humanoid program is still pre-production.

That's not a verdict that Tesla is behind on the underlying autonomy — Chinese volume leadership so far is a hardware-shipment lead, not a demonstrated capability lead — but it is a real gap in shipped hardware and manufacturing execution. And it's not isolated to Optimus. Tesla rolled out FSD version 14.1 Lite on August 11th, after owners of older, HW3 vehicles reported their onboard computers overheating on the prior release. Even Tesla's most mature autonomy stack, the one Optimus's perception heritage descends from, is still shipping reactive fixes for hardware reliability problems, not just capability upgrades. So what: hardware execution, not model capability, is currently the binding constraint on the West's flagship humanoid bet.

That leads to the question this shipment data ultimately forces: if China is going to win the volume race on chassis manufacturing, where does value actually accrue? The emerging answer from the labs is the model layer. Google DeepMind shipped Gemini Robotics 2 at the end of July, a family of three models covering whole-body control, embodied reasoning, and on-device inference, meant to plug into multiple robot platforms rather than being tied to one. Only the reasoning model is broadly available; the whole-body model remains gated to early-access partners, so treat its capability as unproven outside curated demonstrations for now. But the strategic bet, alongside Nvidia's competing GR00T stack, is the same one that played out in generative AI: if a shared "brain" becomes the standard across hardware makers, the chassis itself commoditizes, and the software and compute layer captures the value regardless of who wins the unit-shipment race. That's a hypothesis, not a result. Nobody has proven it in robotics the way it played out in phones or in cloud compute. But it's the frame that makes sense of a market where China can own 97% of shipments and still not have obviously won the fight for economic value.

So where does that leave us. Hardware volume is concentrating hard in China, and AgiBot, backed by Tencent, is now the name to watch there over Unitree, whose fresh public valuation carries a volume-share overhang it didn't have a week ago. Value is genuinely contested at the model layer, where DeepMind and Nvidia are racing to become the shared brain across hardware platforms. And nobody — not AgiBot, not Unitree, not Tesla, not a Western lab — has yet published a real, paying-deployment number that would settle who's actually winning the underlying economics. Watch for three things next: a second research firm corroborating or contradicting the Smart Analytics Global count, any company finally disclosing a paying-deployment figure rather than a shipment figure, and whether Unitree's newly public stock holds up now that the volume story it was priced on has changed hands.

That's the briefing. Have a good weekend, and I'll see you next time.
