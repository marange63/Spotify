Good morning, this is The Throughline for Wednesday, September 2nd, 2026. Today's headline: two arms of the same government are pulling the Treasury curve in opposite directions inside a single week. Treasury starts buying long bonds back on September 9th to push yields down, and the Fed weighs a hike on the 16th. That same gap, between what is promised and what is actually delivered, runs through almost every other briefing today.

Start with the macro backdrop. Strategic Power has one energy lever stuck and one working. Stuck is the Senate-passed sanctions bill that would let the President tariff buyers of Russian oil up to 100 percent, now sitting in a House on recess with no position yet from Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries's caucus. Working is the courts. The DC Circuit upheld FERC's approval of Venture Global's CP2 LNG terminal and dismissed all eleven challenges, lowering litigation risk across the Gulf Coast export build-out. But cargoes are only targeted for 2027, so it is a resolved case, not gas in a ship.

From a stalled instrument to a market pricing the fight between two working ones. Capital Markets Radar has Treasury Secretary Bessent doubling those buybacks to at least four billion dollars per operation, an explicit attempt to cap long yields. Pulling the other way, Fed Chair Kevin Warsh's Jackson Hole tone pushed September hike odds to roughly two-thirds. The thirty-year hit its highest level since 2007 before the buyback announcement stabilised it. Gold, the dollar and bitcoin had been refusing to confirm that hawkish move. This week they capitulated, gold to a two-week low and the dollar to a recent high. That new unanimity is itself the risk. If the Fed holds on the 16th, a lot of positioning has to reverse at once.

That same unresolved rate picture is what the sponsors are trying to exit into. Private Equity has the exit market cracking open, but only for a narrow, thematic slice of it. Apollo and Triton are selling the data-center cooling maker Kelvion to oilfield-services giant SLB for four point one billion dollars, the arm's-length strategic exit the industry has been waiting for. It does not close until the first half of 2027, so it is a pending deal, not cash back to investors. A day earlier, Apollo put nine billion dollars behind ONEOK's Permian pipeline purchase, structured as capped-return credit rather than open-ended equity. Exits are clearing by story now. An asset with an AI-infrastructure label finds a buyer. Everything else takes roughly a forty percent cut to deal size to clear the IPO window, or goes back into a sponsor-controlled vehicle.

Into the AI and compute stack, where the pattern repeats at the input level. Semiconductors and Compute Infrastructure has Samsung's HBM4 jumping from about 5 to about 35 percent of its own memory shipments in a single quarter. That is a yield win, not evidence that Nvidia has reallocated any orders toward it. HBM prices set a record anyway. So the shortage is deep enough to absorb Samsung's gains with no price relief for AI buyers, and none for the phone and PC makers now paying up for scarce consumer memory either.

Frontier AI Lab Competition has the buyer's side of that question. The Pentagon onboarded OpenAI and xAI onto its GenAI dot mil platform alongside Google, a pool of three million personnel. Treat that as an enrollment count, not usage data. And it lands days after a federal judge voided the Pentagon's designation of Anthropic as a national-security risk. On the capability side, the top American models now sit within two points of each other on the most-argued agentic benchmark. When the leaderboard stops sorting anyone, the contest moves to who can prove things to one specific buyer.

AI Products and Applications has the price version of that. Anthropic cut effective agent costs by up to 45 percent without touching the sticker price at all, by slashing cache-read fees rather than the headline rate. It is a discount on repetition, so it only pays off for workloads already built to exploit it. Enterprise Technology has the physical version of the same constraint. Dell exited the quarter with a record 95 billion dollars of AI-server orders booked and unshipped. On the beat's own arithmetic, that is roughly six quarters of already-sold demand it cannot yet convert into hardware. Commitment keeps outrunning delivery.

Into the applied frontier, where the gap shows up as a measurement problem. Robotics and Physical AI went through the filings of Symbotic and found Walmart at nearly 83 percent of revenue. So the headline backlog is largely one retailer's rollout schedule, not proof of a broad automation category. Tesla's unsupervised robotaxi miles are growing fast off a fleet of roughly 85 cars, with no intervention rate published anywhere.

Space and the Orbital Economy has the sharpest version. SpaceX now sends 86 cents of every capex dollar to AI compute rather than to rockets or satellites. Starship's September 15th flight aims at a genuine orbital insertion, which would be a first. It is not attempting the tower catch that would actually cut launch cost.

AI in Biopharma R&D closes the tour with the same shape. The largest disclosed AI deployment at a major pharma company turns out not to be in drug development at all. Novo Nordisk has more than twenty-five thousand employees on Amazon Bedrock, scoped explicitly to nonregulated work. The one hard number anywhere in that stack is a ninety percent cut in the time to draft a clinical study report, on Novo's own account. That is real, and it is documentation, not discovery.

One thread ties the day together, and it is sharper than the disclosure skepticism this show leaned on yesterday. Nearly every beat today is measuring a promise rather than a delivery. A buyback that has not been tested against issuance. A Kelvion sale that does not close until 2027. A Samsung mix shift that is not a confirmed Nvidia order. A Dell backlog six quarters from shipping. A Starship flight that would prove orbit but not cost. The paper is real. The conversion is not scored yet.
