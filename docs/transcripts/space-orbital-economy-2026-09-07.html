Good morning. It's Monday, September 7th, and this is your space and orbital economy briefing.

The direct-to-cell race has quietly moved past its engineering question. Hardware in orbit now talks to unmodified handsets at real bandwidth. The open question is a commercial one, and it has a deadline attached. So today I want to work through three operators who are all buying the same asset — deployed satellites holding spectrum priority — and who face wildly different bills for it. A listed company with four months to make its own guidance. A trillion-dollar retailer that missed its deployment clock and found out what the regulator charges. And a state program that does not have to sell anything at all.

Start with the listed one. AST SpaceMobile's own announcement, dated August the fifth, says it launched and fully deployed BlueBird satellites eleven, twelve and thirteen on a Falcon 9. That brought its direct-to-cell constellation to thirteen spacecraft in orbit. BlueBirds fourteen, fifteen and sixteen are already staged for the next mission, and production has advanced through satellite forty-two. These are the larger units, aimed at nearly doubling peak downlink speed to roughly two hundred megabits per second, delivered to a standard smartphone with no modification. That is confirmed hardware, not a rendering. Note also how it gets up there. The constellation grows on somebody else's rocket, so AST's deployment clock is set by another company's manifest.

Now the part the hardware does not settle. AST's SEC filing for the quarter ended June thirtieth shows revenue of thirty-one and a half million dollars. That splits into twenty-four point four million of products revenue and seven point one million of services revenue. Set that against a net loss of two hundred and thirty million dollars in the same three months. Nobody expects a constellation to pay for itself while it is being built. But the company has made a public promise about how fast that revenue arrives, and the promise is now testable.

Here is the arithmetic the filing makes possible, and it is the sharpest number on this beat today. Across the first six months of the year, products revenue was thirty-seven point eight million and services revenue was eight point four million. Call it forty-six million dollars of revenue in the first half. The company has reaffirmed full-year guidance of a hundred and fifty to two hundred million. So the back half has to deliver somewhere between a hundred and four and a hundred and fifty-four million dollars. That is between two and three times the front half, in the same number of months, and it is due in under four. It is not impossible, because this revenue is equipment-weighted and lumpy by nature. It is simply the most demanding commitment the company has on the record.

Watch which line it has to come from, because the two lines mean very different things. Products revenue is hardware and equipment sales. Services revenue is the closest thing in this filing to the recurring wholesale carrier money that would actually validate a constellation's payback period. Services were seven point one million in the second quarter, out of eight point four million for the entire half. By subtraction that leaves roughly one point three million in the first quarter. That is my arithmetic off the company's own numbers, not a figure AST states. So the direction is right and the growth is fast, but the base is tiny. And AST does not break out recurring carrier revenue from one-time sales anywhere in its disclosure, so nobody outside the company can yet tell how much of that services line is a subscription business at all. The so-what is that the guidance gap will most likely be closed by selling equipment, not by signing subscribers — which would tell you very little about the unit economics that matter.

Then there is the cash side, and it is less dramatic than the bull-bear argument around this name usually assumes. The filing's cash-flow statement shows net cash used in operating activities of a hundred and forty-five million dollars for the first six months of the year. That is up from seventy-two million in the same period last year, so the operating burn has roughly doubled year over year. One important caveat: operating cash flow is not the whole bill. Building satellites is capital expenditure, and capex sits in the investing section, which I could not read directly. So a hundred and forty-five million is a floor on what this business consumed in the half, not the total. Against that, the balance sheet carries two point two nine billion dollars in cash and equivalents, and about two point seven billion if you include restricted cash. That is a company spending well ahead of its revenue with a burn that is accelerating, but it is not a company at the edge of a liquidity event.

Which brings us to what the market is actually pricing. Cantor Fitzgerald has a ninety-dollar price target on the stock, citing a possible twenty twenty-seven revenue run rate of around five hundred million dollars. Say that plainly. Five hundred million is a sell-side forecast. It is not company guidance and it is not booked revenue. The gap between that number and the thirty-one and a half million actually recognized in the quarter is the whole position. Production scaling toward satellite forty-two is announced capacity. Thirty-one and a half million is recognized revenue. Shares rallied nine and a half percent on September the second, to around sixty-one dollars, and a one-session move is not a verdict on either figure. The thing that would settle it is conversion. AST is running integration testing with the carrier US Mobile, a new gateway in New Zealand, and a European carrier group that includes Vodafone, Orange, Telefónica and Deutsche Telekom. None of that is revenue today. AST has proven it can put working hardware in orbit and step up bandwidth. It has not yet proven it can turn that hardware into a subscription business, and the next real event on this name is a financing decision as much as a launch.

So that is the price of deploying. Now the price of not deploying, and it explains why an operator would rationally spend at that rate. Back in June, the FCC's Space Bureau granted Amazon's Kuiper constellation, now branded Amazon Leo, a conditional waiver of its interim deployment milestone. Amazon would have missed the original July thirtieth, 2026 deadline. It now has until July thirtieth, 2027 to deploy half its licensed constellation, sixteen hundred and eighteen of three thousand two hundred and thirty-six satellites. This is a standing regulatory fact from the summer rather than a development of the last few days. It matters because of the condition the Bureau attached.

Any satellite Amazon launches after that original deadline is temporarily demoted in spectral priority until the fifty percent mark is reached. In plain terms, those spacecraft drop down the interference-coordination pecking order. That is not a fine, and it is why the easy read is wrong. The obvious reaction was that the FCC blinked on the deadline. The more useful reading is that the Bureau kept the constraint and changed its currency, from licence risk to interference priority. And that generalizes past Amazon. Once a regulator is willing to price lateness in interference priority rather than in dollars or licence revocation, a rival's deployment schedule stops being a compliance matter between an operator and Washington. It becomes a competitive weapon between constellations. The waiver is conditional, so the Commission can tighten it again.

Now the operator for whom none of that conversion arithmetic applies. China's Qianfan program, part of the state-backed SpaceSail buildout, launched a dedicated direct-to-cell test satellite in June, designated DTC-01. This is trendline material from mid-year, not a fresh catalyst, and it is one test satellite. A demo, not a service, and it deserves exactly the skepticism we just applied to AST's revenue lines. But name the asymmetry, because it is the real point. Qianfan does not need a paying subscriber to make that launch worthwhile. The spectrum coordination and the orbital-shell position accrue to the state whether or not direct-to-cell ever ships commercially. AST has to convert integration testing into carrier tariffs before its balance sheet forces a raise. Beijing does not carry that constraint, and a competitor who never has to clear a payback hurdle is a different kind of competitor.

Pull the threads together. What is genuinely inflecting in this sector is deployed hardware and delivered bandwidth. What is still a demo is everything downstream of it: the carrier tariffs, the five hundred million dollar 2027 number, and China's direct-to-cell capability. And what the Kuiper waiver makes explicit is the currency the whole business actually trades in. Not subscribers. Deployed satellites holding spectrum priority. Three things to watch from here. Whether US Mobile or the European gateway group turns on an actual commercial tariff. Whether AST raises capital before that happens. And whether the FCC reaches for the spectral-demotion tool again, on a constellation that isn't Amazon's.
