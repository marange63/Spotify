Good morning. It's Friday, September 11th, and this is your frontier AI labs briefing.

For five straight days now, the real contest in this sector has not been decided on a benchmark. On Sunday the 6th it was a neutral harness gutting a marketed score. On Tuesday it was component suppliers buying seats on cap tables. On Wednesday it was Washington setting the terms of access. Yesterday it was a third party replacing self-attestation with audit. Today the competition moves one layer further back. It moves to two inputs nobody publishes an audited number for: the researchers and the gigawatts.

Start with the researchers. Reporting out in the last two weeks, citing a talent-tracking firm called Zeki, says Google DeepMind's position in the European research market has collapsed. DeepMind's share of elite AI hires in Europe fell from 49 percent across 2022 and 2023 to 18.6 percent in the most recent period. Zeki calls that the sharpest regional market-share drop it has recorded for any major lab.

The global picture is the same shape. DeepMind's ratio of new elite hires to departures fell from roughly twelve-to-one in the second quarter of 2023 to about two-to-one by the third quarter of this year. The same wave of coverage flagged four long-tenured departures in August: Jeff Dean, Sanjay Ghemawat, Oriol Vinyals and Quoc Le, all gone within the same month. The initial framing was that this was talent bleeding to rivals.

That framing is wrong, and it matters that we say so. Those four did not scatter to Meta, OpenAI or Anthropic. On August 5th, Google announced all four were leaving together to co-found Discovery Loop, an independent public-benefit corporation aimed at automating AI and machine-learning research itself. Google is a founding investor in Discovery Loop and will serve as its cloud provider.

Set the names against that. Jeff Dean was Google's chief scientist after 27 years at the company. Sanjay Ghemawat was a senior fellow of the same vintage. Oriol Vinyals ran research at DeepMind, and Quoc Le co-founded Google Brain. The same announcement had Demis Hassabis stepping back from the DeepMind chief executive role to become chairman and Alphabet's chief scientist, with Koray Kavukcuoglu promoted to run day-to-day operations and reporting directly to Sundar Pichai. Alphabet shares fell about 4 percent on the news. So the headline exit is a spinout with Google's money and Google's compute still attached. It is not a poaching loss to a competitor. Drop that frame entirely.

Dropping the frame does not kill the underlying story, though. It sharpens it. The twelve-to-one to two-to-one collapse, and the European share drop, describe DeepMind's broader pipeline rather than these four names. That data stands on its own. Zeki's read of DeepMind leavers over the past year puts roughly a quarter of them at Anthropic, with Meta and OpenAI taking most of the rest — a genuine outflow to competitors, distinct from the Discovery Loop story. And of the 29 named authors on the original AlphaFold2 paper, 13 have since left DeepMind. That is a real pipeline problem sitting underneath a headline event that turned out to be something else.

Here is the skeptical turn, and it is a real one. Every ratio in this story traces back to a single commercial vendor. Zeki tracks about 20,900 people in research and engineering roles across ten companies, using public information only. It explicitly excludes managers, executives and non-technical staff. By its own admission it may undercount actual headcount, because it can only see what is publicly visible. No competing dataset exists anywhere in the reporting to check it against.

Google DeepMind did not respond to a request for comment on Zeki's specific findings. The only on-record DeepMind statement in circulation is an older, unrelated comment about research-publication policy. So this is an unrebutted claim from an unaudited source. That cuts both ways. Nobody with better information has stepped up to contest it, which is a reason to take it seriously. But a cohort with 27 years of tenure reaching a natural exit point is at least as consistent a story as competitive attrition, and the available evidence cannot distinguish the two.

So what this tells you is not about Gemini today. Gemini 3.8 Flash shipped competitively nine days ago. This is a claim about the 2027 and 2028 research roadmap. A thinning pipeline shows up with a lag of years. That is exactly why it is invisible in this quarter's releases, and exactly why it is likely mispriced right now. Current Gemini quality is a lagging indicator of Google's position, not a leading one.

The sharper number in this data set is not Google's decline. It is Anthropic's. Anthropic's arrivals-to-departures ratio sits at roughly 22-to-1, against Meta's 3-to-1 and OpenAI's 5.7-to-1. That is a striking counter-signal for a lab with far less compute than Google. It is the best available evidence that Anthropic's mission and safety branding function as a real recruiting subsidy — an input-cost advantage that shows up nowhere on an income statement. And here is the callback worth naming. That 22-to-1 figure sits underneath a prospectus this show has watched slip from an early-September target to mid-October. It is one more number investors will be asked to price that nobody will ever have to verify.

Now the other input no lab can buy on a rate card: power. SemiAnalysis, relayed through secondary coverage, pushes back hard on the narrative that huge swaths of planned US data-center capacity have been cancelled. On its own tracking, the forecast for North American hyperscaler self-build capacity has barely moved over the past six months. Roughly 20 gigawatts of new capacity came online in 2026, with about 30 gigawatts expected in 2027 — and that 2027 figure is a forecast, not a delivered number. The framing that matters is this: model capability is still expanding faster than the physical buildout can keep pace, and power, not chip supply and not capital, is now the binding constraint on new projects. Treat those gigawatt figures as a secondhand relay of a paywalled source rather than a confirmed count. The direction is clear enough to matter anyway.

These two constraints compound rather than sit side by side. If DeepMind's pipeline really is thinning, some of that departing talent seeds new, well-funded startups. Discovery Loop is one example, whatever its ownership structure. And those startups bid for the same scarce gigawatts SemiAnalysis says are already the limiting factor. So the two binding inputs on frontier competition, researchers and power, are now feeding the same shortage.

That is the through-line for the week. The checkable numbers in this sector keep turning out to be the least informative ones: marketed benchmarks, undisclosed supply terms, an agency assessment with no evidence attached, and now a commercial talent tracker's ratios. The investable version is blunt. If capital cannot convert into gigawatts on the timeline a valuation assumes, then a headline raise buys less incremental capability than it implies.

One thing to watch this weekend: xAI's Grok 4.7. Elon Musk said it would arrive within about ten days of his early-September comments, which points to on or around tomorrow, September 12th. A parameter count near 2.1 trillion has been reported by release trackers. None of that is confirmed, and as of this morning Grok 4.7 has not shipped. xAI is now the one major US lab that sat out the release cluster every rival shipped into during the first week of September. That absence has run long enough to be a competitive fact rather than a scheduling accident.

Also worth watching: any on-record account of where DeepMind's broader outflow of researchers actually lands, and any independent check on either the talent or the capacity figures. Right now both rest on a single source each.

That's the briefing. Have a good weekend.
