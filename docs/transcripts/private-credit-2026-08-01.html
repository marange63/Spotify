Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Credit & the Corporate Credit Cycle

Transcript · August 1, 2026

Good morning. It's Saturday, August 1st, and this is the Cautious Optimism private-credit briefing.

We flagged this week that Fitch's private-credit default index had climbed to a record and that Ares Capital's non-accruals were creeping higher too. The fresh Q2 print out yesterday confirms the index kept climbing, and it widens a gap that's becoming the central question in this asset class. Fitch says the US private-credit default rate rose to a record 6.0% in the twelve months through June, up from 5.7% the prior quarter. The agency recorded 32 default events in the second quarter alone, involving 20 new borrowers, and industrials and manufacturing carried the highest sector default rate of any major category, at 10.4%. Fitch monitors roughly 1,500 private-credit issuers and logged 14 default events in the most recent month by itself.

Set that against Ares Capital, the largest public BDC, whose non-accruals at cost ended the quarter at 2.4% — still, in management's words, well below its roughly 3% historical average. Those two numbers are not one continuous scale. Fitch's universe is a broad, roughly 1,500-name monitored population; Ares's 2.4% is one manager's own book, marked quarterly by that manager. There are two honest readings. Either the largest, most scaled lenders like Ares and Apollo are adverse-selecting into the better credits, leaving the deterioration to concentrate among smaller, less-marked managers that Fitch's broader sample captures — or private marks generally are lagging a stress that hasn't hit the largest books yet.

Now, is Fitch an outlier among the agencies? This week a second monitor weighed in, and it points the same way. KBRA's own Q2 middle-market compendium, published Tuesday, shows its default rate rising too: the count-weighted rate increased for the first time in a year, to 3.3%, and the debt-weighted rate hit a new record peak of 2.4%. So Fitch and KBRA now agree on direction — both up — and disagree only on level and methodology, not on which way the cycle is moving. That agreement is the more troubling read. When two independent monitors move the same way, it strengthens rather than dissolves the case that credit quality is deteriorating; the open question is no longer whether defaults are rising, it's why the largest managers' own marks haven't caught up yet. Worth noting separately: the 2026 leveraged-loan maturity wall has also nearly vanished, down to $22 billion by month-end from $65.6 billion at the end of 2024 — a sign that a good share of what would otherwise be defaults is being amended and extended rather than resolved. So what: the level of default isn't the real story here — it's a widening gap between what the index sees and what the biggest lenders are marking, and that gap is the thing to watch into Apollo's earnings next week.

If the underlying book is deteriorating, the obvious next question is why capital keeps flooding in. Ares Management raised a record $36 billion in the second quarter, including $23.7 billion for credit strategies, and ended June sitting on a record $170 billion of uninvested capital. At the same time, Ares Capital's own origination pricing is moving in the lender's favor: new senior loan spreads widened 20 basis points versus the fourth quarter of last year, with upfront fees up 50 basis points, even as management describes fewer deals closing across the market. That combination cuts two ways. It could mean lenders are finally regaining pricing power after a borrower-friendly stretch through 2024 and 2025. Or it could simply mean deal volume has thinned enough that any capital chasing the same shrinking pipeline mechanically bids spreads wider — discipline dressed up as leverage. Note too that about 90% of Ares Capital's portfolio carries structurally originated PIK provisions — that's optionality embedded in loan documents, not a statement that 90% of the book is actually being paid in kind. And set the record Ares raise against the industry as a whole: private-credit fund inflows are down 25% year-to-date versus 2025. So the record raise isn't evidence the asset class is thriving broadly — it's evidence capital is consolidating toward the biggest brand-name managers, exactly the managers whose marks look benign next to a rising index. So what: dry powder is piling up at the top of the market while the money supply to everyone else contracts.

Where is that concentrated capital actually going? Increasingly, into a single trade: AI data-center financing. Blue Owl's Stack Infrastructure has been reported by Bloomberg to be seeking financing on the order of several billion dollars for AI data-center capacity, a sharp step up from the roughly two billion it raised as recently as February — that figure hasn't closed and should be treated as reported, not settled. KKR, meanwhile, launched its Helix Digital Infrastructure vehicle in June with more than $10 billion in capital commitments for AI data centers, power generation, and connectivity. The financing cadence here is accelerating, not plateauing. And the risk is concentration marketed as diversification: these are asset-backed loans secured against long-term contracted hyperscaler revenue, but a handful of managers are writing multiple such deals against a handful of counterparties. If one hyperscaler slows its capex cycle or renegotiates a contract, the collateral thesis underneath billions of dollars of paper from the same manager gets tested all at once, not in isolation. So what: what looks like a diversified private-credit sleeve is really a correlated bet on a small number of AI buyers staying committed at current scale.

One more valve worth naming briefly. The global credit secondary market more than doubled to $20.4 billion in the first half of this year, and GP-led transactions — largely continuation vehicles — drove about 83% of that volume. That's a market letting limited partners exit positions in an asset class that markets itself as illiquid and hold-to-maturity by design. It's not clear yet whether that's healthy — a pressure valve preventing forced sales — or another way stress could transmit quickly across managers. What is clear is that continuation-vehicle pricing is becoming a second, GP-influenced benchmark for what these loans are worth, alongside the managers' own quarterly marks. So what: another layer between the reported value of this asset class and a real market-clearing price.

Pull it together and the pattern across all three channels is the same. The default index is rising and now confirmed rising by two agencies, even as the largest managers' own marks stay benign. Record capital keeps flowing to the same handful of managers who are placing an increasingly concentrated bet on AI-financing demand. And a doubling secondary market is supplying an exit valve without a real price-discovery event. None of that proves the marks are wrong. It does mean the burden of proof now sits with the managers, not the skeptics. Watch Apollo's earnings next week. Apollo is large and scaled like Ares, so if its non-accruals also print near or below the historical average, the divergence with the rising index only widens — and that, not a reassuring headline, is the thing that would actually demand an explanation. And watch whether the Fitch trend shows up in more managers' non-accrual disclosures before you take this quarter's benign framing at face value.

That's the private-credit briefing for August 1st. Talk to you next time.
