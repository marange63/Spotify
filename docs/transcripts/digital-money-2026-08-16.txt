Good morning, it's Sunday, August 16th, and this is your digital money briefing.

The week's biggest legitimacy story in stablecoins just landed, and it's a real one. Tether says KPMG has issued an unqualified audit opinion on its 2025 financial statements. That's the first full audit in the company's history — not another quarterly attestation, but a complete Big Four sign-off, down to a physical inspection of Tether's gold bars. The opinion confirms reserves exceeded liabilities by $6.814 billion as of December 31st, 2025, on a USDT base now above $180 billion. For years, the single most-cited attack on Tether was that it had never submitted to a full audit — a talking point used by skeptics and by rival issuers like Circle alike. That talking point is now gone. So what: a full audit is a genuine credibility upgrade, and it likely lowers the bar to USDT being accepted as eligible collateral wherever audited financials were the gating requirement. It also flips the competitive script. Circle has long leaned on being the transparent, regulation-first stablecoin; a full Tether audit pressures Circle to go produce comparable assurance of its own rather than just point at its attestations. And the timing isn't an accident — it lands just ahead of the CLARITY Act's stablecoin provisions in Washington, and just as Tether pushes a US-regulated product it calls USAT.

But look hard at the date on that opinion, because it changes what the audit actually tells you. It's a year-end snapshot — reserves as of December 31st, 2025 — and KPMG's spokesperson declined further comment on client-confidentiality grounds, so the underlying report isn't independently public yet. You're taking the numbers as Tether announced them. For a stablecoin, the snapshot problem is the whole ballgame. You audit a stock or a bond once a year and that's fine, but a stablecoin promises to be worth a dollar every second of every day. An eight-month-old opinion proves the reserves were there at year-end. It does not prove a redemption you send in today gets honored at par. And the harder number is what's happening right now. Data from August 12th shows USDT's supply has shrunk by roughly $4 billion over the trailing sixty days, with $870 million redeemed in just an eleven-day span. So what: the legitimacy milestone arrived at the exact moment the float started leaving. Credibility up, dollars out.

And it's not just Tether. USDC's supply also declined over that same window. That matters because it breaks the tidy story everyone reached for a week ago — that dollars were simply rotating out of USDT and into its more heavily regulated rival. They're not. Both of the two dominant stablecoins are contracting at once. That's the third time this week this briefing has flagged the stablecoin float thinning underneath the headlines — Tether's excess reserve buffer halving on Thursday, USDC grinding down toward $73 billion on Friday, and now the two of them shrinking together. We don't have a confirmed cause, and I'm not going to hand you one. It could be exchange-specific deleveraging, it could be broader risk-off positioning, it could be tax. Don't take a mechanism on faith. But the direction isn't in doubt. So what: if issuers are holding fewer dollars in reserve, they're buying fewer short-dated Treasury bills to back them. That's a small but real marginal drag on short-end Treasury demand. You can't size it precisely, but the sign is negative, and it runs directly against the bullish case that stablecoins are a durable new bid for T-bills.

That same demand-fade shows up a layer up, in the Bitcoin ETF complex. US spot Bitcoin ETFs posted a net outflow of $389.7 million for the week of August 10th through the 14th — the largest weekly withdrawal in six weeks. And here's the whipsaw. The week before, August 3rd through the 7th, those same funds pulled in $853.5 million, their largest weekly haul since mid-April. So we've gone from the best inflow week in four months straight into the worst outflow week in six, back to back. Bitcoin itself has drifted from roughly $65,000 in early August down to just above $63,000 this weekend. On Saturday this show flagged a two-day reversal and cautioned that two sessions don't make a trend. That reversal has now hardened into a full negative week. So what: institutional flows chased the early-August bounce and gave most of it back within days once price faded. That's reflexive positioning, not conviction — and it's exactly why spot-ETF flow is a poor standalone signal for direction right now.

The number worth sitting with, though, isn't the aggregate outflow. It's the concentration underneath it. BlackRock's IBIT alone accounted for $693 million of that early-August inflow — roughly 81 cents of every dollar that entered the entire category. Then on August 14th, IBIT again led the outflows, at $55.5 million, with Fidelity's FBTC losing $6.8 million, while Bitwise's BITB actually took in a small $6.1 million. So the same fund is dominating both directions — leading the way in and leading the way out. So what: the ETF complex isn't diversifying custody and flow risk the way its boosters claim. It's concentrating it. A redemption shock in IBIT specifically would move the market disproportionately, because at this point it isn't really a basket. It's one product with a wrapper around it, and one custodian sitting behind the bulk of the coins.

Not every corner of the complex is bleeding, though, and the exception is worth a beat. Solana ETFs drew $10.26 million on the week — their strongest showing since May — even as Bitcoin funds bled and Ether funds ran slightly negative, losing $2.25 million. Now, that's one week of data on a much smaller base, so treat it as a tentative signal, not a confirmed rotation. I'm not going to call a trend off a single print. But it fits something we flagged on Friday too: hands are still moving in this market, capital isn't fleeing wholesale — it's just not moving toward Bitcoin beta right now. The marginal institutional dollar is getting more selective about which crypto exposure it actually wants.

Put the whole week together and you land on the same test this show has been running for days: the marketed number against the harder number sitting underneath it. Tether markets a clean, audited balance sheet; the harder number is a float that's actively shrinking. The ETF complex markets institutionalization and adoption; the harder number is the worst outflow week in six, concentrated in a single product. And here's the honest part — both stories are true at the same time. The credibility infrastructure of digital money is genuinely improving. A full audit is real. A maturing ETF market is real. But the money itself is, for now, moving out the door. That gap is the thing to watch into September, when the CLARITY Act gets its first real Senate test and issuers start planning around whatever regulatory clarity — or lack of it — comes out of that vote.

That's your briefing for August 16th. Have a good Sunday.
