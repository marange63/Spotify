Good morning, it's Thursday, August 6th, 2026, and this is your enterprise technology briefing.

The question in enterprise software has quietly moved on. A few months ago the debate was whether AI demand inside the enterprise was real. Now it's settled that the demand is real. The live fight is how the market prices the disruption that AI creates for the incumbents selling into that demand, and this week gave us the clearest look yet at how unsettled that pricing model still is.

Start with the whipsaw in per-seat software. In the last week of July, Intuit, Adobe, and Salesforce each fell more than ten percent, part of a broader software selloff. Morgan Stanley put a number on the skepticism, initiating coverage of Workday at Underweight with a hundred-forty-five dollar price target, writing that the company's AI initiatives are unlikely to, quote, "drive a meaningful growth acceleration yet in the near term." The read on Intuit was sharper still. Analysts worried large language models could handle tax filing and entry-level accounting at a fraction of the cost, eating directly into the moat that justifies its per-seat pricing.

Then the trade reversed, hard. Workday was July's top-performing software stock, up thirty-one percent for the month. Adobe rose twenty-two percent, Intuit almost twenty-two, Salesforce over seventeen. The catalyst was Palantir's blowout second-quarter print earlier this week, which we covered in detail Tuesday — revenue up ninety-three percent, U.S. commercial growth of a hundred forty-nine percent, guidance raised for a third straight quarter. That print didn't just validate Palantir. It reset sentiment across the whole software cohort, dragging the very incumbents it implicitly threatens back up with it.

Here's why that matters beyond the price action. The market still can't agree on whether AI features like Salesforce's Agentforce or Adobe's AI Companion are genuine new revenue lines or defensive crouches against agentic substitution. It's worth being honest that the causal story here is contested. A broad, fast reversal within days looks at least as much like sentiment and positioning unwinding as it does a considered re-rating of anyone's competitive moat. But the dividing line the market is groping toward is clear enough. Vendors monetizing AI through consumption or outcome-based pricing, like Palantir and Snowflake, are getting rewarded outright. Vendors still selling AI as a bolt-on to a per-seat license are getting tested every time sentiment turns.

And there's a reason the doubt keeps resurfacing. Industry surveys this year keep finding the same gap. AI agents are now embedded in the large majority of enterprise applications, but only a minority of enterprises have one actually running in production. Treat the exact percentages as directional rather than precise, because the underlying survey data is noisy. But the shape is consistent, and it's telling. Embedding an agent is not the same as depending on one. Until that gap closes, the substitution thesis stays a question the market can talk itself into, or out of, on any given week. Expect that test to run again at third-quarter earnings, because nothing about the underlying substitution question got resolved this week — only the stock prices moved.

Now, who's funding all of this. The same capital that whipsawed those software multiples is also underwriting the infrastructure buildout beneath them, and here the story has shifted too — from proving demand to proving capital discipline. Microsoft's Azure crossed a hundred billion dollars in annualized revenue for the first time, growing forty-three percent. Amazon Web Services took in forty-two point two billion dollars for the quarter, up thirty-seven percent, putting it on a hundred-sixty-nine billion dollar run rate. Google Cloud grew fastest of the three, at eighty-two percent. None of that is new tension — cloud growth accelerating across all three hyperscalers has been the backdrop all week.

What is new is the capital allocation picture underneath it. Google has raised its full-year 2026 capex guidance to a hundred eighty to a hundred ninety billion dollars. Microsoft is tracking toward roughly a hundred twenty billion. Google is now outspending Microsoft on infrastructure despite running a cloud business roughly a third the size of Azure's. That gap is the story investors will be watching into next year — not whether the demand is there, since growth answers that, but whether Google's return on that capital holds up. And here's the mechanical catch. Depreciation on a buildout this size doesn't hit the income statement all at once. It lands in tranches over the following years, which means the margin bill for 2026's spending is really a 2027 and 2028 story. Strong consumption growth today doesn't settle that. It's precisely the combination of growth and spend at this scale that makes capital discipline, not demand, the argument now.

Someone has to secure everything hyperscalers and enterprises are building, and Fortinet's print this week is a useful signal for who's provisioning that. Fortinet reported second-quarter revenue of two point zero five billion dollars, up twenty-six percent, but the number worth sitting with is product revenue, which grew fifty-two percent — double the total. Billings rose thirty-three percent to two point three seven billion, free cash flow more than tripled to nine hundred sixty-six million dollars, and the company raised full-year guidance again. Management pointed to broad-based demand across secure networking, SASE, and AI-driven security operations, with operational-technology billings up more than fifty-five percent. Product revenue running well ahead of total revenue looks like a hardware refresh cycle tied specifically to securing AI data centers and industrial environments, not routine renewal. Fortinet also announced AI-security collaborations with Intel, Anthropic, OpenAI, and Nvidia — worth flagging as announced partnerships, not proven, revenue-generating deployments, since no financial detail was disclosed. Still, the underlying provisioning trend is real, and it points toward a coming refresh cycle at Palo Alto Networks, Zscaler, and CrowdStrike as enterprises harden the perimeter around their AI and industrial infrastructure. Watch the operational-technology line especially — critical-infrastructure security is quietly becoming its own faster-growing budget, separate from the core firewall.

Last, a dated deadline worth having on your calendar. Starting August 17th, Atlassian begins using customer content and metadata from Jira, Confluence, and related products to train its AI tools, affecting roughly three hundred thousand organizations. In-app content collection defaults on for Free and Standard tiers and off for Premium and Enterprise. Metadata collection defaults on across every tier, and only Enterprise customers can opt out of that at all. GitLab, a direct competitor, has built marketing around the contrast, stating flatly that it does not train on customer data at any tier. Take that framing for what it is — a competitor's sales pitch — but the underlying deadline is real, and it turns a policy announced months ago into a live decision point for IT and legal teams this month. Expect data-governance terms to become an explicit line item in procurement conversations wherever this comes up, particularly in regulated industries evaluating Atlassian against alternatives.

Pull it together and the theme holds across all four items. The market is repricing on delivered results — Palantir's print, Fortinet's guidance raise — while it's still whipsawing on unproven claims about substitution risk and AI training practices. Demand for enterprise AI is no longer the open question. Where the money actually settles is.
