Cautious Optimism Briefings

AI in Biopharma R&D

Transcript · October 15, 2026

Good morning. It's Thursday, October 15th, 2026, and this is your briefing on AI in biopharma R&D.

For the first time this week, a big pharma company paid for AI-picked biology, not AI-optimized chemistry. That's the headline. It paid on the same option terms as every other AI deal. And on the same day, the public market was asked to fund two AI-native biotechs in cash, against much thinner evidence. Today's briefing is about that split.

Start with the lead. AstraZeneca announced a strategic collaboration with insitro, Daphne Koller's AI biotech. The goal is to find new drug targets in MASH, the severe fatty liver disease, and in cardiomyopathies. Both companies' own press releases, out today, give the same terms. insitro gets a 40 million dollar upfront payment. It is eligible for up to approximately 1.46 billion dollars in development and commercial milestones. And it earns single-digit royalties on anything that reaches the market. You may see a larger headline total in some coverage. Neither company's release supports it, so treat 1.46 billion as the real milestone pool.

Here's why this one is different. On Wednesday, this show argued that the week's AI deals were paying for chemistry. That means designing and optimizing a molecule once the target is already chosen. Nobody was paying to pick the target itself. That distinction matters because target selection is the lever on Phase 2 efficacy failure. Phase 2 is where most drugs die, and it's the stage AI has touched least. Koller describes insitro's platform in exactly those terms. In her words, it "simultaneously deconvolutes disease biology and identifies causal drivers of disease pathologies." That's a bet on causal biology from human and cellular data, made before any wet-lab campaign starts. And it's aimed at two indications where conventional drug hunting has a poor hit rate. For comparison, Zealand Pharma paid Generate Biomedicines 30 million dollars upfront on Tuesday for AI-designed obesity proteins. The cash is similar, but that deal buys molecule design, not target biology. AstraZeneca's 40 million is the first upfront this week attached to the harder problem.

Now the skeptical turn, because the structure here is familiar even if the subject isn't. AstraZeneca's 40 million dollar upfront is under three percent of the 1.46 billion dollar milestone pool. That's a cheap option on the biology, not a committed check. The division of labor tells you the rest. insitro takes AstraZeneca-nominated targets from discovery through to a development candidate. At that point AstraZeneca holds exclusive worldwide rights. It runs the clinical trials, the regulatory work, and the commercialization. From there, insitro's upside is milestones and royalties. So insitro's economics still ride on AstraZeneca choosing to fund those candidates through Phase 2. The causal-biology pitch doesn't remove that attrition risk. It only changes which failure point the deal is priced against.

And the track record is still thin. insitro's earlier pharma partnerships include Bristol Myers Squibb in ALS, which selected a first novel genetic target, and Eli Lilly in metabolic disease. We found no public report of an IND filing or a trial start from either one. That's an absence of disclosure, not proof that nothing is moving. But it means insitro's demonstrated output so far is target nomination, not clinical validation. Target-ID claims take years to validate, not quarters. You only know a causal target was right once a drug against it clears an efficacy endpoint. And MASH is a crowded, high-failure indication, so it will be a visible test case either way. The honest read: AstraZeneca bought a cheap, informed look at the hardest problem in the pipeline. It hasn't bought a result.

One more open question is worth flagging. Neither company's release says whether insitro keeps any rights to the data or models it builds while working on AstraZeneca's biology. If insitro retains them, its platform compounds across every pharma deal it signs. If it doesn't, each of these collaborations looks less like a platform bet and more like a service contract. That ties back to Wednesday's argument from Xaira, that proprietary data, not the model, is the moat. My inference: if causal-discovery deals like this multiply, leverage in the stack shifts toward whoever owns the underlying genetic and cellular datasets. The contracts will decide who that is, and so far they're silent.

That's what pharma pays privately, on an option, with years to prove out. Here's what the public market was asked to pay, in cash, up front.

Iambic Therapeutics priced its Nasdaq IPO at 16 dollars a share, and trading under the ticker IAM begins today. This is the Nvidia-backed, AI-native oncology biotech this show covered on Monday. Back then, the open question was where it would price and how it would trade. Now we know the price. The aftermarket verdict is still pending, so don't read the pricing as an endorsement. What investors are buying is one clinical asset, IAM1363, in a Phase 1/1b trial. The company behind it logged 12.8 million dollars of revenue in the first half of this year, against a 50.1 million dollar net loss. As we noted Monday, the filing disclosed safety data but no efficacy figure. An IPO price is a financing event. It is not a readout. So the listing window for AI-native biotechs is open, but not yet confirmed by trading.

And here's what life after that kind of IPO looks like in practice. Recursion Pharmaceuticals is the most closely watched public AI-discovery name. Today it priced a 200 million dollar equity offering. That comes on top of roughly 561 million dollars of cash at the end of September, a preliminary and unaudited figure. In the same stretch, Sanofi exercised its option on the first development candidate from its collaboration with Recursion. It's a potential first-in-class immunology candidate, and it isn't in the clinic yet. Put those two facts side by side and you get the business model for a listed AI-discovery company. Partners like Sanofi monetize the platform by paying for options on specific candidates. Public shareholders fund the burn on everything Recursion chooses to own and run itself. Keep the share-price reaction to the dilution separate from any verdict on the science. The so-what: dilution is the price Recursion pays for owning its pipeline.

Now set all of that against the highest-profile AI-first drug designer, which still has no patient at all. Demis Hassabis has said that Isomorphic Labs, the Google DeepMind spinout, expects its first clinical trial by the end of this year. That target was itself already a delay from an earlier goal. As of the end of September, Isomorphic had announced no IND filing and no trial start. There are about eleven weeks left in the year. Sponsors don't always disclose an IND the moment it's filed, so silence isn't proof of a miss. But the tension is real. The platform with the most prestige is the one with the least to show against a disclosed clinical deadline.

So pull the threads together. On clinical success rates, AstraZeneca and insitro have made the first bet this week aimed at the lever that matters most. That lever is picking targets that survive Phase 2. Nothing today proves it moves. On R&D spend, AI-driven discovery is still option-priced. Pharma puts up small guaranteed cash against large contingent tails. Clinical budgets only shift once AI-picked targets clear trials at a demonstrably better rate than targets picked the old way. One gap to note: we found no fresh, well-sourced developments this cycle in real-world data or clinical operations, so those stay off today's ledger. Three things to watch from here. Does AstraZeneca or insitro name an actual target under this deal? How does Iambic trade once the market digests its pricing? And does Isomorphic file before the year runs out?
