Good morning. It's Friday, September 25th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

A word on freshness before we start. Nothing here broke in the last three days, and none of it has run on this show before. Some of it is months old. I'll date each piece as we go, because the age changes what it means.

Start with the foundry, because it reframes everything after it. Reporting from The Information, relayed through secondary outlets, says TSMC has told both Nvidia and Broadcom that it cannot provide as much production capacity as they want. That is the reported wording, not a TSMC statement. The paywalled original, as relayed, doesn't tell us which nodes, which packaging lines, or which years. We also can't pin down when that report first ran. What we can date is the underlying constraint. Back in March, a Broadcom product-marketing executive said it on the record. Quote: "We are seeing that TSMC is hitting production capacity limits, and that capacity has become a bottleneck, or that has kind of choked the supply chain in 2026." So this is not a sudden shock. One of the two customers named in the rationing story acknowledged the squeeze at least six months ago.

Put that next to what we covered on the 21st. Then, Samsung was making Qualcomm wait on its 2-nanometer process over price. Now TSMC is reportedly telling its marquee AI customers no on volume. Both leading-edge foundries are turning big customers away in the same month. One is doing it on price, the other on quantity. That is demand constrained by compute availability, not by economics.

The capex response is real, and it is older than it looks. TSMC raised its 2026 capital spending guidance to between $60 and $64 billion. The prior budget was $52 to $56 billion. That came with TSMC's second-quarter results in July, so it is about two months old.

Chief financial officer Wendell Huang put 70 to 80 percent of the total on advanced process technologies. It is still the company-level dollar figure this show kept saying was missing. On the 22nd, the Kaohsiung packaging park came with no capex number. On the 24th, TrendForce's leading-edge wafer targets came with none either. Now there is one, and it is large. The first beneficiaries sit upstream. Foundry capex lands as tool orders for lithography, deposition, etch and inspection long before it lands as wafers. So the equipment makers' order visibility should stretch further out, even while the chips those tools will make are still being rationed.

Now the skeptical read. A rationing headline suits nearly everyone who repeats it. It supports TSMC's 2027 pricing, including the wafer increases and over-allocation surcharges we covered on the 21st. It also gives customers cover to dual-source. And "cannot supply all you want" is fully compatible with shipping record volumes. It measures excess demand, not shrinking supply. The capex raise cuts against permanence, too. A foundry spending more on advanced nodes is telling you it expects the shortfall to close. So here is where the lead lands. For the next year, allocation, not chip design, decides who ships. A design win or a launch turns into revenue only as far as TSMC's queue allows.

That puts a sharp question to one of the two customers in that queue, because Broadcom just raised its guidance anyway. Its fiscal third quarter, reported in early September, showed AI semiconductor revenue up 221 percent year over year, to $16.7 billion. That print is three weeks old, but it hasn't run here before. The outlook matters more. Broadcom now expects about $115 billion of AI chip revenue in the fiscal year ending October 2027. Its prior forecast was over $100 billion. Be clear about what that is. It is management's own outlook, not booked revenue. And custom-accelerator revenue sits with a small number of hyperscaler and lab customers.

So how does a company reportedly short of foundry capacity raise its number? There are two honest readings, and the record doesn't let us pick between them. In the first, the Broadcom outlook is already backed by allocation booked earlier. TSMC's refusal then bites only on incremental orders above the plan, and the guide is safe. In the second, the guide assumes capacity TSMC hasn't committed. Then the risk isn't demand. It's timing. We found nothing on the record from Broadcom's third-quarter call on whether that outlook rests on secured foundry supply. So this stays open.

There is one structural point in Broadcom's favor either way. A custom accelerator is built to a customer's committed volume. That makes it easier to size to a fixed slice of foundry capacity than a merchant GPU chasing open-ended demand. That is an argument, not a disclosed fact. But in an allocated market, predictability of volume is worth something. The takeaway is that Broadcom's headline number is now partly a bet on its place in TSMC's queue.

The same rationing logic shows up in memory, where it has a visible price. Secondary reporting, from an aggregator site rather than any supplier, says a 36-gigabyte HBM3E module traded at roughly $2,100 on the spot market in early September. Under long-term agreements, buyers typically pay $300 to $400. Even against the top of that contract range, the spot price is more than five times higher.

Read that spot number carefully. Most HBM moves under contract. So $2,100 is what a buyer without allocation pays, not the industry's average selling price. Treating it as the price level would overstate supplier margins. What it does measure is the cost of showing up late.

The contract side, meanwhile, is spoken for. In its fiscal third-quarter release this summer, Micron said its HBM3E and HBM4 are fully booked through calendar 2027. It added that HBM4 has already generated over a billion dollars in revenue. On the 22nd, we reported Micron's calendar-2026 supply as sold. This extends that by a full year. The point to bank is that early allocation is itself the competitive advantage. Buyers holding long-term agreements are protected. Latecomers pay spot or wait. It is the same logic as TSMC's queue.

Now a correction we owe you. On the 24th, we said the counter-signal to SK hynix's claim of shortage beyond 2030 was capex. We said nobody had put a number on that yet. Micron already had. In that same fiscal third-quarter release, Micron raised fiscal 2026 capital spending to about $27 billion. It also said fiscal 2027 capex will rise above the mid-$40 billion range. Most of that increase is tied to construction. That was the number we said was missing, and we should have found it.

It changes the argument in two directions. First, it confirms the shortage is real enough to justify a much bigger budget. Second, construction-heavy capex buys buildings before it buys bits. My inference, not a disclosed schedule, is that most of that capacity yields output later in the decade rather than in this cycle. So the near-term squeeze stays. But the claim that scarcity is structural past 2030 now has a cyclical clock running under it. When that capacity lands, today's scarcity pricing has an expiration date.

Put the pieces together. In this stretch of the AI buildout, the binding constraint has moved from design to allocation. TSMC is reportedly rationing two marquee AI customers. Broadcom's raised outlook depends on where it sits in that queue. Memory buyers without contracts pay more than five times the contract price. And the investor error to watch runs in one direction. Anyone capitalizing today's foundry and memory pricing as permanent is ignoring the supply those same companies are now paying for. TSMC's raised capex and Micron's construction-heavy capex are the mechanism that eventually turns scarcity rents back into an ordinary cycle.

Two dated tests from here. Micron reports its fiscal fourth quarter on September 30th, and that report has not happened yet. And watch whether TSMC, Nvidia, or Broadcom confirm the allocation limits on the record, rather than through secondhand reporting.
