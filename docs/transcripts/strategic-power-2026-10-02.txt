Good morning. It's Friday, October 2nd, 2026, and this is your strategic power briefing.

This week, capacity moved through private contracts, not through policy. A Chinese leasing balance sheet carried current-generation Nvidia servers past US export controls. And a hyperscaler's twenty-year check did in Maryland what a suspended grid auction couldn't do in the mid-Atlantic. In both cases, the headline dollar figure overstates the physical capacity actually created or denied. Let's start with the chips.

This beat has been tracking an escalating arc. On Wednesday, the story was Beijing deciding which tier of Nvidia chip it lets in. Yesterday, it was Aivres, a California-based server maker whose Blackwell servers, shipped to Southeast Asia, ultimately served Chinese customers. That was a side door, and one that may be legal. Today, it's the front door. Bloomberg's review of Chinese filings shows a leasing firm called Semi-Tech Leasing Group financed physical Nvidia B300 servers. They were Asustek machines, and they were slated for a China Mobile computing hub in Ningxia. The filings itemize one such deal, covering 32 of those servers. Separately, Semi-Tech says it has deployed more than 1.6 billion dollars into compute infrastructure overall. According to coverage of that Bloomberg review, Semi-Tech was seeded with money from China's national semiconductor fund. It is now controlled mainly by municipal government entities in Shenzhen and Beijing. That's specific enough to call it state-linked, though not formal state ownership. And after scrutiny began, Semi-Tech refiled its credit-registry paperwork with the hardware descriptions, supplier names, and server model numbers stripped out. That reads less like an accounting correction than an attempt to make the paper trail harder to follow.

The second case is older conduct, newly charged. Federal prosecutors in Los Angeles have charged a tech executive, Greg Lui, with smuggling more than 300 million dollars of export-controlled servers to China between 2023 and 2024. He is charged, not convicted. The Justice Department's own press release lays out the route. Servers went to Malaysia and Singapore, where no license was required, and were then illegally re-exported to China. Notice what that release does not say. It never names Nvidia or Asustek, referring only to unnamed hardware makers. So the legal exposure in this case sits with the alleged smuggler, not the chipmaker. For Nvidia and server makers like Asustek, the risk is political and reputational. Indirect China exposure becomes a due-diligence target, but so far it is not a legal finding.

Now, scale honesty. That 32-server deal is the only Blackwell transaction the Semi-Tech filings itemize. The 1.6 billion dollars is Semi-Tech's whole compute book, not a Blackwell number, and the two shouldn't be conflated. The Lui case covers 2023 and 2024, including orders for H100-equipped servers. That's a generation behind today's B300s, so prosecution is running roughly two years behind the chip cycle it's meant to deter. One financing deal and one backward-looking indictment can't measure the aggregate volume still getting through. My read, and it's analysis: the evidence leans toward controls that are leaky but still biting. Thirty-two servers routed through a state-linked lessor, with paperwork scrubbed after the fact, looks like a costly workaround, not an open pipe. But these cases can't close the question, because nobody has an aggregate number.

What the cases do show is that leakage now runs through three distinct channels. There's remote use of servers abroad, the Aivres case. There's state-linked financing of physical hardware, the Semi-Tech case. And there's individual smuggling through transshipment hubs, the Lui case. Each needs a different enforcement tool. The lever used to be which chip tier gets a license. Increasingly, it's who finances the purchase and who resells the hardware. And the financing is the real signal. The same reporting shows Glory View, the developer behind the Ningxia deployment, separately raised more than 450 million dollars through sale-and-leaseback deals since mid-2025. Chinese capital-markets plumbing is funding this buildout, so the counterparty to watch is a lessor, not just a chip buyer. There's a cost for Malaysia and Singapore too. Southeast Asia has now shown up as a conduit in two straight briefings, which raises the odds of tighter end-use checks on those routes. That's a regulatory risk to the region's data-center investment pitch.

If the chip leak runs through financing, so does the fix on the power grid. Amazon and Constellation Energy announced a twenty-year power purchase agreement on Wednesday, tied to Calvert Cliffs, the nuclear plant in Maryland. Per Constellation, it unlocks more than 3 billion dollars of investment. Amazon gets 690 megawatts from a plant rated at 1,790 megawatts. The timing matters. It landed one day after FERC suspended PJM's emergency backstop auction, largely over who pays for new data-center load. Where the public mechanism stalled, a private one moved: Amazon simply paid, bilaterally, for two decades.

Set that against yesterday's US-Korea nuclear framework. That was an "up to" ceiling with no named sites. This is a signed contract on a named plant, tied to a relicensing decision. That's the difference between a press release and an operating asset.

But look at what's actually new. Only about 190 megawatts of the deal is new generating capacity, and it doesn't arrive until 2030 to 2032. The bulk is Amazon contracting existing output. That gives Constellation the revenue certainty, in its words, to relicense the plant for another twenty years. No regulatory approval of that renewal is confirmed yet. So the deal protects against a future loss of capacity. It does nothing for PJM's 2028-29 shortfall. My read, as analysis: a twenty-year hyperscaler contract is a financing template for other nuclear plants facing relicensing, because it turns relicensing into a contracted-revenue decision. But there's a catch. If hyperscalers keep contracting around the existing fleet, the shared backstop costs fall on whoever is left in the pool. Bilateral deals don't answer FERC's cost-causation question. They route around it.

The fuel underneath those twenty-year nuclear contracts is tightening too. According to market commentary, long-term uranium contract prices hit a nominal all-time high of about 96 dollars and fifty cents a pound in September. That edges past the 2007 record in nominal terms, though not adjusted for inflation. Yet Kazakhstan's Kazatomprom has cut its 2026 production guidance from roughly 32,800 tonnes to about 29,700. Supply falling while price sets records is producer discipline, not a market rushing to meet demand. For anyone signing long-dated nuclear contracts, the fuel they're banking on is getting scarcer, not cheaper.

One more place the relative-power math shifted this week: oil. Brent traded near 96 dollars and 76 cents a barrel on October 1st. US benchmark crude, WTI, was trading near 92 dollars and 63 cents overnight. That puts the spread at roughly four dollars. On Tuesday, this beat put that gap at about twelve dollars, and argued Brent's Gulf premium had room to persist. That call hasn't held. The most consistent reading is that the move follows the recovery in tanker traffic through the Strait of Hormuz that this beat reported Wednesday. But demand and positioning could be contributing, and the evidence doesn't let us settle on one cause. Either way, the US input-cost edge over oil importers in Europe and Asia is narrowing. Meanwhile WTI remains up about 52 percent on the year. The inflation pressure from expensive oil hasn't gone away. The US just isn't as insulated from it, relative to its rivals.

Pull it together. In chips and in power, contracts and capital flows moved faster than the policies meant to govern them. And in both, the headline number overstates the physical change. A lessor with more than 1.6 billion dollars deployed has just one itemized Blackwell deal on the record, and it's 32 servers. A 3 billion dollar nuclear investment buys about 190 new megawatts, and not before 2030. In oil, the market moved faster than this beat's own frame. The edge America was banking on is thinner than it looked a week ago.

Watch three things, each following from today's stories. First, any Commerce response aimed at lessors and resellers rather than chip tiers. That would confirm the enforcement lever has moved to financing. Second, whether the Calvert Cliffs relicensing actually moves toward regulatory approval, which is the step that turns Amazon's contract into twenty more years of capacity. And third, FERC's follow-on proceedings on PJM before the backstop's new February 28th effective date. That's where the who-pays question either gets answered or keeps getting routed around.

That's your briefing. Have a good weekend.
