Good morning, it's Wednesday, August 5th, and this is your semiconductor and compute briefing.

Let's start with a genuine puzzle. AMD reported second-quarter results after the close yesterday, and by almost every measure it was a record quarter. Total revenue came in at eleven point five four billion dollars, beating the roughly eleven point two five billion dollars Wall Street expected. Data Center revenue hit a record six point seven two billion dollars, up a hundred and seven percent year over year. That segment swung from a hundred and fifty-five million dollar operating loss a year ago to two point one billion dollars in operating income this quarter. AMD didn't just beat, it raised: third-quarter revenue guidance came in around thirteen billion dollars, against a Street estimate of twelve point five one billion. That is a textbook beat-and-raise. And the stock fell roughly eight percent anyway.

That reaction is this week's cleanest signal about how the market is treating AI-silicon names right now. Reporting attributes the selloff partly to AMD's run-up — the stock was already up about a hundred and forty percent this year, so a solid beat wasn't the blowout some investors had priced in. That's an analyst's read on price action, not something AMD said, so treat it as interpretation. But the pattern itself is real, and it's not new this week. Arm fell about eight percent on its own beat, which we covered on August 1st, and Qualcomm was caught in the same re-rating that day. AMD, with the sharpest example yet, makes it three-for-three: the market has stopped paying for AI-silicon growth on its own and started pricing execution and margin instead.

Now, part of the initial explanation floating around was a gross-margin miss — the idea that AMD's margin fell short of a fifty-six percent consensus. That framing doesn't hold up once you look at the actual numbers on a like-for-like basis. AMD's non-GAAP gross margin for the quarter was fifty-six percent, and the company guided third-quarter non-GAAP gross margin to approximately that same fifty-six percent. There's no miss there — the guide and the actual line up. The fifty-four percent figure that got compared against fifty-six percent elsewhere is AMD's GAAP gross margin, a different and lower accounting basis, so that comparison was apples to oranges. So the margin story isn't what sold this stock off.

What's left is the Data Center segment's underlying profitability. Two point one billion dollars of operating income on six point seven two billion dollars of revenue works out to roughly a thirty-one percent segment margin — a real and dramatic swing from a loss a year ago, but still a profile built on a much smaller, newer revenue base than Nvidia's. AMD didn't offer specific shipment or customer-qualification numbers for its new Instinct MI400 lineup on the call. CEO Lisa Su's on-record comment was that the company enters the second half with, quote, "strong momentum as EPYC demand accelerates, Instinct deployments scale and Helios begins to ramp." CFO Jean Hu added only that Data Center sales are expected to accelerate in the second half, without a quantified margin target. So the real open question the market is pricing isn't last quarter's margin — it's whether that ramp language converts into shipped, profitable volume. And AMD isn't yet putting numbers behind it.

That's the hand-off to why investors might be skeptical in the first place. AMD used this earnings cycle to formally launch its MI400 series, including the MI455X as the flagship part and the MI430X aimed at sovereign and HPC workloads. A SemiAnalysis technical teardown published Monday gives the clearest read yet on what that flagship actually looks like in the field versus on the spec sheet. On paper, the MI455X is genuinely impressive: a five-and-a-half-times-reticle CoWoS-L package with over thirty-four hundred square millimeters of logic silicon, four hundred thirty-two gigabytes of HBM4 memory, and a claimed twenty petaflops of FP8 compute — ahead of Nvidia's still-unshipped Rubin part at seventeen and a half petaflops. That's a spec sheet comparison against a competitor's roadmap number, not two shipping chips going head to head, so hold it loosely.

Here's the more interesting part. SemiAnalysis reports that Meta — one of AMD's largest committed GPU customers — actually ordered a cut-down version of that same chip: only six of eight compute dies, a single I-O die instead of two, which halves the interconnect lanes, and eight-high memory stacks instead of twelve. The result is scale-up bandwidth of nine hundred gigabytes per second, against Rubin's eighteen hundred. SemiAnalysis calls that configuration weak for large-scale training and inference. This is a single-source report — neither AMD nor Meta has confirmed the specifics — and it's not clear whether Meta's choice was about cost, an older commitment locked in before better options existed, or a genuine technical tradeoff. But the same teardown flags concrete production friction too: AMD's Helios racks reportedly need more than five hundred fifty Broadcom networking retimers apiece just for backplane reliability, and AMD's own software teams are said to be short on stable internal GPU clusters. So what: the market's skepticism about AMD's ramp has real, specific substance behind it — announced gigawatt-scale customer commitments are not the same thing as fielded, competitive silicon, and this week's evidence says the gap between the two is still wide.

Now let's zoom out from accelerators to the memory feeding all of this. Samsung Electronics posted record second-quarter operating profit of eighty-nine point five trillion won — about sixty-two billion dollars — on revenue up a hundred and thirty percent year over year, both records, driven by DRAM and NAND prices at all-time highs. But in the same quarter, Samsung's own mobile division swung to an operating loss of seven hundred billion won, as the same rising memory costs that are enriching Samsung's chip business squeezed its own phone margins. The company that's profiting most from the memory shortage is now paying a tax on it in its other business. That's a sharper illustration than any downstream customer complaint could be, because it's happening inside one company's own books. It also functions as the industry's rebuttal to last week's scare over Chinese memory maker CXMT flooding the market — the incumbent with the best visibility into its own order book is still describing supply as tight, not loosening, though the fuller shortage-through-2028 guidance and this quarter's contract-price forecasts were both already covered earlier this week, so treat that context as a callback rather than new information.

So tie it together. The AI compute and memory boom is not in question — record Data Center revenue at AMD, record memory profit at Samsung, both genuinely happened this quarter. What's changed is that the market has stopped rewarding those headline numbers on their own. It's now sorting names on margin trajectory, on whether flagship chips actually ship in the configuration that was advertised, and on who is absorbing the cost of scarcity versus who is collecting it. Even the winners this week are redistributing somebody else's margin, not just creating new value from nothing. That's the lens to carry into Nvidia's own results later this month.
