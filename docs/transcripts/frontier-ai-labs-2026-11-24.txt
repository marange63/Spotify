Good morning. It's Tuesday, November 24th, and this is your frontier AI labs briefing.

One standing disclosure before we start. Anthropic makes the model that powers this show, and today's lead story is OpenAI responding to Anthropic.

This week, the competition among the frontier labs shifted from shipping models to controlling the gates around them. Three gates moved: a proposed capability brake, the openness of model weights, and the chip supply chain itself. Let's take them in order.

On Saturday, this desk said Anthropic chief executive Dario Amodei's pacing pledge had no metric attached, so for now it couldn't be falsified. The next day, OpenAI's chief scientist proposed one. On November 22nd, Jakub Pachocki published an essay titled "Perplexity and Intelligence." It proposes shared limits on frontier AI development. Reporting describes the yardstick as pretraining log-perplexity. It describes the limits as holding off on certain capability thresholds until AI-monitoring tools reach specific milestones. Sam Altman endorsed it directly, on X. He wrote that frontier developers should jointly establish shared norms for evaluating and red-teaming their models. Then, in his words, he proposed "common limits, based on perplexity." That's a real, public, specific endorsement, not a vague nod.

Here's why it lands now. On November 17th, OpenAI's system card rated its new GPT-6.2 Astra model High for AI self-improvement. As reported, it's the first externally released OpenAI model to reach that tier. That rating is OpenAI's own self-assessment, under its own framework, and nobody has audited it. Still, the lab that just flagged its own model at that tier is the one now asking for a shared brake before the next one.

Here's where the skepticism has to sit. There are three readings, and the evidence picks none of them. One is sincere coordination. It's an honest attempt to slow a race before it outruns anyone's ability to monitor it. Two is positioning. The essay landed three days after Anthropic began trading on Nasdaq, with safety now part of a public pitch. Proposing a voluntary brake costs OpenAI nothing, and it claims a share of that narrative. Three is yardstick-setting. Whoever proposes the metric gets to pick where the line falls. A pretraining perplexity measure tracks gains from pretraining. But much of the recent capability gain is attributed to post-training, reinforcement learning, and inference-time compute. So a perplexity line might not bind where the frontier is actually moving.

What's missing matters as much as what's proposed. No numeric threshold has been confirmed from the essay's own text. Reporting offers several candidate figures, and they don't agree with each other. That disagreement itself tells you no bright line has been fixed. No verification or enforcement mechanism has been adopted either. And no other lab has been reported responding to or endorsing this specific proposal. That includes Anthropic, Google DeepMind, xAI, and Meta. So right now, this is one lab's idea, offered unilaterally, with no reported commitment from anyone.

There's a second-order effect worth naming, and it's this desk's inference. If labs ever did coordinate on a capability ceiling, that would be coordination among direct competitors. That invites antitrust scrutiny. It also hands regulators and plaintiffs a concrete yardstick. Earlier this month, four nonprofits sued five labs in San Francisco, OpenAI and Anthropic among them, asking the court for a capability freeze. A lab-proposed metric gives that kind of litigation something to point at. So the bottom line on the lead is simple. A brake only matters once rivals sign it, and so far nobody has.

And the rivals least likely to sign are the ones outside the US entirely. Last Wednesday, this desk reported Alibaba's Qwen4-Max as an API-only model with closed weights. Today that read firms up. According to the tech site modelfit, citing Alibaba's own changelog, Qwen4-Max exited preview and went generally available on November 16th. It still has no released weights. That's secondary sourcing, and Alibaba's own page hasn't been independently checked. Alibaba has not said when, or whether, smaller open-weight versions will follow.

Why does general availability matter? Because a preview without weights can be a phase. A general release without weights is a decision. Qwen is one of the Chinese model families most identified with giving weights away, and with forcing Western labs to compete on price. Here, Alibaba made the closed-API choice for its flagship instead. Alibaba hasn't explained why. One explanation is monetization. Keeping the flagship on Alibaba Cloud captures the serving revenue directly. Another is compute scarcity. When chips are tight, selling inference beats giving the model away. A third is simply a staged rollout, with open weights to come later.

One design detail is worth holding. The model activates only about 71 billion parameters per token, out of a much larger mixture-of-experts total. That's an inference-cost signal, not a capability claim, since no independent benchmark of Qwen4-Max has turned up. And no new DeepSeek flagship has surfaced this month. So at the very top tier, the thesis that China gives the frontier away for free just got weaker. Our inference is that open-weight diffusion increasingly depends on whichever Chinese labs still release weights, and today's evidence on those labs is thin. The takeaway: Alibaba is now running its flagship the way the US labs run theirs.

Compute is the other gate on the Chinese labs, and its keeper just changed its own rules. Nvidia's Jensen Huang resigned as president, CEO, and board member on November 12th. Nvidia never formally gave a reason, but the board's later statement points to a national-security concern. Eleven days later, on November 23rd, the board reversed course. It voted unanimously to reinstate him, effective immediately. In its release, the board said it had consulted senior US government officials. It said it was confident his reinstatement poses, in its words, "no national security risk." Alongside the reinstatement, Nvidia created a new National Security and Export Control Committee. It is made up solely of independent directors, and it will oversee trade compliance and national-security risks.

On Monday, this show named two open questions over Nvidia. One was Huang's departure, and that one just closed. The other was reported scrutiny of Nvidia's accounting. Nothing in this release touches it, so it stays open. For every lab whose training and inference run through Nvidia's Blackwell and Rubin generations, the reinstatement restores roadmap continuity. The committee adds something different. It's a board-level layer of friction on top of the government's export rules, aimed squarely at China-facing decisions. Whether that slows anything in practice is this desk's inference, not a confirmed fact. It could be real friction, or it could be governance theater that buys Washington's comfort. But the direction is clear. Export control is becoming a corporate-governance function at the single chokepoint supplier. And that friction falls hardest on the labs already outside the gate.

One point on relative exposure, and the disclosure applies again here. Amazon and Google each hold Anthropic stakes valued at over one hundred billion dollars, according to CNBC. Both sell Anthropic non-Nvidia silicon, Amazon's Trainium and Google's TPUs. That doesn't make Anthropic independent of Nvidia, and the reporting doesn't say how much Nvidia capacity it uses. But two of Anthropic's largest backers are Nvidia's rivals. So Anthropic likely carries somewhat less exposure to an Nvidia governance scare than labs that lean on Nvidia supply alone.

Quickly, by dimension. Safety posture: OpenAI takes the framing initiative, with no binding effect yet. Ecosystem control: Alibaba keeps its flagship in-house. Chip access: US labs regain Nvidia continuity, while Chinese labs face one more gate. Capability: nothing independently verified this week. Pricing: no new move today. And Google made no new model move since the Gemini 4 Neon launch we covered last Friday. This week, its leverage runs through its TPUs and its Anthropic stake, not a release.

Three things to watch. First, whether any lab beyond OpenAI formally signs onto a shared perplexity metric, or whether it dies as a one-lab proposal. Second, whether Alibaba puts a date on an open-weight Qwen4 variant. That would tell us whether this was a staged rollout or a lasting strategy shift. Third, what the first China-facing decision from Nvidia's new committee looks like, and whether that separate accounting question ever resolves.

That's the briefing. Stay cautiously optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
