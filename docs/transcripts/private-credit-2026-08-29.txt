Good morning, it's Saturday, August 29th, and this is your private credit briefing.

This show has spent the week watching outside parties price private credit below the managers' own marks. A bankruptcy court did it on First Brands. A rival lender's earlier mark did it on AmeriVet. A retail redemption queue did it. A discount tender did it. Today, for the first time all week, an outside party paid full freight. That buyer is the institutional limited partner. That's the allocator who commits capital directly into these managers' funds, at par, with the fullest diligence access anyone outside the manager gets. Institutional LPs just wrote the largest quarterly check in the history of the asset class.

Start with that number. Limited partners committed a hundred and nineteen billion dollars to private credit funds in the second quarter, the fastest single quarter on record. That pushed first-half fundraising to a hundred and ninety billion, up fifty-three percent versus the first half of last year. So this is not a one-quarter spike. It's a half-year acceleration. Ares alone took in a record thirty-six billion in the quarter, twenty-three point seven billion of it into credit strategies.

Now set that against the denominator. Fitch's trailing twelve-month private credit default rate hit a record six percent through the second quarter, up from five point seven in the first. Fitch's July read, which this show cited yesterday, came in at six point one. That measures a specific universe, roughly thirteen hundred to fifteen hundred rated U.S. borrowers, not the whole market. It is still the cleanest read we have.

The same picture shows up one layer down, inside the funds. Non-performing loans at Blue Owl's flagship direct-lending fund hit two point eight percent in the second quarter, its worst level in at least five years. The reporting behind that number extends the same five-year-high language to Ares, to Blackstone's Secured Lending Fund, and to Golub Capital's BDC. That last comparison is reported once removed, not verified off the filings themselves, so hold it a notch looser. Meanwhile Blue Owl's co-chief executive told analysts that across the direct lending strategy, credit health remains strong. That line landed in the same quarter as the fund's worst non-performing print in five years.

So here's the divergence: record capital going in, record defaults already there. Before you read that as denial, steelman it properly. The default stress is not evenly spread. Borrowers with twenty-five million dollars or less of EBITDA defaulted at twelve point three percent in July, roughly triple the rate one size band up. The managers pulling in record capital are the mega-funds, lending largely to the larger, better-documented end of the market. On that reading the institutional allocator is pricing two different markets correctly, rather than ignoring one bad one. That version of the bull case partly works.

Here's where it stops working. Committed is not deployed. A record raise still has to find a home, and deployment pressure is exactly what loosens documentation and compresses spread. That mechanism is already visible in the data. The Boston Fed study this show covered Wednesday found median BDC lending spreads compressed by about one percentage point over the past two years, while payment-in-kind usage and borrower credit metrics were deteriorating. Lenders were already being paid less to absorb more deferral risk before this record quarter arrived.

So the binding risk here has moved. It isn't recognition, whether a stressed loan finally gets marked down. It's vintage. Capital raised at the top of a cycle has to go somewhere, and there are only two directions available. One is down-market, toward the small-EBITDA cohort already defaulting at three times the rate of the tier above it. The other is up-market, into territory that banks and the investment-grade bond market are also bidding for. That second path compresses the illiquidity premium that is supposed to justify the private wrapper in the first place. Either direction narrows the spread cushion just as the denominator gets worse.

And note where that shows up in the numbers investors actually see. It won't show up in reported gross yield, which will look fine. It surfaces two to three years out, in net-of-loss-and-fee returns, long after the fundraising headlines have been banked. That's the gap this asset class is quietly writing today.

So where does a record quarter of capital actually go? A big piece of the answer is the AI buildout. Morgan Stanley's research puts the data-center financing gap at roughly one and a half trillion dollars, and expects private credit to fund about eight hundred billion of it, led by asset-based finance structures. Two caveats before you lean on that. It's a forward projection through 2028, not completed financing. And Morgan Stanley has its own book on the bank-senior side of the same trade, so it is not a disinterested estimate. Wednesday's briefing gave you one live instance of the template already running, in Eagle Point's mezzanine tranche inside an Anthropic-anchored data center project in Texas.

Now watch the metric trap, because it's the actual finding. Separate research from Wellington, tracking deal count rather than dollar volume, identified three hundred and thirty data-center financing transactions from January 2024 through this June. Banks and public markets each took about a third of that count. Private credit took only about a quarter. Compare that to Morgan Stanley's roughly half-of-the-dollars estimate. Those are two different metrics from two different sources, and the gap between them is the information. Private credit isn't winning more deals. It's writing fewer, much bigger, single-obligor checks.

That's concentration, not diversification. If private credit becomes the designated funder of a large slice of one capex thesis, its next loss event is more likely to be correlated than idiosyncratic. An air pocket in hyperscaler spending would hit many funds at once. And it would arrive as a valuation shock rather than a payment default, which is precisely the event a mark-to-model book recognizes slowest. That's the recognition problem this show has tracked all week, relocated onto a newer asset. Meanwhile banks, the investment-grade market and the securitization bid are all competing for the same paper, which compresses private credit's spread premium on that exposure exactly as its share of the exposure grows. That is the opposite of what the asset class sells itself as.

Now flip to the other capital channel, because it's moving the other way. Cox Capital, the firm that tried and mostly failed to buy discounted non-traded BDC shares this week, is planning to extend that same discount-tender playbook to interval funds. That's a stated plan, not a launched offer. At Variant's Alternative Income fund, investors sought to redeem around half their shares last quarter. At Cliffwater's flagship interval fund, about seventeen percent of investors requested redemptions. Those are requests, not fulfilled exits. Interval funds are built to meet only a fraction of them by design. But they are a clean read on retail sentiment, and Cliffwater's next redemption window closes Monday, August 31st, with a discount buyer now circling it.

Put it together and you get a clean decoupling. Institutional limited partners are accelerating into private credit at a record pace, at par. The retail wrapper holding much the same underlying paper is queuing to leave, and getting bid at a discount to do it. Same asset class, opposite direction of flow. State that as the day's structural finding, because it is the thing that was not true a quarter ago.

So where does the cycle actually stand? Not in denial, and not in crisis either. The risk has moved from recognition to vintage. The question is whether the capital piling in at a record pace this quarter gets deployed into the same discipline that built the mega-managers' track record, or into whatever is left once the best paper is gone. Two near-term tests are concrete. Monday's Cliffwater redemption window, with a discount buyer already positioned against it, tells you whether retail stress is deepening or stabilizing. And the third-quarter BDC earnings due in November, with non-accrual and payment-in-kind disclosures from Ares, Blackstone, Golub and Blue Owl, will tell you whether the credit data catches up to what the fundraising is ignoring, or whether the bifurcation holds.

That's the state of the machine. Thanks for listening, and I'll see you next time.
