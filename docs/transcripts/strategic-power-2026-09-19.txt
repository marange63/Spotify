Good morning, and welcome to Strategic Power for Saturday, September 19th, 2026.

I have to correct our own framing this week. This beat called Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline, the line that carries crude across the kingdom to Yanbu on the Red Sea, bypassing Hormuz, the kingdom's single remaining point of failure. On Thursday we said to watch whether it came back inside its repair window. The pipeline is still down. But the repair window has stopped being the variable that matters, because Saudi Arabia is reportedly routing around its own bypass.

Start with the facts. The pipeline shut on September 11th and 12th after a drone attack. The drones were launched from Iraqi territory. Iraq's prime minister's office traces them to Maysan province in the southeast. The target was Saudi, not Iraqi. No attacker has been named on the record, and Baghdad has confirmed only the launch point. Two regional officials told the Associated Press that repairs could take three to five weeks. One analyst quoted by Al Jazeera expects a restart within a couple of weeks, at forty to sixty percent of capacity. So the timeline is a range, not a date. Fortune puts recent flow through the line at 2.6 to 4 million barrels a day.

Now the part that broke our pattern. Trade sources say Saudi Arabia has sold about 60 million barrels from Ras Tanura, inside the Strait of Hormuz, for ship-to-ship transfer at the Omani port of Sohar this month and next. Chinese and South Korean refiners are among the top buyers. Note the sourcing: Aramco did not respond to a request for comment, and no Saudi or Omani official has confirmed it. But the market has behaved as if it is real. OilPrice.com says the rebound in exports through Hormuz has capped the upside in oil prices despite the outage. Brent sat near 104 dollars on Friday morning, per Fortune, about 37 dollars above a year ago.

So what did we get wrong? We were right that the pipeline was a single point of failure, and wrong about what that failure costs. Commercial improvisation outran the engineering schedule, and the workaround, not the repair crew, set the price.

Do not mistake that for restored supply. Al Jazeera reports Saudi crude loadings topped 7.5 million barrels a day in January and February. They fell to about 2.3 million in August, and ran roughly 2.1 million in the first half of September. That is a drop of more than 70 percent, and Al Jazeera names no data provider. Trade sources put the rebound in Gulf-side exports at one to one and a half million barrels a day, but that is a narrower measure, so do not net the two. The workaround moved barrels. It did not refill the pipe.

The price structure is where the shortage actually shows. Brent is the screen everyone watches. Physical Asian crude tells a different story. DME Oman crude printed just over 132 dollars on September 16th, on the OilMonster series. A day later it printed just over 124. So the premium is volatile, and it has to be dated. Even on the softer day, Oman sat roughly twenty dollars above Brent. The shortage is being paid as a delivery premium by Asian buyers, not printed on the futures screen.

Now the official forecast. The Energy Information Administration's September 9th outlook assumes Brent averages about 90 dollars in the second half of this year. Spot is roughly fourteen dollars above that, and I lean toward the tape. The constraints here are physical: a damaged pipeline, one transfer anchorage, and a second chokepoint under pressure. The same agency has US crude output rising to 14.3 million barrels a day next year, which is the energy-abundance advantage in one line, and it does nothing for the premium Asia is paying today.

The second chokepoint is where the risk has changed class. On September 8th the Houthis launched a major offensive. Foreign Policy reports they seized key points across Yemen's west coast and now control the approach to Bab el-Mandeb. Land control is more durable than a missile threat. And the concentration risk has moved, not vanished. Much of what Saudi Arabia can still export now depends on transfers at a single Omani anchorage, and one successful strike near Sohar would strip the last workaround. The futures curve does not price that.

Much of that delivery premium is paid by China. This week Iran's foreign minister told Beijing that Iran has agreed a plan with Oman to reopen Hormuz. No Omani official has confirmed it, so treat it as a claim. But note the venue. Beijing is visibly pressing Tehran, and Chinese refiners are buying the Sohar cargoes. That is why next week's summit is an energy meeting as much as a trade one.

Xi Jinping meets Donald Trump in Washington on September 24th, and Treasury Secretary Bessent and Trade Representative Greer meet Vice Premier He Lifeng this weekend. No outcome exists yet. The dated lever is November 10th, and it is now a double deadline. The Busan truce expires that day. The Bureau of Industry and Security's Affiliates Rule, suspended for a year, is reported to snap back that day unless extended. Washington wants a six-month extension. Beijing wants one through the rest of Trump's term. The administration has already delayed its overproduction tariffs until after the summit. That is the cheapest signal available, and it reverses on a date already in the calendar.

The same pattern shows up in a different medium. A New York Times investigation found that Aivres, the US subsidiary of Inspur, exported advanced servers to Southeast Asia that ultimately served Chinese customers including ByteDance and Alibaba. Asia Times quotes an unnamed assessment that the Bureau of Industry and Security has no authority under current law to require a license for remote access to advanced chips. So the leak is in the statute, not in an enforcement lapse.

Put the two together. A closed strait and a leaky export-control regime are the same phenomenon: interdiction raises costs and degrades volumes without stopping flows.

Now the counter-case to the idea that Beijing holds all the leverage. China's exports rose 25 percent year over year in August. Rare earth exports reached 4,735 metric tons, up 12.1 percent from July. The first number says Beijing's exposure is to market access, not only to chips. The second says the rare-earth tap is open month to month, but it is a tap Beijing controls. And only one side has an election on November 3rd. A six-month extension recreates this cliff in spring 2027, and a full-term one locks in rare-earth licensing as the standing bargaining chip.

From foreign chokepoints, turn to the one Americans have to fix themselves. The input is power. On September 16th the House passed the Ratepayer Protection Act by 417 votes to 3. It targets AI data centers drawing 100 megawatts or more. They would bear the full incremental cost of any generation, transmission or distribution upgrade they trigger. On Wednesday we asked who pays for load that does not yet exist. The answer arrived as politics, not as rate design.

Read the vote carefully. The bill hangs off PURPA, the federal statute that only obliges state regulators to consider a cost-allocation standard. Consider, not adopt. A state can hold the hearing, say no, and still comply. Senate passage before the midterms is speculative. A ClearView Energy Partners analyst calls the bill somewhat behind the regulatory curve. Congress is ratifying a shift already underway in state tariffs and claiming the credit. Near-unanimity is the tell that it is cheap.

What binds is PJM. Its backstop auction runs September 30th to October 21st, with results due December 2nd. It answers a 6.8 gigawatt shortfall, after the last base auction missed a 20 percent reserve margin. PJM will also curtail large loads without their own supply as the grid nears an emergency, for data centers online after June 2027. Bring your own power becomes a condition of interconnection. That raises capex per megawatt, favors behind-the-meter gas and contracted generation, and drags on hyperscaler returns. And the demand is real: the EIA expects record US electricity sales of 4,135 billion kilowatt-hours this year.

Last, the price of money under all of it. The Fed hiked a quarter point on Wednesday. Hiked, not cut. And sixteen of eighteen participants expect at least one more before year-end. The ten-year Treasury yield stood at 5.027 percent on September 15th. Debt-financed data centers, infrastructure funds and private credit now compete with a higher risk-free rate, while power and diesel costs rise too. That is a squeeze from both ends. And the hard-money trade is not confirming the story. Bitcoin was near 78,000 dollars on Friday, roughly 39,000 below a year earlier.

So the week's lesson is that chokepoints leak, and the leak is paid for by whoever cannot route around it. Watch two things: whether Sohar stays unmolested, and whether November 10th is extended or snaps back.

That is Strategic Power for today. Thanks for listening.
