Good morning, it's Friday, November 13th, and this is your private equity briefing.

Yesterday we argued that capital for new deals isn't the bottleneck in this cycle. Our evidence was the record financing package behind the Recordati take-private, which showed lenders will still back a jumbo deal at today's rates. This week handed us a case that tests that read from the other side. Nothing in the reporting points to financing as the obstacle, and the deal still died. That's GFL Environmental.

A consortium of private-equity fund managers offered $52 a share for the Canadian waste hauler, valuing the company above $22 billion. According to reporting carried by Transport Topics, that was about a 13% premium to GFL's closing price on November 9th in New York.

GFL's special committee rejected the offer and shut down its strategic review. The committee chair, Dino Chiesa, said it had unanimously determined that remaining a public company is the best path forward for all shareholders. He conceded the valuations were based on a premium to where GFL trades. But he said they were "not reflective of the full value of our standalone strategy."

One important wrinkle on the buyer side. Blackstone has been widely reported as part of that consortium. But a Blackstone spokesperson told the Globe and Mail the firm never "placed a bid," and has no active dialogue with management or its advisers. So treat the buyer group as unconfirmed beyond a consortium of fund managers. It's also possible that what reached the board was a looser indication than a fully committed offer.

Here's why this matters more than a single rejected bid. The board's stated objection was price, not certainty of funding. A real premium was on the table, and a public board still said no. Put that next to Recordati, where lenders did show up at size, and financing looks like the easier half of the problem. The harder half is whether sponsors' return hurdles and a public board's view of intrinsic value can meet.

Be a little skeptical of the board's language, though. "Not reflective of full value" is the line nearly every target board reaches for. Treat it as a negotiating position, not a valuation finding. That leaves three ways to read the standoff.

One: the board is right. On this reading, sponsors' cost of capital simply can't clear what steady, infrastructure-like cash flows are worth to a patient public owner.

Two: the board misjudged its own shareholders. Here the tape gives us a real data point, not a guess. Per the Globe and Mail, GFL's shares fell 7.6% to close at $42.41 on the New York Stock Exchange, on the news that deal talks were off. The stock didn't rally on independence. It sold off, and it closed well below the $52 that had been on the table. That's evidence, not proof, that at least some holders would have preferred the cash.

Three: the rejection is a step in a negotiation, not the last word. GFL's CEO, Patrick Dovigi, said "certain parties remain interested in acquiring some or all of the stakes held by our financial sponsor shareholders." He added that GFL "will facilitate that process as required." So bidders may still get their exposure, just by buying blocks instead of paying a control premium to every holder.

Now look at who was waiting on that deal to exit. According to the Globe and Mail, the London-based fund BC Partners owns 13% of GFL. A straight take-private would have paid BC Partners a control premium on that stake. Instead, GFL is facilitating a process for sponsor shareholders to sell part or all of their holdings. There's no named buyer, no disclosed structure, and no timeline yet. For the fund that owns the stake, that's a materially different outcome. It probably still means cash, but likely closer to the market price than to the premium a full takeover would have paid.

This sits in the same toolkit as the continuation vehicle. It's a way to manufacture partial liquidity when the clean exit fails on price. You give up the control premium. In return, your limited partners get money back sooner. That's the trade a stuck exit market forces on sponsors: liquidity now, at a lower price, instead of a bigger number on somebody else's timeline.

And the GFL standoff cuts both ways for the industry. If the same kind of price gap is what slows sponsors' exits, it now stops them buying from public holders, too. If more boards follow GFL's lead, take-privates become a less reliable way to deploy capital. That would happen at exactly the moment financing is available to fund them. So both ends of the deal market would stall on valuation, not on money.

If the deal market is stuck on price, the managers' own third-quarter prints show who doesn't have to wait. KKR's fee-generating assets under management rose 28% from a year earlier, per its earnings release. Apollo's fee-related earnings rose 23%, per its filing with the SEC. Neither number depends on a single asset changing hands. The fee engine compounds on inflows and AUM growth, deals or no deals.

Two cautions on those numbers. Both are firm-wide, and Apollo's fee line is driven mostly by credit and retirement services, not buyout. And growth in fee-generating assets is growth in assets, not evidence of performance.

Carlyle is the inverse case, and it's the more interesting one. Carlyle's fee-related earnings fell 6% from a year earlier. Yet its distributable earnings came in at $433 million, driven by a five-fold increase in net realized performance revenues. That makes Carlyle the one firm in this set where the realization line and the fee line moved in opposite directions.

Here's the mechanism that could connect those two lines. When a fund sells a company, that capital stops paying fees. Apollo pointed at exactly this on its third-quarter call, which we covered yesterday. It said management-fee growth was "partially offset by realization activity in equity." Carlyle's mix fits that mechanism. But Carlyle's release doesn't give a cause for its fee decline, so this corroborates the reading rather than proving it.

Two more guardrails on Carlyle. A five-fold jump off what was likely a small prior-year base is a ratio, not a scale signal. And realized performance revenue is carry booked by the manager. It is not cash distributed to limited partners.

The takeaway: fees and cash to limited partners run on different clocks. The managers growing fees fastest are the ones growing fee-paying AUM, not the ones monetizing portfolios. The manager with the strongest realization quarter had shrinking fees. That's why the managers' public shareholders are insulated from the price standoff in a way their limited partners are not. A real exit thaw is unambiguously good for limited partners. For the managers, it's mixed. Carry goes up, but fee bases shrink.

So where does the cycle actually stand? GFL is a price standoff on the buy side. Sponsors couldn't buy at a price the board would accept. That's consistent with the exit-and-distribution logjam this cycle keeps circling back to, where the same kind of gap makes it hard to sell at the values sponsors' models assumed. That logjam remains the biggest unresolved risk. Meanwhile, the largest managers' fee lines keep compounding regardless.

Where's the market complacent? In reading strong fee-related earnings as proof the cycle is healthy, when they mostly tell you AUM is growing. Where might it be over-discounting? In reading one rejected bid as proof take-privates are dead. It's one data point, from one board, on one company.

Watch three things from here. First, whether bidders come back for GFL or buy the sponsors' stakes directly, and at what price relative to that $52 bid. Second, Carlyle's private-equity segment, where its own filing guided to lower distributable earnings in the fourth quarter. Third, the fourth-quarter manager prints in January and February. Those are the real test of whether cash distributions to limited partners finally move. Otherwise this remains a backlog that keeps getting discussed and not resolved.

That's the briefing. Have a good weekend, and I'll catch you next time.
