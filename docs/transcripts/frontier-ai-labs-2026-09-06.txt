Good morning. It's Sunday, September 6th, and this is your frontier AI labs briefing. The headline is that a benchmark still capable of telling frontier models apart finally ranked the two leading American labs against each other, and OpenAI came second.

Start with the number. On Humanity's Last Exam with tools, GPT-6 Astra, OpenAI's new flagship released September 3rd, scored 57.2 percent. Claude Fable 5.1, which Anthropic shipped two days earlier, scored 65.0 percent. That's nearly eight points ahead. Now the sourcing, because here it matters more than usual. That figure set recurs identically across several independent write-ups, which is consistent with a single common origin, most likely OpenAI's own launch comparison table. No publisher confirms that. And none of them states whether the models were run under the same tool harness. A with-tools score is a harness score as much as it is a model score. So treat this as directionally real, not as a certified ranking.

Here's why it matters more than the number itself. On Friday, this show argued that Astra's headline scores — 98 percent on FrontierMath, 99.9 percent on ARC-AGI-3, a perfect 100 on an exploit benchmark — told us something about saturated benchmarks rather than about a clean ranking against Anthropic or Google. That read has now been confirmed by the benchmark's own maintainer.

The ARC Prize Foundation built and runs ARC-AGI-3, and it has no stake in OpenAI's marketing. It published its own evaluation of Astra using a provider-neutral test rig it calls the Standard harness. Astra's best score on that rig was 62.7 percent, not 99.9. The marketed figure requires OpenAI's own Provider Adapter harness, which preserves reasoning state between calls in a way the neutral rig does not allow. That is a roughly 37-point swing produced by how the test is run, not by what the model can do. And note that even the marketed number is reported inconsistently, at 99.9 in some places and 98.6 in others, which suggests it moves with the reasoning-effort tier.

So the honest framing this week is not that nobody outside the labs has scored Astra. An outside evaluator did score it, and found that most of the headline gain was the harness. Humanity's Last Exam, by contrast, has not saturated the way ARC-AGI-3 has, which is exactly why its result is the more informative one. There is a fair counter-read on that eight-point gap, though. OpenAI may simply have optimized for the evals it chose to publish, and not for Humanity's Last Exam, in which case the gap measures target selection rather than capability. Either way, the useful news isn't the gap. It's that a number exists that can still discriminate at all.

If the scoreboard is only just becoming legible again, the thing that actually decides who gets the top tier isn't on any scoreboard. It's permission. Anthropic's Mythos 5.1 is the gated sibling released alongside Fable 5.1. On Thursday this show described that gate as trusted access for cybersecurity and life-sciences users. That was incomplete. Mythos 5.1 is restricted to United States organizations specifically, with any international expansion coordinated with the US government. That is a private company running what amounts to its own export-control regime, with no statute behind it and no published criteria for who qualifies.

It is also the fourth step in an escalating pattern this beat has tracked for a week. On August 31st, OpenAI hedged that it could not rule out its models reaching a Critical risk tier. By September 3rd, all three US labs had gated cyber-offense capability behind vetted programs. On Saturday we covered the next turn: OpenAI's Daybreak program sits above the published rate card, so money alone doesn't buy the top capability. And now there is a nationality condition layered on top of that. Each step tightened access further. At no step has any lab published the rules for how you clear the bar.

Google, to its credit, did put an actual number on its own cyber claim this week. Its Chrome Security team found that Gemini 3.8 Flash Cyber produced correct security patches 2.6 times more often than a competing commercial model. On Thursday this show flagged Google's vulnerability-discovery claim as having no independent benchmark named. This answers that gap in form. It does not answer it in substance, because the evaluator is another Google team.

The bigger point stands. Capability at the frontier is now allocated by permission and passport, not by willingness to pay. Think about what that does to a multinational buyer. If Anthropic's top tier is legally unavailable to a company's non-US operations, that company cannot standardize globally on Anthropic's best model. It has to run more than one vendor. That is the same forced multi-homing this show tracked in the Pentagon's three-lab platform and in CrowdStrike turning up on both OpenAI's and Google's partner lists. And it caps the addressable market for the highest-margin tier at exactly the moment Anthropic is marketing itself to public investors ahead of an expected listing.

One lab is competing on neither the score nor the permission. It is competing on the bill. Meta released Muse Spark 1.3 on September 2nd. Rather than chase a leaderboard it isn't winning, Meta cut what the model actually costs to run. It needs roughly 20 percent fewer tool calls and about 25 percent fewer tokens than its predecessor to finish comparable engineering tasks. The rate card did not move: a dollar twenty-five per million input tokens, four twenty-five per million output. That is a real price cut for the customer that never shows up on a pricing page.

The caveats here are serious. Those efficiency numbers are Meta's own, measured against Meta's own prior model. No independent benchmark comparison of Muse Spark 1.3 against Astra, Fable 5.1, or Google's new Flash tier has surfaced. And fewer tokens per task is one of the easiest metrics in this industry to manufacture, because a model that simply gives up earlier on hard problems posts the same improvement. Meta also hasn't shipped a max-reasoning mode yet, pending safety testing, so the model's real ceiling isn't testable.

Still, the shift in what is being optimized is the point, and it has a consequence. If the durable win in coding agents is fewer tokens per completed task rather than a higher score, then value migrates off the model checkpoint and onto the agent harness and the tool integrations. Anyone whose business is a markup on token consumption — API resellers, coding-assistant startups — loses margin to an efficiency gain they did not build. And notice where price competition went this week. Anthropic cut cache-read pricing rather than base rates. OpenAI and Anthropic converged on an identical published rate card. Now Meta has cut the customer's actual bill by cutting consumption instead. The posted price may be the least informative number in this market right now.

The same pressure is arriving from below, and cheaper. Alibaba's Qwen3.8-Max-0902, a coding-focused refresh released the same week, more than doubled its score on one coding sub-benchmark, TerminalBench 3.0, going from 11.3 to 29.0. That came from a post-training update alone. No new pretraining run, no architecture change, and no change to its pricing. Its broader claim to the top spot on a crowd-voted coding leaderboard we have already covered twice this week, and its margin there sits inside that board's own noise. Placement is not adoption, and there is still no routing or enterprise-share data for Qwen outside China. But doubling a real sub-benchmark without retraining the model is the more durable fact. Coding capability is commoditizing faster than general reasoning, and it is happening without anyone spending on a new frontier training run.

Put the pieces together and the frontier labs are now running three separate competitions: capability, price, and permission. Only one of them, price, is fully published, and even that one just stopped meaning what it used to. Anthropic's prospectus is expected to become public sometime this week. When it does, it will be the first document in this sector that an outside investor can check against reality rather than take on faith.
