Cautious Optimism Briefings

Strategic Power, Energy, and Institutional Change

Transcript · August 15, 2026

Good morning, it's Saturday, August 15th, and this is your Cautious Optimism briefing on the strategic power beat.

We flagged the Strait of Hormuz's de-facto closure earlier this week as an acute crisis. As of this week, it isn't one anymore. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth said on August 13th that the US Navy can sustain its blockade of Iranian ports indefinitely, rotating ships in and out as needed. Here's his exact framing: "Indefinitely the United States Navy can maintain a blockade like that because we'll rotate ships in and out, as we have, and we'll continue to." That's not crisis-management language. That's the Pentagon describing a permanent posture.

The numbers now back that up. The International Energy Agency's August 12th Oil Market Report cut its 2026 global demand forecast to a decline of 1.6 million barrels a day, 510,000 barrels worse than its July estimate, citing high fuel prices and the Hormuz disruption directly. Supply is now forecast to fall 4.3 million barrels a day to 102 million barrels for the year. Regional exports, including routes that dodge the strait entirely, fell a sharp 2.1 million barrels a day to 15 million, after the passageway was effectively closed again in early July. Daily tanker transits through Hormuz have gone from roughly 130 a day before the war to just 8 to 15 a day in the first week of August. Brent settled at $88.52 a barrel on August 14th, WTI at $82.40, both rising on Hegseth's comments.

The so-what here is durability, not a fresh spike. This is a floor, not a scare. Iran's foreign minister has said the strait won't reopen until Washington eases sanctions and pays war reparations, conditions that make a near-term negotiated exit unlikely. So treat mid-$80s Brent as the new baseline, not a peak waiting to mean-revert. There is still a genuine tail risk in the other direction. An indefinite blockade instead of a settlement leaves room for further escalation, and any strike on Gulf infrastructure or a Chinese-flagged tanker could jolt prices higher from here. But treat that as a qualitative risk to the tail, not a base case. The base case that changed this week is the floor.

Two second-order effects follow directly. First, a structurally elevated oil floor is a fresh cost impulse landing right ahead of September's US inflation prints. That's a risk to price into the inflation outlook, not a specific call on what the Fed does with it. The dossier carries no CPI or Fed figure, so I won't pretend to one. The honest read is that an oil floor that doesn't come back down puts upward pressure on the September prints, and that pressure is now more durable than a war premium that fades.

Second, the same disruption that's gutting Gulf and Russian supply is a structural tailwind for US producers. The IEA says only 1.4 million barrels a day of growth from the Americas is even partly offsetting those losses. Read that carefully. The barrels being knocked out sit in the Middle East and Russia. The barrels still growing sit in the Americas. That is the energy-abundance advantage showing up in the actual supply math, not just the talking point. A Hormuz constraint that markets now treat as semi-permanent is a slow, structural bid under US crude exporters, Permian producers, and US LNG cargoes that reach Europe and Asia without ever touching that chokepoint. It raises input costs for everyone, but it hands relative pricing power to the one large producing region that sits outside the blast radius.

Now the same base-layer question shows up at home, just inverted. Abroad, energy is a chokepoint someone else controls. At home, the fight is over who pays for the power AI needs. FERC's compliance deadline for its data-center colocation order lands this coming Monday, and PJM, the country's largest grid operator, has already shown its hand. Reporting from August 10th lays out the plan: PJM is leaning on states and utilities to force data centers to secure their own power supply, or risk getting cut off during grid emergencies, and to pay the full cost of any grid upgrades tied to their own connections. That's against a backdrop of real scale: PJM forecasts data centers and other large loads will add 30 to 34 gigawatts of new demand by the early 2030s, and as much as 70 gigawatts by 2038.

Don't read Monday as a national inflection point, though. Keep two dates apart: the original FERC order came down last December, and Monday is only the compliance-filing deadline that follows from it. And PJM is punting enforcement to the states, which means Ohio, Pennsylvania, and Virginia could all land in different places, not one uniform national rule taking effect on a single date.

That cost-allocation pressure is exactly why hyperscalers are already routing around the utility queue. Big tech has signed contracts for more than 10 gigawatts of possible new US nuclear capacity over the past year: Microsoft's 20-year, 835-megawatt power purchase agreement to restart Three Mile Island, Google ordering up to 500 megawatts of small modular reactors from Kairos Power, and Meta lining up as much as 6.6 gigawatts across Vistra, Oklo, and TerraPower, including one 20-year deal worth 2,600 megawatts from three plants. If you have to fund and secure your own power anyway, a dedicated nuclear contract starts looking a lot more rational than waiting in an interconnection line. Just keep the usual caveat in mind: announced gigawatts are not operating gigawatts, and SMR and restart timelines still carry real execution risk.

Follow that logic to who benefits. A rule that forces data centers to bring their own firm power, plus a wave of dedicated PPAs, tilts the field toward independent power producers with contracted, dedicated-load structures — think Vistra, Talen, and Constellation — and toward the uranium and SMR developers that feed them. The mirror image is the speculative campus with no secured generation, which now faces a higher bar just to get interconnected. In other words, the same announced-versus-delivered distinction this show tracks abroad is exactly what regulators are now forcing onto the domestic power queue: prove your generation is real, or wait.

Let's close on the hedge underneath all of this. Central banks bought a record 289 tonnes of gold in the second quarter of 2026, even as prices posted their steepest quarterly decline in a decade. That's official-sector buying continuing straight through a price drop, which is not how a purely tactical trade behaves. It reads as a slow, structural hedge against exactly the kind of durable energy-cost pressure and currency-credibility questions running through today's brief, not a reaction to any single week's headlines.

Put together: a chokepoint the Pentagon now calls permanent, a domestic grid fight over who funds AI's power draw, and central banks quietly hedging against both. That's strategic power in August 2026, priced in real time.
