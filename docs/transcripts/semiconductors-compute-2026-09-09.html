Good morning. It's September 9th, and this is your semiconductor and compute briefing.

All week this beat has put the binding constraint somewhere physical. Logic yields, then memory allocation, then packaging slots, then construction crews. Today it moved somewhere else entirely. The instrument of competition is a balance sheet.

The headline is Qualcomm and Amazon Web Services. Yesterday the two companies announced a multi-generational collaboration to co-develop custom silicon for AI inference, plus optical connectivity that scales up to 1.6 terabits. Qualcomm's shares rose sharply on the news. Reporting on the size of that move varies enough across outlets that the exact percentage isn't worth quoting.

The part that matters is how the deal was paid for. Qualcomm issued Amazon warrants to buy 25 million Qualcomm shares at $161.26 apiece. That is roughly four billion dollars of notional value handed to the customer, not taken from it.

Qualcomm's own 8-K spells out how those shares vest. They vest in tranches. The triggers are the execution of certain commercial arrangements, the placement of binding purchase orders, and actual purchases of Qualcomm's server chips, technology and manufacturing services by Amazon. All of it is capped, in the filing's words, at a maximum of sixty billion dollars in payments. The warrant runs until September 3rd, 2036.

Read that structure carefully, because the framing matters far more than the headline number. Sixty billion dollars is a ceiling on what AWS could pay in to fully vest the warrant. It is not a purchase order. It is not a backlog entry. It is not a volume Amazon is contractually obliged to buy today. Binding purchase orders will have to appear for the later tranches to vest at all, which is real. But the filing discloses no minimum dollar or unit commitment on Amazon's side right now, and the vesting window runs a full decade.

This show has a running standard for exactly this kind of number. We discounted Broadcom's gigawatt customer schedule on September 3rd, and we applied the same discount to AMD's twelve committed gigawatts from OpenAI and Meta on Sunday. A gigawatt is a statement of intent, not a shipment. Sixty billion dollars of vesting headroom gets the identical treatment, and for the identical reason.

What is genuinely new here is the direction the money flows. A supplier handed its prospective customer roughly four billion dollars of equity optionality in order to win a design relationship. That is worth pausing on. A company confident of winning purely on the merits of its silicon does not usually need to pay its customer to show up. Read the warrant as the price of entry against incumbency, not as evidence that Qualcomm's chip is good.

Beyond the structure, there is very little to hold onto. Neither company has disclosed an architecture, a process node, a foundry, or a packaging approach for whatever Qualcomm is building. Some coverage has circulated a claim that the chip is already in production, and that revenue starts in Qualcomm's fiscal first quarter of 2027. Neither of those traces to a verbatim company statement we could verify. And multiple outlets summarizing the announcement itself say it discloses no production status at all. So treat both claims as unconfirmed, not as fact. What Qualcomm has bought is a seat at the table. It has not yet won a market.

Now the second-order effect, and it does not land on Nvidia. It lands on the incumbent that currently holds this account. AWS's custom-silicon programs, Trainium and Graviton, have been reported as Marvell's franchise, with Broadcom holding Google's and Meta's. Adding a second design house to future generations is a second-sourcing hedge, and second-sourcing hedges do their damage through price rather than through volume. The incumbent doesn't necessarily lose the socket. It loses the ability to name its own terms on the follow-on generation. No dollar exposure figure exists for that, so don't put one on it. The mechanism is the point.

The near-term effect on Nvidia, by contrast, is zero. Nothing ships on a disclosed schedule, no volume has been announced, and the merchant-GPU business is not the thing being bid for here.

For scale, measure the ambition against what a mature custom-silicon franchise actually produces. Broadcom, the incumbent Qualcomm is now chasing, reported AI semiconductor revenue of $16.7 billion in its fiscal third quarter, up 221 percent year over year. It guided the next quarter to $21.7 billion. We covered that print in full last week, so take it here purely as a yardstick.

The yardstick has a time axis as well as a size axis. Broadcom's scale was built over years and multiple generations of hyperscaler accelerator programs, most visibly Google's TPU line. That customer attribution comes from secondary reporting rather than Broadcom's own release, so hold it loosely. The timeline does not depend on it. Qualcomm is signing up for a multi-generation build, which is what the word multi-generational in the announcement is actually telling you. It is not a share shift that resolves inside two years.

Now follow the money to the input every one of these programs needs, which is memory. The clearest new supply-side data point of the week comes out of China.

ChangXin Memory Technologies, CXMT, reached ten percent of global DRAM revenue share in the second quarter. A year earlier it was around four percent. That is the first time a Chinese DRAM maker has cracked double digits.

The other side of that ledger is Korean. SK hynix's share fell to twenty-five percent, down fourteen points year over year. The gap between the two companies has closed from thirty-five points to fifteen in a single year. Samsung, for its part, held roughly flat.

Behind the share number is physical capacity. CXMT runs around 300,000 wafer starts a month today, and is targeting 600,000 a month by 2028. One caveat before any of this does any work: CXMT is private and files nothing. Every one of those figures is a third-party tracker estimate relayed through Korean and trade press, not company disclosure.

That result splits into two very different verdicts on export controls. At commodity DRAM, the controls have plainly failed. A challenger closed most of a thirty-five point gap with the world's second-largest supplier in twelve months. At the high-bandwidth memory that actually feeds AI accelerators, they are still holding. CXMT has reached only risk production on HBM3E, with small sample quantities going to Alibaba's T-Head chip division and to Cambricon for qualification testing. Risk production is a pre-qualification stage. It is well short of volume manufacturing, and no accelerator vendor of Nvidia's or AMD's class has qualified the part.

So the new supply is arriving precisely where the market is not short. That is the so-what. It adds bits to the commodity layer while the constraint sits one layer up.

It may even tighten things. If cheap Chinese DRAM pressures legacy-node margins at Samsung and SK hynix, it sharpens rather than blunts their incentive to convert capacity toward HBM4. That is the two-speed memory market this show has been circling all week. Standard DRAM stays tight for the PC, phone and automotive buyers who absorb the contract-price increases we walked through yesterday, and who have no AI revenue to offset them.

The thing to watch on the China side is qualification status, not share. If CXMT's HBM3E clears qualification with Cambricon and the domestic accelerator ecosystem, the HBM leg of US export controls loses its bite on China's indigenous AI stack. Not this year. But on a visible clock, and share data will not tell you when.

One more item to close the loop. This show has been running a standing test for Intel Foundry since Sunday, and repeated it yesterday. Does a named external customer put real volume behind an advanced node? The only new candidate is a Korean trade-press report that SK hynix is weighing Intel for HBM4E base dies. The economic logic behind it is the interesting part. TSMC's base dies reportedly cost three to four times what SK hynix can produce on its own 1b process. But SK hynix has partly denied the report, saying some of what is circulating is not true. A denied report is weaker evidence than no report at all, because it invites you to score a rumour as progress. The test remains unmet.

So rank today's evidence by what it is actually worth. Broadcom's numbers are booked revenue. CXMT's share gain is a verified shift in commodity supply, within the limits of third-party estimation. Qualcomm's AWS deal and the SK hynix rumour are both statements of intent, one priced in equity and one not even confirmed. Two things would settle them: a disclosed Qualcomm data-center revenue or shipment figure, and a CXMT HBM qualification confirmed by a named accelerator customer. Until either arrives, neither development adds a single HBM bit or packaging slot to the world in 2027. One adds a bidder. The other adds capacity in the wrong layer.

That's the briefing for September 9th. Thanks for listening.
