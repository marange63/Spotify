Good morning, it's Saturday, August 22nd, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

Before today's lead, a correction to how this show has framed a running story. On Tuesday we told you Nvidia's China share had gone effectively to zero, and we called that a permanent loss. On Thursday we told you the first H200 shipments into China were the beginning of a loosening. Both readings assumed the question was about physical imports. New evidence says that was the wrong channel to watch.

The Bureau of Industry and Security is now mapping how Chinese AI firms legally rent Nvidia compute through data centers in third countries. This is not an enforcement action. No charges have been filed and no rule has been proposed. BIS is compiling lists, reportedly naming Thailand, Malaysia, Singapore and other jurisdictions where the compute is actually landing. One sourcing flag before we go further. The original Bloomberg and CNBC reporting behind this could not be read directly, so the specifics reach us through a secondary summary of that reporting. Treat them as reported rather than confirmed, and treat any per-firm chip counts you may have seen elsewhere as unverified.

The trigger was Moonshot AI's Kimi K3. That's a 2.8-trillion-parameter open-weight model, released in late July, and it beat Anthropic's current flagship on at least one blind benchmark, LMArena's Frontend Code Arena. So this is not a marginal model. It is genuinely frontier-competitive. White House technology official Michael Kratsios publicly accused Moonshot of, quote, acquiring GB300-equipped servers and running workloads on GB300s in Thailand. Moonshot has not confirmed that. It has published nothing about where Kimi K3 was trained, or how much compute the training run consumed. So hold that as a government official's accusation, not an admission.

Here's the mechanism that makes this a real gap rather than a minor wrinkle. Under the export administration regulations, an export requires a physical transfer of the hardware. Renting time on a GPU that never crosses a border doesn't meet that definition. So a Chinese lab paying for compute in Bangkok breaks no rule under current law. That means the compute-denial policy was never binding on the labs that mattered most. It constrained imports, not access. And note what does not change: Nvidia's own China revenue is untouched either way, because the rental revenue accrues to whoever owns the servers abroad, not to Nvidia. The loser here is the policy's stated objective, not the vendor.

Can the gap be closed? There is already a legislative vehicle aimed at exactly this problem, the Remote Access Security Act, which passed the House with a lopsided bipartisan margin back in January and has been sitting in the Senate Banking Committee ever since, with no floor vote scheduled. So the honest framing is that closing this takes an act of Congress, not a Commerce Department memo, and Congress hasn't moved on it in seven months. Whether BIS could gain authority some other way is an open question, not a prediction.

Two more things worth entertaining before we move on. First, the less obvious read: Washington may be tolerating this on purpose, because compute rented abroad still runs on American silicon, in jurisdictions America can pressure. From a leverage standpoint that beats pushing Chinese labs fully onto Huawei's own stack. Second, notice where the cost lands if the rules do tighten. A remote-access rule doesn't bite a Chinese lab directly. It bites the operator. Data-center companies in Malaysia, Thailand, Singapore and Japan would inherit end-customer vetting obligations they don't have today, and that friction lands on exactly the ex-China AI-cloud buildout Nvidia and the hyperscalers are counting on for growth.

So if legal access isn't the binding constraint, what is? The same thing constraining every other buyer of AI accelerators: advanced packaging. TSMC's CoWoS process is the ceiling now, not lithography. TSMC is ramping monthly CoWoS capacity from roughly 75 to 80 thousand wafers today toward a target of 120 to 130 thousand wafers a month by the end of this year. Hold onto the word target. That is a year-end goal, not installed capacity you can buy from today. On concentration, Nvidia alone is estimated to hold roughly 60 percent of CoWoS output, with Nvidia, Broadcom and AMD together above 85 percent. Those shares come from third-party foundry-allocation analysts, not from TSMC's own disclosure, so treat them as informed estimates rather than company figures. Lead times run 52 to 78 weeks, which means capacity not already booked is a 2027 delivery at the earliest.

This is a direct correction to something we said on Monday, when we called packaging a problem that had gotten solved. It hadn't. The supply-demand gap has narrowed, it has not closed, and a year-end capacity target is not the same thing as capacity that exists today. One caution on how to read that scarcity: a sold-out book at 52-to-78-week lead times tells you about orders placed a year ago, not about demand this morning.

The so-what here is the important part. Access and supply are two different problems, and only supply actually binds. Whatever compute a Chinese lab rents in Thailand or Malaysia was carved out of the same fixed packaging queue that Nvidia's own hyperscaler customers are standing in. Nobody manufactured extra capacity for the workaround. And there's a second edge to that 60 percent concentration figure worth naming. It looks like Nvidia's moat today. It is also TSMC's single-customer exposure, and packaging capex that is scarcity rent this year becomes stranded plant fast in any Nvidia demand air-pocket.

Follow that constraint one layer up the bill of materials, into memory, and you find the same scarcity, except here it is being priced rather than rationed. Start with the base rate. Micron's fiscal third quarter, reported back in June, put revenue at 41.5 billion dollars against 9.3 billion a year earlier, at a GAAP gross margin of 84.6 percent. That margin is the number that matters. An 84.6 percent gross margin in what is supposed to be a commodity industry is not a business model. It is a shortage, priced. Two caveats on it. That report is two months old, so it is an established base rate, not this week's news. And a revenue jump that large is measured against a trough year, so part of it is cyclical recovery rather than pure AI demand.

The fresher signal comes from TrendForce in mid-August. Contract DRAM prices are set to rise 13 to 18 percent sequentially this quarter, with NAND up 10 to 15 percent. And HBM4 for Nvidia's GPUs is running 31 to 32 dollars a gigabyte, nearly double what HBM3E cost per gigabyte. Samsung's expectation that HBM4 revenue more than triples sequentially this quarter is a figure this show already used on Monday, so treat it as a prior data point holding up, not as news. The so-what: right now it is the memory makers, not the accelerator vendors, capturing the widest spread on AI infrastructure spending. That spread gets paid by somebody, and the bill runs through PC makers, smartphone vendors, and ultimately hyperscaler capex.

One thing to hold onto about that margin: it is also the strongest possible invitation for Samsung, SK hynix and Micron to over-invest. That is the oldest pattern in this industry, and it is the setup to watch for a 2027 or 2028 reversal.

Which brings us to who can actually pass all this cost through. Chip stocks split sharply on Wednesday. Intel and AMD each fell about 4 percent while Nvidia held flat, and that split happened even against a supportive bond-market backdrop. Bank of America argued around that selloff that Nvidia trades at a 40 to 50 percent discount to AI-compute peers on an enterprise-value-to-free-cash-flow basis. That is one sell-side estimate relayed through secondary press, not a consensus. But it is the market's own answer to today's question: it prices Nvidia as the one name positioned to pass rising packaging and memory costs straight through rather than absorb them in margin.

Be skeptical of how neat that story is. These are names carrying very large year-to-date gains, and a single session's divergence is at least as consistent with profit-taking as with any input-cost thesis. The real test is Nvidia's fiscal second-quarter print on Wednesday, August 26th. That report has not happened, and nothing about its outcome is knowable today. It is the thing that will tell you whether the sector's next leg of growth is volume or price. Until then, everything in this briefing is a thesis rather than a result.
