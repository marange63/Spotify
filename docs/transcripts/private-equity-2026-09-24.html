Good morning, it's Thursday, September 24th, and this is your private equity briefing.

Start with the story that best captures where this cycle actually stands. Apollo Global Management is in talks to nearly double a loan it has made against SoftBank's Vision Fund 2 portfolio. The reported plan would lift the facility from five point four billion dollars to as much as nine billion. Apollo first wrote this loan in 2021, and upsized it by nine hundred million dollars last year. It's a net-asset-value loan, meaning SoftBank borrows against the marked value of an illiquid venture portfolio rather than a single traded asset. The timing lines up with SoftBank's plan to commit sixty four point six billion dollars to OpenAI for roughly a thirteen percent stake. Nothing here is finalized. Apollo and SoftBank have both declined to comment, so treat this as reported and in talks, not done.

One caveat first. SoftBank is a listed holding company, not a buyout fund with outside limited partners. But the structure is exactly the one buyout sponsors use. You borrow against a portfolio's marks instead of selling it. So read this deal as a stress test of the buyout NAV market. A lender is negotiating a facility of up to nine billion dollars against marked, hard-to-sell holdings. For a sponsor sitting on unsold companies, that signals leverage can stand in for a missing exit. For its limited partners, it shows how large a senior claim can quietly build up in front of them.

Here's why the mechanism matters more than the headline number. A NAV loan lets SoftBank fund an enormous new commitment without selling a single hard-to-trade venture holding. It substitutes leverage for a sale. It adds exposure to the AI bet rather than realizing anything, and it does so while cash exits across private markets are scarce. The borrower's case deserves a fair hearing, too. Borrowing against the portfolio is a rational alternative to dumping venture stakes into the secondary market at a discount. But look at who sets the terms. The lender sets what's called a borrowing base, based on its own view of what the portfolio is worth and how quickly it could be sold. That's worth sitting with. Almost nobody in private markets is forced to mark an asset to an actual sale. A NAV lender with its own capital at risk is one of the few parties doing something close to real price discovery. The catch is that this mark-check is procyclical. The borrowing base shrinks exactly when the underlying marks weaken, which is exactly when a borrower least wants its financing to tighten. And the lender sits ahead of the equity, which is the heart of the NAV-lending governance debate.

We don't have to guess whether lenders write reflexive triggers into AI-linked financing, because SoftBank already signed one. Separately, in August, SoftBank secured a ten billion dollar margin loan against its OpenAI stake. Apollo Global Funding was one of five lenders in that syndicate, alongside Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan, Mizuho, and Sumitomo Mitsui. It's a two-year facility, guaranteed by SoftBank itself. And it requires SoftBank to post cash or repay early if the value of OpenAI's preferred shares declines substantially. That's a separate loan, and the NAV loan's own terms haven't been disclosed. But the lenders around SoftBank's AI exposure are clearly comfortable writing decline-triggered, forced-cash provisions into these structures. So Apollo sits on two separate credit exposures to the same borrower's AI bet. That concentrates counterparty risk right where the cycle's biggest valuation question lives.

Now connect that to Tuesday. On Tuesday this show led with New York City's retirement systems turning down a private equity fund because it carried too much AI exposure. We also cited Apollo's own data putting AI-related deals at eighty seven percent of this year's venture funding. Two days later, Apollo is the lender financing more of that same bet. Here's the so what. An allocator can screen AI concentration out at the fundraise. It can't stop that concentration from compounding elsewhere in the capital structure, through leverage.

Why is NAV borrowing the tool of the moment rather than a footnote? Because the cash isn't coming from funds. Distributions-to-paid-in for the 2022 vintage now sits at a median of just zero point zero nine times, according to benchmark data compiled by Dakota. That's a fund roughly four years into its life that has returned almost nothing. Some of that is simple immaturity. But even the 2021 vintage, a year further along, has a median of only zero point one six times. When a portfolio won't throw off cash, borrowing against it becomes the substitute for distributions. It defers the exit problem. It doesn't solve it.

So the managers get paid to carry the backlog either way. Apollo earns a lending spread financing SoftBank's AI exposure, while carried interest across the industry waits behind the same exit logjam. KKR shows the same thing from a different angle. It structured or syndicated more than eighty billion dollars of private investment-grade transactions in the first half of this year, according to a company presentation reviewed by Bloomberg. By September first, its volume this year was already more than double its full-year twenty twenty-five total. That's origination volume, not disclosed revenue, so it tells you the earnings mix is shifting without sizing the shift. But directionally it says what the Apollo loan says. The largest alternative managers are building fee-like income that doesn't depend on a portfolio company actually selling. The gap between what managers earn and what limited partners get back in cash keeps widening. Late October's third-quarter earnings are where that gets tested, fee-related earnings against realized carry.

Meanwhile, at the front door, the last few days produced a fresh take-private that shows how new deals are actually clearing. Priority Technology Holdings, a payments and banking-as-a-service company, signed a definitive agreement to go private. The buyer is an investor group led by its own chairman and chief executive, Thomas Priore, paying eight dollars and five cents a share in cash. That's a thirty-eight percent premium to the close the day before the announcement. It's a sixty-five percent premium to where the shares traded before the group's preliminary proposal became public last November. Equity financing comes in part from funds advised by Searchlight Capital Partners, and a special committee of independent directors backed the deal.

Take that sixty-five percent with a grain of salt, though. In a management-led buyout, the buyer has the best information on what the business is worth. A premium that looks generous to public shareholders can still be a bargain for the insider. The pattern to notice is who's writing the check. Yesterday we covered Advent holding just thirty-seven percent of the InPost consortium. Now a chief executive's group is the buyer, with a sponsor supplying equity only in part. Entry keeps clearing where an insider or a partner shares the capital, not where a sponsor shows up alone with a fresh underwrite. Two limits, though. This is a signed deal, not a closed one, and it's capital going in, not cash coming back to limited partners. And at small-cap scale, it says nothing about financing conditions for a real megadeal. Priority also took roughly ten months to get from that first public proposal to a signed agreement. That's one more sign that even modest take-privates are taking longer to get done.

Pull it together. The private equity cycle is being carried with leverage and insider capital rather than cleared with sales. The exit and distribution logjam is still the binding constraint. Now there's a potential forcing mechanism sitting on top of it: a lender's borrowing base that tightens exactly when marks come under pressure. Watch three things from here. First, whether the Apollo-SoftBank facility actually signs, and at what size and on what terms. Second, the large managers' third-quarter earnings in late October, which will start to separate fee-related income from realized carry. Third, Blackstone is arranging a secondary sale for investors in an eleven billion dollar fund inside its Blackstone Property Partners real estate strategy. No price has been reported yet. Watch whether it prints near or below net asset value. That's the moment marks get checked against cash rather than against each other.
