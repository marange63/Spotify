Good morning, and welcome to Cautious Optimism Briefings. It's Sunday, November 8th. One standing disclosure before we start: Anthropic makes the model behind this show. Today's lead is a Google product, and one item later is Anthropic's own.

This week's marginal news in AI products isn't on the feature list. It's on the invoice. All week, this beat has tracked a metering escalation. GitHub dissolved the Copilot seat into a metered budget. OpenAI pooled ChatGPT and Codex into one allowance. Zendesk priced agent resolutions beyond the monthly allowance at ninety-nine cents each, and Snowflake started selling hard spending caps. By Friday, our enterprise technology desk had boiled it down to one line: the marginal AI dollar is metered, not seated. Today that pattern shows up in four moves. A price falls. An allowance tightens. Spend gets attributed to a cost code. And the work itself moves inside whichever assistant you already have open. Let's take them in that order.

Start with the price. On November 5th, Google took Gemini Omni 1.2 Flash to general availability on the Gemini API and Google Cloud. Google's blog pitches it for "your production needs." Clips now stretch to fifteen seconds, up from ten.

The headline, though, is a thirty percent price cut. Video at 720p now runs seven cents a second. Google's own pricing page puts the old Omni 1.1 rate at about ten cents.

On Friday, this beat flagged that Omni 1.1 traffic auto-routes to 1.2 on December 3rd, and warned builders to test for behavior drift. Google's pricing page now answers the money side of that question. Requests that auto-migrate are, in Google's words, "billed at that model's rates." That means 1.2's lower rate, not 1.1's. So the migration carries no price shock. The remaining risk is how the output behaves, and that still needs testing before the switch.

But don't budget off the seven-cent headline. That rate covers 720p only. Google's pitch is a two-step workflow. You draft fast at low resolution, then upscale the takes you keep. The drafting end really is cheap. Per Google's pricing page, a 360p draft costs about a third of the headline rate.

The finishing end is where the bill lands. Upscaling to 4K runs about twenty-one cents a second, three times the new headline rate. Google lists that as one rate for 4K output with upsampling. The page doesn't say whether the drafts you made along the way are billed on top. Our working assumption is that they are, because each draft is its own generation request.

Do the arithmetic on one finished fifteen-second clip at 4K, and it comes to a little over three dollars, before any drafts. Google disclosed no customer or volume numbers, so this is a unit-economics change, not yet a proven adoption story. The takeaway: drafting short-form video just got cheaper, but the price of a finished, high-resolution asset rides on an upscale step the thirty-percent headline doesn't cover.

One inference here, and it's ours, not Google's. Cheaper units on a metered product can raise total spend if volume responds. That's a plausible reason the caps and the attribution tools are arriving in the same week as the price cut. Which brings us to the caps.

Prices fell at the unit. At the account level, the meter tightened. Microsoft is phasing in fair-use limits on Copilot Chat. They reached organizations with two thousand or fewer seats on November 2nd. They reach every larger organization tomorrow, November 9th. That schedule comes from ChangePilot, a partner blog that summarizes Microsoft's monthly updates, not from Microsoft's own release notes. Per that summary, the limits apply to "additional Copilot Chat features and capabilities" for users without a full Copilot license.

What Microsoft hasn't disclosed matters just as much. There are no published thresholds and no list of affected features. There's also nothing on what happens when a user hits the limit. Without that, we can't tell a hard wall from a soft cap almost nobody reaches. What we can say is the direction. The included tier of the biggest office suite in the world becomes a metered tier tomorrow, for anyone without the full license. The obvious reading is that this nudges unlicensed users toward a paid license, or opens a door for cheaper rival copilots. That's our inference, not anything Microsoft has said. Either way, the free ride now has a meter on it.

Notice that today's two stories so far both run on a vendor's clock. Microsoft's limits reach large organizations tomorrow. Google's Omni auto-route follows on December 3rd. In both cases, buyers learn about a change in price or behavior on the vendor's schedule, not their own. On a metered product, the terms can move under you, and the customer's job is to be watching the calendar.

Once usage is metered and capped, the next question is who pays for it. To restate the disclosure, this next item is Anthropic's own product, from the company that makes the model behind this show. On November 5th, Anthropic's release notes added a security center and other new admin features to Claude Enterprise. The next day, Anthropic shipped a beta for cost tracking. In the release note's words, "Eligible Enterprise organizations can now tag projects with the codes they already use." They can then view Claude spend broken out by tag.

Now the skeptical read. None of this makes Claude cheaper. A security center is an admin dashboard, not proof of better security. Cost-code tagging attributes spend, but it doesn't reduce it. Several of the November 5th features are still in beta. Anthropic doesn't say which organizations count as eligible for the tagging beta. And no adoption figures were disclosed for any of it.

What tagging does change is where the bill lands. An organization can pass AI spend through to the business unit or client that owns the code, instead of carrying it as undifferentiated IT overhead. That moves the budget fight out of IT. It also changes who has to approve the spend. Professional-services firms that already bill by project are the obvious fit. But that's our inference. Anthropic says only "codes they already use," not client-matter numbers. The point stands either way. Attribution doesn't cut the bill. It decides whose budget absorbs it.

So where does all this metered, attributed work actually get done? Increasingly, inside someone else's assistant. On November 4th, Adobe made its CX Enterprise Coworker, an agent for marketing and ad operations, available inside ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot, and at least one other assistant, per trade press. That coverage says the hookup runs through prebuilt MCP connectors into ad and marketing systems. None of this comes from Adobe's own newsroom yet.

Adobe's move escalates an arc from earlier this week. On Wednesday, athenahealth said Claude was its first assistant partner, but "not intended to be the only one." Yesterday, this beat reported that Anthropic's launch page names Plaid, the financial-data connection layer, as the on-ramp between your bank and Claude's Personal Finance feature. Adobe skipped the first-partner step entirely and went multi-assistant from day one. The pattern across all three is the same. Whoever holds the system of record keeps the leverage, and the assistant becomes the interchangeable front end.

One real caution on Adobe. Trade coverage notes that enterprises still need to validate permissions, approval paths, and logs before trusting an agent to take campaign actions. So being available inside an assistant isn't the same as being trusted to spend ad budget there.

So, the spine. The unit price of generated video fell. The included Copilot allowance got rationed. Anthropic made enterprise AI spend attributable to a cost code. And enterprise software is choosing to live inside whichever assistant the customer already has open. This week, the product decisions that matter are being made on the meter, not the feature list.

Three things worth watching from here. First, whether builders report behavior drift once Omni 1.1 traffic auto-routes on December 3rd. Second, whether Microsoft or its customers surface real fair-use thresholds after tomorrow's larger-organization rollout. Third, whether Adobe publishes the permission and approval controls enterprises would need before letting its Coworker agent act on campaign budgets.
