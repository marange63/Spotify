Cautious Optimism Briefings

Strategic Power, Energy, and Institutional Change

Transcript · September 24, 2026

Good morning, it's Thursday, September 24th. The constraints that moved on strategic power this week were political decisions, not physical shortages. A governor, an ally, and two leaders meeting in Washington each overrode what the market or the war would otherwise have done.

Start in Texas. On Monday, September 21st, Governor Greg Abbott widened his data-center freeze from the grid queue to the permitting desk. He did it by letter, not executive order. The letter directs the Texas Commission on Environmental Quality to withhold new air and water permits until ERCOT completes its audit of large data centers seeking to connect to the grid. Per a Foley and Lardner summary of the directive, the freeze applies to all pending and new permits for data center projects.

Until now, the Texas posture was an interconnection pause. That was a delay, not a stop. A queue pause delays a hookup. A permit freeze stops the project upstream, whether or not grid capacity exists. For anything not yet permitted, Texas is now much closer to a full stop.

Now the scale. By the governor's own August directive, as reported by Utility Dive, ERCOT's queue holds about 474 gigawatts of requests. Roughly 90 percent of that is data centers. Abbott's statement puts the total at more than five times Texas's record peak demand. Those are the governor's numbers, not an independent ERCOT measurement.

There are two ways to read that ratio. The first is that Texas simply cannot keep pace with the AI buildout. The second is that a queue five times peak is what speculative load looks like. Developers option several sites and see which one clears first. On that reading, a freeze pending audit is the cheapest way to separate real load from optioned load. ERCOT had already suspended its batch process for classifying new large loads, so the screening was not happening on its own. That makes the freeze look less like anti-growth populism and more like forced triage. Both readings are live. The freeze stops the clock while someone finds out.

Next, the clock itself. No end date for the freeze has been published. The one fixed date is October 19th. That is when TCEQ must send the governor's office a progress and compliance report. It is a checkpoint, not an expiration.

Then the escape route. Community Impact reports that data centers building their own power, unconnected to the grid, are not subject to ERCOT oversight. That is the obvious hyperscaler workaround. But the TCEQ freeze covers air permits, and air permits are what on-site gas turbines need. No source states plainly whether the freeze reaches those behind-the-meter projects. That is the open question that decides the outcome. If it does reach them, Texas has closed the self-supply route as well as the grid route. That would be tighter than PJM's bring-your-own-power rule. If it does not, the freeze pushes developers toward on-site gas. The same question hangs over deals like the one NRG reached in principle with an unnamed hyperscaler for a Texas gas plant.

Now the politics. Abbott is running for re-election, and his Democratic challenger, Gina Hinojosa, is on record. Her line is that Abbott can take action now to end the data center madness. That puts data-center siting into a state executive's campaign. It follows the House's 417-to-3 vote on the Ratepayer Protection Act last week. It also follows the customer-benefit framing Georgia Power attached to Google's nuclear uprate. In about a week, data-center cost has moved from utility rate design to campaign politics.

The market consequence is my judgment, not a reported figure. A Texas freeze with no end date should push marginal hyperscaler siting toward other states. It favors projects already through the queue, and incumbents with sites, over speculative developers. No figure yet shows any project actually relocating. So in Texas, the permitting desk, not the grid, now decides how fast AI capacity gets built. And it runs on an election calendar as much as an audit calendar.

Texas is a governor overriding the market. The next case is an ally overriding Washington.

The question since mid-month has been whether Kyiv would actually pause refinery strikes. The background is worth one line. On September 14th, with US diesel at a record above six dollars a gallon, Trump said Ukraine's president had to stop knocking out diesel fuel in Russia. He then claimed a mutual energy truce that officials said did not exist. That question has now resolved. The answer is no.

The strikes kept coming. On September 15th, Ukrainian drones hit a Rosneft-owned refinery in the Samara region. On September 17th, they hit the Yaroslavl refinery, less than two hundred miles northeast of Moscow, and started a fire. Bloomberg reports that the Moscow refinery itself was hit in the September 20th barrage. Bloomberg gives no damage detail.

That barrage carries the headline number. Russia's Defense Ministry, as reported by Al Jazeera, said it downed 1,110 Ukrainian drones across nineteen regions and Crimea. That was its largest overnight total of the year, against a prior high of 822 in August. Treat it as Moscow's claim about interceptions. It measures attack intensity as Moscow chooses to report it, not damage done.

Now what we don't know. There is no figure for Russian refining capacity taken offline, and none for the effect on product prices. Part of the Russian export shortfall is also self-inflicted, because Moscow extended its own diesel export ban. So Kyiv's campaign is one channel, not the whole of the tightness. The direction is still clear. Continued strikes keep Russian product exports impaired. That is a diesel channel distinct from crude. The so-what is simple. Washington's pump-price preference did not bind its own ally, and this source of diesel tightness persists.

That refinery file is one of several Trump carries into today's meeting with Xi Jinping.

Xi arrived Wednesday evening to a rare red-carpet welcome at Joint Base Andrews, per Al Jazeera. That is choreography, not evidence of concessions. Two things going in are new.

First, Bloomberg reports that negotiators have formalized a bilateral U.S.-China Board of Trade. It is meant to carry ongoing talks and tariff-cut frameworks for non-strategic goods. Second, Trump has imposed 12.5 percent tariffs on goods from sixty trading partners, China included. The stated reason is their alleged failure to curb imports made with forced labor.

Those two point in opposite directions. One is a body built to cut tariffs. The other is a live tariff layer on the same counterparty, heading into the same meeting. My read, and it is a judgment, is that non-strategic trade is being normalized while chips and critical minerals stay the coercive zone.

The Board itself can be read two ways. If it functions, it turns the November expiry of the Busan truce from a binary cliff into a managed process. That would lower tail risk for magnet- and rare-earth-exposed supply chains. But no rate, product list, or dollar figure is attached to it in the reporting. Until one is, it is an institution, not a tariff cut, and it can be switched off.

On chips, remember the baseline. In May 2026, the Bureau of Industry and Security began requiring export licenses for advanced computing sales to any company whose ultimate parent sits in an arms-embargoed country or Macau. That applies wherever the buyer itself is located. Enforcement has been tightening, not loosening. So any chip easing today would be a political override of that direction, not a return to normal.

As of this recording, no outcome from the summit is known. There is no deal, no joint statement, and no truce extension to report.

Three cases, one thread. A governor, an ally, and two heads of state set this week's binding constraints, and none of them was taking orders from the market. Two things to watch. First, whether today's meeting produces anything with a number or a date attached, rather than another institution or photo op. Second, whether Texas publishes an end date or a defined scope for its permit freeze, and above all whether that scope reaches on-site generation.

That's the briefing. Have a good day.
