Good morning. It's Saturday, September 12th, 2026, and here's what's moving underneath corporate credit.

Two numbers came out of the leveraged-loan market this week that flatly disagree with each other. Today's briefing is about which one is telling the truth.

Start with the benign one. Deutsche Bank's CLO collateral data, as relayed by PitchBook and running through September 1st, puts the cumulative default rate for US CLOs at 1.1 percent. That is roughly half the 2.5 percent rate at the same point last year. One qualification before we build anything on it. This is a model-derived cumulative rate on CLO-held collateral. It is not a market-wide issuer default rate, and it does not line up against a BDC non-accrual percentage or a private-credit default study.

The dollar figures tell the same story. Year-to-date defaults across CLO-held collateral total 9.1 billion dollars, spread across 45 issuers. For all of last year the comparable figure was 26.8 billion dollars across 60 borrowers.

Deutsche Bank models the full-year rate finishing just north of 2.2 percent. That would nearly match the four-year low of 2.1 percent set at the end of 2022. On the headline, this is one of the quietest loan-default years of the post-pandemic cycle.

Now the number that disagrees. The Morningstar LSTA US Leveraged Loan Index shows 11.18 percent of loans priced below 90 cents on the dollar, up from 8.04 percent a year earlier. That comes from separate PitchBook reporting, not confirmed to share a dataset or an as-of date with the default numbers, so take it as a companion signal rather than a matched pair. But the direction is the point. Realized defaults roughly halved while the distressed tail of the index grew by about three percentage points.

Those two things cannot both be describing improving credit quality. The reason is that a default is not a fact of nature. It is a scheduled event. A borrower and a lender jointly elect to trigger it, or decline to. That choice turns mostly on whether refinancing is cheap and available. When the window is open, amend-and-extend defers the count without changing the exposure underneath it. So a four-year low in the default rate right now is a statement about how open the refinancing window is. It is not a verdict on the borrowers sitting behind it. And the 2.2 percent projection is conditional on that window staying open. Nothing in the report tests whether it will.

There is a second selection effect layered on top of that one. The borrowers strong enough to refinance have been leaving private credit and moving back into the syndicated bank market, because a bank can underwrite them more cheaply. The ones who cannot make that move stay where they are. So the residual private book is not necessarily deteriorating on its own terms. It is being selected for weakness, one departure at a time. What the public loan market shows is that same selection viewed from the other side. The paper that stays keeps trading worse even as the count of formal defaults keeps shrinking.

You can watch the mechanic at the manager level, in prints that are about five weeks old rather than fresh this week. Crescent Capital's BDC saw its non-accrual rate fall from 5.7 percent to 4.8 percent of cost in its second-quarter report. The improvement came specifically from completing three restructurings. It did not come from the underlying credits getting healthier, and it sits on top of eight consecutive quarters of NAV decline. Whether those restructurings improve ultimate recovery or merely defer recognition is not resolved by anything in that report.

Golub Capital's BDC is the honest counterweight. Its non-accruals ticked up only slightly, to 1.9 percent of total investments at fair value, which is a genuinely low number at a disciplined lender. Do not rank the two. Golub measures at fair value and Crescent measures at cost, so the percentages are not comparable line for line. The point is the label, not the level. Same word, non-accrual, describing two completely different events. Golub's is credit performance. Crescent's is the accrual clock getting reset. So do not take an improving non-accrual print at face value without asking what produced it.

Who is still buying into this arbitrage, then? Crescent Capital Group, the same firm's asset-management arm, closed its second captive CLO equity fund in late August at 232 million dollars. That is more than double the 103 million it raised for the first one back in 2018, and institutional investors reported to include insurers and pension funds took part. Two caveats. This is committed capital, not deployed capital. And an eight-year gap spans two very different CLO regimes, so the doubling is not a clean like-for-like.

The size is a rounding error against CLO formation volumes, so do not read it as a market signal on its own. Read it as control. Captive equity lets a manager fund its own CLO liability stack without having to persuade a third-party buyer. That matters most precisely when third-party equity appetite for a book with a rising distressed share is the thing in question.

And there is a mechanism here that bites well before any default does. CLO equity is underwritten on par-based cashflow assumptions. But overcollateralization and reinvestment tests bind on market prices and on ratings, not on the default count. So collateral drifting into the eighties can start diverting cash away from the equity tranche while the default statistic is still printing a four-year low. That is the channel to watch, and it runs months ahead of the headline.

The insurer participation is also the handoff to where the funding behind all of this actually sits. The insurance leg behind this lending is normally a black box. One part of it just became visible.

Apollo's retirement-services arm, Athene, carries over 470 billion dollars in total assets. Roughly 27 percent of that sits in illiquid private debt. That much is the model working as advertised, with long-dated annuity liabilities set against long-dated illiquid assets.

The funding side is the surprise. As of June 30th, Athene had 27.7 billion dollars of Federal Home Loan Bank funding agreements outstanding, alongside 33.9 billion dollars of funding-agreement-backed notes.

And as of March 31st, Apollo itself was the third-largest borrower in the entire Federal Home Loan Bank system, at 28.2 billion dollars of advances. Two things to hold. These are quarter-end filings, not news from this week. And they reach us through a blog aggregating Apollo's 10-Q and Athene's investor materials rather than the primary filing text, so hold the precision loosely even though the picture is clear.

The pitch for the annuity-funded lending model has always been permanent capital. Patiently matched to long-dated illiquid assets. Uncorrelated with the banking system's funding cycle. What these disclosures show is that a meaningful slice of that capital is itself wholesale leverage, borrowed from a government-sponsored system.

That is not a solvency argument. FHLB advances are secured and overcollateralized, and a long-duration insurer can hold illiquid assets far more honestly than a retail interval fund ever could. But it does mean the correlation the asset class denies runs through a channel nobody underwrites. And note the asymmetry with the big non-traded retail credit funds, which capped investor redemptions at their standard 5 percent quarterly limit again earlier this month. Retail money leaves slowly, at a capped and measured toll. FHLB funding can leave on a policy decision. So how regulators view insurer FHLB membership belongs on the risk list alongside any market event.

So where does the cycle actually stand? Borrowers are still making their payments. Grinding rather than breaking. And this is worth saying plainly rather than forcing into one story: the loan-collateral layer genuinely looks healthier by headline than the retail funding layer does. That is dispersion across the stack, not uniform deterioration. But the week's two cleanest data points cut the same way. The statistic that is supposed to measure credit health is demonstrably elective. And a chunk of the capital funding the lending is levered on borrowed, recallable money.

Three things resolve this. The September 16th Fed decision is the near-term test of whether the refinancing window stays open. If it narrows, the gap between an 11 percent distressed share and a 1 percent default rate closes upward, and this month's four-year low becomes the top of the cycle rather than a reading on its health. Then the imminent third-quarter BDC earnings season, for whether Crescent's improvement holds or reverses, and whether Golub's low non-accrual rate is the outlier or the norm. And in November, the third-quarter non-accrual and payment-in-kind disclosures, which is where restructuring-driven improvement usually shows its real cost.

That's the briefing for today. Talk to you next time.
