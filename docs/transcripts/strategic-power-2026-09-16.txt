Good morning. It's Wednesday, September 16th, and this is your strategic power briefing.

For the past week this beat has kept returning to one argument. Announced capacity keeps losing to delivered capacity. And twice we guessed that some of the demand sitting in grid queues was speculative without being able to prove it. Today PJM's own market monitor put a number on that guess.

Start with the mechanics. In PJM's capacity auction for the 2027/28 delivery year, data centers accounted for six and a half billion dollars of the sixteen point four billion dollars in total auction cost. That is forty percent. Of that, six point two billion is tied to data centers that have not been built. That figure is the monitor's attribution, reported by Utility Dive and corroborated by a second outlet. Neither is PJM's published report itself, so treat the split as well-sourced secondary reporting rather than an audited primary number. Zoom out and the pattern holds. Across the three most recent auctions combined, data centers drove forty-five percent of forty-seven billion dollars in cleared capacity costs.

And Monitoring Analytics, PJM's market monitor, didn't just supply the number. It questioned the premise. In its own words, the load-forecast uncertainty tied to large data center additions is "unique and unprecedented" and "raises questions about the meaning of clearing a capacity auction based on those forecasts." Hold one thing about the messenger. Monitoring Analytics has a long institutional position that PJM's market design overpays incumbent generation. That is a reason to take the attribution seriously, and also a reason to read the framing as an argument rather than a neutral audit.

Now the mechanism worth sitting with. A capacity auction is a forward purchase. Generators get paid now to guarantee power years out. So when six billion dollars of that cleared cost is attributed to load that does not exist yet, real money is changing hands today against a forecast rather than against anything being consumed. That is the cleanest instance this beat has found of the distinction it keeps insisting on. A forecast is being priced as if it were a delivered asset.

The obvious follow-up is who eventually pays if that forecast load never shows up. Does it get trued up, clawed back, or does it simply sit with ratepayers? I went looking for a primary answer and did not find one. There is a related FERC proceeding on large-load interconnection rules. And there is a separate estimate that the large majority of network-upgrade costs tied to big new loads get socialized across all customers. But that is a different cost bucket. Those are transmission upgrades, not capacity-auction clearing prices. No true-up mechanism for cleared capacity cost turned up in any tariff, order or docket I could check. So I am not going to give you a dollar-per-month bill figure. That question is genuinely open, and anyone quoting you a precise household impact this week is reporting further than the evidence goes.

Two consequences follow anyway. The more of the grid's cost that gets allocated through an auction pricing speculative load, the more attractive behind-the-meter and on-site generation becomes for a hyperscaler. That is a bypass of the interconnection queue, not a fix for the generation shortfall — the megawatts still have to be built, just outside the market. And generators with PJM exposure capture windfall capacity revenue right now while accumulating a different kind of risk. The larger and more attributable that revenue becomes, the more political pressure lands on the speed and terms of the next round of interconnections. Today's revenue and tomorrow's regulatory exposure come from the same line item.

That leaves the sharper objection. If PJM is pricing data centers that don't exist, is the demand behind that forecast even real? For that, go to TSMC. Its filed August revenue was up fifty-three percent year over year. That is well past the company's own full-year guidance of growth "slightly above forty percent," and past its second-quarter pace of thirty-six percent. So the demand curve is steepening against the company's own plan, not flattening. Handle that with the right weight: monthly revenue is unaudited and lumpy, and one strong month is not a guidance raise.

The constraint matters more than the growth rate. Per reporting on TSMC's commentary, the binding limit right now is not wafer capacity. It is advanced packaging. The gap between what customers want and what TSMC can package is, in the company's words, "a very big gap." The company has also lifted its own estimate of quarterly equipment purchases to roughly one point nine times what it projected in December. Discount that the way we discounted GE Vernova's turbine backlog last week — it is an internal planning number, not a delivered tool.

So here is the so-what. In aggregate, the AI compute demand behind these power forecasts looks real rather than fantasy. But that validates nothing about any specific data center in PJM's queue, on PJM's timeline. Chips can ship into a buildout that lands somewhere else entirely. And there is a clock mismatch worth naming plainly. Packaging capacity can be added in quarters to a couple of years. PJM's commitment runs out to the 2027/28 delivery year, and the generation to serve it does not exist yet. Compute and power are being rationed on completely different calendars. That is exactly why the compute side can accelerate past guidance while the power side gets auctioned as scarcity.

Now follow that input chain further down, because both power and compute assume a supply chain running through China — rare earths, battery chemistry, solar cells. A new Chinese exit-control regulation is now in force, as reported. It lets commerce authorities bar people from leaving the country if their departure "may endanger national industrial or technological security." The reported effective date is mid-September, though the underlying State Council announcement dates to July, and I could not check either against a primary Chinese gazette. The stated targets are engineers carrying know-how in electrolyte formulas, N-type solar cell technology, and rare-earth separation. Those are precisely the people foreign firms have reportedly been recruiting, with higher pay and green cards, to replicate Chinese factories in Vietnam, India and Mexico.

Track the ladder here, because this beat has now watched three rungs of it in one week. First there was a published licensing rule on rare-earth exports — something a trade negotiation can actually address. Then came unattributable supplier refusals, which we covered Sunday, with no rule behind them and therefore nothing to formally negotiate against. Now it is control over the engineers themselves. Each step sits further from anything a summit can move. And there is a tell in the choice. Fencing in people rather than materials is an admission that Beijing's real moat is tacit knowledge, not the export license. Tacit knowledge diffuses on a timescale no regulation fully stops. One limit on all of this: there is no enforcement data yet. No count of individuals barred, no case. This is a rule, not an observed effect.

The second-order effect runs straight at Western friend-shoring. Every rare-earth separation plant, magnet line, electrolyte facility and N-type solar project outside China that quietly assumed it could hire Chinese-trained process engineers just got more expensive and slower. That strengthens the case for subsidy-dependent Western capacity at exactly the moment a trade detente would weaken the political urgency behind funding it.

Which brings us to that detente. This lands about a week before Xi Jinping's expected Washington visit and a meeting with Trump around September 24th — dates still sourced to a law firm's briefing note rather than a confirmed diplomatic calendar. The two sides are reportedly discussing lower tariffs on "nonsensitive" goods, including US energy and agricultural exports, with no agreed scope. So here is the honest limit on what that meeting can produce. A truce extension can move a tariff number. It cannot touch a talent-mobility control. Trade detente and technology dependency can ease and harden at the same time, and that is likely exactly what is about to happen.

Two things worth watching. Whether PJM's next auction cycle shows any correction in the not-yet-built share. And whether any state utility commission docket in Ohio, Virginia, Maryland or Pennsylvania produces an actual cost-allocation ruling. That is where the open ratepayer question gets settled, not in a press release.

That's the briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
