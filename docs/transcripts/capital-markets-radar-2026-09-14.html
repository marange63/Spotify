Cautious Optimism Briefings

Capital Markets Cross-Asset Radar

Transcript · September 14, 2026

Good morning. It's Monday, September fourteenth, 2026. The Fed decides Wednesday afternoon, and after last Thursday's producer price report the market has all but stopped asking whether it hikes. Hike odds ran somewhere in the high eighties to ninety percent through the weekend, depending on which tracker you asked. Nobody knows what comes after it. And what changed over the weekend isn't the policy outlook anyway. It's the positioning map underneath it, and that map has quietly become internally contradictory.

Start with the print that did the repricing, because its composition matters later. August producer prices rose five point four percent year over year. More than three-quarters of that increase was energy, with diesel alone up twenty-four percent on the month as the Iran war fouls Gulf shipping and refining. Now strip out food, energy and trade services. Core producer prices rose two tenths of a percent, below the three tenths economists expected and slower than July. So this is an oil shock passing through a price index, not broad-based demand inflation. Hold on to that distinction, because it is the whole argument about what the Fed does after Wednesday.

Now the positioning. On Friday this desk called the stagflation trade — short equities, long crude — the consensus speculative position, and on Saturday we built on that with UBS data showing trend-following funds at their largest-ever bearish exposure to global government bonds. New data from Citadel Securities corrects the equity leg of that story. CTA, vol-control and risk-parity strategies have all rebuilt equity exposure from the July lows, with the largest increases concentrated in the S&P 500 and the Russell 2000. So the same systematic complex carrying a record short book in government bonds has also been adding long equity exposure. That book cannot be right on both legs. Wednesday resolves, at most, one of them.

Before we go further, state the evidence honestly. Both halves of this picture are dealer models of client order flow. Citadel Securities is inferring systematic exposure. UBS was modelling its own dataset. Neither is an exchange-reported position. Read them as capacity and direction, never as a measured fact.

There are two honest ways to read that book. One: it's incoherent. Different desks running different signals happened to collide in the same week, and either Fed outcome forces someone to unwind. Two: it's a perfectly coherent single trade. Short duration because the Fed hikes. Long equities and long small-caps because the oil shock fades and the hike turns out to be one-and-done — which is exactly what cooling core producer prices would let the Fed argue. We can't resolve which from here. But we can name the observable that separates them, and it shows up Wednesday afternoon. Watch small-caps specifically. Russell constituents carry more floating-rate debt than large-caps, so a hike transmits to them through earnings, not just through the discount rate. If small-caps absorb a hike, the de-escalation read was the right one. If they break first, the book was incoherent.

That distinction inverts the consensus reflex, and this is the part worth sitting with. The standard story says a hawkish Fed is the painful outcome. But if the systematic complex is short bonds and long stocks, a hike read as one-and-done validates both legs at once. It may be the outcome that hurts the fewest positions, not the most. The genuinely painful path is the one where the Fed hikes and signals more to come. That breaks the equity leg while doing nothing to relieve the bond leg.

There's a mechanical point underneath it. Exposure rebuilt by vol-control and risk-parity programs is not discretionary. If yields or a hawkish statement drive a drawdown, the seller is price-insensitive and rules-based. It sells because realized volatility rose, not because it changed its mind. That amplifies a move rather than absorbing it, which is why the size of the initial shock matters less here than usual.

None of this shows up on the surface. The S&P 500 has closed at a record high twenty-seven separate times this year. An index behaving that calmly tells you almost nothing about the leverage sitting underneath it. And the same gap between surface and structure runs one rung down the capital stack, into credit.

Credit is the third leg of the same book. High-yield spreads sat at two hundred and seventy basis points over Treasuries as of last Thursday's reading, inside the tightest five percent of readings over the past twenty-five years. This desk called that complacent in six straight briefings and formally retired the call on Sunday. We're not reviving it. Take it as a positioning fact instead. Credit is priced for exactly the calm outcome the rebuilt equity longs are priced for. Neither has moved to reflect what the bond market's own record short book is saying.

Now the fastest gauge in the whole complex — crypto flows. Bitcoin briefly touched just under eighty thousand dollars on Saturday, then settled back around seventy-seven thousand two hundred. It held that level through the hawkish repricing. Spot Bitcoin ETFs logged a fourth straight day of net outflows on Friday, but the outflow shrank to about thirteen million dollars. Set that against the two hundred and eighty-three million dollar single-session outflow this desk reported on Saturday. The selling didn't accelerate into last week's inflation print. It exhausted itself.

That cuts against our own framing, and we'll say so. On Friday and again Saturday we told you Bitcoin was trading as high-beta risk, selling off with the hawkish repricing. The honest read now is narrower. The marginal seller ran out, which is not the same as a new buyer arriving. The other half of the flow picture supports that reading. Ethereum funds added one point eight billion dollars of assets in August. That's an August figure, not a this-week figure, so the timing doesn't line up cleanly and the inference is loose. But the institutional dollar clearly didn't leave the asset class. It rotated inside it. That weakens both the digital-gold story and the pure-risk-asset story this desk has alternated between. The marginal allocator looks like it's treating crypto as a relative-value sleeve.

Last stop, and the slowest clock in this briefing: private credit. Direct lending's share of private-debt fundraising has fallen from fifty-eight point four percent in 2024 to thirty-one percent by the first quarter of this year. The capital moved into infrastructure debt, real estate credit and asset-backed strategies. Be careful with that number. A falling share is not a falling dollar amount. If the total private-debt pool grew, direct lending could still be raising as much in absolute terms while losing ground in relative terms. So don't read it as flight. Read it as the slowest pool of capital in the market having already repositioned, over two years, away from the strategy that defined the entire cycle. And note where that leaves the next stress. Everyone is watching direct lending. The money went somewhere else, into vehicles that mark even more slowly than the ones we're all monitoring.

So here's the picture heading into Wednesday. Fast money has rebuilt equity length against a record short bond book. Credit is priced for calm. Crypto flows say the selling has stopped. And private capital has been quietly rotating out of direct lending for two years. Every pool of capital in this briefing has been repositioning. They're simply not all pointing the same way, and that is what a genuinely contested macro outcome looks like from the inside. What to watch Wednesday afternoon isn't the decision itself. It's which leg of that systematic book gets unwound first.

That's the radar for today. Trade carefully into it, and we'll see you tomorrow.
