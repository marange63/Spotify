Cautious Optimism Briefings

Semiconductors & Compute Infrastructure

Transcript · September 14, 2026

Good morning, it's Monday, September 14th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

For five straight sessions this beat has had to discount the industry's biggest numbers. In every case the number was measured by the company that benefits from it. Broadcom's gigawatt schedule, AMD's committed gigawatts, Qualcomm's vesting ceiling, DeepSeek's compression ratios — all self-reported, all taken with the same grain of salt. This week that changes. Somebody with actual subpoena power started checking the industry's claims, instead of this show having to. And what's getting checked isn't a number at all. It's a structure.

Start with the lead. The Justice Department has opened a formal antitrust probe into Nvidia's roughly twenty billion dollar licensing deal with the inference-chip startup Groq. Bloomberg reported it on September 10th and put the deal at twenty billion dollars. That matches how the transaction was described in contemporaneous reporting last December, so treat twenty billion as the figure rather than a range. The arrangement itself was structured as a non-exclusive technology license. Alongside it, Nvidia hired Groq's founder, Jonathan Ross, and several of his executives. As for what the Justice Department actually sent, every source covering this uses the same unspecific phrase — a formal demand for information. Nobody has said whether that means a civil investigative demand, a second request under Hart-Scott-Rodino, or something less formal. That detail simply isn't on the record yet.

Here's the actual legal question, because the headline obscures it. This isn't about whether Nvidia has too much market power. It's about whether a non-exclusive license, combined with hiring a startup's leadership team, counts as a reportable transaction at all under premerger notification rules. Nobody has confirmed that Nvidia filed an HSR notification for this deal. Nobody has confirmed that it didn't. That's the open question the investigation exists to answer, not a settled fact in either direction. And keep the posture straight: a probe is not a complaint. No allegation has been tested, and no remedy has been proposed. But if the answer turns out to be that a license-plus-hire doesn't trigger merger review, then the premerger notification regime has a structural gap that any large compute owner can drive through. That's a bigger fact than anything a remedy against Nvidia specifically would fix.

So what's the so-what. Even if this probe ends with no finding against Nvidia, it raises the cost of the template going forward. The parties who feel that first aren't Nvidia and Groq. They're the remaining independent inference-chip vendors — companies like Cerebras and Tenstorrent — whose fastest realistic path to an exit just got slower and more expensive to execute. One line of context belongs here. Nvidia's separate twelve point nine billion dollar acquisition of Hugging Face, the open-model hosting platform, is still awaiting regulatory clearance. Two structural deals, one antitrust apparatus. That's context, not a second item.

Because the same failure mode is showing up somewhere else this week, at a different agency, over a different kind of border. The New York Times reported that Aivres — the Fremont, California server maker partly owned by the Chinese firm Inspur — exported at least five point six billion dollars in advanced technology to Southeast Asia between April 2024 and February of this year. More than three billion dollars of that was servers equipped with Nvidia's Blackwell chips. Those servers reportedly ended up serving Chinese customers, including ByteDance and Alibaba, through cross-border compute rental, without a single physical GPU entering China. One sourcing note before those numbers travel further. This show could not retrieve the original Times investigation, so the figures reach us through secondary summaries of it. Take the precision accordingly, and note that whether those servers actually ran Chinese workloads rests on the report's characterization, not on verified shipment data.

The mechanism is the story. Inspur holds a thirty-three percent stake in Aivres, just under the fifty percent threshold that would have extended Inspur's own export blacklisting to its American subsidiary. This isn't smuggling, and nobody is alleging a violation. It's ownership-percentage arbitrage, operating inside the rules as written. Here's the harder point underneath it. Export controls are written to restrict where a chip physically sits. What was actually sold here was compute time delivered over a network, and no ownership-threshold fix reaches that. Close the sub-fifty-percent gap and you've addressed one structure while leaving the rental channel that made the whole thing work completely intact. Worth noting the timing. This surfaced days ahead of a planned US-China AI summit and President Xi's scheduled September 24th visit to the White House. There's no confirmed rule change and I won't predict one, but the pressure is now on the record at the least convenient possible moment.

Now the third structure, and this time Nvidia is the one building rather than the one being audited. Reuters and Bloomberg reported over the weekend that Nvidia is in preliminary talks to anchor up to ten billion dollars into Anthropic's planned IPO, an offering the reporting describes as potentially the largest ever. Those talks are explicitly preliminary. Nothing is signed, and Anthropic isn't a public filer, so none of this is company-disclosed. The reported growth behind it is still striking. Anthropic's annualized revenue run rate climbed from about nine billion dollars at the end of last year to above sixty-five billion by the end of July — again, that's reporting-attributed, not a financial statement. The obvious read is circular financing: a chip vendor propping up demand for its own hardware by buying equity in its own customer. I'd push back on that here. Ten billion is a small slice of an offering of that scale. And Anthropic has been named in reporting as one of Broadcom's custom-silicon customers. So Nvidia would be taking a stake in a lab that's simultaneously moving part of its compute off Nvidia GPUs. Read it as a hedge against its own displacement, not a scheme to inflate demand signals.

Now the counterweight, because three structures under outside audit deserve one number that still has nobody auditing it. Broadcom's Hock Tan spoke to investors on the company's fiscal third-quarter call, in a transcript published September 9th. He said Broadcom has, quote, secured the supply to again double AI revenue to approximately one hundred fifteen billion dollars in fiscal 2027. He added that demand actually exceeds that outlook, and pointed to another doubling on top of it in fiscal 2028. Apply the same discount this beat has applied all week. That's a management outlook on an earnings call, not backlog and not bookings, with no disclosed order figure behind it, from the executive whose stock benefits from investors believing it. But it's marginally sturdier than a warrant or a capped guarantee, for one specific reason. It's paired with a claim that supply, not just intent, has been secured. Take it at that weight and it's the strongest evidence this week that demand at the frontier-lab tier is still constrained by compute availability rather than by economics. It is also one vendor characterizing its own order book, which is thin ground for an industry-wide claim.

One correction before we close. The instinct is to read Broadcom's custom-chip momentum as GPU market share moving away from Nvidia. Structurally, it isn't. Broadcom's custom accelerators and Nvidia's GPUs both draw on the same constrained pool of high-bandwidth memory and the same advanced packaging capacity. A dollar of revenue shifting from one to the other doesn't relieve the physical bottleneck either company is competing to book around. It just changes whose income statement it lands on.

So here's where this leaves us. Three structures are now under some form of outside scrutiny — a license, an ownership stake, and potentially an equity position of Nvidia's own — while the largest forward number in the industry still has nobody checking it. Watch two things from here. First, whether the Justice Department converts its demand for information into an actual complaint. Second, whether any export-rule change follows the Aivres report, before or after Xi's September 24th visit. Until one of those lands, the structures hold, and nothing physical in this industry has actually changed.

That's your briefing for September 14th. Talk to you tomorrow.
