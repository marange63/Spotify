Good morning. It's November 2nd, and this is your AI products briefing. One standing disclosure up front: Anthropic makes the model behind this show, and today's lead story benchmarks a competitor's product against an Anthropic model, so weigh that accordingly.

The theme today is that the model is dropping behind the product. Three separate moves this week take the "which model" decision away from the user. GitHub now picks it per task. Apple picks it per country. And Google is betting the real value has shifted to wherever the work actually gets finished. Let's take those in order.

Start with GitHub, which took Copilot Fusion to general availability on October 28th. Fusion is not a model. It's a routing layer that sits inside Copilot's existing model picker. Instead of choosing a single model for a coding task, a developer chooses an effort tier: Standard, High, or Max. Fusion then routes the work to a model, or to several. GitHub's own changelog spells out three execution patterns. In the simplest, "one model solves the task directly." In Cascade, "a quality check determines whether to accept it or escalate to a stronger model." In Critique, "one model drafts an initial response, another model reviews it, and the original model revises." GitHub also shipped experimental support for routing to local models. That's genuinely new: models checking each other's work, built into the picker developers already open every day.

This escalates something we flagged on this beat on October 27th. Looking at Notion's Auto router, we argued that routers get more valuable as the cheap tiers underneath them improve. GitHub has now built that logic into an incumbent's default picker, not a startup feature. And Fusion shipped the same day GitHub's changelog dissolved the Copilot seat into a metered enterprise budget, which we covered on October 29th. Put those together, and Fusion's router now decides which models draw down that budget. Cost control has moved away from the buyer picking a cheap model. It now sits with GitHub's routing policy. The buyer keeps one lever: an admin policy that can disable specific models for Fusion without disabling them everywhere else.

Now the skeptical turn, because the marketing on this one needs correcting. GitHub's changelog is explicit that there's no separate orchestration fee. In its words, "when you use Copilot Fusion you're billed for each model it uses, at each model's standard rate." Paid plans also get a 30 percent discount on model costs, which GitHub describes only as "for a limited time." Notice what's missing from GitHub's own words: an end date. Press coverage has converged on November 30th as the cutoff. But that date comes from third-party reporting, not from GitHub's page. So treat it as reported, not confirmed, and expect GitHub to control that lever quietly.

The bigger correction is on performance. VentureBeat's headline framed Fusion as "near-Opus 5.5 coding performance at half the per-task cost." That sounds like parity at a discount. The numbers underneath come from the benchmarking firm Artificial Analysis, not from GitHub. On cost, Fusion Max averaged $5.59 per task, against $11.42 for Claude Code running Anthropic's Opus 5.5. That's roughly half, and genuinely cheaper.

Quality is where the headline stretches. In VentureBeat's words, "Fusion earned an overall score of 60, compared with approximately 62 for Claude Code with Opus 5.5 Max." Near, yes. Parity, no. The honest framing is cheaper at a modest quality discount, not cheaper at equal quality.

There's a billing catch, too. No orchestration fee doesn't mean no added spend. Cascade can escalate to a second model, and Critique runs at least two. So a team's per-task bill could rise even though GitHub charges nothing for the routing itself. GitHub also doesn't publish which models sit in the Fusion pool. It only draws from models your organization's existing policy already allows. So you can gate the pool, but you can't see from GitHub's documentation how the router chooses within it. And as a fresh GA release, there are no adoption figures yet. The bottom line: the router is now the product, and GitHub owns the router, discount terms and all.

GitHub swaps the model per task. Apple just showed the same move per country. According to TNW, Apple confirmed this week that Alibaba's Qwen model is coming inside Apple Intelligence, but only for devices and accounts set to China. As TNW puts it, "Alibaba's Qwen model will work inside Siri, Writing Tools and Visual Intelligence." A developer preview is reported to open November 4th, with a public rollout bundled into iOS 27.3 in December. On October 30th, this beat covered Apple's Creator Studio assistant, built on Apple models and OpenAI. Now there's a second outside model in the stack. The pattern is visible: one Apple Intelligence interface, with a different model underneath depending on the product and, now, the market.

Hold the enthusiasm, though. Nothing here has shipped yet. This is a developer preview followed by a December public release. The November 4th date traces to secondary reporting rather than an Apple press release. Apple hasn't disclosed whether Qwen handles these requests on-device or in the cloud. That split is exactly what decides the privacy and latency story for users in China. And this probably isn't Apple choosing a multi-vendor architecture for its own reasons. Reporting says China's regulator only added Apple Intelligence to its approved-service list this past July. So this reads more as compliance than strategy. It's tempting to call this a template Apple will reuse in the EU or India. That's a reasonable guess, but it's my inference, not anything confirmed. What the move does show: feature parity across markets is no longer something you can assume by default.

If the model underneath is this swappable, the value has to live somewhere else. Google is betting it lives at the surface where you actually finish the work. On October 29th, Google rolled out an in-chat editor across the Gemini app. Users can preview and edit documents, PDFs, slide decks, spreadsheets, and code without leaving the chat window. In Google's words, you can "expand to full screen to type edits directly, highlight text to ask questions, or have Gemini refine your files." Generated slides open in Google Slides, spreadsheets open in Sheets, and documents can be pushed to Drive. And critically, this isn't a limited beta. Per Google, "these capabilities are now available to all users in all languages where Gemini app is available."

Frame this as the third distinct route an incumbent has taken to the same question: who owns the document once an AI generates it. On October 25th, we covered Microsoft's curated plugin registry inside Word. On October 30th, we covered Apple's announcement that it will open its iWork apps to outside agents through MCP. Google just pulled the document straight into the chat window. Three different bets on the same real estate.

The skeptical read matters here too. On mobile, only PDFs work. In Google's words, "PDF preview and editing is supported on both the web and mobile." Everything else, from docs to sheets to slides to code, is web-only for now. There are no usage numbers yet, so we don't know whether this changes behavior. And the native handoff into Slides, Sheets, and Drive suggests this is really about retention. It pulls people deeper into Workspace rather than replacing office software. Any read on Notion or Canva losing ground to this is my inference, not a confirmed effect. The takeaway: in Google's version, AI is the front door to the suite, not a way around it.

So, three stories, one spine. GitHub chooses the model per task. Apple chooses it per country. And Google is betting the money sits in the surface where the work gets finished, not in whichever model drafted it.

Three things worth watching from here. First, whether GitHub publishes which models sit in the Fusion pool, and what happens to pricing once that discount ends. Second, whether Apple's November 4th developer preview lands, and whether it discloses the on-device versus cloud split for Qwen. Third, whether Google extends editing beyond PDFs on mobile, or finally shares a usage number.
