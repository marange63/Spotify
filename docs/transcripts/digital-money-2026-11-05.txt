More than five hundred sixty-three million of that was short positions being forced out. Trackers disagree widely on these totals, so treat the size as directional. The split is the point. This was mostly short covering, not fresh buying.

Going into the move, the futures book was already crowded. Aggregate open interest hit seven hundred one thousand five hundred forty-eight bitcoin on November 3rd, the highest since late September. That reading is dated before the rally. So it describes how loaded the book was walking in, not new longs piling on afterward. Perpetual funding, meanwhile, has held close to eleven percent annualized over the past two weeks, according to Bitfinex. That isn't euphoric. But it means longs are paying a real running premium to stay positioned.

Bitfinex's research desk draws the conclusion in its own words. The early recovery, it writes, was driven by leverage dynamics rather than spot demand. The bid under seventy-seven thousand dollars, it adds, is primarily underpinned by perpetual futures positions. And Bitfinex notes that only about three hundred fifty-three thousand bitcoin was acquired between seventy-five and seventy-seven thousand. The takeaway is that leverage, not conviction, is setting the swings in both directions right now.

If leverage lifted the price, the spot wrapper should tell us whether cash followed. Here the tape is more ambiguous than the rally headline suggests. On November 3rd, the day of the low, they slowed to a provisional one hundred fifteen million. That's a sharp deceleration. On its own, it could read as sellers running out of ammunition.

Then came the squeeze day itself. On Wednesday, November 4th, U.S. spot Bitcoin ETFs took in a net seventy-four point six million dollars, ending the outflow run. That's a real data point, and it complicates any flat claim that ETFs weren't buying. But look at which fund moved. There's an issuer read-through too, and it's our inference. With net new money near flat, the competition shifts from growing the pie to taking share, and this week IBIT is the one giving it up. So the honest read is this. The rally wasn't spot-backed in any broad institutional sense, but it wasn't abandoned by ETF money either. It's a thinner, more fragmented bid than either the bull or the bear headline implies.

So why did the floor sit where it did? Glassnode's on-chain research answers that. Bitcoin has now pulled back about ten percent from its September high, back into the levels it broke above in August. Glassnode puts the Short-Term Holder cost basis at seventy-five thousand seven hundred dollars. That's less than a hundred dollars above Tuesday's low. A step above it sits the True Market Mean, at seventy-seven thousand seven hundred. Linking the low to the cost basis is our observation, not Glassnode's claim. But it lines up cleanly. The band this market has tested three times in the past week is the average entry price of its most recent buyers.

And those buyers are hurting. Short-term holder supply in profit sat at or above ninety-seven and a half percent in early October. It has since fallen to about thirty-eight percent. By our arithmetic, that leaves roughly sixty percent of recent supply underwater. There are two honest readings, and the data doesn't force a choice. The bearish read is overhead supply. With most recent buyers underwater, any rally back toward break-even invites selling from people just trying to get out even. And if seventy-five thousand seven hundred breaks, capitulation becomes more likely. The bullish read is a shakeout. A floor that held at the cost basis, after a long flush and a short squeeze inside a week, is what clearing out weak hands looks like. Cost-basis levels are reference points, not mechanical support, and holding once doesn't guarantee holding twice. So the floor is real as a positioning level. It is not yet proven as demand.
