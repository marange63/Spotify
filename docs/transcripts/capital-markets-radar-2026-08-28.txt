Good morning, it's Friday, August 28th, and this is Capital Markets Radar.

Let's start with a correction. On Wednesday, this program said the inflation leg of this market had broken. We said there was no live inflation surprise left in the near-term data, and that September had become a growth-and-labour argument rather than an inflation one. That same Wednesday morning, the Bureau of Economic Analysis released the July personal income and outlays report, and it said otherwise. We didn't cover it Thursday. We're covering it now.

Headline PCE ran at 3.7 percent year over year in July. Economists polled by FactSet had forecast 3.6. That is a miss against expectations, not merely a high level holding steady.

Core PCE, which strips out food and energy, ran at 3.3 percent. The same FactSet poll had penciled in 3.2. Flag that one honestly: a separate survey had core already sitting at 3.3, so treat the core beat as directionally right but not universally agreed. What is not in dispute is the monthly pace. Headline and core both rose 0.2 percent on the month.

The income side stayed firm alongside it. Personal income rose $115.1 billion in July, with spending up as well. This is an economy still generating income and still spending it, which is not the backdrop that argues a central bank into cutting.

So what does this print actually change for the Fed? Look at the composition, not just the level. Headline is running above core, which tells you energy and food are doing the work at the top line. There is no fresh burst of underlying pressure beneath it. The hawkish case is therefore not that inflation is reaccelerating. It is that inflation is stuck in the mid-threes with no visible path down in the series the Fed actually targets. That is a stickiness argument, and stickiness arguments are the hardest kind to trade against, because there is no momentum to fade.

One clause of context, and only one. At the July 29 meeting, three regional presidents — Cleveland's Hammack, Minneapolis's Kashkari, and Dallas's Logan — dissented in favour of a hike. That was the first three-way same-direction dissent since September 2016. It is old news, and it is not a reaction to this data.

The market's response to the new print, though, was not ambiguous. The ten-year Treasury yield edged up to 4.65 percent on Wednesday, the day of the release, and rose again on Thursday for a second consecutive session. Those are aggregator levels rather than a Treasury par-curve print, so take them as approximate. We do not have a clean, quotable probability for a September hike, so we won't invent one. The direction is what matters: hike odds have moved up from where they sat earlier in August. Here is the so-what. The discount rate the market applies to every long-duration asset just got harder to argue down, and it did so hours before the Fed's new chair, Kevin Warsh, gives his first Jackson Hole keynote. That speech had not happened as of this recording. Treat it as the open catalyst, not a resolved one.

If the print was hot, why didn't risk assets flinch? Part of the answer is crude. Brent traded below $88 a barrel this week, and by Thursday it was below $87, a fourth straight down session, taking the weekly decline past 7 percent.

For the last several runs, this program has argued the Iran risk premium was leaving crude on probability rather than on physics — that the Hormuz constraint itself hadn't actually changed. This week produced the first reported fact that touches the physics. Iran's military said it reached a revenue-sharing agreement with Oman over the Strait of Hormuz, easing the specific supply-disruption risk that had been holding up a war premium. That is a Bloomberg-reported arrangement. It is not a confirmed change in tanker flows, so nobody should call Hormuz throughput normalised on the strength of it.

Here's the tension worth naming. The IEA's August oil market report cut its third-quarter refinery-run estimates by a further 370,000 barrels a day, citing attacks on Russian refineries and Middle East product-export disruptions. So the supply side of the ledger is deteriorating while the price falls more than 7 percent on the week. Either the market is confident the Hormuz risk is genuinely retired, or it is trading positioning rather than barrels. Given how one-sided and how recent that de-escalation report is, the positioning read looks the more likely of the two. What would falsify it is simple: a single credible transit disruption should snap the premium straight back if the physics were never really resolved.

The so-what runs straight back to the lead. Falling Brent is the mechanism that lets next month's headline print look more benign even while core stays stuck. Cheap crude has been quietly capping the long end for weeks. But that offset is now hostage to a diplomatic arrangement rather than to actual barrels, which means it carries reversal risk that the current price is not paying anyone to hold.

That same disinflationary offset is part of why equities shrugged off the print entirely — and the week's biggest single data point landed the same night. Nvidia reported fiscal second-quarter revenue of $96.2 billion, up 106 percent from a year ago. Data centre revenue alone was $89.0 billion. Gross margin, GAAP and non-GAAP both, came in at 75.0 percent.

The guide is the part that matters for this beat. Nvidia expects $108 billion in the current quarter, plus or minus 2 percent. That is another 12 percent sequential step on top of a quarter that itself stepped up 18 percent. And it was struck with, in the company's own words, no assumed data centre compute revenue from China at all. The Nasdaq rose 1.57 percent on the day and the S&P 500 rose 0.72 percent, with European tech following.

Frame that as the concentration test this program flagged on Wednesday, resolving to the upside for one quarter. But the bullish read is the lazy one. Zero China cuts both ways. It is upside optionality if licensing ever relaxes, and it is equally a company stripping a line item it cannot forecast out of a number it has to hit. And a 0.72 percent index move on a print that size says the market took the beat as confirmation of a thesis it already held, not as new information. Concentration risk was deferred by one quarter. It was not retired.

Here is the collision. This is the capex cycle that underwrites hyperscaler bond issuance and power-infrastructure demand, and it just accelerated into a rate path that got harder to argue down in the same twenty-four hours. Those two facts have to be reconciled, not reported side by side. Estimate revisions from here are asymmetric in one direction and not the other: any China relaxation is pure upside no published number carries, while the downside case has to come from hyperscaler capex, not from geopolitics.

Now the one corner of the market that answers the liquidity question with structure instead of price. Blue Owl's non-traded business development company, OBDC II, said on August 18, according to trade-press reporting rather than a filing we retrieved, that it will permanently halt quarterly share redemptions. That reverses its own prior claim of having resumed them.

This program's read on Thursday was that the fund's discount looked like a dispute over exit price, not a run, because fewer than one percent of shareholders had taken a recent tender offer. This is the other half of that fact. The exit didn't stay open because holders declined it. The sponsor is closing it. A tender nobody takes and a redemption window that never reopens look identical on a screen and mean opposite things about who is choosing not to transact. The correction is real but bounded. This is a fund-level liquidity decision, not proof of a sector-wide run. The so-what is that the next repricing in this complex arrives through fund terms and distribution policy rather than through any spread you can watch.

Back to the front end for the close. Bitcoin is holding near $79,000, up modestly on the day, essentially unmoved by a hawkish inflation print. That is the same rates-proxy asset behaving without any rates support underneath it. The more durable story sits under the price. On US exchange volume, USDT's share has fallen from 72 percent in January to roughly 64 percent in August, while USDC's share has risen from 18 to 26 percent. Those figures come from a research newsletter tracking exchange data, not from a Treasury or exchange release, so hold them loosely. If they are right, they describe a slow migration of stablecoin activity toward a US-chartered issuer whose reserves sit in short-dated Treasury bills. That is a small structural bid arriving at the front end of the curve in the same week the front end is repricing for hikes. It is volume migrating, though, not necessarily outstanding float, and the bill-demand case only holds if balances move rather than turnover.

Two things are genuinely unresolved as we close. Kevin Warsh had not spoken at Jackson Hole as of this recording, and September 16 is the meeting that prices everything we've covered today. That's Capital Markets Radar for August 28th. Have a good weekend.
