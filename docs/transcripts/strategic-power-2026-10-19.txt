Good morning, it's Monday, October 19th, 2026, and this is your Strategic Power briefing.

The thesis today is simple. Prices are being set by physical flows, not by policy promises. That's true at the world's most important oil chokepoint. And it's true on the American grid, where the question isn't missing supply anymore. It's demand that may not actually exist.

Start with oil. Brent crude touched a hundred and ten dollars a barrel on Friday for the first time since May, then settled at one hundred nine dollars and eighty-six cents. That was a gain of three point seven percent on the day. WTI settled at one hundred one dollars and forty-one cents, up three and a half percent. The headline story is Iran escalating attacks on tankers in the Strait of Hormuz. But the number that matters is a flow number, not a headline. The analytics firm Kpler puts crude shipments through Hormuz at nine point four million barrels a day for the week ending October 14th. That's about seventy percent of the average before the Iran war began. Put plainly, roughly three in ten barrels of normal Hormuz flow are simply not moving. So this is a realized physical shortfall, not a speculative risk premium bolted onto the price.

Here's where oil and sanctions policy collide. Treasury's sanctions office has extended the general licenses covering Lukoil's overseas retail network. Lukoil stations outside Russia can now keep serving customers until April 25th, 2027. Funds still can't be transferred to any person or account inside Russia. So this is not relief on Russian crude exports, and it shouldn't be read as one. On Thursday, this desk told you to watch the Lukoil wind-down license due to expire October 22nd. Lukoil's licensing has since been pushed out into 2027.

That makes it another rung on a ladder we've been tracking for about ten days. First came the early-October license letting Russian diesel back into global markets. Then, on Friday, came the Graham Act waiver, which shelved the tariffs aimed at the biggest buyers of Russian energy. Now there's this Lukoil extension. So why does a retail carve-out matter? Lukoil and Rosneft are the two sanctioned majors that together produce about half of Russia's crude. Every carve-out for either one signals how hard Washington intends to enforce. And every Russia-energy catalyst this desk has flagged in that window has resolved toward loosening, not tightening.

There are two ways to read that ladder. One is accommodation: Washington easing pressure on Moscow for reasons that have little to do with the oil market. The other is supply security. With roughly three-tenths of normal Hormuz flow missing, keeping Russian barrels and Russian-linked retail networks in the market is a hedge against a worse shortage. The evidence in front of us doesn't discriminate between those two readings yet.

Here's what would. If Hormuz flows recover and Washington starts letting these licenses lapse, the supply-security reading wins. If the licenses keep rolling even after Gulf barrels come back, that points to accommodation. Watch the sequence, not the rhetoric.

Either way, the cost is the same, and this part is our inference. Each extension teaches buyers of Russian energy that Washington's sanctions bend when pump prices bite. That lesson carries over to every future secondary-sanctions threat, including ones aimed well beyond Moscow. And notice what Washington got for the credibility it spent. Brent still printed near a hundred and ten dollars. The chokepoint, not the sanctions regime, is setting the price.

That matters heading into the Federal Reserve's October 28th meeting. A sustained Hormuz premium is a live inflation pressure going into that decision, not an outcome of it. So what: Washington is spending sanctions credibility to buy barrels, and it still isn't setting the price.

Energy is the base layer at home too. And on the American grid, the story inverts. In PJM, the question isn't missing supply. It's demand that may not show up.

PJM's independent market monitor, Monitoring Analytics, filed with federal regulators on September 25th. It said that, as of September 21st, about four gigawatts of forecast data-center load had been canceled or delayed. That's forty-two percent of the data-center load growth PJM had forecast over a two-year window running through twenty-twenty-nine. Be precise about that base. It's a specific forecast window, not PJM's entire data-center queue. And delayed is not the same as canceled, because delayed load still shows up eventually. So the number is real, but it's narrow.

On Saturday, this desk said data centers didn't cause PJM's reliability alert, but they were making the margin more expensive to hold. Now the market monitor is telling regulators that a large slice of the forecast load behind those prices has already been canceled or slipped.

The money at stake is real. Across PJM's last three base capacity auctions, data-center forecasts above existing load totaled twenty-one point three billion dollars. That's forty-five percent of the forty-seven point two billion dollars in total cleared capacity costs. Not all of that forecast load is phantom. Much of it is real, and the fight is over the residual. But ratepayers are paying firm-capacity prices today against forecasts that are proving partly soft.

Who ends up paying is still open. In a September 29th order, the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission accepted PJM's reliability backstop procurement. But it suspended the backstop's effective date to February 28th, 2027, subject to refund. And it sent the question of cost allocation to a paper hearing. So don't hear this as regulators blessing a ratepayer bill. FERC approved the mechanism, not the cost split, and that fight is still fully live.

Why is the load canceling? That isn't settled, and the reporting on the filing doesn't say. There are three live hypotheses. It could be power availability itself, which would be reflexive: projects slipping because the grid can't serve them. It could be financing stress on developers. Or it could be queue-gaming, where developers file for more sites than they ever intend to build. Hold all three open.

Here's the part that cuts against our own earlier read. On Saturday, this desk inferred that firm, already-built capacity collects the scarcity rent, meaning incumbent nuclear and the independent power producers. That rent is real today. But its durability depends on forecast load that's proving soft at the margin. If cancellations keep coming, capacity prices embedded in PJM-exposed names like Constellation, Vistra, and Talen are at risk of being overstated. That's a risk to flag, not a call. So what: ratepayers are buying firm capacity against soft forecasts, and the scarcity trade now has a demand-side question attached.

Texas is running the mirror-image test. ERCOT's interconnection queue has grown to more than five times the grid's record peak demand. Data centers make up about ninety percent of those requests. Following Governor Abbott's August order, ERCOT paused approvals for sites of seventy-five megawatts or more. It now owes the state utility commission a formal eligibility and community-impact report by December 10th. One skeptical note. A freeze may not reduce buildout at all. It may simply push hyperscalers behind the meter, onto their own generation.

So both of the country's biggest grids are now forcing AI load to prove itself before it gets priced into everyone else's bill. That raises the effective cost and time of interconnection, even for the projects that do proceed.

One calendar correction before we close, and it comes from minerals, where the same gap between promises and leverage shows up. Yesterday, this desk cited a November deadline on China's suspension of its rare-earth export controls. According to Treasury Secretary Bessent's September statement, his team settled for a two-month extension to January 10th, 2027. That was shorter than the six-month extension many traders had expected. Some sources still cite November 10th, so flag the discrepancy. But treat January 10th as the operative date. The short window is itself the signal. Beijing is keeping this lever short-dated rather than resolving it.

Here's the watch list. First, the October 28th FOMC meeting, and how near-hundred-and-ten-dollar oil figures into that debate. Second, Kpler's next weekly read on Hormuz flows. Third, ERCOT's December 10th report to Texas regulators. And fourth, Ukraine's threatened strikes on Russian refineries. Those would tighten the same Russian fuel flows Washington is licensing back in, and so far they remain unproven.
