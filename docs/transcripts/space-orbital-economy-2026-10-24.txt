Good morning. It's Saturday, October 24th, 2026, and this is your space and orbital economy briefing.

Today the scoreboard that matters isn't satellites announced. It's satellites actually serving someone. On that measure, the gap between the leader and everyone else widened again this week.

Start with a number from SpaceX's third-quarter call on Tuesday. Starlink Mobile, the direct-to-cell service, passed twenty-two million monthly users, according to Via Satellite's coverage of the results. That's up thirty-seven percent from the prior quarter. Two caveats before we lean on it. First, these are monthly users, not paying subscribers, and SpaceX disclosed no direct-to-cell revenue. A user bundled into a carrier's basic texting plan is worth far less than a broadband customer paying for a dish. Second, like everything from this quarter, the figure comes from trade-press coverage and the call transcript, not a primary SEC filing we've been able to verify. So read it as reach, not revenue.

As a scale fact, though, it lands. Direct-to-cell is the one business line where carriers were visibly organizing an alternative to SpaceX, through their partnerships with AST SpaceMobile. AST's next batch of satellites is reported to be waiting on Blue Origin's grounded New Glenn rocket. Meanwhile SpaceX is serving more than twenty million users off a constellation that is already in orbit. That shifts the negotiating balance. A carrier hedging with AST is now hedging against an incumbent that already has scale, not against a rival that is still proving its service. The so-what is optionality. SpaceX is building a second connectivity business inside the first one, on infrastructure it already operates.

That new reach arrived just as the core Starlink business grew at a slower pace. Connectivity revenue was five point two billion dollars, still up twenty-two percent from the second quarter. But Via Satellite reports that this was a slower rate of growth than the second quarter's, and it doesn't say how much slower. So the business is still growing fast, just less fast than it was, and we won't guess at the size of the step down. On Wednesday we said subscribers and revenue per user rose together, and that's still true. What's changed is the pace underneath. SpaceX itself pointed to enterprise and government as the growth pockets. Put that together with direct-to-cell, and the next leg of Starlink's growth looks less like consumer dishes and more like everything else.

The capacity behind that next leg depends on Starship closing its economics, and that question now has a rough date attached. Flight 14, on September 28th, was the first Starship flight ever to reach orbit. It deployed twenty-six Starlink V3 satellites before a shortened flight ended in a Pacific splashdown. On the earnings call, Elon Musk said the next step goes further. In his words, "For Flight 15, which we expect to happen next month, we're going to attempt to catch both the booster and the ship." That is stated intent, and the date isn't firm. Catching the Super Heavy booster is no longer new. The marginal step is catching the upper stage. Coverage of the call describes that as what would be the first full recovery of an orbital-class vehicle.

On Wednesday we said SpaceX's profit told us nothing about Starship's unit economics. Flight 15 is the first dated test of that open question. The mechanism is straightforward, even without a cost-per-kilogram figure we can source. An upper stage that isn't recovered means building new hardware for every V3 deployment. A caught and reflown ship turns that hardware into a reusable asset. The cost of each launch then falls toward propellant, refurbishment, and turnaround time. If the dual catch works, SpaceX cuts its own marginal cost of putting V3 satellites in orbit, at the very moment its rivals are bottlenecked on launch access. If it fails, Starship's operational cadence stays behind the narrative. In that case the gap we're describing still exists, but it widens more slowly than SpaceX's own roadmap implies.

Meanwhile, the nearest Western rival to Starlink can't get a satellite to orbit at all. Amazon's Leo constellation, the rebranded Kuiper, last launched in early July, according to the public launch logs. That was the ninth and final mission on ULA's Atlas V, and nothing has flown for the program since. The cost of that drought is now measurable in regulatory terms. Under its FCC license, Amazon owed one thousand six hundred sixteen satellites in orbit by July 30th. Via Satellite describes what it had as just a fraction of that. The FCC responded with a limited, conditional waiver, and the condition has teeth. Per Via Satellite's reporting on the order, any first-generation satellite Amazon launches after July 30th temporarily loses its spectrum-priority status. That penalty runs twenty months, until March 30th, 2028. The final deadline, for the full constellation by July 2029, hasn't moved. So the binding constraint for Amazon isn't money or demand. It's rides, and the regulator has already turned that constraint into a competitive cost rather than a paperwork formality.

The next attempt to close that gap is Amazon Leo's first flight on United Launch Alliance's Vulcan, listed no earlier than October 29th. Per the launch logs, it's the first of thirty-eight Vulcan launches planned for the program. We've flagged that date in each of our last two briefings, and it still hasn't flown. If it slips again, the slip itself becomes the story. The other half of Amazon's ride problem is Blue Origin. According to SpaceNews, Blue Origin is rebuilding New Glenn's launch pad around a crane rather than a transporter-erector. Payloads will be mated at the pad instead of inside the integration facility. New Glenn still has no firm return-to-flight date. Read optimistically, the crane design is a simpler, faster rebuild. Read skeptically, it moves integration risk onto the pad, and could cap how quickly that pad turns a vehicle around. Neither reading is proven yet. And the bench behind those two isn't getting deeper. Rocket Lab's Neutron, another would-be Western alternative, has now slipped its first launch into 2027. Either way, Amazon's capacity plan rides on rocket programs it doesn't control.

China has the inverse problem. According to the tracker China in Space, Guowang launched its twenty-seventh batch around October 9th. That was nine satellites on a Long March 12 from Wenchang, bringing the count in orbit to two hundred twenty-two. The plan eventually calls for roughly thirteen thousand. Its sibling constellation, Qianfan, is planned at fourteen to fifteen thousand. And according to tracking sources, neither has started commercial service. So Beijing has the rides and is filling the shells, but it doesn't yet have a working product. Call it occupancy ahead of service. One plausible motive, and this is our inference rather than confirmed fact, is locking in orbital-shell and spectrum priority before the business case has to exist. The same discount we apply to Amazon applies here. Two hundred twenty-two satellites against a plan of thirteen thousand is a launch campaign, not a network. That cuts against both the story that China is racing ahead and the story that China is irrelevant.

Europe shows a third variant of the same gap. Airbus delivered the first thirty-two next-generation OneWeb satellites to Eutelsat on October 2nd. That's real capital committed to a refresh. It tells you Eutelsat is choosing to stay in low-orbit broadband rather than cede it to SpaceX and Amazon by default. But delivered from the factory isn't launched, and launched isn't in service. Until those satellites fly, Eutelsat's refresh is capital spent, not capacity added.

So here's where this stands. Genuinely inflecting is Starlink's reach, now extending into direct-to-cell at a scale no independent rival can match. Still a demo are Starship's full-reuse economics, Amazon's deployment pace, and Chinese commercial service. All of it is real hardware, and none of it is yet a repeatable business. There are two open questions. Can direct-to-cell users be monetized, or are they mostly bundled reach? And with Starlink's core growth slowing, can enterprise and government carry the next leg? Watch three catalysts. First, the Vulcan Amazon Leo flight, no earlier than October 29th. Second, the FAA's public comment window on New Glenn's launch cadence, closing October 30th. Third, Starship Flight 15's attempt to catch both stages, sometime next month. That's where the real test of this gap happens next.

That's the briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
