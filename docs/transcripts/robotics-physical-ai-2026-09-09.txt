Good morning. It's September 9th, and this is your robotics and physical AI briefing.

Yesterday this show argued that the scarce asset in physical AI is provenance, and that provenance is unevenly distributed. Today the country holding most of it said out loud what it wants to do with it.

The lead: the People's Liberation Army's official newspaper published a call, around September 7th, for researchers to accelerate the transfer of humanoid robotics from laboratories to military training grounds. The word used in the reporting is combatants. The structural fact underneath that editorial is not new, but it is the one that matters. Chinese manufacturers accounted for roughly 95 percent of global humanoid robot shipments in 2025, according to BofA Global Research. That is a full-year 2025 figure, not a fresh data point. It is also the entire Western humanoid supply chain sitting downstream of one country's factories, at the moment that country's military press starts talking about robotic soldiers.

Now discipline the lead the way this show has disciplined every Chinese hardware claim this week. This is an editorial, not a program. A deeper look at the public record makes the distinction sharper. Reuters reviewed more than one hundred Chinese military procurement notices, academic studies, patents, official publications and defense-company materials. It found plenty of paper. It found no named program, no unit count, and no budget line. Reuters also said plainly that it found no evidence China has deployed an armed humanoid with an operational PLA unit. The record is wide, and it is shallow.

One more discipline point, on sourcing. The original Chinese-language editorial has not been retrieved. Every outlet reporting this traces back to the same English paraphrase. So treat the combatants language as translation, not as verbatim text. And the machines themselves remain power-hungry and unreliable outside staged demonstrations. That is exactly what this show has said about their commercial cousins all week. The takeaway is signaling, not procurement.

Here's the second-order effect that is actually load-bearing. This is the inverse of the chip fight. In semiconductors, the West restricts what flows into China. In humanoids, the West is the buyer. China holds the shipment share, and it holds the magnet and actuator inputs underneath it. Export-control logic does not transfer cleanly when you are the importer.

The rule that matters here is a real, dated Chinese legal instrument. MOFCOM Notice 2025 Number 61 requires an export license on any item where Chinese-origin rare earths make up a tenth of a percent or more of that item's value. It explicitly names permanent magnet materials as a controlled category. Two honest limits on that. Whether the scope reaches the actuators inside a humanoid robot is not stated in any primary text I could retrieve, so that link is inference rather than a sourced fact. And the November reactivation date that trade-compliance reporting keeps citing could not be confirmed against MOFCOM's own notice. Hold both of those loosely.

Now the policy mismatch, and it is the part Washington should be uncomfortable about. The American answer so far runs through the finished machine. This beat flagged the FCC step adding foreign-built humanoids and other mobile robots to its Covered List. Take that as reported and not independently verified. The effect, if it holds, is to block the equipment authorization an imported robot needs before it can be sold here. But that does nothing about the magnets and actuators already sitting inside robots assembled domestically. The chokepoint is upstream of the machine, and the policy is aimed at the machine.

So what. If that rare-earth rule reactivates in full, Western humanoid bills of materials rise. They rise at exactly the moment the sector has no demonstrated unit economics to absorb the hit. That is direct cost pressure on programs like Figure, Apptronik and Boston Dynamics. It also points the trade somewhere other than the humanoid brands themselves. If the West's answer to Chinese manufacturing scale becomes industrial policy rather than product competition, the beneficiaries are domestic magnet and actuator supply, plus the defense-adjacent integrators. Not the badge on the robot.

If that is the adversary's supply chain, what is the West's defense-autonomy money actually buying right now? Increasingly, it is buying software rather than platforms.

L3Harris has licensed Shield AI's Tracker counter-drone software into its VAMPIRE platform, announced September 1st. By reported account, and not by Shield AI's own release language, this is the first time Shield AI has licensed that software to another company. VAMPIRE is not a prototype. The platform has logged more than 350,000 operational hours since 2023, and it has shot down hostile drones in combat, mainly in support of European operations. Keep those hours attached to the platform rather than to Shield AI's software. They were earned before this deal existed.

What Tracker adds is passive detection and tracking, using electro-optical and infrared video. No radar, no radio-frequency emissions. The interesting point there is economic, not technical. This is a capability increment delivered as a software load onto a weapon that is already fielded and already paying for itself. Compare that with humanoids, where every capability increment still requires new hardware and unproven unit economics. It is the cheapest increment available anywhere in this sector.

Line that up against what this show has covered over the past week and it looks like a pattern rather than a coincidence. Shield AI's Hivemind autonomy software won an Air Force production contract while flying aboard an Anduril airframe it does not build. The Army's TITAN production award then split roughly two-thirds to Palantir and one-third to Anduril. And now a prime is paying to embed a startup's perception stack inside its own hardware. Three instances of defense value landing on the autonomy software layer rather than on whoever bends the metal.

Take the counter-read seriously, though. No dollar figure was disclosed on the L3Harris deal, so a licensing pattern is not yet a licensing business. And licensing into a prime's fielded platform is the position with the least pricing power in the chain. The prime owns the customer, the installed base and the renewal. That matters for how private marks in this category get underwritten. Recurring software revenue inside somebody else's hardware is a different business from winning platform competitions outright. Better margins, worse customer concentration, and much less control over volume. Shield AI and Anduril should not be valued off the same template.

So what. The corner of embodied autonomy that is fielded, combat-proven and being paid for today is counter-drone perception. The capital is concentrated in humanoids that nobody has yet shown running an unattended shift.

Briefly, on that humanoid brain race. Sam Altman confirmed on a podcast that OpenAI will build both a humanoid body and the physical-AI model to control it. Asked to choose between the two, he said "both." There is no timeline, no spec and no funding attached to that. It is an intent to spend, not a launch. It does extend the fork this show named on September 4th, when Figure answered the same brain question with a multibillion-dollar compute commitment rather than a transfer metric. A frontier lab entering on those terms raises the capital bar again. And it raises it before anyone has shown that compute alone buys embodied reliability. Intent to build a humanoid has never been the scarce input in this sector.

A quick catalyst check to close, on Tesla's Cybercab. Tesla is now on record telling NHTSA that it certified the vehicles as compliant with all applicable federal safety standards. It also told the agency it plans to gradually expand deployment to more vehicles and more locations. The audit opened September 3rd has produced no finding. It has not slowed the rollout either. That is continuation, not escalation.

What would settle this week's open questions is easy to name. The primary text of the Chinese rare-earth rule, and whether its scope names robot actuators. A disclosed dollar figure on an autonomy-software license. And any NHTSA finding at all on the Cybercab. Until then, the money keeps flowing to compute, and to software riding inside someone else's hardware, while the fielded reliability numbers on the humanoids themselves still do not exist.
