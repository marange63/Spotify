Good morning, this is your Capital Markets Radar for Friday, November 13th.

The loudest shock this week stayed inside one stock. The quieter one is spreading across assets. That quieter shock is the discount rate. We'll take Nvidia first, then follow the discount rate through the dollar, gold, and credit. Each of those moves only makes sense once you see the same force behind all three.

Start with Nvidia, because the arc closed yesterday. Earlier this week, Nvidia's board opened an independent review of Jensen Huang. Per press reports, it concerned his personal relationship with a woman alleged to be a Chinese spy, a link that has never been confirmed. The stock fell about four percent on Monday while AMD jumped. Tuesday's session took off roughly another five and a half percent. On Thursday, the CEO was gone. Nvidia's 8-K says Huang resigned as President, CEO, and board member, effective immediately. The filing gives no reason, and it does not mention the review. Colette Kress, the company's CFO, becomes interim CEO and keeps the CFO title. Harvey Jones becomes the company's first-ever board chair, and the filing does not describe him as independent. The board is searching for a permanent CEO and will consider internal and external candidates. Nvidia's third-quarter results stay on the calendar for Tuesday, November 17th. The filing says nothing about guidance either way.

Now the tape. Nvidia opened down more than thirteen percent and touched a new fifty-two week low. By the close it had pared that to a loss of about four and a third percent, per CNBC.

AMD closed up nearly six percent the same session. And here is the number that tells you how this was actually priced. The S&P 500 fell just over a third of a percent. The single most important stock in the AI trade lost its chief executive, and the broad index barely blinked.

There are two ways to read that recovery from down thirteen to down four. One is relief. A clean, immediate resignation closes an open-ended overhang, and two sharp down sessions earlier in the week had already priced part of it. The other is mechanical: dip-buying and short-covering in a market that, as we're about to see, was already thin on risk. Don't over-read AMD's pop as a durable share shift, either. The same rivals-up, leader-down pattern showed up on Monday, and pairs trades alone can produce it. On key-person risk, our judgment is that the near-term threat to Nvidia's product roadmap is probably over-read. What Huang carried personally, his customer relationships and his standing in Washington, is harder to replace, and nobody can yet put a number on it. So what: Thursday's market priced a governance and key-person discount on one company. It was not a verdict on AI demand.

So why didn't a thirteen percent gap in the AI trade's anchor stock break the index? Because the market it hit was already de-risked. Morgan Stanley's prime-brokerage data show global hedge funds de-grossing more than eight percent of total gross exposure since June. Separately, Citadel Securities' model put aggregate US equity CTA positioning at minus 3.3 standard deviations. That's its lowest reading since March. The caveat is timing. That Citadel reading is dated November 3rd, so it predates the November 5th selloff, the CPI shock, and the resignation. It's also one model, and desks disagree on direction from here. Still, our read is that it fits: a market already short of risk can absorb a single-name shock without cracking. The flip side is that the same thinness makes this morning's PPI release, due at 8:30 Eastern, a two-way squeeze risk. A soft print could force mechanical buying. A hot one adds to systematic supply. Nothing in the positioning data tells us which way it tips.

The shock that's actually spreading isn't Nvidia's. It's the discount rate. You know the CPI story from earlier this week, so just the callback. Core inflation printed 0.3 percent for a third straight month, above consensus, and the December debate moved from whether the Fed hikes to how much. The new fact is what that's done to the dollar. Early this week, the dollar was softening even as hike odds rose. That's over. The Dollar Index has since pushed to a nineteen-month high.

And the short end isn't the only part of the curve paying. On CPI day, the 30-year Treasury yield rose about nine basis points, to 5.35 percent. The 2-year rose about twice as much, so the curve still bear-flattened. But the long end is moving too. That raises the discount rate on exactly the long-duration, AI-heavy equities at the center of this week's other story.

The dollar leg has its own second-order bite. A stronger dollar tightens offshore dollar funding. It leans on dollar-denominated carry trades and on emerging-market borrowers. That reverses the funding relief a softer dollar offered at the start of the week. So what: capital is getting more expensive across the curve and across currencies. That is the backdrop long-duration equities, and next Tuesday's Nvidia print, now trade against.

Now the clearest sign of where the defensive bid went on a risk-off headline day. Gold fell about three percent on Thursday, to roughly $3,866 an ounce, per USAGold. That was the same session Nvidia lost its chief executive, and the same day the Dollar Index hit its nineteen-month high. On Wednesday this desk called gold a rate-sensitive asset rather than a haven. Thursday was a sharper test of that read. A governance shock hit the market's most important stock, and gold still fell. Our interpretation is that money that might have run to gold ran to the dollar instead. That's inference from one day's co-movement, not flow data. So what: gold is being priced by the discount rate right now, not by fear.

Last stop is credit, because that's the channel Nvidia's equity shock would have to travel through to become systemic. A Bank of America fund-manager survey found forty-eight percent of respondents name AI hyperscaler debt as the most likely trigger for the next systemic credit event. That's a sentiment read. When a risk is flagged that widely, it's arguably crowded rather than hidden. Separately, CoreWeave's five-year credit default swaps spiked above 1,289 basis points on November 5th, through their October peak. But that reading is a week old, and it predates both the CPI print and the resignation. There's still no post-CPI, post-resignation reading showing broad high-yield spreads pricing AI-financing risk the way single names are. So what: the transmission channel is identified, but it isn't yet in use. The test is whether credit desks reprice Nvidia's leadership vacuum alongside a higher discount rate. That combination, not either shock alone, would turn a single-name story into a market-wide one.

Three things to watch. First, October PPI lands at 8:30 this morning. It hadn't printed as of this taping, so treat any number you hear before then as unconfirmed. Second, Nvidia reports third-quarter results next Tuesday, the 17th, its first print under interim leadership. That's the first real test of whether Colette Kress's team can hold the line on guidance. Third, the first post-CPI broad high-yield reading, which will tell us whether AI credit stress is spreading or staying contained.

That's the Radar for November 13th. Stay sharp out there, and I'll see you tomorrow.
