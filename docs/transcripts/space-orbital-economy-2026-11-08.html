Cautious Optimism Briefings

Space & the Orbital Economy

Transcript · November 8, 2026

Good morning, it's Sunday, November 8th, and this is your space and orbital economy briefing.

Most of this week's space news was launch slips: Neutron, New Glenn, and Amazon Leo's lagging deployment. But the more important action moved to the ground side of direct-to-cell. Two companies are now running opposite bets on who owns the terrestrial network, and a catalyst-heavy week follows.

Start with SpaceX. On Tuesday, this desk relayed reports that Verizon, AT&T, and T-Mobile had all turned down SpaceX's request for network access. That left reported talks for EchoStar's Boost Mobile as the fallback. Since then, the posture has escalated twice. On Wednesday, November 4th, SpaceX said it had selected Qualcomm to supply radio and baseband technology for a terrestrial 5G network in the US, under the Starlink Mobile brand. Deployment is due to start in 2027. Starlink's senior vice president, Michael Nicolls, framed the ambition plainly. He said Starlink is building the first fully integrated nationwide satellite and terrestrial network.

Then, on Friday, Elon Musk went further. Speaking by video with Ron Baron at the Baron Investment Conference in New York, he said SpaceX could buy a legacy carrier outright. In his words, SpaceX could use that carrier's airwaves "ten times more effectively." He named no target, and nothing has been filed. The direction of travel across one week is clear. SpaceX is moving from renting access to the terrestrial network toward owning it.

The motive is in the third-quarter numbers. Starlink made up thirty-six percent of SpaceX's revenue but eighty-five percent of its operating profit. That is the one segment carrying the company's margin. SpaceX is trying to extend it into terrestrial wireless, not carve it out. No Starlink spin-out has been announced, and every step toward a ground network makes one harder to picture. The profit engine is being wired deeper into SpaceX. For SPCX holders, that means valuing the connectivity business as a would-be carrier, not just a satellite operator.

Now the skeptic's case, because the trajectory outruns the facts. The Qualcomm deal has a real technical scope, but its financial terms were not disclosed. Musk's carrier remark is intent, not a transaction. His "ten times" figure is his own assertion, with no stated basis. And the remark may be leverage rather than a plan. Its real audience could be the carriers that just refused SpaceX access, or EchoStar ahead of any Boost negotiation.

The fallback route also looks harder than it did a week ago. Boost has run since November of last year as a hybrid carrier, on network services from T-Mobile and AT&T. Analysts quoted by RCR Wireless say flatly that SpaceX cannot buy Boost and inherit those agreements. So two of the carriers that turned SpaceX down still sit astride its fallback route.

Spectrum is the other gap. Per EchoStar's own filing, the spectrum SpaceX has already agreed to buy from EchoStar doesn't close until late next year. A later add-on of EchoStar's unpaired AWS-3 licenses closes only, quote, "after all required regulatory approvals are received," with no date given. Then there's capital. SpaceX's capex already runs above its quarterly revenue. A ground-network build, let alone a carrier purchase, lands on an already stretched capital plan. So what exists today is a supplier contract with no price tag and a conditional remark with no legal substance. That's real momentum. It is not yet a carrier.

The opposite bet played out on AT&T's network. On November 2nd, AT&T and AST SpaceMobile said they completed what they believe is the industry's first handoff between a ground cellular network and a direct-to-device satellite network. Ericsson and Nokia supplied the ground equipment. SpaceX is trying to build or buy its way around the carriers. AST is integrating directly into one, using that carrier's own incumbent vendors. AST is also developing its own rocket, Blacklift, to control how fast its satellites reach orbit. That buys AST independence in space, not on the ground. For subscribers, it still depends on its carrier partners.

Notice the supply chain splitting along the same line. Qualcomm is now a network supplier to a satellite operator's terrestrial ambitions. Ericsson and Nokia sit on the AT&T and AST side. Direct-to-cell is pulling the terrestrial radio vendors into the space story, on opposite teams.

Now the caution on AST. The handoff is a genuine engineering milestone, but it is a demo, not a service. "Industry's first" is the companies' own characterization. And AST has launched thirteen BlueBird satellites, eight of them the newer Block 2 design. That is far too thin for continuous, repeatable coverage, and no commercial launch date is established. There's also a reading in which the demo matters more to AT&T than to AST. It shows AT&T a way to offer satellite fill-in without ceding the customer to SpaceX. That gives the partnership a defensive logic. It also leaves AST dependent on carrier loyalty, because a SpaceX with its own network would chase the same customers. The market has already priced that worry once. AST shares fell when the Boost talks were first reported. So the two models are now clear. AST bets the carrier keeps the subscriber and adds satellite fill-in. SpaceX bets it can become the carrier.

Who wins depends partly on constellation scale. The tracker KeepTrack counted roughly eleven thousand two hundred working Starlink satellites as of November 2nd. Other trackers count materially fewer, but on any count it's five figures against AST's thirteen. That points to where the binding constraints on direct-to-cell really sit: spectrum, distribution, and constellation density, not satellite engineering. On density, SpaceX leads by orders of magnitude.

Onto the week ahead. One call tests AST's model directly. The rest of the calendar tests launch cadence, the layer both direct-to-cell bets ride on. Monday brings two earnings calls. AST SpaceMobile reports, and the question is whether last week's handoff demo gets a dated path to commercial service. Listen too for how AST management frames the SpaceX overhang on its carrier partners.

Rocket Lab is also expected to report Monday. Its stock closed up more than fifteen percent on Friday, at fifty-three dollars and seven cents. That puts its market cap near thirty-two billion dollars. No Neutron news has been identified behind the move, so treat it as positioning or sector rotation. A rally into the print raises the bar for the call rather than signalling good news. The test is whether management finally names a quarter for the delayed rocket.

Friday the 13th is a launch doubleheader. ULA's Vulcan is targeting that date to carry forty Amazon Leo satellites, pending range approval. SpaceX is targeting the same day for Starship Flight 15, also pending regulatory sign-off. KeepTrack, relaying SpaceX's launch leadership, describes it as the first attempt to catch the ship at the tower. SpaceX itself hasn't confirmed that, so treat the ship catch as stated intent. Musk called a successful catch more likely than not on the third-quarter call. That's a probability claim from an interested party, not an engineering disclosure. And even a clean catch proves recoverability, not cost per kilogram. For the direct-to-cell race, the stake is density. Starship is the vehicle meant to keep widening Starlink's lead.

Ten days later, no earlier than November 23rd, Arianespace adds thirty-six more Leo satellites from French Guiana. Here's the yardstick for Amazon. Under its FCC license, Amazon must have all 3,232 first-generation Leo satellites in orbit by July 30th, 2029. Starting from fewer than four hundred, that works out to more than eighty satellites a month, every month. These two flights add seventy-six satellites combined this month. That still falls short of that pace, and two scheduled flights are not a cadence.

Pulling it together. What's genuinely inflecting this week is strategic, not technical. SpaceX is moving visibly onto the terrestrial layer, and AST has a carrier-validated satellite handoff. What's still a demo is almost everything downstream. That includes any SpaceX carrier purchase, a dated AST commercial service, and Friday's Starship catch attempt. One note on what didn't move. No fresh national-security space award surfaced this week, and nothing new emerged on China's megaconstellations after Tuesday's Qianfan launch. The open question to carry into next week is who captures the value in direct-to-cell: whoever owns the satellites, or whoever owns the subscriber.

That's the briefing for today. Talk tomorrow.
