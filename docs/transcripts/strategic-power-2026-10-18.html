Strategic Power, Energy, and Institutional Change

Transcript · October 18, 2026

Good morning, it's Sunday, October 18th, and this is your Cautious Optimism briefing on strategic power.

Here's the thesis up front. A sanctions law that was due to go live today did not go live. That tells you exactly what kind of instrument it was.

Start with what happened. The Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act was set to take effect on October 18th. Its headline tool was a tariff of up to 100 percent on goods from the five largest importers of Russian crude and gas, if they kept buying on or after today. On Friday, President Trump signed an order implementing the act. In the same stroke, he waived many of its sanctions and tariffs. That headline tariff on the biggest buyers is among the provisions waived.

Here's the callback. On October 8th, this desk argued that the act's mandatory tariff had a floor of just above zero. Congress had legislated a posture and left the price to the President. Since then, we've called today the test of whether the law was a tool or a posture. We now have the answer. Nobody was targeted, because the provisions were waived before they could bite. The President didn't set a token price. He declined to set any price at all.

Now the mechanics, because the White House's own text corrects the way this has been reported. The waiver runs through the act's own waiver authority. The President certified to Congress that the waiver is in the national interest. That matters. It makes this a lawful use of the statute, not an executive end-run around it. And the order has no sunset. In its words, the waivers "shall remain in effect until such time as the President or the Secretary of State" decides otherwise. Secretary Rubio's widely reported sixty days is something different. He said the President "wants to take the 60-day period of time" to analyze what sanctions and tariffs would mean for the global and US economies. That's a stated intention to study, not a deadline written into the waiver. So this isn't a pause with a built-in reset. It's open-ended, by design.

Now the why. Here it's worth holding open more than one explanation, because the evidence doesn't settle it. The first reading is accommodation of Moscow. That fits the Russian diesel license Treasury issued on October 9th. The second is protection of the US-China track. As this desk noted on October 8th, the five largest buyers of Russian energy include China and India. Firing a tariff at China would have collided with the trade truce Washington is managing with Beijing. On that reading, the waiver protects the China channel more than it rewards Russia. The third is domestic fuel-price management. Rubio's own mention of the US economy is consistent with that, but it doesn't rule out the other two.

So what would discriminate between them? Two things. First, whether the review ever produces targeting of any buyer, and if so, which one. Targeting India while sparing China would point to the China track. Targeting nobody would point back to Moscow or the pump. Second, any on-record reaction from Chinese officials or refiners, and so far there is none. Until one of those arrives, anyone stating the motive with confidence is guessing.

That brings us to leverage. A waiver with no sunset is an option the President still holds, renewable at will. That argues for leverage kept in reserve, not leverage given away. But set against that, every major buyer of Russian energy just watched a tariff Congress called mandatory fail to fire on its effective date. My read is that this cheapens every future secondary-tariff threat, not only this one. Secondary tariffs are an instrument of American market-access power. Their force depends on buyers believing they'll actually be used. So what: Washington kept the hammer, but everyone just watched it not swing.

The commercial data so far fits a quiet equilibrium, not a rush. The analytics firm Kpler expects India's Russian crude imports in October to stay in a range of 1.8 to 1.9 million barrels a day. Meanwhile, the Urals discount to Brent averaged about twenty-three dollars a barrel in September, which is still wide. Steady volumes at a wide discount suggest the waiver removed a looming risk for existing buyers rather than unlocking new demand. One caveat. That discount is a pre-waiver figure, and there's no quoted Brent or diesel move tied to the waiver itself. The mechanism is still worth naming, as inference. A lower sanctions-risk premium on Russian barrels takes some pressure off energy prices. That matters while the inflation debate is back on the table.

There has been pushback in Congress, though not yet an institutional kind. Senator Darline Graham, who holds the Senate seat of the law's late namesake, has broken with the administration over the waiver. Even so, her framing was notably restrained. She called the sanctions bill one of the tools the President can use, adding, "It's up to him whether or not he chooses to use it." Senators Cornyn and Blumenthal also criticized the move. None of them has announced a countermove, whether a resolution of disapproval or a hearing. So call this rhetorical friction, not a check on the executive. The open question is whether a statute that passed both chambers by wide margins can be shelved on its effective date without a vote. So far, the answer is yes.

If Washington has stepped back, the question is who is still pushing. The European Union isn't waiting. On October 12th, it adopted its largest-ever package of sanctions listings, covering nearly sixteen hundred individuals and entities tied to Russia's military-industrial complex. As we said on Thursday, a listing is not enforcement. But the direction of travel is the opposite of Washington's. And Kyiv has said it will resume strikes on Russian energy infrastructure. The coordinated-pressure model needs both sides of the Atlantic. With one side standing down, the largest buyers have less reason to hedge away from Russian supply. So what: the risk premium on Russian oil exports is now set more in Brussels and along Ukraine's drone flight paths than in Washington.

Here's the irony sitting next to all of this in the same week. Taiwan's economics minister, Kung Ming-hsin, confirmed that Taipei will add advanced AI chips and servers to its controlled-goods export list. Unauthorized shipments to China could then lead to criminal charges under Taiwan's Foreign Trade Act. The minister said Taiwan's measures "will correspond with those of the United States." Taipei presents this as a commitment under this year's trade agreement with Washington. It isn't law yet. An amendment still has to clear Taiwan's legislature, and there's no effective date. But notice the asymmetry. In the same week Washington waived its own statute, an ally is writing Washington's preferences into its criminal code.

The second-order effect lands on the supply chain. A criminal-law gate raises compliance cost and legal risk for Taiwanese server integrators and Nvidia's channel. That's the same channel where, in the Keelung smuggling case, most of an intercepted order still reached China. And the durability question cuts straight back to the lead. If the gate is the price of a trade deal, it's only as durable as the deal. A Washington that adjusts its own commitments this quickly gives Taipei less reason to treat its side as permanent. So what: American leverage increasingly runs through deals that bind partners, not statutes that bind the United States.

To close, the thesis again. The Graham Act was supposed to bite today, and it didn't, because the President chose not to let it. Washington's Russia sanctions are a discretionary bargaining chip, not a fixed constraint. Watch three things from here. First, whether Rubio's sixty-day review produces any actual targeting. Second, whether congressional criticism ever turns into a vote. Third, whether Taiwan's chip-export amendment clears the legislature. One more date stays on the calendar. The November deadline on China's suspension of its rare-earth export controls is still ahead, and that clock runs regardless of what Washington does.
