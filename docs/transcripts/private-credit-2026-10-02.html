Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Credit & the Corporate Credit Cycle

Transcript · October 2, 2026

Good morning, it's October 2nd. Here's the state of the credit machine.

Today's thread is a warning from inside the industry. One of the big credit managers now says the AI financing wave could turn private credit's core diversification claim into a single, correlated bet. And the retail money that would help fund it is still working down an exit queue, not building fresh confidence.

The warning comes from Carlyle's Global Credit division. It's a white paper published October 1st, written by Jason Thomas and Mark Jenkins. The headline number isn't Carlyle's own. Carlyle cites JPMorgan research from this past June, which estimates that private credit will finance roughly one trillion dollars of the AI compute buildout. That's more than half the industry's current assets under management. Sit with that framing for a second. This isn't a side bet. If the estimate holds, one theme would be financed on a scale comparable to everything else the asset class does. An industry that sells diversification across hundreds of middle-market borrowers would be carrying a single macro bet.

The concentration runs deeper than the theme. In coverage of the paper, Mark Jenkins is quoted saying that just seven or eight top-tier counterparties make up most of the underlying financings. That quote comes from secondary reporting, not the paper's own text, so hold it a little more loosely. But if it's right, the exposure isn't really to a sector. It's to a handful of names.

Carlyle's sharpest move is the historical parallel. From 2020 to 2022, software made up about half of private equity deals, and direct lenders piled into software credit behind them. The premise was cash flows that seemed sticky and uncorrelated with the broader economy. Then generative AI arrived. In Carlyle's words, it made technological obsolescence a risk shared across the entire sector. The uncorrelated thesis that justified the tight spreads broke all at once. Carlyle's verdict on today's version is blunt: AI-related lending is more speculative than SaaS ever was.

The structural point is about the cycle. Carlyle expects data-center default rates to move with the broader economy rather than against it. That's the opposite of what a lender wants from collateral meant to diversify a book. If AI exposure is procyclical, it adds to losses exactly when the rest of the portfolio is already under stress. Recovery and loss assumptions built for a diversified book would then be too optimistic just when they matter. Be clear about the status of that claim, though. It's an argument about the future. There's no default history yet for this collateral in a real downturn.

Two caveats on the source change how much weight it carries. First, the trillion dollars is a forward projection, not a measure of what private credit holds today. Neither the paper nor its coverage gives any figure for current AI exposure. Second, Carlyle has a commercial angle. Rivals like Blue Owl are far more visibly tied to data-center deals, so a warning about concentration also works as competitive positioning. What survives both caveats is the logic itself. Even as a projection from an interested party, the correlation argument stands on its own.

We've already seen what correlated AI-infrastructure risk looks like in a single deal. That deal is Project Jupiter, the data-center campus in New Mexico. Oracle is the tenant. Blue Owl is the developer and equity holder. A bank syndicate holds the construction loans. Oracle sent Blue Owl's project unit a force-majeure notice after the gas pipeline right-of-way was denied twice. And about eighteen billion dollars of those bank loans were quoted at 89 to 91 cents on the dollar. That's bank paper, not a private-credit fund loss. But the lesson generalizes. Power and permitting sit behind many AI-infrastructure deals at once. That is exactly the shared risk Carlyle is describing. And there's a marking corollary. Jupiter's bank debt has a quoted price. A comparable private AI-infrastructure loan doesn't, so its mark can lag the same stress for quarters.

Public investors in the managers themselves appear to be pricing something. Blue Owl's stock has fallen to roughly nine dollars and thirty cents. That's down 27 percent from its August high, according to Benzinga's figures, which are approximate. Apollo and Blackstone have each slid sharply from their own August highs too. That has happened even as the managers' reported redemption metrics improved. There are two honest readings, and the evidence doesn't settle between them. One is that equity holders are ahead of the non-accrual and mark data, pricing credit losses that fund-level reporting hasn't shown yet. The other is that this is a repricing of fee growth and rate sensitivity. Expectations of slower wealth-channel fundraising could hit these stocks hard with no change in loan performance at all. And that second reading has its own sting. If the managers most reliant on retail money face a tighter funding base, it arrives just as the AI financing ask is growing. The divergence from improving operating headlines is the signal. Its cause isn't settled.

That brings us to those improving redemption metrics, which deserve a closer look than the headlines give them. Ares Strategic Income Fund, new to this briefing, saw withdrawal requests ease to 13.1 percent of shares in its latest quarterly tender. That's down from 14.4 percent the quarter before. The fund still limited repurchases to the standard 5 percent. The useful number is the decomposition. After stripping out repeat submissions from investors already in the queue, net-new withdrawal requests were only about 3 percent of net asset value.

That partly answers a question we raised last Sunday. Is an easing queue confidence returning, holders who've already left, or fatigue? At Ares, it looks mostly like a backlog being worked down. Most of the queue is the same holders resubmitting. Fresh exit demand is now below the 5 percent cap. Two caveats. The split depends on how Ares classifies repeat requests. And it's one fund, one quarter, and it says nothing about credit quality. On the optimistic read, the backlog drains over several more quarters. On the cautious read, a stock of trapped holders persists, and roughly 3 percent of NAV in new exit demand keeps joining it every quarter.

Now tie it back to Carlyle. Semi-liquid, wealth-channel vehicles are among the pools of capital the industry would lean on to fund that trillion-dollar ask. They already ration exits at a 5 percent cap in a benign environment. Suppose AI-linked credit turns out to be the correlated, procyclical exposure Carlyle describes. Funding it partly with capital that can't exit on demand puts credit risk and liquidity risk in the same place, in the same downturn. That's an implication about the funding model, not a claim about what Ares holds today.

Before the verdict, be honest about what this week didn't bring. There was no new default or non-accrual data. The last hard read is from second-quarter filings. Per PitchBook, non-accrual debt at the ten largest BDCs reached 3.95 percent of debt at cost, up twenty basis points from the first quarter. That's deterioration measured in basis points, not a spike.

So where does the cycle actually stand? On that last read, realized credit stress is a slow grind rather than a break. The liquidity queues are draining, and nothing this week contradicts that. But the biggest unresolved risk in private credit is changing character. It's moving from scattered single-name marks to a concentration question: correlated AI exposure meeting a funding base with thin liquidity.

Watch four things to confirm or refute that read. First, the third-quarter BDC filings in November. They're the real test of the grind, because they finally update non-accrual and payment-in-kind trends past the second quarter. Second, alternative-manager earnings in late October and November. That's the first chance for Apollo, Ares, and Blue Owl to answer Carlyle publicly and disclose their actual AI exposure. Third, SB Energy, the SoftBank-backed developer building an Ohio data-center campus for OpenAI. It pushed its IPO back from September after struggling to find buyers at its target valuation, and its rescheduled window in mid-to-late October tests whether public equity will share the AI-infrastructure load. Fourth, Blue Owl Credit Income Corp's tender result, which closed September 30th and still hasn't surfaced.

That's the state of the lending machine. Talk tomorrow.
