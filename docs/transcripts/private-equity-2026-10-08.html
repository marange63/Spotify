Cautious Optimism Briefings

Private Equity & the Deal Economy

Transcript · October 8, 2026

Good morning, it's Thursday, October 8th, 2026, and this is your private equity briefing.

Here's the thesis for today. Exits aren't waiting for the window to reopen anymore. They're being built directly into the deal structure. A strategic buyer locks in a purchase right at entry. A general partner sells an asset to a vehicle it also controls. Liquidity keeps showing up, but less of it is being priced by an independent, arm's-length buyer. So the open question shifts from whether sponsors can sell to who actually sets the price when they do.

Start with the deal we flagged yesterday as unresolved. Yesterday we said the terms were the whole story. Here they are. CD&R and McKesson have signed an agreement to take Option Care Health private. The price is $32.05 a share, a premium of about 37 percent. The primary filing measures that premium against Option Care's October 5th closing price. The Financial Times report that moved the stock broke in after-hours trading that same evening. So this is a premium to an unaffected price, not one flattered by a leak. The deal is worth about $5.8 billion including debt, which is enterprise value, not the equity check. McKesson is putting in roughly $1.4 billion for a 49 percent stake, and CD&R takes the other 51 percent. McKesson also holds the right to buy CD&R's 51 percent at a later date. Expected close is the first half of 2027, so this is signed, not closed.

Here's what that answers. Yesterday's question was whether CD&R is a conviction buyer or a financing partner. The signed structure leans toward the second. This looks less like a classic leveraged buyout and more like a staged strategic take-out, with CD&R as the interim majority owner. It also extends a pattern this show logged last week, which is liquidity in slices. On October 2nd that meant partial sales on the way out. Here the partial strategic stake is built in on the way in.

Resist the easy reading, though, that healthcare take-privates are back. This is one deal, and it needed a strategic to underwrite almost half the equity. On our read, that is evidence a pure sponsor buyout at this size was hard to clear, not evidence of broad sponsor conviction. It also tells you who is paying for a 37 percent premium. McKesson's strategic synergies are the likelier justification, not CD&R's operating plan.

What the deal doesn't answer is the price of that future purchase. The filing describes McKesson's right only as a framework for its future acquisition of CD&R's interest, subject to specified conditions and regulatory approvals. There is no exercise price and no timing window. Nothing says whether CD&R holds a matching right to force a sale. That isn't a shallow-search problem. The terms simply aren't in the public deal documents yet.

The financing shows who carries the risk in the meantime. Secondary reporting puts committed debt at up to $3.15 billion, against $2.87 billion of combined equity from CD&R's Fund Twelve and McKesson. Those figures aren't yet confirmed line by line in the primary filing. On our own arithmetic, McKesson's $1.4 billion is about half that equity, which leaves CD&R's fund writing a check of roughly $1.5 billion. So CD&R holds majority exposure to a company carrying a bit more debt than equity, plus the operating risk, until McKesson acts. McKesson, meanwhile, holds an option whose value nobody outside the deal can price. And one more listed healthcare-services comparable leaves the public board. The bigger point is simple. The sponsor's exit is effectively pre-sold at signing, but the price of that exit is still a blank.

So why would a sponsor accept bounded, undisclosed upside instead of holding out for a clean sale? Because cash now is what the next fundraise needs. PwC's mid-year deals outlook put it bluntly. General partners going to market without credible distributions to paid-in capital face what it called an existential fundraising challenge. PwC adds that some sponsors are already accepting lower exit valuations just to generate the realized returns their next fund requires. That is a consultant's characterization, not a count of how often it happens. But it names the motive running underneath everything else in this briefing. Right now, certainty of cash is worth more than certainty of full value.

The other way to pre-solve an exit is to be your own buyer. That's what's happening in continuation vehicles. Evercore's review of the first half of 2026 puts GP-led secondary deals at about $65 billion, up 35 percent from a year earlier. That makes GP-led deals the larger segment of the secondary market. Other trackers count GP-led volume differently, so treat that as Evercore's number, not a consensus. On Tuesday we cited the record overall secondary total, and we said we had no pricing relative to net asset value. Now we do. Campbell Lutyens data puts the average discount to NAV on single-asset continuation vehicles at just 2.9 percent. That's still first-half data, not an October print.

Read that discount two ways. The first reading is that buyer demand is genuinely strong. Investors are paying close to the sponsor's own mark for a single, known asset. The second reading, and this is our analysis, is that the discount is close to circular. The selling general partner picks the asset, often anchors the reference valuation, and stays in control after the deal. So a near-par price is not independent proof that private marks are right. On Tuesday we said a widening discount would give sibling funds' marks an uncomfortable outside cross-check. A tight discount gives that check less force than it appears.

There's a second wrinkle, also our analysis. In these transactions, only the limited partners who elect to sell get actual cash. The ones who roll over keep a paper mark, under the same sponsor, in a new wrapper. So distribution numbers built on continuation-vehicle volume can overstate how much of the exit logjam has actually cleared.

The same logic shows up in NAV lending, which means borrowing against a fund's asset value rather than selling anything at all. A limited-partner survey found 86 percent support for using these loans to fund add-on acquisitions, and 71 percent support for refinancing existing debt. But when the loan funds a distribution back to investors, with no underlying sale, sentiment flips. Limited partners oppose that use by 62 percent to 38. That is survey sentiment, not a measure of how much money-out lending actually happens. Still, the pattern is consistent. The further liquidity moves from an actual arm's-length sale, the less comfortable limited partners get with it.

One more case belongs here. In late September, the Financial Times reported that Apax was in advanced talks to buy out Warburg Pincus's stake in the Dutch telecoms operator Odido. That would give Apax sole sponsor control. People familiar with the talks said an agreement could come within days. But we can't confirm whether anything has been signed since, so treat this as reported talks only. It's a third flavor of the same move. A co-sponsor buys out its partner, rather than finding a third-party buyer or an IPO window. Warburg gets liquidity, but the buyer and seller already know the asset intimately, so there's no independent price-setter on either side.

So where does that leave the cycle? Private equity is still producing liquidity, and the volume numbers are real. But more of that liquidity is being priced by strategics holding purchase rights, by co-owners, and by sponsors trading with themselves, rather than by open-market buyers. That isn't necessarily a crisis. It is a reason to discount the headline recovery narrative until it's tested by cash from someone with no stake in the outcome. Our private credit desk has been asking the same question about capped redemption queues, and I'll leave those mechanics there.

The biggest unresolved risk is still realized distributions versus engineered ones. The question is how much of what gets reported as returned capital was actually sold at an independent price. Two catalysts will start to answer it. First, watch for any proxy or amended filing that discloses the terms of McKesson's right on Option Care. That will tell you what CD&R's upside is really worth. Second, watch Blackstone's third-quarter call on October 22nd, the first big manager print of the quarter. It will start to show whether realized performance revenue is actually moving, or whether fee income is still doing all the work.

That's the state of the machine. Thanks for listening, and I'll catch you in the next one.
