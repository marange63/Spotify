Good morning, it's Friday, November 6th, and this is your semiconductors and compute briefing.

The Democrats' midterm sweep pushed the AI selloff into the silicon itself on Thursday. The move had been building all week. Memory names were already the hardest hit in the chip selloff earlier in the week. On Wednesday, the neoclouds fell even as the Dow rallied. On Thursday, it was the chipmakers' turn.

Here are CNBC's numbers, published during Thursday's session rather than at the close. Nvidia and Qualcomm had both dropped more than 9 percent. Nvidia had lost almost 450 billion dollars in market value by mid-morning. The Nasdaq had slid 5.5 percent, its steepest slide since April of 2025. Other outlets report a smaller drop for Nvidia, so treat these as intraday marks, not closing prints. Advanced Micro Devices, Micron and Applied Materials all suffered double-digit percentage losses on the day. CNBC's attribution is plain. Midterm election results raised concerns on Wall Street that the artificial intelligence buildout is poised to slow down.

So what actually changed? Our read is that this was a repricing of the political risk to the buildout, not of orders. In everything we reviewed, no hyperscaler has cut capex. Two days before the selloff, AMD guided fourth-quarter revenue to approximately 17 billion dollars. That compares with about 13.4 billion in the third quarter. AMD then fell double digits anyway, alongside the rest of the sector. That wasn't a reaction to its own print. It was the market marking down the whole category. The order book hasn't moved. The multiple did.

What sits behind the political fear is, for once, traceable to named people. Senator Bernie Sanders posted on the record, the day after the Democrats' sweep: "National data center moratorium now." In Texas, governor-elect Gina Hinojosa defeated incumbent Greg Abbott. She campaigned on exactly that promise for her state. In her words: "I will issue a moratorium, a ban, on all data center approvals." That's a real, quoted threat to siting and permitting. But it is not law. No federal bill, executive action, or state measure tied to this week's results has turned up in the reporting. What exists is a senator's social post and a governor-elect's campaign pledge.

One cause needs separating out cleanly, and that's Qualcomm. It's tempting to fold its drop into the political story. CNBC's own account says otherwise: "Qualcomm contributed to the selloff after issuing a disappointing earnings forecast for the current quarter." That's a company-specific guidance miss that happened to land in election week. It isn't evidence of a policy shock. Keep the two causes apart, because conflating them overstates how much of Thursday belongs to the moratorium story.

There's also a rival reading of the whole day, and it deserves fair weight. This trade was already crowded, and already falling before the vote. By November 3rd, the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index sat more than 7 percent below its October high. On that view, the election was the trigger rather than the cause. Thursday was profit-taking with a political headline attached. The breadth of the move tilts us slightly toward the political read. Over two days it ran from neoclouds to chips, memory and tools, which is what you'd expect if investors were marking down the pace of the buildout itself. But breadth is also what crowded de-risking looks like. The test that separates them is simple. A filed siting or power measure points to a real inflection. A quick rebound with no policy follow-through points to positioning.

Here's the frame we'd use going forward, and it's the desk's framing, not a sourced one. The usual split puts AI compute demand into two kinds: demand constrained by chip availability, and demand constrained by economics. Add a third, demand constrained by permission. Not by silicon, and not by returns, but by voters, permits and import licences. That's the political repricing we described. Thursday is the clearest sign yet of that third constraint reaching chip valuations.

And American voters weren't the only gatekeeper Nvidia ran into this week. Reuters reporting, relayed through secondary outlets, says Nvidia's H200s are piling up in Hong Kong awaiting Chinese import approval. Per that reporting, China has allowed only small H200 shipments, about 10,000 per major tech firm, while largely blocking large-scale imports. Neither Nvidia nor Beijing has confirmed it. The twist is that Washington already relaxed its own rule to let Nvidia sell H200s to approved Chinese customers. Washington loosened, and now Beijing is holding the line. Two readings compete, and both are our judgment. Beijing could be forcing domestic substitution toward Huawei and SMIC silicon. Or it could be holding approvals as a bargaining chip, to be released in a trade deal. Either way, Nvidia's China upside now needs permission at both ends, not just one. It's the same permission constraint, this time at the border rather than the ballot box.

Now the other side of the ledger, because the supply chain is still pricing for scarcity. TrendForce reports that TSMC is weighing CoWoS advanced-packaging price hikes of up to 20 percent, with some packaging lead times already stretching. That pricing report dates from late October, so it isn't new. TSMC has confirmed neither the hike nor any capacity figure, and weighing a hike is not implementing one. What's new to this desk is the capacity side. Goldman Sachs, as relayed by TrendForce, projects CoWoS capacity growing 114 percent year over year, to about 227,500 wafers a month in 2027. TrendForce also says packaging demand is broadening beyond AI GPUs into server CPUs and custom ASICs.

A price hike on a sold-out product can be rationing ahead of a capacity ramp. Goldman's figure is the size of that ramp. So look at the tension. On Goldman's numbers, packaging capacity more than doubles, and the price may rise on top. Both assume demand growth that Thursday's selloff just marked as politically contingent. The broadening into CPUs and ASICs doesn't hedge that risk. Those buyers sit inside the same data-center capex budget the market just questioned. Here's our judgment on where it breaks. If the binding constraint on AI deployment shifts from silicon to power and permits, packaging stops being the bottleneck sooner than planned. Then the scarcity rents in CoWoS and in memory are the first to soften. TSMC's leverage in the next round of price talks weakens with them.

Thursday's tape also shows where the fragility sits. Micron fell double digits alongside the AI names. So memory sold with the demand story, rather than trading as a separate scarcity asset. That's the correlated-bet risk this desk flagged on October 31st, now realized, and by a demand-side shock rather than a supply one. Applied Materials' double-digit drop fits the same pattern. Equipment trades as a pro-cyclical derivative of memory-maker confidence, and it's fragile for it. The takeaway is that the links priced on scarcity are trading as the most sentiment-sensitive in the chain, not the safest.

For contrast, consider the one shock this quarter that touched real wafers rather than sentiment. It isn't new, but it's worth revisiting now because it shows what a hard constraint looks like next to this week's political one. An explosion at Intel's Fab 28 in Kiryat Gat, Israel, killed two workers. Intel hasn't disclosed the cause. On Intel's third-quarter earnings call, CFO Dave Zinsner said the fourth-quarter guidance already absorbs the hit. In his words: "Our guidance includes an impact of roughly 300 million dollars to revenue and 200 basis points to gross margin." He added, "That's likely to be similar in the first quarter." Almost all of it, he said, is tied to 10-nanometer server products. Intel hasn't confirmed a restart date.

That's what a quantifiable constraint looks like: a dollar figure, a margin hit, and a specific process node. This week's political story has none of those yet. Fab 28 also shows that one fab outside Taiwan can still be a single point of failure. Geographic diversification alone doesn't remove fab risk. And the timing matters. Intel's server-chip shortfall lands in the same quarter AMD is guiding sharply higher, which makes AMD's EPYC the plausible marginal beneficiary. So the only measured supply loss in this whole story is Intel's, and it sits in server parts, right where its biggest rival is growing.

Here's how it ties together. Fabs, packaging lines and memory makers are all still planning and pricing for scarcity. The demand they're planning for now needs political permission, in Washington, in Texas, and in Beijing. The one thing to watch is whether any of this week's rhetoric becomes a filed siting or power measure. Without one, Thursday was a positioning event. With one, the scarcity rents in memory and packaging are the first thing to reprice.

That's the briefing for November 6th. Have a good Friday.
