We recorded Friday's briefing before the August consumer price print and refused to guess at it. It landed a few hours later, and it landed hot on the one leg that actually matters. Core consumer prices rose three tenths of a percent in August, a tenth of a point above most estimates, after two tenths in July. The headline was four tenths on the month and three point four percent over the year, and that part was in line. So the beat was narrow, and it was entirely in the core.

Composition is the story, not breadth. Gasoline rose three point nine percent in August, and on its own it accounted for more than a third of the entire monthly headline increase. Core, meanwhile, is running two point four percent annually, which is not what a demand-inflation regime looks like. This is the second inflation report of the week pointing the same way, because producer prices ran hot a few days earlier on the same energy pressure. And that pressure is a supply story, not a demand story. Middle East export constraints and production shut-ins kept Brent crude averaging ninety-one dollars a barrel through August. The distinction matters enormously for how the Fed ought to respond. It has not mattered at all for how the market is pricing that response.

Because that tenth of a point did something wildly out of proportion to its size. The Fed decides Wednesday, the sixteenth. Odds of a hike at that meeting jumped to around ninety percent on CME FedWatch, up from roughly seventy percent before the release. Take that as direction, not as precision. Kalshi and Polymarket were both reading meaningfully lower than CME into this same window, and this desk is not going to pretend one venue speaks for the whole market. And the meeting has not happened. Every probability in this briefing is pre-meeting pricing, not an outcome and not a forecast. What is worth sitting with is the ratio. One tenth of a percent on a single core reading moved implied policy pricing roughly twenty points in a session. That gap between the size of the input and the size of the reaction is itself the information. It tells you the market was leaning, not reasoning.

Now to the curve, where the shape changed. The ten-year Treasury closed Friday at four point nine six percent, with the two-year at four point six three. Do the subtraction and two-to-ten sits at thirty-three basis points. Midweek this desk was quoting thirty-nine on the par curve. So the spread compressed, and it compressed because the front end moved more than the long end. That is not a clean inflation-expectations repricing, which would push the long end. It is a market pricing policy tightening against growth. That shape is harder for equities to digest than a simple hawkish surprise, because it compresses the window in which risk assets can defend their multiples at all. It also means the forward guidance in Wednesday's statement matters more than the twenty-five basis points itself.

One more thing about the ten-year, and then I want to be careful about it. John Higgins at Capital Economics described five percent as a level some see as a threshold above which markets might go into meltdown. Treat that as psychology and positioning, not as valuation. There is nothing fundamental about a round number. What is real is that stop levels, risk limits and hedging programs get written around it, so a breach forces mechanical selling from people who have no view at all. Equities, for their part, have not repriced any of this. The S&P, Dow and Nasdaq all snapped a four-session losing streak on Friday, each closing up roughly a percent. Bonds moved to near-certainty on a hike. Stocks rallied into it. Both cannot be right.

So who is left to sell into that. This is the single most important number in the briefing. UBS data show commodity trading advisors carrying their largest-ever bearish exposure to global government bonds, having tripled those underweights in the second half of July. The book's sensitivity now runs about three hundred million dollars of profit and loss for every one basis point move in the ten-year. That is the largest such sensitivity in UBS's data going back to 1990. And the bank's own rates strategist says there is not a lot of room left to add to the short.

Two honest limits before you use that. Trend-following programs are mechanical, so this is momentum expressed in size, not a view on the Fed. And the three hundred million figure is a bank's model of its own data set, not an exchange-reported position. Read it as capacity, not as a prediction. What capacity tells you is payoff shape. The yield backup is running out of marginal sellers at precisely the moment the data turned hawkish. So the trade into Wednesday is asymmetric. A further hawkish surprise has less fuel left to run on. A dovish outcome, whether a hold or a hike paired with a telegraphed pause, has a very large book to unwind into, mechanically, regardless of what the Fed actually says.

That distinction matters afterward, and it is where people will get the tape wrong. If yields collapse Wednesday afternoon, you have to decide whether you are watching a short-cover or a story. A mechanical unwind exhausts itself and retraces. A genuine growth scare, or a Fed-credibility rally, keeps going and drags credit and equities with it. Those two look identical for about a day and imply opposite trades from there.

I think credit is late rather than right, because spreads at these levels are priced for no policy error of any kind, and the front-end-led curve is telling you a policy error is exactly the risk. Credit is the gauge to watch after Wednesday, not before it, because it has the furthest distance to travel from here.

That same policy-error risk runs through a slower channel, and this one compounds rather than offsets. If the Fed tightens while other G10 central banks hold or ease, rate differentials widen and dollar funding tightens. That raises the cost of hedged dollar borrowing for anyone funding offshore, and the squeeze lands first on the most rate-sensitive holders rather than on the broad index. So it pressures dollar-denominated flows into emerging markets and into speculative assets independently of what happens to US equity multiples. It is slow, it is mechanical, and it does not need a Fed statement to keep working.

You can see that channel in crypto already. Bitcoin opened Friday at roughly seventy-six thousand five hundred, down two point two percent from Thursday. So in this episode Bitcoin traded as high-beta risk and not as a hedge alongside gold. That is the call this desk made earlier in the week without the data to back it. Now there is data. Two caveats on that data. One week of outflows against an asset base north of a hundred billion dollars is a rounding error in stock terms, so the claim is about the marginal buyer's direction and nothing more. And ETF flows settle after the fact and largely follow price, so they are weak evidence of causation in either direction.
