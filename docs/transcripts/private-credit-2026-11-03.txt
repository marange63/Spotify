Good morning. It's Tuesday, November 3rd, and this is your private credit briefing.

The soft defaults holding private credit together have a clock running on them. This week we finally get a rough reading of how fast it ticks.

Start with the consensus view. On Monday, Moody's Marc Pinto told CNBC that private credit is holding firm despite the AI debt wave. That's the establishment read, and on payment-based measures it's fair. But calm payments are exactly what a deferral phase looks like. A loan that flips to PIK, or gets its maturity pushed out, keeps performing on paper until a maturity forces the issue. Last week this desk spent several episodes counting those soft defaults. Today the question isn't how many there are. It's what they turn into.

A Columbia Law School Blue Sky post, published October 28th, gives the first real answer. It summarizes academic research on a hand-collected dataset of coercive, non-pro-rata liability-management exercises. Those are the deals where one creditor group gets primed ahead of another to buy the borrower time. The headline finding is stark. Thirty-six percent of those deals ended in bankruptcy. Seventy-six percent filed, defaulted, or had to restructure again. The mean time from the liability-management exercise to bankruptcy was about seventeen months.

And the finding that matters most is the quietest one. Aggregate leverage does not decline after the deal, and credit ratings stay largely flat for two years. That's the mechanism. A transaction that buys time without cutting debt leaves the capital structure exactly where it was. It just stops the alarm from ringing for a while.

Now the honest caveats, and there are two. First, this is one academic sample, not an agency default study. It measures a coercive structure typical of syndicated loans and bonds with dispersed holders. Private credit's soft defaults are mostly bilateral or club-deal PIK toggles and extensions. There, a single lender or a small group holds the whole tranche, and there's no one left to prime. So it's plausible those amendments fail less often. That's my inference, not a finding in this data. Second, there's a selection effect. Companies that reach a liability-management exercise are already distressed. A high re-default rate partly reflects who ends up in the sample, not only what the tool does to them.

Even so, I don't think the private-credit exception rescues the story. Cooperative or coercive, an amendment that defers cash interest without delevering leaves the same unsolved balance sheet. And deferral costs more than time. While interest accrues in kind, the debt grows against an enterprise value that may not be growing. So recovery assumptions erode before any default is ever recorded. Here's the so what. The soft-default wave of the past year looks less like a closed book and more like a pipeline. If private credit's deferrals behave even partly like this sample, the clock points roughly toward the 2027 and 2028 maturity wall this show has been tracking. That timing link is my inference, but it's the right one to test.

If deferral isn't fixing anything, the next question is whether it's starting to show up in the books. Chicago Atlantic's third-quarter private-credit update gives a data point. Across the large business development companies it tracks, debt marked below eighty cents on the dollar rose sixty-four percent over the past year. That pile now stands at six-point-eight billion dollars. And nearly ten percent of those portfolios is now tied to borrowers with at least one distressed position.

There are two ways to read that, and they point in opposite directions. One reading is stress finally surfacing inside marks that have been slow to move. That's the lagging-valuation thesis this show has been running. The other reading is that managers are marking the tail down in something like real time. That would cut against the pure story that private marks never move. I lean toward the first reading, and here's why. Last Thursday we said the public discount on listed BDCs implied losses the marks hadn't yet shown. Marks moving in the deep tail, after a year of deferrals, looks like the books catching up to that price, not getting ahead of it.

Two cautions, though. A sixty-four percent rise can come off a small base, and this report gives no total-book figure to size it against. And "tied to a distressed position" is an exposure measure, not a loss or non-accrual rate. Don't confuse the two. The so what: the tail of the marks has started to move, which makes the marks-lag argument testable quarter by quarter.

The same quarter puts a number on the selection effect this show flagged last week. Per PitchBook LCD data, sixteen-point-four billion dollars of direct-lending loans were refinanced into the broadly syndicated market in the third quarter. Only two-point-six billion moved the other way. That's roughly six dollars leaving private books for every one coming in. The credits that can exit into syndicated paper are, almost by definition, the stronger ones. What stays behind skews toward borrowers who can't make that exit. That's the same population leaning on PIK and extensions. So the sub-eighty pile and the outflow ratio are two views of one process. The private book is being left with its weaker tier.

So what is that tier being paid? Chicago Atlantic puts current middle-market spreads at SOFR plus four-seventy-five to plus five-twenty-five. Hold that number with some care. Chicago Atlantic is itself a lender to smaller companies, so its note has a book to talk. There's also no prior-quarter figure in hand, so this is a level, not a direction. The open question is whether that compensation prices a book that's quietly tilting weaker. My inference is that it doesn't fully. But we don't have the data to settle it today, only to ask it.

And the risk doesn't stop at the fund wall. On Friday this show covered the Bank of England examining Barclays' private-credit swap marks. On Saturday it was Barclays and HSBC seeking credit insurance on AI-related debt. Today, a U.S. capital proposal we haven't yet covered pulls in the other direction. American Banker reported in September on proposed changes to bank capital rules. One would cut the minimum risk weight on certain senior securitization exposures from twenty percent to fifteen. Industry experts say that change could increase banks' ties to private credit. A separate change would trim the standard corporate-loan risk weight from one hundred percent to ninety-five. Both remain proposals, expected to be finalized by the end of the year, per that reporting.

Keep the size in proportion. These are a few points of relief on each weight, not a wholesale reclassification of fund lending. And the linkage they would deepen is already large. Federal Reserve Bank of Boston research, cited in the same article, shows bank lending commitments to BDCs rising from about ten billion dollars in 2013 to more than fifty billion by 2025. So the rule would accelerate an existing trend, not create one.

There's also a fair defense of the change. A senior facility to a diversified fund sits above the fund's own equity. Recognizing that subordination with a lower weight may simply be more accurate, not looser. The risk that matters isn't the expected loss on one senior line. It's correlation. Many banks are financing many funds, all exposed to the same deferred-default pipeline from this morning's lead. So what: supervisors are scrutinizing bank exposure to private credit just as capital rules may modestly cheapen it.

So where does the cycle stand? Last week this desk called it a grind, not a turn. Today I'd update that to a grind with a timer attached. The biggest unresolved risk is that the soft defaults we counted last week convert into hard ones as the 2027 and 2028 maturities arrive. And many of those loans are still marked as though nothing has changed.

Watch three things. First, Apollo reports this morning, after this research closed, so I have no figures on it. Treat it as a live catalyst, not a data point. Second, watch PIK share and sub-eighty marks as the rest of the BDC earnings wave lands. Third, watch whether that bank capital proposal actually finalizes by year-end.

That's the briefing. Stay cautious, stay optimistic, and I'll talk to you tomorrow.
