Good morning, and welcome to the Capital Markets Radar for August 13th, 2026.

Yesterday we flagged the July CPI print as the tiebreaker on the market's live rate debate and refused to call it ahead of time. It landed, and it landed cool. The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported headline CPI up 0.1% month over month and 3.4% year over year, down from 3.5% in June. Core CPI rose 0.2% on the month and sits at 2.5% year over year. That matched consensus. It didn't surprise anyone to the downside, it just came in softer than the hawkish fear that had been building.

Here's why that fear existed in the first place, and it's worth naming because it inverts the last two years' playbook. The Fed is holding at 3.50 to 3.75%, and the live debate has been whether the next move is a hike, not a cut. This CPI print pulled the market-implied odds of a September 25 basis point hike down to around 40%, from near 50% the day before, per futures pricing. The ten-year Treasury eased to about 4.66%, down from a 19-month high of 4.75% set just two sessions earlier. So the tail risk got thinner, but it's still a hike tail. This is not the 2024-2025 cuts-are-coming regime, and duration, the dollar, and gold are all trading off that different backdrop.

Gold is the clearest example of that distinction, and it's a nuance worth holding onto. Gold opened above $4,400 an ounce for a fourth straight session as hike bets faded. That is not the classic falling-real-rates trade you'd get from cut expectations. It's a fading-hike-odds and positioning bid. Different driver, similar-looking rally. Keep those two gold theses separate, because they'll behave differently if the data flips.

And today's data is the flip risk. July PPI is due at 8:30 Eastern this morning, after this record was cut, so I'm not calling it. It's simply the next test of whether this disinflation trend holds or reverses. Bottom line on the print: the Fed's live tail risk moved from roughly a coin flip to about two-in-five, not to zero. Still hold-versus-hike, just a thinner hike case.

Now, that discount-rate relief is exactly what's fueling something happening one level down, in credit. Investment-grade issuers rushed the market this week. Nineteen firms priced deals in a single session, the busiest day since a twenty-issuer session back on January 5th. Weekly supply hit roughly 80 billion dollars, the third-highest week of the year, with dealers forecasting another 40 billion the following week. August typically averages about 95 billion dollars of investment-grade supply for the entire month, going back to 2019. One week nearly matched a normal full month. Year to date, issuance is running near 1.4 trillion dollars, about 9% ahead of 2020's record pace.

Why does that matter? Borrowers are front-loading supply into a market where high-yield spreads sit near 272 basis points, versus a long-run median closer to 450. That's the richest, tightest decile of spread compensation on record. An easing-but-still-hike-biased Fed rewards locking in coupons now, before any reversal. That's not evidence of stress today. It's a late-cycle behavioral tell: thin cushion, asymmetric downside if growth or inflation surprises turn unfavorable. When there's almost no spread to absorb bad news, the repricing when it comes tends to be non-linear, not gradual.

The clearest single-name version of that divergence is CoreWeave. On its Q2 print, revenue came in at 2.575 billion dollars, up 112% year over year, with a backlog of roughly 104 billion dollars at quarter-end plus another 25 billion in commitments added early in the third quarter. Equity investors loved it — shares gained 19% on the full trading day, joining a broader neocloud rally that also lifted peer Nebius 34%. But look at the other side of the balance sheet. Interest expense was 640 million dollars in the quarter, more than double the 267 million a year earlier, against roughly 31.4 billion dollars of total recourse debt. And a 2.6 billion dollar delayed-draw term loan that closed just days before the print tells the same story from the lender's side: price talk started around SOFR plus 425 to 450 basis points, and it closed at SOFR plus 550, a blowout of 100 to 125 basis points, with a steep discount pushing the yield to maturity to 10.44%. Lenders also extracted maintenance covenants that have been largely absent from leveraged loans for over a decade.

So equity is pricing backlog and hyperscaler demand as the dominant signal, while lenders are pricing leverage and coverage risk as the dominant signal, and those two views are not converging. CDS markets had priced roughly a 50% five-year default probability back in late July — that reading is dated and pre-print, so treat it as directional, not a fresh verdict from today. But the loan pricing that closed just before earnings confirms the split was already hardening going in. Beneath a calm aggregate high-yield spread, credit is discriminating hard on the leveraged compute complex, and this is likely a template other neocloud borrowers get forced into even where their equity stories look strong. That financing-cost pressure feeds straight back into hyperscaler capex math and the channel assumptions underneath the whole AI-infrastructure trade.

Briefly, on the digital-asset side, there's a rotation worth flagging. Spot Ethereum ETFs pulled in 365 million dollars in a recent month, their best month on record — and for the first time on record, that topped what spot Bitcoin ETFs took in over the same stretch. That follows a rough first half for Bitcoin ETFs — 5.4 billion dollars in net outflows across the first six months of the year, the product's first negative half since its 2024 launch. It's a marginal shift, not a collapse — the very same window included a strong single week, 853 million dollars into Bitcoin ETFs in early August, with BlackRock's IBIT taking over 80% of that. But the direction is notable: institutional allocation logic may be shifting from Bitcoin as a digital-gold hedge toward Ethereum as programmable settlement infrastructure. And it raises a sharper question — whether Bitcoin is now trading as rate and liquidity beta rather than the macro hedge, a seat gold is quietly filling on the very same tape in today's lead.

Let's close on positioning, because it's the fragility underneath all of this calm. The S&P 500 is near a record high and the VIX sits near year-to-date lows. But beneath that headline, semiconductor stocks pulled back roughly 25%, and traders bought more puts on the VanEck Semiconductor ETF than at any point in its history. Fund managers went from almost fully de-risked to, by recent accounts, one of the most bullish Nasdaq options days in a decade. That whipsaw, alongside a quiet VIX, is the setup where a single data point — like this morning's PPI — can move this complex far more than implied volatility levels alone would suggest.

Pulling it together: cool CPI bought the market relief on the margin, but it didn't erase the hike tail, and the real signal today is that relief being spent — by IG issuers front-running the window and by credit markets pricing leveraged compute borrowers on their own merits, regardless of what the equity tape says. Watch PPI this morning as the next tiebreaker.
