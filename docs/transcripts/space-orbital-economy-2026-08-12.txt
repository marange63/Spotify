Good morning. It's August 12th, and this is your space and orbital economy briefing.

SpaceX reported its first quarterly results as a public company this week, and the numbers rewrite how you should think about the company. Total revenue hit $7.8 billion in the second quarter, up 92% from $4 billion a year earlier. That is the headline. The twist is where the growth came from. Nearly $2 billion of that year-over-year increase came from SpaceX's AI and compute division, cloud-services contracts tied to infrastructure buildouts, while Starlink and the rest of connectivity grew by roughly $1.7 billion. For the first time, the marginal dollar of SpaceX's growth is coming from AI compute, not from broadband subscriptions.

That doesn't mean Starlink is standing still. On the earnings call, SpaceX disclosed that its Connectivity segment, which includes Starlink, brought in $4.3 billion in revenue, up 66% year-over-year and 32% sequentially, and management said it's the one segment that's actually profitable. Subscribers doubled to 12 million from 6 million a year ago, with a record 1.7 million net adds in the quarter alone. So the constellation business is real and growing fast on its own terms. It's just no longer the fastest-growing part of the company.

The compute side is where the story gets structurally interesting. SpaceX said it has already contracted an additional $6.7 billion of cloud-services revenue, set to ramp starting in October over a six-month window. CFO Bret Johnsen guided to $100 billion in annualized recurring revenue by year-end. But when asked directly on the call how that $100 billion breaks down between Starlink, AI compute, and Starshield government work, Elon Musk declined to give a number, saying only that the mix is "probably similar to what we shared pre-IPO," just arriving a year early. That's a management team that was asked for the composition and chose not to disclose it. Meanwhile SpaceX still lost $541 million in the quarter, an improvement from a billion-dollar loss a year ago, but a loss nonetheless, even as revenue nearly doubled. The stock only closed back above its $135 IPO price on Monday, for the first time since mid-July.

So what does this mean. SpaceX is no longer cleanly a launch-and-satellite company you can value against Rocket Lab or AST SpaceMobile. It's becoming an AI-infrastructure compute business with a launch and satellite operation attached, and one that's still burning cash to build that compute capacity. That complicates every comp on the space side and it also plugs the sector directly into the AI-capex story that's been straining chips, power, and private credit all week. The same capital that's stretching data-center financing is now flowing straight through Hawthorne.

Now, the same company that's become the sector's gravity well is also the thing everyone else has to launch around, and that dependency is exposing real shortfalls elsewhere. Take Amazon's Kuiper constellation, rebranded Amazon Leo. Amazon's FCC license required half its 3,232-satellite constellation, 1,616 satellites, in orbit by July 30th. Amazon told the FCC it expected roughly 700 satellites by that date. Independent trackers put the actual count at around 330 production satellites across 12 launches. That's only about a tenth of the full authorized fleet, and barely a fifth of the way to the half-constellation milestone that just passed. The FCC declined Amazon's request for a multi-year extension but granted a narrower waiver removing the July 30th half-constellation requirement, while leaving the full 3,232-satellite deadline at 2029 untouched. The catch: any satellites launched after July 30th forfeit their original 2020 and 2021-round spectrum priority for twenty months, until March 2028, or until Amazon reaches 50% deployment, whichever comes first. That's not a paperwork slip. That's Amazon losing regulatory priority against interference claims from rivals sharing the same Ku and Ka-band spectrum, including Starlink and OneWeb, for as long as nearly two years.

The binding constraint here isn't satellite manufacturing, it's launch-vehicle availability, spread across Atlas V, Falcon 9, and Ariane 6. And with Blue Origin's New Glenn still grounded and Vulcan's cadence limited, marginal launch demand keeps routing back to the one provider flying reliably at scale. So the gap between an announced constellation and a deployed one just got a real price tag attached to it, in the form of forfeited spectrum standing.

And where cadence gets pushed for pace, it shows in the failure rate too. China's Long March 7A exploded roughly 85 seconds after liftoff from Wenchang on Monday, destroying a geostationary communications satellite, reported as ChinaSat 4B, though some Chinese state coverage used the designation Zhongxing-4B, which carries military associations. Neither the payload identity nor the root cause has been confirmed by CASC. What is confirmed is the tally: this was China's 56th orbital launch attempt of 2026, and its fourth failure of the year, following Long March 3B and Ceres-2 failures in January and the Tianlong-3 debut loss in April. Four failures in 56 attempts is still roughly a 93% success rate, so this isn't a program in collapse. But after a run of briefings tracking China's Guowang and Qianfan megaconstellation buildout as relentless, near-monthly cadence, this is the first real reliability crack in that story. It raises the question of whether Beijing is trading margin for pace as it races to bank orbital slots and spectrum priority before rivals close the window.

One more data point worth a beat before we close. National-security demand keeps scaling regardless of how the commercial side is performing. The Space Development Agency awarded roughly $1.75 billion for 36 more satellites under the Golden Dome missile-defense architecture, split between L3Harris at $955 million and Sierra Space at $798 million, eighteen missile-warning satellites and eighteen missile-defense variants, all expected ready for launch by the end of 2028. Sierra Space, a newer entrant, is winning close to parity with an established prime. And every one of those 36 satellites still needs a ride to orbit, so this quietly feeds the same launch-demand funnel that keeps routing back to Falcon 9. That's a second launch-demand pool, insulated from commercial pricing pressure, and it's growing even as commercial constellations hit deployment and reliability walls.

Pulling the threads together: the orbital economy is genuinely inflecting in exactly one place right now, which is compute-and-connectivity infrastructure at SpaceX, where real revenue and real capital are moving, even at a loss. Everywhere else, the gap between announced and deployed capacity is the story: Kuiper's spectrum penalty, China's reliability crack, and a defense-procurement track that runs on its own schedule. Watch two things over the next few weeks. Whether SpaceX's $6.7 billion cloud ramp actually shows up in October as contracted, and whether Starship's Flight 14, expected as soon as late August, finally delivers payloads into a genuinely operational deployment cadence rather than another qualified test.
